IBN UMAYL AND THE ALLEGORICAL COMMENTARY

Egypt · Muḥammad ibn Umayl al-Tamīmī (fl. c. 900–960)

// THE SILVERY WATER AND THE STARRY EARTH :: ALLEGORY AS METHOD //

An Egyptian alchemist of the tenth century, known in Latin as Senior Zadith, the Elder, who left almost no biography and left, instead, a method: take an inherited poem, an inherited image, an inherited saying of the ancients, and decode it, image by image, until it yields the operations and substances of the art. This article treats that method as its subject — what it preserved, what it proved, and what it could not prove.

I. A Man Known Only by His Books: The Thinness of the Record

Begin with what is not known, because with Ibn Umayl the not-known is most of the biography. Muḥammad ibn Umayl al-Tamīmī was an Egyptian alchemist who flourished in the tenth century CE — the floruit this article uses, c. 900–960, is the conventional scholarly placement, inferred from the authorities he quotes, from the authorities who quote him, and from the internal evidence of his works, not from a dated document of his life. No contemporary biography of him survives. The Arabic biographical literature that preserves lives, teachers, journeys, and death dates for physicians, philosophers, and transmitters in abundance preserves, for Ibn Umayl, essentially the name, the tribal nisba al-Tamīmī, the Egyptian setting, and the books. He has no anecdotes. He has no recorded master whose biography can be used to date him, no recorded pupil whose career carries his teaching forward under his name, no court, no patron, no city quarter, no tomb. The thinness of the record must be stated plainly, in the form this archive uses wherever the sources invite invention: almost nothing is documented about Ibn Umayl’s life, and this article invents none of it.

What survives is a body of writing — substantial, systematic, and unlike anything else in the Arabic alchemical corpus in its concentration on a single procedure: commentary. Where the Jābir corpus builds a system, where al-Rāzī builds a workshop classification, Ibn Umayl builds readings. His characteristic act is to take a text or an image that he presents as inherited — a poem, a pictured tablet, a saying ascribed to Hermes or Democritus or Maria — and to unfold it, term by term, into the doctrine and practice of the art as he understands it. The man disappears behind the method so completely that the method has to serve as the biography: a reader trained in the Greek sources, writing in Egypt in the first half of the tenth century, in Arabic, for readers he expects to need the ancients explained — and explained at a length that suggests the explanation, not the poem, is the real book.

WHAT THE RECORD GIVES

The name Muḥammad ibn Umayl al-Tamīmī; an Egyptian milieu; a tenth-century floruit (c. 900–960 in this article’s convention); a small shelf of Arabic works, one of them very large; a Latin afterlife under the name Senior Zadith.

WHAT THE RECORD DOES NOT GIVE

Birth and death dates; teachers and pupils by secure attestation; travels, patrons, offices, or incidents. Every fuller life of Ibn Umayl in popular literature is a construction from the works, and should be read as one.

THE LATIN NAME

In Latin he is Senior Zadith — Senior, the Elder, rendering an Arabic honorific of seniority, with Zadith a Latinised deformation whose exact Arabic substrate is itself a small philological problem. The Latin name is a reception fact, not a second identity: see Section XIV.

THE EGYPTIAN SETTING

Egypt is not decoration in his works. The temple vision of Section III is set in an Egyptian temple landscape, and his pictured tablet belongs to the same Egyptian furniture of vaults, statues, and pictured writing that the Greco-Roman hub’s Emerald Tablet article traces through the Arabic discovery narratives.

A note on method, in the form this hub uses wherever a tradition speaks in quotations. No invented quotation is given in this article. Ibn Umayl’s Arabic is paraphrased throughout, and paraphrases are labelled as paraphrase in the text. Where a published translation exists and is named — above all the extracts translated by H. E. Stapleton and his collaborators in 1933, and the modern Corpus Alchemicum Arabicum editions discussed in the Sources — the article names that access as the reader’s route to the wording, rather than dressing a paraphrase in quotation marks. The discipline matters doubly here, because Ibn Umayl’s own books are built out of quotations of earlier authorities, many of them otherwise lost: a modern article that invented his wording would be falsifying the very evidence — his value as a quoting witness — that makes him important.

// HOW TO DATE A MAN WITHOUT A LIFE //

The floruit c. 900–960 rests on termini, stated here so the reader can weigh them. He quotes authorities of the ninth century and the compilation known as the Turba (Sections IX–XI); he is himself quoted and used by later Arabic authors; and the Latin translations of the twelfth century and after presuppose an Arabic author already canonical. Termini give a window, not a birthday, and this article keeps the window labelled as one.

One further caution belongs at the front, because it governs the article’s centre. Ibn Umayl is the Arabic continuation of a subject the Greco-Roman hub has already treated from the Greek and Latin side: the alchemical reading of inherited images. That hub’s article, Alchemical Allegory, documents how readers trained in the art read Greek myth as a veiled account of the work, and it states its discipline in terms this article adopts without alteration: the alchemical reading is presented as the alchemists’ reading, a documented interpretive tradition, and it is never silently promoted into the inherited images’ original meaning. Ibn Umayl stands inside that discipline perfectly — indeed, he is one of its earliest systematic practitioners in any language — because his subject is not, in the first instance, myth at all. His subject is alchemical texts and alchemical images themselves, which he holds to have been composed in symbols deliberately, by ancients who meant the symbols to be decoded. Whether the ancients meant any such thing is a separate question, and Sections VI and XVI keep it separate. What is not in question is that Ibn Umayl believed it, built a method on it, and left in his books the fullest surviving demonstration of the method at work.

II. The Works: A Shelf in Three Titles

Three works carry Ibn Umayl’s name in the tradition, and a fourth element — the sheer mass of quotation inside the first of them — is in practice a fourth work in its own right. This section files the shelf. The detailed treatment of each title occupies the sections named in the cards; the point here is the shape of the whole: a commentary, a key to images, and an epistle — three forms, one procedure.

1. AL-MĀʾ AL-WARAQĪ WA-L-ARḌ AL-NAJMIYYA

The Silvery Water and the Starry Earth — his major work: a commentary (sharḥ) on an alchemical poem, unfolding its images into doctrine, and quoting the earlier authorities at a scale that makes the book a library of lost Greek alchemy in Arabic dress. Sections III–VIII.

2. KITĀB AL-MIFTĀḤ FĪ AL-ṢUWAR

The Book of the Key to the Images — a shorter work whose title states the programme exactly: the pictured figures of the art, the ṣuwar, require a key, and the book supplies one. Section XII.

3. RISĀLA AL-SHAMS ILĀ AL-HILĀL

The Epistle of the Sun to the Crescent Moon — a short allegorical epistle in which the two luminaries, the art’s standing pair, speak the doctrine in person. Known above all through its Latin life. Section XIII.

THE FOURTH PRESENCE: THE QUOTATIONS

Inside the Māʾ al-waraqī stands a second, shadow corpus: extended quotations of Hermes, Democritus, Zosimos, Maria, and the Turba, many preserved nowhere else in this form. Modern scholarship mines Ibn Umayl as a witness to Greek alchemy precisely because of this stratum. Section VII.

Transmission of the Shelf

The works survive in Arabic manuscripts, in the partial modern editions and translations listed in the Sources, and — for the Māʾ al-waraqī and the Risāla — in Latin versions that gave their author a second, European career as Senior Zadith (Section XIV). The transmission is uneven in a way the reader should register. The Latin West received Ibn Umayl early and read him hard; the modern Arabic editions arrived late, and the full critical presentation of the Māʾ al-waraqī is a twenty-first-century achievement of the Corpus Alchemicum Arabicum project (CALA), whose editions of Ibn Umayl — issued under the title The Book of the Explanation of the Symbols for the work known as al-Māʾ al-waraqī wa-l-arḍ al-najmiyya, 2003–2009 — are the current scholarly access named in this article’s Sources. Between Stapleton’s 1933 extracts and the CALA volumes, most readers who cited Ibn Umayl cited him at second hand, through the Latin Senior or through Ullmann’s catalogue. The article’s claims are held at the strength that layered transmission supports, and the layers are named wherever a claim depends on one of them.

III. The Temple Vision: Statues, a Tablet, and an Egyptian Ekphrasis

The Māʾ al-waraqī opens its authority-claim not with an argument but with a scene, and the scene must be told as what it is: Ibn Umayl’s own report, presented in his book as the account of how the pictured wisdom at the centre of his commentary came to be seen. This section expounds the vision narrative as he reports it, labelled as his report throughout. It is not presented as an excavation, a travel record, or a verifiable event, and nothing in this article builds a historical claim on its having happened. What it establishes — and this is considerable — is how a tenth-century Egyptian alchemist framed the origin of the images he spent a book decoding.

In paraphrase of Ibn Umayl’s report, then: he recounts entering an Egyptian temple — in some accounts of the narrative the place is specified as the temple (in other tellings, the pyramid) at Būsīr — and finding, in its inner chamber, statues: ancient figures in stone, among them a sage figure holding, or presenting, a tablet. The tablet is the vision’s centre. It is covered not with a running text but with figures — pictured forms, in the report’s terms the pictured, hieroglyphic writing of the ancients: human and symbolic figures arranged as a pictured doctrine. The Greco-Roman hub’s Emerald Tablet article, treating the same passage from the Tablet’s side, states the point in the form this article keeps: in Ibn Umayl’s discovery story a stone table rests on the knees of Hermes in the secret chamber of a pyramid, and the table in the picture is inscribed with pictured writing rather than with the Emerald Tablet’s text, which the treatise quotes separately. Figure and text are two different objects in the vision, and the distinction governs everything Ibn Umayl does with them.

The figures on the tablet, as his ekphrasis — his formal verbal description of a pictured work — unfolds them, form a small company of the art’s standing persons and things: an old man, the sage; younger figures; birds; the sun and the moon; a tree; the apparatus and postures of the work rendered as a pictured scene. The exact inventory varies in emphasis between the report’s parts and its later Latin retellings, and this article does not force a single canonical list where the tradition itself is fluid. What matters for the method is the form of the whole: a scene, with actors in relation — above and below, old and young, flying and fixed, male and female, sun and moon — whose relations, not merely whose identities, are the doctrine. A list of symbols can be glossed word by word. A scene must be read, and reading a scene, posture by posture and relation by relation, is exactly the labour the commentary then performs on the poem (Section V) and the Book of the Key performs on the images directly (Section XII).

// THE VISION, FILED AT ITS REAL STRENGTH //

The temple vision is a discovery narrative, of the same literary family as the vault beneath the statue of Hermes in the Sirr al-khalīqa and the temple vision of the Book of Crates, both treated in the Greco-Roman hub’s Emerald Tablet article: an ancient wisdom is found, hidden in a sacred Egyptian place, in the keeping of a sage figure, in pictured or aphoristic form. To file the vision in that family is not to call Ibn Umayl a liar. It is to locate his report in its genre — the genre in which, in his world, a book of the ancients announced where it came from. The historical content of the report is the framing, and the framing is genuine evidence: for what a tenth-century Egyptian alchemist thought the art’s pictured sources looked like, where he thought they were kept, and why they needed a commentator.

Two features of the ekphrasis repay attention before the article follows the commentary proper. The first is its Egyptian specificity. The vision is not set in a generic ancient ruin. Its temple, its statues, its pictured writing, and — in the Būsīr specification — its named Egyptian locality place the pictured doctrine in the monumental landscape Ibn Umayl’s readers could still see standing around them. Egypt, for this author, is not the art’s remote mythical homeland. It is the building he reports walking into. The Egyptology division’s treatment of Zosimos of Panopolis — the Greek alchemist of Roman Egypt whose visions Ibn Umayl quotes (Section VII) — is the archive’s dossier for the older, Greek-language form of the same Egyptian alchemical world, and the continuity of place between Zosimos’ Panopolis and Ibn Umayl’s temple is a continuity the tradition itself insisted on.

IV. The Poem He Comments: Sharḥ as Form

The Māʾ al-waraqī wa-l-arḍ al-najmiyya is, in form, a sharḥ — a commentary — and the form is the first thing to understand about it, because Arabic literary culture gave the commentary a dignity and a set of conventions that a modern reader, accustomed to treat commentary as a secondary genre, will otherwise miss. A sharḥ takes a base text — a matn — whose authority is presupposed, quotes it piece by piece, and unfolds each piece: glossing its words, supplying its ellipses, resolving its ambiguities, and marshalling the earlier authorities who bear on its sense. Qur’ānic exegesis, legal commentary, grammatical commentary, and philosophical commentary all work in this form. Ibn Umayl’s originality is to apply the full apparatus of the sharḥ — lemma by lemma, authority by authority — to an alchemical poem, and thereby to claim for the art the same textual seriousness the form confers everywhere else it is used.

The base text, stated at exactly the strength the scholarship supports: the Māʾ al-waraqī comments an alchemical qaṣīda, a formal ode in monorhyme, which the work presents as the composition of the author’s master — his teacher figure, the sage under whose authority the poem, and through it the commentary, stands. The ascription is part of the work’s own presentation, and this article reports it at that strength: the poem is ascribed, within the tradition Ibn Umayl transmits, to his master; the master’s secure historical identification is not established by the surviving evidence, and the article does not supply one. That restraint is not pedantry. A commentary tradition habitually attaches its base text to an authoritative name, and the name’s function — to certify the poem as received doctrine rather than the commentator’s invention — is performed whether or not the attribution can be independently verified. What can be verified is the poem’s role: it is the skeleton on which the whole large book is hung, verse by verse.

THE MATN

The base poem: an alchemical qaṣīda whose verses, quoted as lemmata through the commentary, carry the doctrine in the covered register — tree, birds, sun and moon, the old man, the waters and earths of the art’s standing imagery.

THE SHARḤ

The commentary: Ibn Umayl’s prose unfolding of each lemma — gloss, paraphrase, decoding into operations and substances, and a marshalling of earlier authorities in support. The commentary outweighs the poem by an order of magnitude; the poem is the occasion, the sharḥ is the book.

THE AUTHORITIES

Quoted, not merely named: Hermes, Democritus, Zosimos, Maria, the sages of the Turba, and others, produced as witnesses that the decoding of each image is the ancients’ own sense and not the commentator’s fancy. Section VII.

THE CLAIM OF THE FORM

By casting the art as matn and sharḥ, Ibn Umayl claims for alchemy a canon, a tradition, and a school discipline: there is a text, the text is difficult, the difficulty is intended, and the intended sense is recoverable by method.

The relation between poem and commentary is the reverse of the modern reader’s expectation, and Ibn Umayl’s book is unintelligible until the reversal is grasped. The poem does not need the commentary because the poem is badly written. On Ibn Umayl’s stated theory (Section VI), the poem is written exactly as the ancients wrote everything that mattered: in symbols, rumūz, deliberately — plain enough to be carried faithfully by any copyist, dark enough to be useless to any reader who lacks the key. The commentary is the key, delivered in the only form a key to a whole poem can take: the poem again, slowly, with every lock shown open. Nor is the commentary a mere glossary. A glossary would equate each image with a substance and stop. Ibn Umayl’s sharḥ does that, and then keeps going — into the sequence of operations the images encode in their order, into the proportions and regimens the authorities attach to them, and into extended doctrinal digressions in which the poem disappears from view for pages at a time under the weight of the tradition the commentator is determined to deposit alongside it. The book is a commentary the way a cathedral is a chapel: the form is real, and it has been built out, deliberately, into a summa.

It follows that the commentary cannot be sampled fairly by its lemmata. Read for the poem alone, the Māʾ al-waraqī looks repetitive: image, gloss, image, gloss. Read as its author built it — as a systematic decoding in which each gloss is checked against the authorities and against every other gloss — it is the most complete surviving statement of how the Arabic art understood its own symbolic language at the moment that language was at its richest. The next section follows the decoding at work, image by image, at the level of the standing examples the brief of the book itself puts in the foreground: the tree, the birds, the sun and the moon, and the old man.

V. Allegory as Method I: Every Image Decoded

The centre of this article is a procedure, and it is time to watch it run. Ibn Umayl’s commentary takes each image of the commented poem — and, behind the poem, each figure of the temple tablet — and decodes it into the art’s operations and substances. The decoding is systematic in a precise sense: the same image, recurring, receives the same decoding; the decodings are required to cohere with one another across the whole poem; and each decoding is supported, where the commentator can support it, by a quoted authority who used the image in the same sense. What follows is the procedure’s grammar, given through its standing examples. The individual equations are reported as Ibn Umayl’s decodings — the tradition’s readings, in the discipline of Section I — in paraphrase, without invented quotation.

The Tree

A tree, in the poem’s imagery, is a growing thing with roots below and branches above, drawing what is beneath into what is displayed. In Ibn Umayl’s decoding, in paraphrase, the tree figures the work’s matter in its growth: a single stock from which the operations branch, rooted in the body from which the work begins and bearing, in its branches and fruit, the multiplied forms the one matter puts on through the regimen. The decoding is behavioural before it is verbal: a tree is the right picture for the matter because the matter, in the vessel, behaves as a tree behaves — it is fed from below, it differentiates upward, it is pruned and grafted by the operator’s hand, and its fruit is not the stock it grew from. The commentator’s task, as he performs it, is to walk the image’s parts — root, trunk, branch, leaf, fruit — and assign each its operational sense, so that a verse about a tree becomes, under the gloss, a paragraph of bench instruction. Whether the poem’s author planted the instruction there is Section XVI’s question. That Ibn Umayl extracts it, consistently, is the text’s fact.

The Birds

Birds fly, and in the art’s behavioural vocabulary flight is the signature of the volatile: what rises from the body under heat, what leaves the vessel’s floor for its dome, what must be caught, condensed, and returned if the work is not to lose its spirit to the air. Ibn Umayl’s decoding of the poem’s birds, in paraphrase, works this register in full: birds that rise and birds that fall, birds that fight and birds that are caught, figure the volatile principle in its states — escaping, circulating, fixed at last. The image is one of the oldest in the art’s covered language, and the commentator’s authorities run with it: the volatile as bird is already at home in the Greek corpus he quotes. The decoding’s discipline is visible in its constraints. A bird that flies cannot, in Ibn Umayl’s reading, figure the fixed body; a bird brought down cannot figure a spirit still escaping. Behaviour keys the equation, and the equation, once keyed, is held steady across the poem.

The Sun and the Moon

The paired luminaries are the art’s most durable couple, and Ibn Umayl decodes them as the work’s two great principles in their celestial dress: the sun for the hot, dry, active, gold-bearing principle the Arabic tradition theorises in its sulphur doctrine, the moon for the cold, moist, receptive, silver-bearing principle of its mercury doctrine — the pair whose theory this hub treats in its own right in Sulphur–Mercury Theory. In the commentary, in paraphrase, their risings, settings, eclipses, and conjunctions in the poem figure the operations in which the two principles meet: the conjunction of sun and moon is the art’s marriage, the union of the active and the receptive from which the work’s offspring — the stone, the elixir, in the tradition’s several vocabularies — is born. The Risāla al-shams ilā al-hilāl (Section XIII) is this decoding promoted to a whole work: the sun writes to the crescent moon, and the letter is the doctrine of their union.

The Old Man

The old man is the tablet’s and the poem’s presiding figure, and Ibn Umayl’s decoding of him is the method’s most characteristic move, because the figure operates on two levels at once and the commentator keeps both. As a person of the scene, the old man is the sage — Hermes, in the tablet’s identification as the Emerald Tablet article records it; the ancient master, in the poem’s — the authority from whom the pictured doctrine descends. As a term of the decoding, in paraphrase, his age, his posture, and his attributes are themselves glossed into the work’s matter and regimen: age figures what is ancient, fixed, and enduring in the work; the seated stillness of the statue figures fixation; what he holds figures what the doctrine delivers. The double operation — the old man as the teaching’s source and as one of its terms — is not a confusion in Ibn Umayl. It is the method’s signature: in a fully symbolic text, even the author-figure inside the picture is part of the picture, and is read.

VI. Allegory as Method II: Rumūz — Why the Ancients Hid the Art

A method this elaborate needs a theory, and Ibn Umayl supplies one — the tradition’s own theory of obscurity, stated in his books with a completeness that makes him its most useful witness. In paraphrase of his stated position, held consistently across the commentary: the ancients — Hermes and the sages who followed him — possessed the art entire, and they wrote it down in symbols, rumūz (singular ramz): a ramz is a sign, a hint, an allusive token, a word or image that points at its sense without stating it. They did so deliberately, and for two reasons that Ibn Umayl’s tradition always gives together. The symbols hide the art from the unworthy — from readers who would misuse the knowledge, cheapen it, or come to harm by it — and they teach the worthy, who recognise in the symbol a sense they are prepared, by study and by the bench, to receive. Obscurity, on this theory, is not the ancients’ failure to communicate. It is their chosen instrument of communication, calibrated to sort their readers.

The continuity with the Greco-Roman hub’s allegory article is exact, and it is worth drawing in the open. That article’s first section sets out the rationale the mytho-hermetic tradition gives for the veil, in three registers: pedagogy — a story is remembered when a recipe is forgotten; protection of the art and of the adept — the knowledge was valuable, dangerous, and in many places illegal; and protection of the unready reader — the veil sorts, and the labour of lifting it is itself the training. Ibn Umayl’s theory of the rumūz is the same rationale, stated five to seven centuries earlier, from inside the Arabic art, about the art’s own texts rather than about Greek myth. The sorting argument is his argument. The pedagogy of the puzzle is his pedagogy. Where the later European tradition would claim that the Egyptian priests devised hieroglyphics, allegories, and fables as envelopes for a knowledge they wished neither wholly hidden nor equally known to all, Ibn Umayl — an Egyptian, commenting a poem by the pictured tablet of an Egyptian temple — states the doctrine of his own tradition in his own terms, without needing the fables of Greece to carry it. The Arabic hub’s allegory article and the Greco-Roman hub’s are therefore not two articles about two resemblances. They are two chapters of one documented history: the history of an interpretive conviction — that the ancients spoke in symbols on purpose — which the Greek commentators applied to Democritus, Ibn Umayl applied to the whole inherited corpus, and Maier and Pernety applied, after him, to the whole of myth.

RAMZ (PL. RUMŪZ)

A symbol, hint, or allusive sign. The term is the theory’s technical word: the ancients’ utterances are rumūz, and the commentator’s art is their unfolding. Ibn Umayl’s CALA title — The Book of the Explanation of the Symbols — is this word, translated.

THE HIDING

From the unworthy: the covetous, the careless, the merely curious, and the dangerous. The symbol is a lock whose key is fitness — study, discipline, and the master’s or the commentary’s guidance.

THE TEACHING

To the worthy: the symbol instructs by requiring labour. A sense the reader has decoded is a sense the reader owns; the rumūz are a curriculum disguised as a wall.

THE CONSEQUENCE FOR READING

If the ancients spoke in rumūz deliberately, then a plain reading of an ancient alchemical text is, by definition, a misreading — and disagreement among decoders is a problem of keys, not of the texts’ good faith. Section XVI weighs what that consequence costs.

One refinement of the theory is Ibn Umayl’s in emphasis, and it distinguishes his commentary from a mere codebook. The symbols, on his showing, are not arbitrary. A ramz is not a random word assigned to a substance by convention, as a password is assigned; it is drawn from the covered thing’s behaviour and appearance — the bird for the volatile because the volatile flies, the tree for the growing matter because the matter grows, the sun and moon for the two principles because the principles act as the luminaries act, in conjunction and in phases. This is the behavioural keying that the Greco-Roman allegory article identifies, in the Greek corpus, with the aphorism ascribed to Ostanes — nature delights in nature, nature conquers nature, nature masters nature, in Matteo Martelli’s translation — transferred to reading: a decoding is right when the story’s agents behave as their decoded natures behave. Ibn Umayl’s rumūz stand under the same rule. It is the rule that gives his commentary whatever discipline it has, and the rule whose limits — resemblance is cheap, and a determined decoder is rarely refused — Section XVI must weigh on the other side.

VII. A Library in One Author: The Quotation of the Ancients

If Ibn Umayl had decoded his poem in his own voice alone, he would be a curiosity of method. He is instead a major source for the history of alchemy, because the commentary is built as an anthology: at every lemma, the decoding is supported by extended quotation of the earlier authorities, quoted at a length and with a fidelity of attribution that turn the Māʾ al-waraqī into a library of Greek alchemy in Arabic dress. Passages of authors whose Greek is lost, fragmentary, or preserved only in epitome survive in Ibn Umayl’s Arabic in fuller form than anywhere else, and modern editors of the Greek corpus read him, in consequence, as a witness — a tenth-century Arabic manuscript tradition testifying to Greek texts of the first centuries CE. This section files the library’s main shelves and states, shelf by shelf, the strength at which a quoting witness can be used.

The use of a quoting witness requires a discipline, and the discipline should be stated as plainly for Ibn Umayl as this archive states it for any anthologist. A quotation in a commentary is evidence, in the first instance, for what the commentator had in front of him — for the form in which a text circulated in tenth-century Egypt, in Arabic, in the manuscripts available to one reader. It is evidence, at one remove and with the translation’s losses priced in, for the earlier text itself. And it is not transparent evidence: a commentator quotes what his lemma needs, trims what it does not, and — the sharpest hazard — may quote in the wording his own decoding requires, since the authorities are produced to support the gloss. None of this is an accusation against Ibn Umayl in particular; it is the standing condition of anthological evidence, and the modern scholarship that mines him — Stapleton’s generation first, the CALA editors in our own — works with the condition priced in, checking his quotations against the surviving Greek wherever Greek survives to check them against. Where the check can be run, his value rises: the Arabic Zosimos material, set beside the Greek, shows a witness transmitting a real tradition, not inventing one. Where the check cannot be run — where Ibn Umayl is the only witness — his quotations are used at the strength of a single, interested, tenth-century testimony, and this article so uses them.

The library’s existence also corrects a lazy picture of the Arabic art as a fresh start. Ibn Umayl’s commentary presupposes, on every page, an Arabic-reading audience for whom Hermes, Democritus, Zosimos, and Maria are already authorities whose sayings settle arguments — an audience, that is, standing at the end of the translation movement this hub’s Wing I describes, in a culture where the Greek corpus had been naturalised in Arabic deeply enough to be commented like a canon. The Jābir corpus had built its system partly out of the same inheritance; Ibn Umayl’s distinct contribution is to make the inheritance itself the subject — to write the book in which the tradition explains, authority by authority, what it thinks its founders meant. A tradition that produces its own commentator has reached a particular stage of self-consciousness. Ibn Umayl is Arabic alchemy becoming conscious of its library.

VIII. The Emerald Tablet in Ibn Umayl

One item of the library deserves its own section, because it ties this article to the Greco-Roman hub’s most argued dossier and because the two articles must not contradict each other. Ibn Umayl’s Māʾ al-waraqī preserves a version of the Emerald Tablet — the short Hermetic text in the voice of Hermes Trismegistus whose earliest surviving home is the Arabic Sirr al-khalīqa of pseudo-Apollonius (Balinas), compiled in the late eighth or early ninth century. The Greco-Roman hub’s Emerald Tablet article counts four Arabic recensions of the text between the eighth and tenth centuries, and assigns the fourth to Ibn Umayl: the Tablet reproduced inside an alchemical commentary — the recension through which the text passed, in Latin dress as the Tabula chemica, to a further Latin readership. This article follows that account exactly, and adds only what belongs to Ibn Umayl’s side of the story.

What belongs to his side is, first, the setting. In Balinas’s frame, the Tablet is found in a vault beneath a statue of Hermes, held by an enthroned old man, as a written text on emerald. In Ibn Umayl’s discovery story — Section III’s temple vision, as the Emerald Tablet article states it and as this article keeps it — the furniture has migrated: vault become pyramid and temple chamber, written tablet become pictured tablet, the stone table on the knees of Hermes inscribed with pictured, hieroglyphic writing rather than with the Tablet’s text. And the text itself has not vanished: the treatise quotes the Tablet’s sentences separately, as a text, in the commentary’s prose. Picture and aphorism, which the Balinas frame holds together in one emerald object, Ibn Umayl holds apart — the picture on the tablet in the temple, the aphorisms in the book — and his commentary is, in large part, the labour of relating the two. The migration of the frame’s furniture, as the Greco-Roman article puts it, shows the discovery narrative developing a life of its own inside the Arabic tradition, a generation and more before any Latin reader saw it.

THE COUNT, KEPT

The Tablet’s text runs to thirteen sentences in the usual segmentation, fourteen in editions that divide the closing sentences. That is the archive’s agreed formulation, stated in the Greco-Roman article and kept, word for word, here.

IBN UMAYL’S RECENSION

The fourth Arabic recension in the Greco-Roman article’s count: the Tablet quoted inside the Māʾ al-waraqī commentary, in a tenth-century Egyptian alchemical setting, with a pyramid-and-temple discovery frame of its own.

THE LATIN CARRIER

In Latin dress the Ibn Umayl recension travels as the Tabula chemica — the Tablet as a chemical, that is alchemical, table — inside the Latin Senior’s works (Section XIV).

WHAT IS NOT CLAIMED

No Greek original of the Tablet survives and none is attested; the emerald object belongs to the frame stories; and Ibn Umayl’s pictured tablet is not the Balinas emerald — the two frames are cousins, not copies.

Second, the use. Where the Sirr al-khalīqa appends the Tablet to a cosmology, as that cosmology’s epitome or its key, Ibn Umayl sets the Tablet inside a commentary — which is to say, he treats the most compressed text in the tradition exactly as he treats his master’s poem: as rumūz, symbols requiring unfolding. The Tablet in Ibn Umayl is not a relic to be venerated. It is a lemma to be glossed. That treatment is the seed of the Tablet’s enormous Latin commentary tradition — the centuries of gloss that would gather around its few sentences in the West begin, in form, here: with a commentator for whom no Hermetic text, however short, however authoritative, is self-interpreting. The next three sections turn to the library’s most consequential shelf, where Ibn Umayl’s quotations do not merely preserve a text but date one.

IX. The Turba Philosophorum I: A Parliament of the Ancients

The Turba Philosophorum — the Assembly, or Crowd, of the Philosophers — is the strangest successful book in the alchemical tradition, and Ibn Umayl is one of its earliest dated witnesses. This section describes the book as it survives; the next states, honestly and at length, the question of its original language; the third weighs what Ibn Umayl’s quotations of it prove. The three sections are a single argument in stages, and the staging is the archive’s discipline for a dossier in which a famous thesis has often been quoted as a settled fact.

As the Latin tradition transmits it, the Turba is a dialogue of the ancients: an assembly of sages, bearing the names of the earliest Greek natural philosophers — the pre-Socratic company of Anaximander, Anaximenes, Empedocles, and their fellows, with Pythagoras presiding over the circle in the work’s framing — debating the art before one another in set speeches. Speaker rises after speaker: one expounds the elements and the natures, another the work’s matter, another the regimen of fire, another the conjunction of the principles; objections are raised and answered; the assembly proceeds, speech by numbered speech, through the whole doctrine of the art as a parliament enacts a law. The form is unique in the corpus. Greek alchemy speaks in recipes, visions, letters, and commentaries; Arabic alchemy in treatises, systems, and — with Ibn Umayl — sharḥ. The Turba alone speaks in session: doctrine as the minutes of a meeting that never happened, of philosophers who never met, debating an art that postdates the latest of them by centuries.

The anachronism is the book’s open secret, and it is the first clue to its nature. The sages’ names are not a claim that Empedocles practised alchemy; they are the tradition’s dramatis personae, the ancient authorities of natural philosophy conscripted — as Ibn Umayl conscripts Hermes, Democritus, Zosimos, and Maria by quotation — to voice a doctrine their names certify. What the speeches actually contain, beneath the names, is the art of the early medieval centuries in its Arabic-period form: the elements and their qualities, the two principles in their pre-systematic dress, the operations of solution, coagulation, sublimation, and fixation, the insistence — which the assembly repeats like a refrain, in paraphrase — that the work’s matter is one thing, despised and everywhere, and that the speakers’ apparent disagreements are the disagreements of names, not of doctrine. That last insistence is the Turba’s hermeneutic in miniature, and it is Ibn Umayl’s: many voices, many symbols, one sense, recoverable by the reader who holds the key. It is no accident that a commentator of Ibn Umayl’s convictions quoted the Turba heavily. The assembly is a commentary that has eaten its base text and speaks in authorities instead.

THE FORM

A dialogue in set speeches — a parliament of pre-Socratic-named sages debating the art in session, under Pythagoras’s presidency in the framing. No other alchemical work is built this way.

THE DOCTRINE

The early medieval art in speech form: elements and natures, the one matter under many names, the operations and the regimen, and the refrain that the sages differ in words, not in sense.

THE ARABIC TITLE IN THE TRADITION

The Arabic original, where the scholarship posits one, is cited under the title Kitāb Muṣḥaf al-jamāʿa — the Book of the Assembly’s Codex — a title given here with the question mark Section X argues it must always carry.

THE SURVIVING WHOLE

The complete Turba survives in Latin — printed in 1572 among the early collections of the art — from a Latin translation of an Arabic original that is lost or, more precisely, unidentified among surviving Arabic manuscripts. That asymmetry is the whole of Section X.

X. The Turba Philosophorum II: The Arabic-Original Question

State the evidentiary position first, in one paragraph, because everything in this section is commentary on it. The Turba Philosophorum survives complete only in Latin. No complete Arabic Turba is known to survive. The Latin text presents itself, by its character and its tradition, as a translation from Arabic. An Arabic original is therefore posited by the scholarship — lost, or surviving unidentified, or surviving in part in quotations and cognate texts — and the positing is where agreement ends. When the Arabic original was composed, out of what Greek materials, by whom, and how faithfully the Latin represents it: on all of this the scholarship has argued for a century, and the argument is not closed. The Arabic-original question remains, in this article and in the scholarship it follows, visibly a question.

Ruska’s Thesis (1931)

The founding modern treatment is Julius Ruska’s Turba Philosophorum: Ein Beitrag zur Erforschung der Alchemie des Mittelalters (Berlin, 1931) — the same Ruska whose Tabula Smaragdina (1926) had established the Emerald Tablet’s Arabic priority five years earlier. Ruska’s thesis, in paraphrase of his argued position: the Turba’s Arabic original was composed around the year 900, by an Arabic author working from Greek sources — Greek alchemical and philosophical material, of the kind the translation movement had made available in Arabic, reworked into the dialogue’s assembly form. The thesis explains the book’s double character at a stroke: Greek sages and Greek doctrine, because the sources were Greek; Arabic composition and Arabic doctrine in its details, because the composer was an Arabic author of about 900; Latin survival, because the Latin translators of the twelfth century took the Arabic book west and the Arabic exemplar tradition then failed where the Latin succeeded. Ruska’s dating also made a prediction, whether or not he stated it as one: an Arabic Turba of c. 900 should be quotable by Arabic authors of the following generations. Section XI examines the witness who fulfils the prediction — the author of this article.

The Debate After Ruska: Plessner and the Reworking Problem

The thesis did not go unexamined. Martin Plessner, whose studies of the Turba and of the Arabic transmission of Greek science are the mid-century’s weightiest engagement with Ruska’s construction, pressed the questions Ruska’s elegance had smoothed over — in paraphrase of the debate’s main lines: how much of the surviving Latin Turba is the posited Arabic original, and how much is Latin-era reworking — expansion, rearrangement, and doctrinal tidying performed in the Latin transmission itself, by translators and editors whose own art differed from the Arabic book’s? The speeches’ order, the assembly’s framing, and individual doctrines have all been argued, passage by passage, between an Arabic core faithfully translated and a Latin surface substantially recomposed. Plessner’s own work on the Turba’s place in the development of alchemy — including his 1954 Isis study of that title — kept the Arabic origin while complicating the Latin text’s transparency: the Latin Turba is a witness to an Arabic book, but a witness that has itself been edited by its journey. Later reference scholarship — Manfred Ullmann’s Die Natur- und Geheimwissenschaften im Islam (1972), the standard handbook of the Arabic occult and natural sciences, among it — catalogues the Turba in the Arabic tradition while registering the same open seams. The debate’s present state, stated fairly, is this: an Arabic original of roughly Ruska’s date is the majority construction and the one this article’s argument uses; the Latin text’s fidelity to that original, in detail, remains contested; and no Arabic manuscript has appeared to settle the contest from the other side.

// A QUESTION, KEPT VISIBLE //

Three things must not be conflated, because popular accounts conflate all three. (1) That the Latin Turba derives from Arabic transmission: supported by the text’s character and by the quotations of Section XI, and not seriously doubted. (2) That a single Arabic book, composed c. 900 from Greek sources, stands behind the Latin as Ruska constructed it: the leading thesis, held as a thesis. (3) That the Arabic original can be quoted, dated to a decade, or assigned an author: not established by any surviving evidence. This article asserts (1), reports (2) as Ruska’s thesis with Plessner’s complications, and declines (3). The question mark in Section IX’s card — Kitāb Muṣḥaf al-jamāʿa? — is the dossier in one punctuation mark.

Why does the question matter enough for three sections? Because the Turba is a load-bearing text in two histories at once. In the history of Latin alchemy, it is a founding authority, and its date and provenance fix the character of what the Latin masters received: Greek wisdom at first hand, or Arabic doctrine in Greek dress. In the history of Arabic alchemy, it is a test case for the transmission this hub exists to document: if Ruska is right, the Turba is the translation movement’s most creative product — Greek sources so thoroughly digested that they could be reissued as the Greeks’ own assembly. Either way, the evidence that disciplines the construction is quotation evidence: dated authors, quoting Turba material, in datable books. The earliest and weightiest of those authors is Ibn Umayl.

XI. The Turba Philosophorum III: What Ibn Umayl’s Quotations Prove

Ibn Umayl quotes the Turba. The quotations stand in the Māʾ al-waraqī, attributed to the assembly and its sages, produced — in his commentary’s manner — as authorities for the decoding of the poem’s images. That simple bibliographic fact is the hardest evidence in the whole Turba dossier, and this section’s business is to state, with pedantic exactness, what it establishes and what it does not. The pedantry is the point: the Turba’s Arabic-original question has been damaged less by scepticism than by witnesses made to testify beyond their strength.

WHAT THE QUOTATIONS ESTABLISH: A TERMINUS

That a Turba-like Arabic text circulated by the mid-tenth century — by Ibn Umayl’s floruit — in Egypt, in a form an Arabic commentator could quote as an authority. His citations are the terminus evidence: the Arabic Turba tradition is at least this old, whatever else is disputed.

WHAT THEY ESTABLISH: ARABIC, NOT LATIN, PRIORITY OF CIRCULATION

Ibn Umayl quotes the material in Arabic, two centuries before the Latin translation. Whatever the Latin text’s later reworking, the tradition he attests was already an Arabic tradition, circulating in the Arabic art’s own commentary literature.

WHAT THEY DO NOT ESTABLISH: THE LATIN TEXT’S FIDELITY

Quotations of Turba material do not prove that the complete Latin Turba, speech for speech and in its surviving order, reproduces Ibn Umayl’s Arabic book. Plessner’s reworking problem (Section X) survives Ibn Umayl’s evidence untouched: he attests a tradition, not the Latin edition of it.

WHAT THEY DO NOT ESTABLISH: RUSKA’S FULL CONSTRUCTION

Nor do they prove the c. 900 composition date, the single Arabic author, or the Greek-source stemma of Ruska’s thesis in its detail. They are consistent with that construction — an Arabic Turba must predate its quotation by an Egyptian commentator of c. 900–960 — and consistency is corroboration, not proof. The thesis remains a thesis, now with a dated witness beneath its lower edge.

Put the positive result at its full, exact strength, because at that strength it is genuinely strong. Before Ibn Umayl’s quotations are priced in, the Arabic Turba is an inference from a Latin text’s character — a good inference, but an inference that a determined Latinist could resist. After them, the Arabic Turba is an attested circulation: a named Arabic author, in a datable Arabic book, in tenth-century Egypt, quoting the assembly’s sages as authorities his readers are expected to recognise. The inference has acquired a witness. That is what Ibn Umayl, the commentator who preserved so much else, preserves for the Turba: not its text entire, not its original, but its date-line — the proof that by his lifetime the parliament of the ancients was already sitting, in Arabic, and already canonical enough to gloss a poem by.

There is a final, quieter consequence, and it returns the dossier to this article’s centre. Ibn Umayl quotes the Turba the way he quotes Hermes, Democritus, Zosimos, and Maria: as a voice in the library, one authority among the ancients. For him, the Turba’s sages are not a puzzle of transmission; they are simply the ancients, assembled — the rumūz-speaking founders whose symbols his commentary exists to unfold. The modern scholar who separates the Turba out, dates it, and argues over its original is doing work Ibn Umayl never attempted, on a book Ibn Umayl treated as beyond argument. Both relations to the text are documented in this article, at their strengths: his, as the tenth-century reception that happens to be our best evidence; ours, as the open question his reception helps, and cannot finish helping, to answer.

XII. The Book of the Key to the Images: Ṣuwar and the Visual Commentary

The Kitāb al-Miftāḥ fī al-ṣuwar — The Book of the Key to the Images — is the shortest road into Ibn Umayl’s mind, because its title is a table of contents for his whole method. The ṣuwar (singular ṣūra) are the images: the pictured figures of the temple tablet, and by extension every pictured or pictured-in-words figure in which the ancients deposited the doctrine. A miftāḥ is a key: not a summary, not an appreciation, but an instrument that opens what is locked. The book’s title therefore promises, in four words, the entire programme of Sections V and VI — images, locked on purpose, and a key cut to open them — addressed to the images directly, without the commented poem as intermediary.

The work’s scale and survival should be stated at their real strength. The Miftāḥ is a shorter work than the Māʾ al-waraqī, and it has been less fully served by modern editions than the great commentary; this article treats it as the programme’s compact statement, known through the manuscript tradition and the modern catalogues and studies listed in the Sources, and it builds no claim on the Miftāḥ that requires a fuller text than that transmission currently puts in general circulation. What can be said on that basis is still considerable. The Miftāḥ presupposes the temple tablet’s company of figures and treats them as a system: the figures are not a gallery of separate emblems but a pictured sentence, whose sense lies in the figures’ identities, attributes, and relations taken together — the same relational reading the commentary performs on the poem’s verses. A key to images, in Ibn Umayl’s handling, is therefore not a picture-dictionary. It is a grammar: who stands, who flies, who holds what, who faces whom — and what operation, in each configuration, the sentence performs.

ṢŪRA (PL. ṢUWAR)

Image, figure, form. The word ranges over the pictured figures of the tablet, the emblematic figures of the texts, and — in the wider Arabic philosophical vocabulary Ibn Umayl’s readers brought to the book — form itself. The range is a resource, and a hazard, of his terminology.

MIFTĀḤ

Key. The genre-word of the programme: the same claim the sharḥ makes for the poem, the Miftāḥ makes for the pictures — they are locked, deliberately, and openable by method.

THE TABLET AS SENTENCE

The figures read relationally: posture, attribute, and facing as syntax; identity as vocabulary. The Miftāḥ is the tablet’s grammar, where the commentary is the poem’s.

THE VISUAL LINE FORWARD

The pictured doctrine Ibn Umayl keys is the ancestor, by transmission through the Latin Senior, of the Latin art’s pictured books — the line Section XV follows into the Latin manuscripts.

XIII. The Epistle of the Sun to the Crescent Moon

The Risāla al-shams ilā al-hilāl — The Epistle of the Sun to the Crescent Moon — is Ibn Umayl’s smallest work and, by the reach of its Latin life, disproportionately his most travelled. Its form is a letter between the two luminaries whose decoding Section V filed as the commentary’s standing couple: the sun, the art’s active, hot, gold-bearing principle, writes to the crescent moon, the receptive, moist, silver-bearing principle, and the letter’s discourse — in paraphrase of its allegorical course — is the doctrine of their relation: the moon’s light as the sun’s gift, her waxing as his approach, their conjunction as the union from which the work’s offspring proceeds. What the commentary glosses as an equation, the epistle performs as a courtship. The two principles do not merely correspond to the luminaries; in the epistle they are the luminaries, speaking in person, in the first person, in the art’s most intimate register.

The choice of the crescent — al-hilāl, the new moon’s first visible arc — is the epistle’s precision, and it repays the behavioural reading Ibn Umayl taught his readers to perform. A full moon is a completed thing; a crescent is a beginning that visibly promises completion, a receptivity caught at the moment of first illumination. The epistle’s moon is the mercurial principle at the work’s opening: dark in herself, newly lit by the solar principle, and destined — if the regimen holds — to wax to the fullness in which sun and moon, gold and silver, the fixed and the volatile, stand in their perfected relation. The astronomy is exact, the allegory is keyed to it point by point, and the whole miniature is the method of Section V distilled to a single image-pair and allowed to speak. Readers who find the great commentary’s scale forbidding meet Ibn Umayl entire in the epistle: the rumūz, the behavioural keying, the marriage of the principles, and the conviction that a natural relation, rightly contemplated, is an alchemical instruction.

The epistle also marks the limit of Ibn Umayl’s range, and the limit is informative. He is not a system-builder on the Jabirian scale, not a classifier on al-Rāzī’s, and not a recipe-writer on the Greek model at all. His three works are a commentary, a key, and an allegorical letter: three forms of reading. Even his most original-seeming composition, the epistle, is a reading performed in the first person — the sun and moon of the tablet and the poem, unfolded until they can speak their own gloss. The Arabic art produced greater chemists. It produced no greater reader, and the Latin West, which took from Arabic alchemy its systems and its substances, took from Ibn Umayl something it could get nowhere else in the same purity: a hermeneutics — a complete, practised, self-aware art of reading the art. The next section follows that export through the door.

XIV. Senior Zadith: The Latin Life

Through the Latin door — the twelfth-century translation movement from Arabic into Latin whose alchemical threshold this archive dates to Robert of Chester’s 1144 Liber de compositione alchemiae and treats in full in The Latin Door — Ibn Umayl passed west and became Senior Zadith. The name is a small transmission history in itself: Senior, the Elder, renders the Arabic honorific of the aged master — the old man of his own tablet, one might say, promoted to his author’s title — and Zadith carries an Arabic patronymic element deformed past certain recovery in its Latin passage. Under that name, the Māʾ al-waraqī circulated in Latin dress as the De chemia — Senior’s book On the Art, in the period’s spelling of the art’s name — accompanied by the Sun-to-the-Moon epistle, and Senior Zadith took his seat among the Latin authorities: quoted by the medieval masters, excerpted in the florilegia, and accorded the particular reverence the Latin art reserved for an author it believed both ancient and systematic.

Print fixed the Latin form. The De chemia was printed in 1572 in the Artis auriferae quam chemiam vocant — the great Basel collection of the art’s Latin classics, the same 1572 print horizon at which the complete Latin Turba also reached print (Section IX). The coincidence is a transmission fact worth pausing on: the commentator and one of his principal quoted authorities entered European print together, in the same collection, from the same Latin manuscript world — Ibn Umayl’s library reassembled, five centuries on, as a Latin bookshelf. A Latin reader of 1572 who opened the Artis auriferae met Senior and the Turba as companion ancients, and could not have known — what Ruska’s and Stapleton’s generations recovered — that the companionship was older than Latin: that Senior had quoted the assembly, in Arabic, in Egypt, six hundred years before Basel.

XV. The Images’ Afterlife in Latin Manuscripts

The temple tablet did not stay in its temple. Ibn Umayl’s pictured figures — the company of the tablet, the old man with his board of symbols, the birds, the tree, the luminaries in their relations — passed with the De chemia into the Latin manuscript tradition, and there they began a second life as pictures to be copied. Latin manuscripts of Senior’s works transmit the tablet’s scene as drawn images, with the figures disposed as the ekphrasis describes them; copyists redrew them, generation by generation, with the fidelity and the drift that hand-copying always mixes. The pictured tablet became one of the Latin art’s canonical images: a founding picture, attributed to the deepest antiquity the tradition could picture, of the doctrine pictured whole.

The afterlife matters to this article for a reason of method, not merely of art history. Ibn Umayl’s conviction — Section XII’s unity of text and picture as two scripts of one symbolic language — is enacted by the Latin transmission whether or not any Latin copyist held it as a theory. The Latin Senior is a book in which a picture is quoted, exactly as a saying is quoted: reproduced from an exemplar, set among prose glosses, and treated as evidence. The tradition that received him built, in the following centuries, the most picture-saturated literature in pre-modern science — the emblem books, the pictured Atalanta Fugiens of the Greco-Roman article’s Section XII, the whole iconography of kings and queens, birds and trees, suns and moons, in which the Latin art learned to think. Ibn Umayl did not cause that literature single-handed, and this article does not claim he did. It claims the documented segment: that his works carried a pictured doctrine, with its key, through the Latin door; that the picture was copied as doctrine in Latin manuscripts; and that the habit of treating an image as a text — glossable, quotable, authoritative — which the emblem tradition presupposes, has in Ibn Umayl its fullest early practitioner.

THE TABLET, DRAWN

The ekphrasis becomes an image again: Latin manuscripts of Senior draw the tablet’s company, re-picturing in the West what the report had pictured in words from the Egyptian temple.

THE FIGURES, STANDARDISED

Old man, birds, tree, luminaries: the tablet’s inventory settles, through copying, into the Latin art’s stock of canonical figures — the same inventory the emblem books will deploy for centuries.

THE IMAGE AS CITATION

A picture reproduced from an exemplar and glossed in prose is functioning as a quotation. The Latin Senior’s manuscripts practise, in layout, the unity of scripts that Ibn Umayl preached in theory.

THE LIMIT OF THE CLAIM

Transmission of a picture and its key is documented; single-handed origination of the Latin emblem tradition is not claimed. Greek, Latin, and other Arabic pictured sources feed the same river, and the archive keeps the confluence labelled as one.

XVI. Allegory as Method, Assessed: What It Preserves, What It Costs

The assessment this hub owes its reader is the one the Greco-Roman allegory article gives for the mytho-hermetic reading of myth, and it transfers to Ibn Umayl with a precision that is itself the strongest evidence the two traditions are one. That article’s verdict is threefold — on origin, on coherence, and on knowledge — and each fold must be re-cut for a commentator whose texts are not Homer but the art’s own books. This section performs the re-cutting, and states the modern reader’s resulting instructions for use.

What Allegorical Commentary Preserves

First, texts. The preservation is not metaphorical. Ibn Umayl’s commentary is a physical ark for Greek alchemy: quotations of Democritus, Zosimos, Maria, Hermes, and the Turba survive in his Arabic where the Greek tradition is lost, fragmentary, or epitomised (Section VII), and the Turba’s Arabic circulation is dated by his ark’s manifest (Section XI). Whatever the commentary’s readings are worth, its quotations are primary evidence of the first rank, and the modern editions of the Greek corpus are built partly on them. A hermeneutics that preserves its objects while misreading them — if misreading is the verdict — has still done the archive the greatest service an interpreter can do.

Second, a working hermeneutics of the Decknamen. The art’s cover-names are not allegory in the large sense; they are a local code, keyed to behaviour, and in principle checkable at the bench — the Greco-Roman hub’s Decknamen Glossary exists to grade, entry by entry, how securely each covered referent can be recovered, and Lawrence Principe’s laboratory work (Section XVI of the Greco-Roman allegory article; The Secrets of Alchemy, 2013) has repeatedly recovered workable chemistry by breaking exactly that code. Ibn Umayl’s commentary is the largest single early exercise of that hermeneutics: his equations of image to substance are governed, at their best, by the behavioural keying of Section VI — the bird must fly, the water must dissolve, the old man must endure — and where the keying holds, his decodings transmit real knowledge of what the covered substances do, in the only form in which his tradition allowed that knowledge to be written down. The veil, at this level, stored genuine cargo.

Third, a discipline of consistency. A commentator who must decode a whole poem, image by image, under the eyes of quoted authorities, cannot decode opportunistically for long: the bird of verse ten constrains the bird of verse forty, and a gloss that contradicts an authority must be argued, not merely asserted. Ibn Umayl’s system is, in the Greco-Roman article’s verdict-word, coherent — internally consistent, rule-governed, and teachable — and coherence in a hermeneutics is not nothing. It is what distinguishes a method from a reverie, and it is why Ibn Umayl could be used, by Arabic readers and Latin masters alike, as a school author: a student can learn his key, apply it to a new passage, and be corrected. Systems that can correct their students are knowledge-institutions, whatever else they are.

What It Costs

The cost is the mirror of the coherence, and Ibn Umayl’s case states it more sharply than the myth-readers’ case, because his base texts are closer to home. A commented text, under a sufficiently determined commentary, can be made to say whatever the system needs. The mechanism is structural, not a failure of Ibn Umayl’s honesty. The key is drawn from the tradition the commentary serves; the authorities are selected by the gloss they are produced to support; the behavioural keying, which disciplines the single equation, loosens at the scale of the scene, where some resemblance can be found between any pictured relation and some operation; and the theory of rumūz itself seals the system against falsification from outside — for if a passage resists the decoding, the theory replies that the passage is a deeper symbol, and if an authority disagrees, that the authority is speaking in another symbol still. The Greco-Roman article’s sentence for the mythic case applies here with the nouns changed: where the Deckname answers to the bench, the allegory answers to the library — to the consistency of the keyed corpus — and a consistency that only the corpus can check is, in the end, self-sealing. Ibn Umayl’s poem emerges from his commentary saying, in perfect order, the doctrine of tenth-century Arabic alchemy. That the poem’s author intended precisely that doctrine, in precisely that order, is the one thing the commentary, by its method, is constitutionally unable to prove.

THE VERDICT ON PRESERVATION

Sustained, at full weight. As a quoting witness Ibn Umayl is a primary source for lost Greek alchemy and the dated witness for the Arabic Turba. The ark is real whatever the readings are worth.

THE VERDICT ON THE DECKNAMEN LEVEL

Qualified, in the method’s favour. Behaviour-keyed cover-name decoding transmits checkable bench knowledge, and Ibn Umayl is its largest early practitioner. Use his equations with the Glossary’s grading, not as oracles.

THE VERDICT ON THE ALLEGORICAL LEVEL

The decodings are the tradition’s readings — documented, coherent, historically consequential — presented in this archive as the alchemists’ reading, never as the commented texts’ demonstrable original meaning. Reception is not origin, in Arabic any more than in Greek.

THE VERDICT ON VALUE

Ibn Umayl is indispensable twice over: as evidence for what he quotes, and as the fullest surviving demonstration of how the medieval art, Arabic and Latin alike, understood its own symbolic language. No history of alchemical reading can be written without him.

How the Modern Reader Should Use Him

Three instructions follow, and they are this article’s practical close. Use him as a witness before using him as a guide: his quotations, checked against surviving Greek where Greek survives, are evidence; his decodings are doctrine — the doctrine of his century, invaluable as such, and not a transparent window on the centuries he quotes. Use the key, with the grading: where a decoding is behaviour-keyed and bench-checkable, test it as the Decknamen tradition invites testing; where it is scene-level allegory, read it as the tradition’s self-interpretation and enjoy its coherence without mistaking coherence for proof. Use the question marks: the Turba’s original, the poem’s author, the temple vision’s event — Ibn Umayl’s dossier is full of places where the honest state of knowledge is an open question, and this article has kept each one visible rather than papering it with his certainty or with ours. He believed the ancients hid a single sense in symbols, and that he had recovered it. The modern reader’s more modest recovery is Ibn Umayl himself: an Egyptian commentator of the tenth century, almost without a biography, who built out of other men’s symbols the most durable reading-machine the art ever produced — and whose machine, labelled with its dates and its limits, is still the best introduction to how that art thought a text could mean.

The next action for the reader of this article is the shelf itself: the CALA Book of the Explanation of the Symbols beside Stapleton’s 1933 extracts, the Latin De chemia of 1572 beside both — three states of one book, Arabic, Arabic recovered, and Latin, in which the whole transmission this hub exists to document can be watched, page by page, in a single author.
