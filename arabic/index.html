I. Introduction — Scope of This Hub

This hub covers the Arabic-language career of alchemy from the seventh century to the fourteenth — roughly, from the Arab conquests that brought Egypt, Syria and Iraq under new rulers to the moment when the art the Arabic world had rebuilt passed, in Latin dress, into the hands of European readers who would remake it again. Its subject is a transformation, not a relay race. The Greek alchemy of Alexandria — pseudo-Democritus, Maria the Jewess, Zōsimos, the commentators — did not simply survive in Arabic translation. It was rebuilt: given a new technical vocabulary (kīmiyāʾ for the art, iksīr for the agent that perfects metals), a new master theory in the doctrine of the balance (mīzān), a new classification of substances and apparatus, and a literature many times larger than the Greek corpus it inherited. What Latin Europe received in the twelfth century was not Alexandria's art preserved in ice. It was Baghdad's, Basra's and Cairo's art, alive and argued over.

The hub has a defined place in this archive, and it is worth stating plainly. It follows the Egyptology division, which treats the Egyptian crafts and the earliest alchemy from the Egyptian side, and it follows the Greco-Roman hub, whose Wing II carries the Alexandrian art from its Greek beginnings to a closing threshold: 1144 CE, the year of the first dated Latin alchemical translation. This hub's relation to that threshold is deliberately different. It begins before it — with the conquests of the seventh century and with the Syriac bridge that had been carrying Greek learning eastward for two hundred years before Islam — and it runs past it, following the Arabic tradition through the twelfth and thirteenth centuries and out through the Latin translations it fed, to the threshold of the Latin art itself around 1300. Where the Greco-Roman hub ends at the Latin door, this hub walks through it — once, briefly, and only to show what was carried across. The Egyptology division's own later-period treatment, its Islamic Egyptology article, is the companion piece from the Egyptian side.

Two standing cautions govern everything in this hub, and they are stated here, up front, rather than buried in the method section. The first concerns the hub's name. This is an Arabic-language hub, not an ethnic one. Arabic was the learned language of a civilisation far wider than Arabia, and many of this hub's central authors were not Arabs: al-Rāzī and al-Bīrūnī were Persians; Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq was a Nestorian Christian from al-Ḥīra whose first scholarly language was Syriac; Thābit ibn Qurra was a Sabian of Harran; the translators of Cordoba included Jews working between Arabic, Hebrew and Romance. Others were Syriac Christians, Sabians, Jews and converts of every background, writing in the one language that reached from Cordoba to Nishapur. Throughout this hub, "Arabic" names the language of the texts — nothing more, and nothing less.

The second caution concerns a name. The Arabic corpus ascribed to Jābir ibn Ḥayyān and the Latin works ascribed to Geber are different bodies of work, written in different languages, in different centuries, with different doctrines — most sharply on the mineral acids, which belong to the Latin side of the divide. The Latin Summa perfectionis of pseudo-Geber, composed around 1300, is one of the most accomplished alchemical treatises ever written; it is not a translation of Jābir, and its author — a Latin writer working, on William Newman's showing, in a Franciscan milieu — borrowed the great name precisely because it carried authority. This hub never confuses the two: "Jābir" in these pages means the Arabic corpus and its school; "pseudo-Geber" means the Latin author. Where older popular writing merges them into a single legendary figure, the hub says so and separates them again.

// THE HUB IN ONE LINE //

Wing I asks how the art travelled — who translated it, where, and under whose patronage. Wing II asks what the Arabic world made of it — at the bench, in theory, and on the page. Neither wing is a footnote to the other.

A note on names and spelling. Arabic words and names are transliterated consistently throughout the hub, with macrons marking long vowels and the standard diacritics retained: Jābir ibn Ḥayyān, al-Rāzī, Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq, Thābit ibn Qurra, mīzān (the balance), iksīr (the elixir), kīmiyāʾ (alchemy). The letters ʿ and ʾ mark the consonants ʿayn and hamza and are part of the spelling, not decoration. Book titles are given in transliteration with an English rendering at first use — Kitāb sirr al-asrār, the Book of the Secret of Secrets — and thereafter by whichever form the scholarship standardly uses. Dates are CE throughout this hub; a date preceded by c. is approximate, a date given as a range is a lifespan or a floruit, and where scholars disagree about a date that matters — the dating of the Jābirian corpus is the standing example — the timeline says so in the entry itself rather than silently choosing a side.

Finally, a note on what this hub is not. It is not a chapter in a story about Europe: the Arabic tradition is treated here as an end in itself, judged by its own texts and its own questions, and the Latin translations appear because the Arabic authors' afterlife is part of their history — not because translation into Latin is what made them matter. It is not a celebration of a "golden age" in the decorative sense: the period this hub covers contains credulity, forgery, court flattery and failed transmutations alongside its genuine achievements in distillation, classification and laboratory discipline, and the hub reports both registers. And it is not a general history of the medieval Islamic world — caliphs, conquests and dynasties appear on the timeline where they change the conditions of learning, of patronage, or of the art, and not otherwise.

// SCOPE NOTE — DELIBERATE EXCLUSIONS //

The Latin West after the translations — the post-1144 Latin art, from the translators' immediate readers to pseudo-Geber and beyond — is reserved for a future Latin hub, and appears here only at the threshold where the Arabic tradition hands its books across. India and China, whose alchemical traditions are older than, parallel to, and largely independent of the one told here, will have their own hubs later in the archive's sequence; they are not treated as annexes of this one. This hub treats the Arabic-language world only — but it treats all of it, from Harran to Cordoba.

II. The Two Wings

The hub is organised in two wings. The division is not decorative: the two wings use different primary evidence, ask different questions, and are held to the same standard by different means. Wing I works from translators' prefaces, bio-bibliographical catalogues, court and library records, and the manuscripts that survive at the ends of long journeys. Wing II works from treatises, recipe collections, classifications of substances and apparatus, and the commentaries in which the tradition argued with itself. Every article in the index belongs to one wing, and the timeline is written so that a reader can follow either wing down its length — or watch the two converge, as they do in ninth-century Baghdad, where the translators' city becomes, within a generation, the city of the Jābirian school.

WING I

TRANSMISSION — how the Greek art reached the Arabic world. Before there could be an Arabic alchemy there had to be a road: the Syriac schools of Edessa and Nisibis, where Greek medicine and philosophy had been taught in Syriac since the fifth century; the pagan star-city of Harran and the medical academy of Gundeshapur, the two bridge cities where Greek, Syriac, Persian and Arabic learning met; and then Baghdad, where the early ʿAbbāsid caliphs funded the largest organised translation enterprise of the pre-modern world. At the far end of the wing stands the Latin door itself — Toledo, Sicily, and the translators of the twelfth century — treated here from the Arabic side: as the moment the Arabic tradition's own books began a second transmission, into a second new language.

WING II

THE ART — the Arabic golden age itself. The Jābirian corpus and its science of the balance; the sulphur–mercury theory that gave the art its lasting account of what metals are; al-Rāzī's laboratory discipline — his classification of substances, his ordered apparatus, his procedures written to be repeated; al-Kindī's chemistry of perfume, where distillation becomes an industry of scent; the encyclopedia of the Brethren of Purity, in which the art takes its place in a complete map of knowledge; the allegorical commentators, above all Ibn Umayl, who read the ancients' images as the work's own portrait; and the workshop record itself — the furnaces, stills, salts and corrosive waters of the bench. Workshop procedure stands at the centre of this wing: a claim about what the Arabic alchemists believed is only admitted here alongside the evidence of what they actually did.

WING I — ITS KEY DISTINCTION

Translation is not transmission. A text rendered into Arabic is not yet a tradition rebuilt: transmission happened when translators coined a technical vocabulary, when patrons paid for the same work twice to get it right, when readers commented, abridged and argued — when the translated art acquired Arabic masters of its own. Gutas's Greek Thought, Arabic Culture is the wing's model for that distinction.

WING II — ITS KEY DISTINCTION

System is not credulity. The Jābirian balance is a rigorous, quantified theory — and it stands in the same corpus, sometimes on the same page, as bench procedures for sublimation, calcination and solution that any modern chemist can follow. The wing reports both registers at full strength and refuses to explain either one away by means of the other.

The wings meet at three points, and the hub marks each meeting rather than blurring it. They meet in Baghdad, where Wing I's translation movement created the readership — and the library — that Wing II's Jābirian school presupposes on every page. They meet in the figure of Jābir himself, or rather in the corpus that bears his name: a Wing II achievement whose own account of itself reaches back through Wing I's territory, claiming a master, Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq, who died before the translation movement began — a claim the hub reports as the corpus's own account, and dates by the scholarship, not by the claim. And they meet at Toledo, where Wing I's road ends and Wing II's books begin their Latin life: the Liber de septuaginta that Gerard of Cremona translated is Jābir's Book of Seventy, and a reader who wants to know what crossed the threshold in 1144 and after must hold both wings open at once. The Greco-Roman hub's Transmission article tells the same crossings from the Greek side; the two accounts are companions, not duplicates.

III. Method — The Rules of This Hub

Every archive in this project keeps its rules on its hub page, where a reader can hold the articles to them. These are the rules of the Arabic hub. They are the Greco-Roman hub's rules, carried into a tradition whose sources raise the same problems in sharper forms — a larger pseudepigraphic literature, a longer chain of translation, and a modern popular literature considerably less careful than either.

// THE HUB'S RULE //

No invented quotations anywhere in this archive. A sentence inside quotation marks is a verified quotation from a named published translation, or it does not appear. Everything else is labelled paraphrase — including this hub's own summaries of the arguments it admires most.

Texts are named in named editions and translations. The Jābirian corpus is cited from Paul Kraus's Jābir ibn Ḥayyān and from the editions and studies that build on it; al-Rāzī's Secret of Secrets from the scholarship on the Kitāb sirr al-asrār — the early English renderings of Stapleton and his collaborators, and Syed Nomanul Haq's Names, Natures and Things; the translation movement from Dimitri Gutas's Greek Thought, Arabic Culture. Where an article relies on a different edition, that edition is named on the article. A claim that cannot be traced to a named text is not made.

Quotations are only from verified published translations, and are named as such. Arabic alchemical prose reaches most readers through two or three layers of translation, and each layer is a place where wording can be silently improved. No wording in this hub is reconstructed from memory, none is silently modernised, and none is manufactured to give an argument an antique accent it has not earned. Where a famous saying turns out to be a later paraphrase — a common fate of sayings attached to Jābir in particular — the hub says so and prints the paraphrase labelled as paraphrase, with its actual, later source where that source is known.

The Arabic tradition is judged by its own texts, not by what Latin Europe made of them. Latin reception is evidence for Latin history. That pseudo-Geber's Summa systematised the sulphur–mercury theory more cleanly than any Arabic text does not make the Arabic texts failed drafts of the Summa; that the Latin West read al-Rāzī chiefly as "Rhazes" the physician does not shrink the Arabic al-Rāzī to a physician. Each tradition is first described in its own terms, in its own order, by its own standards of success — and only then compared.

Speculation is labelled as speculation. The dating of the Jābirian corpus, the existence of a historical Jābir behind it, the authorship of the Ghāyat al-ḥakīm, the nature of Baghdad's Bayt al-Ḥikma: on each of these the scholarship is divided or the evidence thin, and this hub presents them as open questions with the main positions named — Kraus's dating of the corpus to c. 850–950 is stated as Kraus's dating, and the older picture of a single eighth-century author is stated as the corpus's own account of itself, not as the hub's. A reader should be able to tell, from any sentence in this hub, whether they are standing on a document, on a scholarly consensus, or on an argument.

Workshop procedure stands at the centre for Wing II. The Arabic corpus is unusually rich in bench evidence: al-Rāzī lists his substances by class and his apparatus by function; the Jābirian books describe sublimation, calcination, solution and coagulation as ordered operations; al-Kindī's perfume book is a formulary meant to be worked from. Claims about doctrine in this wing are therefore always checked against the operations the same texts prescribe. Where a theory and a procedure conflict, the hub reports the conflict rather than harmonising it — the conflict is itself evidence about how the tradition actually worked.

// A NOTE ON THE ALCHEMICAL SOURCES //

Pseudepigraphy is the normal condition of Wing II's library, not an embarrassment to be explained away. Works travel under the names of Jābir, Hermes, Aristotle and Apollonius — Balīnās, in his Arabic afterlife — that their named authors did not write; the Secret of Creation is Apollonius's only by ascription, and the books of Jābir are a school's corpus gathered under a master's name. This hub cites such works as the Jābirian corpus, the Hermetica ascribed to Hermes, the Ghāya ascribed to al-Majrīṭī, and so on — the attribution is part of the historical evidence, and silently treating it as authorship would falsify the record the hub exists to keep.

IV. The Arabic Timeline

Forty entries, from the closing of the school of Edessa to the Latin pseudo-Geber's Summa perfectionis. Read it as two interleaved histories: the Wing I history of roads, cities and translators, which runs ahead of the art it carries; and the Wing II history that begins, in earnest, in ninth-century Iraq and ends by walking out of the Arabic world altogether — into Latin, at Toledo and Palermo. Every date was checked for this hub against the standard references named in the Sources section; approximate dates are marked as approximate, and disputed dates carry their disputes inside the entry.

489 CE
The school of Edessa closed — the Syriac road opens. The emperor Zeno closed the Christian school of Edessa in Mesopotamia, whose teachers had been rendering Greek theology, logic and medicine into Syriac for generations. Its scholars moved east, beyond the Byzantine frontier, to Nisibis in Persian territory, and refounded their school there. The move set the pattern for three centuries: Greek learning travelling east in Syriac dress, preserved by the Church of the East, and waiting — though no one yet knew it — for Arabic readers.
d. 536 CE
Sergius of Reshaina — the first great Syriac translator. Sergius (d. 536), a physician-priest of Reshaina trained at Alexandria, translated Galen, Aristotle's logic, and the works of pseudo-Dionysius into Syriac, and wrote commentaries of his own. His versions — and the curriculum they served, in which Aristotle's Categories opened the road to medicine and philosophy — fixed the shape of the Syriac bridge: Greek science ordered for teaching, in a Semitic language, east of the empire that had produced it.
c. 550s CE
Gundeshapur under Khusraw I — the Persian refuge. In Khuzistan, the Sasanian royal city of Gundeshapur (Jundaysābūr) became, under Khusraw I Anūshirvān (r. 531–579), a meeting place of Greek, Syriac and Persian learning: Nestorian Christian physicians taught and practised there, and the philosophers who left Athens after Justinian closed its school in 529 were received at the Persian court. The academy's scale in later Arabic retelling is probably embellished; its medical school and its Syriac-speaking physicians are not. Wing I's article on the bridge cities weighs both.
641 CE
The Arab conquest of Egypt completed. Arab armies under ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ took Egypt from the Byzantine Empire between 639 and 642; Alexandria capitulated by treaty, and the conquest is conventionally dated to 641 CE. Syria, Iraq and Persia had already fallen or were falling. The conquerors ruled, at first, through the existing Greek, Syriac and Coptic administrations — and inherited, intact, the cities, libraries and craft traditions in which the Greek art had been born. For this hub the conquest is not an ending but a change of custody.
c. 683 CE
Masarjawayh translates Aaron of Alexandria — Arabic medicine begins. Masarjawayh, a Jewish physician of Basra, is credited in the Arabic bio-bibliographical tradition with translating the medical Pandects of Aaron of Alexandria — a seventh-century Alexandrian priest-physician — from Syriac into Arabic, conventionally dated to about 683, in the reign of the Umayyad caliph Marwān I. It is the first medical translation into Arabic the tradition records: a Greek work, by a Syriac road, into the new language of learning. The pattern of the next two centuries is already complete in it.
d. 704 CE
Khālid ibn Yazīd — the prince of the legend. Khālid ibn Yazīd ibn Muʿāwiya (d. 704), an Umayyad prince passed over for the caliphate, is remembered by the Arabic tradition as the first patron of translation from Greek and Coptic, and as a student of alchemy under the monk Morienus — Marianos, in the legend's Greek form. The Morienus story, in which the art passes from Stephanus of Alexandria through Morienus to Khālid, is legend, as the Greco-Roman hub also flags it: built on real names, unsupported by contemporary evidence. What is documented is the tradition's need for a founding prince — and the Latin career the legend later had, when the dialogue of Morienus and Khālid became, in 1144, the first alchemical book of the Latin West.
762 CE
Baghdad founded. The ʿAbbāsid caliph al-Manṣūr founded Baghdad on the Tigris in 762 as his dynasty's new capital — a round city of palaces, markets and, very soon, translators. Built at the meeting of the Syriac-speaking east and the Persian administrative tradition, staffed by secretaries and physicians drawn from both, Baghdad became within two generations the largest city in the world and the capital of its learning. Every Wing II achievement in this hub happens in Baghdad's orbit, or in argument with it.
d. 765 CE
Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq — the corpus's claimed master. Jaʿfar ibn Muḥammad al-Ṣādiq (d. 765), sixth imam of the Shīʿa, is named by the Jābirian corpus as Jābir's master and the source of its teaching. That is the corpus's own account of itself, and this hub reports it as such: Jaʿfar was a real and revered teacher in Medina, and later tradition credits him with learning of many kinds, but no contemporary evidence connects him to an alchemical school, and the corpus that claims his authority was composed, on the dating the scholarship standardly uses, a century and more after his death. The claim matters — it is the corpus's charter — and it is a claim, not a document.
765 – c. 825 CE
The Bukhtīshūʿ physicians summoned from Gundeshapur. In 765 al-Manṣūr, ill and dissatisfied with his physicians, summoned Jurjis ibn Bukhtīshūʿ, head of the medical school at Gundeshapur, to Baghdad. Jurjis came, was cured of any wish to leave by the caliph's favour, and founded a dynasty: for six generations the Bukhtīshūʿ family — Nestorian Christians, Syriac-speaking, Galen-trained — served as court physicians to the caliphs, Jurjis's son Jibrīl (d. 828) attending Hārūn al-Rashīd and al-Maʾmūn. Around 800 the family's presence at court was the living proof of Wing I's thesis: Gundeshapur's Greek medicine had not been translated into Baghdad. It had moved there, in persons.
c. 770s CE
Translations at al-Manṣūr's court — the movement begins. Under al-Manṣūr (r. 754–775), translation into Arabic became a court activity rather than a private one: astronomical and medical works were rendered from Persian and, through Syriac, from Greek; Indian astronomy arrived with an embassy and was translated as the Sindhind; Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ (d. c. 756) was already turning Persian statecraft into Arabic prose of the first order. The scale was still modest beside what followed under al-Maʾmūn. But the principle was set — the caliphate would buy Greek learning, at caliphal prices, and expect it in Arabic.
8th–9th century CE
Balīnās and the Secret of Creation — the Emerald Tablet's Arabic home. The Kitāb sirr al-khalīqa (Book of the Secret of Creation), ascribed to Balīnās — Apollonius of Tyana in his Arabic afterlife — frames its cosmology of the elements and the generation of all things with a discovery story: a vault beneath a statue of Hermes, an enthroned elder, and a tablet of emerald bearing the text the Latin world would call the Tabula Smaragdina. No Greek or Latin manuscript of the Emerald Tablet survives from antiquity; this Arabic book, whose own date is debated within the 8th–9th centuries, is its earliest attested witness. The Tablet's text runs to thirteen sentences in the usual segmentation, fourteen in editions that divide the closing sentences — a small fact this archive states identically in every hub that touches it.
786–809 CE
Hārūn al-Rashīd and the Barmakids — patronage at full stretch. Under Hārūn al-Rashīd (r. 786–809) and his Barmakid viziers — a Persian family from Balkh whose fall in 803 was as sudden as their rise — Baghdad's patronage of learning reached its famous pitch: physicians summoned, translators salaried, embassies sent to Charlemagne's Francia and, by report, books requested from Byzantium. The Barmakids' own interests ran to medicine and astronomy in particular. Patronage is a Wing I subject in its own right: the translation movement was not a spirit of the age but a budget line, and budgets have authors.
c. 794 CE
Paper comes to Baghdad — the movement's material basis. A paper mill was established at Baghdad around 794, in the Barmakid period, using a technology that had travelled west from China through Samarkand after the battle of Talas in 751 — the traditional account, whose details historians now qualify, but whose outcome is not in doubt. Within a generation, paper had undercut parchment and papyrus across the caliphate. The translation movement, and the book culture that preserved alchemy itself, ran on cheap writing material: a Wing I fact as material as any furnace in Wing II.
c. 801–873 CE
Al-Kindī — the first philosopher, and the chemistry of perfume. Abū Yūsuf Yaʿqūb ibn Isḥāq al-Kindī (c. 801–873), "the philosopher of the Arabs," stood at the head of the Baghdad circle that commissioned and made translations, and wrote over two hundred treatises of his own. For this hub his bench book matters most: the Book of the Chemistry of Perfume and Distillations (Kitāb kīmiyāʾ al-ʿiṭr wa-l-taṣʿīdāt) gives over a hundred recipes for scented oils, waters and unguents, with the distillation apparatus to make them — the earliest surviving Arabic formulary of applied chemistry. Al-Kindī also wrote against the alchemists' central claim, in a treatise warning that men cannot make what nature makes in metals. The hub reports both books: the golden age argued with itself from the start.
813–833 CE
Al-Maʾmūn — the translation movement institutionalised. Under al-Maʾmūn (r. 813–833), who had won the caliphate in a civil war against his brother and ruled an empire he was determined to make rational and centralised, translation from Greek became state policy on an unprecedented scale: manuscripts sought from Byzantium by embassy, translators paid — by the famous report — the weight of their books in gold, and the work organised around the Bayt al-Ḥikma, the "House of Wisdom." Modern scholarship, Gutas above all, cautions against picturing the Bayt al-Ḥikma as a grand academy on the modern model: it was a caliphal library and bureau whose importance grew in later retelling. The movement it housed needs no embellishment. In two generations it moved the Greek scientific corpus, whole, into Arabic.
809–873 CE
Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq — the translators' translator. Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq (809–873), a Nestorian Christian from al-Ḥīra, learned Greek — by his own account, the hard way, in Byzantine territory — and became the central figure of the Baghdad movement. His method set its standard: collate Greek manuscripts, translate first into Syriac or directly into Arabic as the patron required, then revise against the Greek again. His Risāla, a letter-catalogue to a patron, lists 129 works of Galen and states, for each, whether he translated it, revised it, or could not find it — the single most informative document the pre-modern translation world produced about its own practice. His son Isḥāq ibn Ḥunayn (d. 910) and his nephew Ḥubaysh carried the workshop into the next generation; Aristotle and Plato reached Arabic substantially through this family's hands.
820–912 CE
Qusṭā ibn Lūqā — the movement's second generation. Qusṭā ibn Lūqā (820–912), a Christian of Baalbek who worked in Baghdad, translated and revised Greek works on medicine, astronomy, mechanics and natural philosophy, and wrote original treatises — on the difference between spirit and soul, on contagion, on the celestial sphere — that Latin Europe would later translate in their turn, as Costa ben Luca. He stands here for the movement's maturity: a generation for whom Greek-to-Arabic translation was no longer an experiment but a profession with masters, methods and a market.
836–901 CE
Thābit ibn Qurra of Harran — the Sabian bridge. Thābit ibn Qurra (836–901) was a Sabian of Harran — a member of that city's pagan, star-worshipping community, which had preserved Greek learning, and its astral religion, intact into the Islamic centuries. Recruited to Baghdad (the story says a patron found him working as a money-changer), he became a translator and reviser of Apollonius, Archimedes, Euclid and Ptolemy, and a mathematician and astronomer of the first rank in his own right. His family served Baghdad's learning for four generations. Harran's contribution to the movement was exactly Thābit: a city where Greek had never stopped being read, supplying the capital with readers.
c. 850–950 CE
The Jābirian corpus composed — a school under one name. The vast body of Arabic alchemical writing ascribed to Jābir ibn Ḥayyān — the Books of the Balances, the Book of Seventy, the Book of Stones, some hundreds of treatises in the traditional lists — was composed, on Paul Kraus's dating in his monumental Jābir ibn Ḥayyān (1942–43), not by one eighth-century author but by a school working across roughly a century, c. 850–950, in Iraq, in circles Kraus connected with early Ismāʿīlī Shīʿism. The dating is Kraus's dating, stated as such wherever this hub relies on it: a historical Jābir of the late eighth century may stand somewhere behind the corpus, and some scholars have argued for a longer or earlier composition, but the school-corpus model remains the working account of the scholarship. Its doctrines — the balance (mīzān) as a quantified science of nature, the sulphur–mercury constitution of metals, the elixir (iksīr) as medicine for metals and bodies alike — are Wing II's first subject.
865–925 CE
Al-Rāzī and the Secret of Secrets — the art as laboratory discipline. Abū Bakr Muḥammad ibn Zakariyyāʾ al-Rāzī (865–925) — Rhazes, to the Latin world — physician of Rayy and Baghdad and the most uncompromisingly practical of the Arabic alchemists, composed the Kitāb sirr al-asrār (Book of the Secret of Secrets), the tradition's bench manual: a classification of all substances the art uses — spirits, bodies, stones, vitriols, boraces, salts — an ordered catalogue of apparatus, and procedures for sublimation, calcination, solution and coagulation written so that another worker could repeat them. Al-Rāzī believed in transmutation and defended the art against al-Kindī's attack; he also insisted, in a famous professional credo the hub paraphrases rather than quotes, that the adept earns the name by work at the furnace, not by talk. His classification, not his theory, is what the later tradition — Arabic and Latin alike — actually inherited.
c. 900 CE
The Turba Philosophorum in its Arabic milieu. The Turba philosophorum — the "Assembly of the Philosophers," in which the sages of antiquity, Pythagoras presiding, debate the generation of metals and the one thing from which all things come — survives in Latin, but Julius Ruska argued in his 1931 edition that a lost Arabic original stands behind it, composed around the turn of the tenth century in the same Egyptian-Iraqi milieu that produced the Jābirian books. Ruska's thesis is stated here as a thesis: the Arabic original does not survive, and the argument rests on the Latin text's names, doctrines and Arabic-derived vocabulary. What is not in doubt is the Turba's later authority — for the Latin Middle Ages it was the art's parliament, its debate the form in which generations of readers first met the sulphur– mercury doctrine.
c. 900–930 CE
Ibn Waḥshiyya and the Nabataean Agriculture. Ibn Waḥshiyya (fl. early 10th century), an Iraqi scholar of alchemical and occult interests, presented his Nabataean Agriculture as a translation from ancient "Chaldaean" sources — a claim modern scholarship treats as a literary frame for a genuine compilation of Mesopotamian agricultural and magical lore. Beside it stand works on poisons and on the decipherment of ancient scripts, the latter a real, if partial, anticipation of hieroglyphic studies. He belongs on this timeline because the Arabic art never separated the furnace from the field and the amulet as cleanly as modern categories do — and because his books show Wing II's literature absorbing the lore of the land it was written in.
c. 872–950 CE
Al-Fārābī classifies the sciences — and places the art. Abū Naṣr al-Fārābī (c. 872–950), the "Second Teacher" after Aristotle, included alchemy in his Enumeration of the Sciences (Iḥṣāʾ al-ʿulūm) among the natural sciences — and paired the listing with a short treatise on the necessity of the art, answering its critics. His treatment is measured where the Jābirian books are enthusiastic: the art is a legitimate science of what nature does in the earth, and its claims are to be judged by natural philosophy, not by enthusiasm or contempt. The Enumeration itself travelled — into Latin, as a map of all knowledge — carrying the art's classified place with it.
fl. c. 900–960 CE
Ibn Umayl in Egypt — the commentary of images. Muḥammad ibn Umayl al-Tamīmī (fl. c. 900–960), working in Egypt, composed the Book of the Silvery Water and the Starry Earth (Kitāb al-māʾ al-waraqī wa-l-arḍ al-najmiyya) — a commentary, in prose and verse, on the sayings and images of the ancients, above all on a picture Ibn Umayl describes seeing in an Egyptian temple: a sage holding a tablet on which the whole work is figured. Where the Jābirian school built systems and al-Rāzī built procedures, Ibn Umayl built the tradition's third register — exegesis: the ancients meant something by their birds, waters and kings, and the commentator's task is to say what. Latin readers would meet him, centuries later, as Senior Zadith.
c. 950s CE
The Rasāʾil of the Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ — the Brethren's encyclopedia. In Basra, an anonymous fellowship — the Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ, the Brethren of Purity — composed the Rasāʾil, fifty-two epistles amounting to an encyclopedia of all knowledge, from arithmetic to magic, conventionally dated to the later tenth century, c. 950s and after. Their epistles on minerals and on the crafts give the art a cosmology to live in: metals generated in the earth from sulphur and mercury under celestial influence, crafts ranked as the soul's training in matter. The Brethren's Neoplatonism, their number-lore and their doctrine of the universal soul fed directly into the intellectual world of the Jābirian corpus's readers — and, on Kraus's argument, of its authors.
c. 948–951 CE
Dioscorides in Cordoba — transmission inside the Arabic world. The Umayyad caliph ʿAbd al-Raḥmān III of Cordoba received from the Byzantine emperor a splendid Greek manuscript of Dioscorides' De materia medica — and, because Cordoba had translators but few Greek readers, a monk, Nicholas, sent from Constantinople around 948 to teach them. Working with the court's scholars, among them the Jewish physician Ḥasdai ibn Shaprut, Nicholas and his students produced the Arabic Dioscorides of al-Andalus, fixing the Arabic names of Mediterranean plants for the western Islamic world. The entry belongs on Wing I's road as much as any Baghdad entry: transmission did not stop at the caliphate's first capital, and the art's materia medica was rebuilt at both ends of the Arabic world at once.
c. 950–1007 CE
Al-Majrīṭī and the Ghāyat al-ḥakīm — the Sage's goal. Maslama al-Majrīṭī (d. 1007), astronomer of Cordoba, is credited with revising al-Khwārizmī's astronomical tables for the Andalusian meridian and with the Ghāyat al-ḥakīm (The Goal of the Sage) — the great Arabic summa of astral magic, talismans and the influences of the spheres, which the Latin world would know as the Picatrix. The attribution is debated: the Ghāya as we have it may be the work of al-Majrīṭī's circle or of a later hand writing under his authority, and this hub cites it as ascribed to him. A shorter alchemical treatise, the Sage's Step (Rutbat al-ḥakīm), travels under the same name and the same caution. Either way, Cordoba by 1000 had its own master-text of the occult sciences, written in the west, in Arabic.
c. 987 CE
The Fihrist of Ibn al-Nadīm — the tradition catalogues itself. Ibn al-Nadīm, a Baghdad bookseller, completed around 987 his Fihrist — an index, author by author and book by book, of everything written in Arabic that a tenth-century bookseller could know of. Its tenth discourse, on the alchemists, preserves the traditional lists of Jābir's works, the names of practitioners otherwise lost, and the Khālid–Morienus legend in its early Arabic form. The Fihrist is this hub's indispensable witness for a simple reason: it is the Arabic tradition describing its own library, within living memory of the Jābirian school's great century.
973–1048 CE
Al-Bīrūnī — the measurer at the edge of the art. Abū Rayḥān al-Bīrūnī (973–1048), of Khwarazm and Ghazna, polymath of astronomy, chronology, India and mineralogy, composed the Book of the Multitude of Knowledge of Precious Stones (Kitāb al-jamāhir) — a mineralogy built on measurement, in which the specific weights of metals and gems are determined with a precision Europe would not match for centuries. Al-Bīrūnī handled the alchemists' claims the way he handled everything: he recorded them, tested what could be tested, and declined to believe the rest. His presence on this timeline is the hub's standing reminder that the golden age contained its own severest critics — inside the same civilisation, and often in the same library.
980–1037 CE
Ibn Sīnā — the Shifāʾ denies easy transmutation. Ibn Sīnā (980–1037) — Avicenna, to the Latin world — closed the mineralogical section of his great Book of the Cure (Kitāb al-shifāʾ) with the Arabic tradition's most consequential judgement on the art. Metals, he argued, differ in their very species, fixed in the earth's depths by conditions no furnace reproduces; the alchemists can imitate a metal's colour, weight and ring — can make what passes every workshop test — but cannot transmute one species into another. The passage, paraphrased here as the hub's rule requires, reached Latin in the translation ascribed to Alfred of Sareshel and was quoted against the alchemists for five hundred years — usually without its author's careful concession of how good the imitations could be. The golden age's greatest philosopher was also its greatest internal obstacle, and the art's later history is unintelligible without him.
1085 CE
Toledo falls to Castile — the door is built. In 1085 Alfonso VI of Castile took Toledo, the old Visigothic capital and a great city of al-Andalus, with its libraries, its Arabic-speaking Christian and Jewish scholars, and its bilingual streets intact. The conquest made Toledo the one place in Europe where Latin clerics could sit, for two centuries, beside the Arabic book collections of a fallen Islamic capital — with Mozarabic Christians and Jewish intermediaries to read with them. No translator's biography matters as much as this city's fall: the Latin door was not opened by curiosity alone, but by conquest, real estate and libraries that came with it.
d. 1121 CE
Al-Ṭughrāʾī — the courtier-alchemist of Isfahan. Al-Ḥusayn ibn ʿAlī al-Ṭughrāʾī (d. 1121), vizier, poet — his Lāmiyyat al-ʿajam is still a school text of Arabic verse — and alchemist, wrote treatises defending the art against Ibn Sīnā's denial, and commentaries on the Jābirian inheritance, in the Seljuk east. He was executed in the factional ruin of a sultan's succession. Al-Ṭughrāʾī stands here for the art's twelfth-century condition in the Arabic east: no longer the new science of a rising capital, but an established, argued-over discipline with classics, commentators and enemies — a tradition old enough to have a past.
c. 1080 – c. 1152 CE
Adelard of Bath — the English road to Arabic science. Adelard of Bath (c. 1080 – c. 1152) travelled in Sicily and the Norman and crusader Near East, learned Arabic, and brought home translations that changed Latin mathematics and astronomy — Euclid's Elements from the Arabic, al-Khwārizmī's astronomical tables — together with original works, the Quaestiones naturales above all, written as dialogues with a nephew who stands for the Latin world's suspicion of the new learning. Adelard translated little alchemy directly; his place on this timeline is as proof that by the 1120s the road to Arabic science had several entrances — Spain, Sicily, Antioch — and that the alchemical translations of 1144 were the crest of a wave, not a lone swimmer.
1144 CE
Robert of Chester translates the Morienus — the first dated Latin alchemy. On 11 February 1144, by its colophon, Robert of Chester — an English translator working in Spain, in the circle that also gave Latin Europe its first Qurʾān — completed the Liber de compositione alchemiae, the dialogue of the monk Morienus with the Umayyad prince Khālid ibn Yazīd, rendered from Arabic into Latin. It is generally counted the first alchemical book of the Latin West, and this hub's hinge date, as it is the Greco-Roman hub's closing threshold. The chain the dialogue itself claims — Stephanus teaching Morienus, Morienus teaching Khālid — is the legend flagged at this timeline's entry for 704. The translation is documentary fact, and its preface, in which Robert explains to Latin readers what alchemy is because his world does not yet have the word, is the Latin art's birth certificate. This hub, unlike its predecessor, does not stop here.
1114–1187 CE
Gerard of Cremona at Toledo — the flood. Gerard of Cremona (1114–1187), an Italian who came to Toledo, by his students' account, because the Almagest was not to be had in Latin, stayed for forty years and translated — with the team his biographers list around him — more than seventy works from Arabic: Ptolemy, Aristotle, al-Fārābī, Ibn Sīnā, al-Rāzī. For this hub one title stands for the flood: the Liber de septuaginta, the Book of Seventy of the Jābirian corpus, carried into Latin by Gerard's hand and read thereafter as the work of "Geber." With Gerard, the distinction this hub opened with — Jābir's Arabic corpus against the Latin Geber tradition built partly from it and partly pasted onto it — becomes a practical problem of every Latin library catalogue, which is precisely why the hub insists on it.
c. 1154–1202 CE
Eugenius of Palermo and the Sicilian translators — the second door. Norman and Hohenstaufen Sicily was Latin Europe's other Arabic door: trilingual — Greek, Arabic, Latin — at court, and staffed by translators who worked from both learned languages. Eugenius of Palermo (c. 1130–1202), admiral and court scholar, translated Ptolemy's Optics from Arabic and carried Arabic fable into Greek and Latin circulation; beside him, Henricus Aristippus translated Plato and Aristotle from the Greek — a reminder the hub states plainly, that Sicily's door opened on Greek as well as Arabic, and that not everything Latin Europe learned in the twelfth century came by the Arabic road. The alchemical traffic through Sicily was thinner than through Toledo; the island's role in this hub is to mark the threshold's full width.
c. 1175 – c. 1232 CE
Michael Scot at the court of Frederick II — the art at an emperor's table. Michael Scot (c. 1175 – c. 1232), a Scot trained at Toledo and Bologna, became astrologer and translator to the emperor Frederick II at Palermo, rendering Aristotle's zoology and its Arabic commentaries — Averroes above all — into Latin, and answering, in writing, the emperor's questions on natural philosophy. Alchemy at Frederick's court was court science: tested, questioned, and folded into an imperial programme of inquiry that also produced the De arte venandi's falconry empiricism. Scot's later reputation — Dante sets him among the diviners, and folklore made him a wizard — is the Latin afterlife this hub notes and does not follow: it belongs to the Latin hub to come.
1256–1259 CE
The Picatrix crosses — al-Majrīṭī's summa in Castilian and Latin. At the court of Alfonso X of Castile, the Ghāyat al-ḥakīm ascribed to al-Majrīṭī was translated, by the royal translators' colophons, into Castilian in 1256 and from Castilian into Latin by 1259 — the Picatrix, the most influential book of astral magic the Latin Middle Ages possessed. The translation is a Wing I event performed on a Wing II text: an Andalusian Arabic summa of talismans, planetary images and the timing of operations, reissued as a Castilian king's book and then as Latin learning. What Alfonso's court wanted from it — a science of images and influences fit for a king — is a Latin question. That the book existed to be wanted is an Arabic one, and it is this hub's.
13th century CE
Abū al-Qāsim al-ʿIrāqī — the art in Mamluk Egypt. Abū al-Qāsim Muḥammad ibn Aḥmad al-ʿIrāqī (fl. 13th century), working in Egypt under the Ayyubids and early Mamluks, composed the Book of Knowledge Acquired Concerning the Cultivation of Gold (Kitāb al-muktasab fī zirāʿat al-dhahab) — a mature summa of the Arabic art: the ancients' sayings gathered and commented, the elixir's preparation argued step by step, and illustrated manuscripts that preserve some of the finest alchemical imagery in any language. Al-ʿIrāqī writes as a late master of a classical tradition, conscious of standing centuries after Jābir and al-Rāzī — and his near-contemporary al-Jildakī (d. 1342) would comment on them all in the century after this timeline closes. The Arabic art did not end when the Latin translations began; it continued, in Cairo and Damascus, on its own terms.
c. 1300 CE
Pseudo-Geber's Summa perfectionis — the threshold of the Latin hub. Around 1300, an unknown Latin author — on William Newman's demonstration, a Franciscan milieu, with the Franciscan Paul of Taranto proposed as the author — composed the Summa perfectionis magisterii, the Summa of "Geber": the clearest, most systematic alchemical treatise of the Middle Ages, setting out the sulphur–mercury theory in scholastic order and describing, with a precision no Arabic text matches, the strong mineral acids and their power to dissolve what fire alone could not. Those acids belong here — to the Latin pseudo-Geber — and not to the Arabic Jābir, however often popular writing gives them to him. The Summa is the Arabic tradition's most brilliant Latin heir and, simultaneously, a new beginning that is no longer this hub's subject. The timeline ends, deliberately, on the threshold of the future Latin hub — with the debt stated, the names disentangled, and the door left open.
◆ THRESHOLD — BEYOND THIS TIMELINE · THE LATIN HUB ◆
The translations of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries did not empty the Arabic art into Latin and leave it behind. Al-Jildakī was still commenting on Jābir in the 1340s; the Latin readers of pseudo-Geber were, in the same decades, beginning an art that would run to Newton. This hub's remit ends where that Latin art becomes its own subject. What the Arabic centuries built — the balance, the classifications, the bench discipline, the elixir — is recorded here, in its own language's terms, before the retelling begins.
// WHAT THE TIMELINE TEACHES //

Read down the entries and the hub's wager becomes visible. The translation movement was not a prelude to the Arabic art; it was its first act, and it took two centuries — Syriac schoolrooms, a Persian medical city, a Sabian star-city, and a caliphal capital buying Greek books by the embassy-load. The art that emerged was not Greek alchemy in Arabic dress: the balance, the classifications and the perfume-still are Baghdad's and Rayy's own. And the Latin door, when it opened, opened onto that rebuilt art — which is why the names on the door, Jābir and Geber, must be kept apart by anyone who wants to know what actually crossed.

V. Article Index

Ten full articles develop the hub's two wings — three for Wing I, Transmission, and seven for Wing II, The Art. This index is the eleventh page of the division; the Bibliography, gathering the hub's sources in one place, is the twelfth. Each article is listed here exactly once, under its wing, and each follows the hub's method: named editions, the corpus's pseudepigraphy flagged rather than smoothed over, Jābir kept distinct from pseudo-Geber, no invented quotations, speculation labelled.

Wing I — Transmission

Harran and Gundeshapur — The Bridge Cities

The two cities where Greek learning waited out the centuries between Alexandria and Baghdad: pagan Harran, whose Sabians kept Greek philosophy and its astral religion alive and sent Thābit ibn Qurra to the capital; and Gundeshapur, whose Syriac medical school sent Baghdad its physicians — the Bukhtīshūʿ dynasty — instead. Persons, not manuscripts, were the first bridge.

The Baghdad Translation Movement

The largest organised translation enterprise of the pre-modern world, told as Gutas taught the field to tell it: not a caliph's whim but a social movement — patrons competing, translators professionalising, Ḥunayn's circle collating Greek manuscripts and coining the Arabic vocabulary in which a new science could think. The Bayt al-Ḥikma is weighed at its real size.

The Latin Door — Toledo, Sicily, and 1144

How the rebuilt art crossed into Latin: Toledo's libraries after 1085, Robert of Chester's Morienus of 1144, Gerard of Cremona's seventy translations — the Jābirian Book of Seventy among them — Adelard's English road, and Sicily's trilingual court. The door is described from the Arabic side: what was on the shelves, and what the translators took.

Wing II — The Art

The Jābir Corpus — The Books of the Balances

The school-corpus at the hub's centre: Kraus's dating and its critics, the claimed descent from Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq stated as the corpus's own account, and the doctrine of the balance (mīzān) — nature as weighed proportion, letters and substances alike submitted to number. The most ambitious theory the pre-modern art produced, read in its own terms.

The Sulphur–Mercury Theory

The doctrine that metals are compounded, in the earth, of a fiery sulphur and a watery mercury — Aristotle's two exhalations rebuilt into the art's master theory. Its Arabic career is traced from the Jābirian books through its critics, and its Latin transformation is marked at the border: the clean scholastic version is pseudo-Geber's, and the hub says so.

Al-Rāzī and the Secret of Secrets

The bench manual of the golden age: al-Rāzī's classification of substances — spirits, bodies, stones, vitriols, boraces, salts — his ordered apparatus, and his procedures for sublimation, calcination, solution and coagulation, written to be repeated by other hands. Haq's Names, Natures and Things is the guide; the defence of the art against al-Kindī and Ibn Sīnā frames the whole.

Al-Kindī and the Chemistry of Perfume

The Book of the Chemistry of Perfume and Distillations: over a hundred recipes for scented oils, waters and unguents, and the stills, cucurbits and condensers to make them with — applied chemistry as an industry of luxury, from the pen of the age's first philosopher, who also wrote the age's first refutation of the gold-makers. Both books are read.

The Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ — The Brethren of Purity

Fifty-two epistles from an anonymous Basran fellowship: an encyclopedia of all knowledge in which minerals are generated, crafts are ranked, and the art takes its assigned place in a Neoplatonic cosmos of soul and number. The Brethren's readers overlapped the Jābirian school's — on Kraus's argument, so did their authors — and the article states that argument as his.

Ibn Umayl and the Allegorical Commentary

The tradition's third register: Ibn Umayl's Silvery Water and the Starry Earth, a commentary on the ancients' sayings and on the pictured tablet he describes in an Egyptian temple — birds, waters and kings read as the work's own portrait. The article follows the commentary form from tenth-century Egypt to its Latin afterlife as Senior Zadith.

Mineral Acids and the Workshop Record

The corrosive waters, weighed carefully and attributed exactly: the Arabic workshop's real solvents, salts and distillation practice, set out from the texts — and the strong mineral acids placed where the evidence places them, in the Latin pseudo-Geber's Summa around 1300, not in the Jābirian corpus. The hub's opening caution, discharged at the bench.

VI. Sources and Further Reading

The works below are the backbone scholarship of this hub — the books its timeline was checked against and its articles are built on. Wing I rests on Gutas and Saliba; Wing II on Ullmann's survey, on Kraus's Jābir, and on Haq's al-Rāzī; the Latin door on Newman's pseudo-Geber and Principe's history. The hub's synthesis rests on these works, and the hub says so honestly: primary texts are cited, in the editions used, in the articles' own Sources sections, and no claim in this hub should be traced further than the article that makes it traces it.

Wing I — Transmission and Translation

  • Gutas, D., Greek Thought, Arabic Culture: The Graeco-Arabic Translation Movement in Baghdad and Early ʿAbbāsid Society (2nd–4th/8th–10th centuries) (Routledge, 1998) — the modern foundation of Wing I: the translation movement as a social and political phenomenon, the patrons and the translators named, and the Bayt al-Ḥikma cut down to its documented size.
  • Saliba, G., Islamic Science and the Making of the European Renaissance (MIT Press, 2007) — the Arabic sciences in their own institutional world, and the channels — Spain, Sicily, the crusader states — by which they reached Europe; the frame for this hub's account of the Latin door.
  • Rosenthal, F., The Classical Heritage in Islam (English translation, Routledge & Kegan Paul, 1975) — the older standard survey of the Greek inheritance in Arabic, still the quickest orientation to what was translated, by whom, and what was not translated at all.

Wing II — The Arabic Art

  • Ullmann, M., Die Natur- und Geheimwissenschaften im Islam (Brill, 1972) — the standard survey of the natural and occult sciences in Islam, alchemy included: authors, works and manuscripts ordered as no English-language work has yet replaced. In German; the hub's bibliographical bedrock for Wing II.
  • Kraus, P., Jābir ibn Ḥayyān: Contribution à l'histoire des idées scientifiques dans l'Islam, 2 vols. (Cairo, 1942–43) — the work that redated the Jābirian corpus to a school of c. 850–950 and reconstructed its doctrine of the balance; unfinished at Kraus's death, and still the point of departure for every serious account of the corpus.
  • Haq, S. N., Names, Natures and Things: The Alchemist Jābir ibn Ḥayyān and His Kitāb al-Aḥjār (Book of Stones) (Kluwer, 1994) — the Jābirian science of the balance read through the Book of Stones, with al-Rāzī's classification and the corpus's language of natures and properties set out in full.
  • Holmyard, E. J., Alchemy (Penguin, 1957), and Holmyard's editions and studies of the Arabic alchemists in Isis and elsewhere — the pioneering English scholarship on the Arabic art, dated in places by Kraus and his successors, and still the most readable first map of the whole territory.
  • Ruska, J., Turba Philosophorum: Ein Beitrag zur Geschichte der Alchemie (Berlin, 1931) — edition and study of the Turba, with the argument for its lost Arabic original stated at full length; read here as the thesis it is, on the evidence Ruska himself assembles.
  • Stapleton, H. E., and collaborators, early English renderings of al-Rāzī's Kitāb sirr al-asrār (Book of the Secret of Secrets), published in the Memoirs of the Asiatic Society of Bengal and in Isis in the 1920s–30s — the pioneering translations of the Arabic art's bench manual, on which all later scholarship on al-Rāzī's laboratory practice builds.

The Latin Door

  • Newman, W. R., The Summa Perfectionis of Pseudo-Geber: A Critical Edition, Translation and Study (Brill, 1991) — the demonstration that the Latin "Geber" is a Latin author, the Summa's mineral acids and scholastic sulphur–mercury theory set out in full, and the Jābir/Geber distinction given its documentary basis. This hub's second caution rests on this book.
  • Principe, L. M., The Secrets of Alchemy (University of Chicago Press, 2013) — the current standard short history, from the Greco-Egyptian beginnings through the Arabic rebuilding to the Latin art and its laboratory reconstructions; this hub's check on its own chronology at the threshold where the two traditions meet.
// A STANDING CORRECTION //

Two confusions repeated in popular writing are checked and not repeated in this hub: the Arabic Jābirian corpus is a school's work of c. 850–950, not the product of a single eighth-century author writing in the lifetime of Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq; and the strong mineral acids are described by the Latin pseudo-Geber around 1300, not by Jābir — the five centuries and the language between those two facts are this hub's whole subject.

Dates in this hub will be revised as the literature moves — the dating debate over the Jābirian corpus has moved once within living scholarship, and may move again. Where it does, the timeline entry will be rewritten to say what changed and why, in line with the method set out above.
