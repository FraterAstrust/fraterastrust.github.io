I. A Corpus, Not a Book: What the Name Jābir Names

Every other article in this wing can begin with a person or a text. This one must begin with a caution, because its subject is neither securely a person nor a single text. “Jābir” — in full, Jābir ibn Ḥayyān, Latinised as Geber — is the name attached to the largest surviving body of alchemical writing in Arabic: a corpus of some two hundred extant treatises (the count is argued in Section V), organised by its own internal references into named collections, teaching a single interlocking system of matter, number, and operation, and presenting itself throughout as the work of one master dictating the teaching of his own master, the imam Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq. Modern scholarship, following Paul Kraus's demonstration of 1942–43, dates the corpus in the form we have it to c. 850–950 and reads it as the product of a school — a milieu of writers working under a single prestigious name across two or three generations — rather than of the eighth-century individual the tradition describes. That dating is this archive's canonical position, held consistently with the Egyptology hub's treatment (see Islamic Egyptology, §IX). It is also, as Section IV sets out in full, a debated position: serious scholars have defended an earlier date and a historical Jābir, and the question remains open in a sense this article will define precisely rather than blur.

The discipline of this article follows from that situation, and it is stated here once, at the head, so that its application everywhere below needs no repeating. Where authorship is at issue, this article does not write “Jābir wrote”. It writes “the corpus ascribed to Jābir”, “the Jābirian treatises”, “the corpus teaches”, “the corpus claims”. Where the traditional biography is being reported, it is reported as the tradition's account, in the tradition's own terms, and labelled as such. Where the corpus's doctrines are expounded — and they are expounded here in full, because they are among the most ambitious intellectual constructions of the medieval world — they are expounded as the corpus's doctrines, which they are whoever held the pen. The distinction costs a few words per paragraph. It buys the only thing this material cannot survive without: the reader always knows which kind of claim is being made.

THE NAME

Jābir ibn Ḥayyān, surnamed in the tradition al-Azdī, al-Ṣūfī, and al-Ṭarsūsī or al-Kūfī in different sources — the instability of the surnames is itself part of the evidence surveyed in Sections II–IV. Latin: Geber — a name that will acquire a second, unrelated corpus in Latin (Section XVI), and the two must never be merged.

THE CORPUS

Some 215 extant treatises by the count this archive uses, within a catalogue tradition that runs higher once lost and doubtful works are included (Section V). Organised into the Seventy Books, the Books of the Balances, the Five Hundred, and further collections, and bound together by an explicit system of cross-reference and deliberate dispersion (Sections V–VI).

THE SYSTEM

The science of the balance (ʿilm al-mīzān): every substance carries the four natures — hot, cold, wet, dry — in a determinate, computable proportion, in its outer and its inner constitution alike; the letters of a thing's Arabic name encode that proportion; and the canonical proportion of a perfected substance is 1:3:5:8. Expounded in Sections VII–IX.

THE QUESTION

One eighth-century author, or a ninth- and tenth-century school? The traditional biography, Kraus's redating, and the century of argument since are the spine of this article: Sections II–IV state the positions, name their holders, and specify what evidence would settle the matter and why it has not.

A second caution concerns quotation. The Jābirian corpus is, in its great majority, untranslated. A handful of treatises exist in modern editions and translations — the works listed in the Sources are the working shelf — but most of the corpus can still be read only in Arabic, much of it only in manuscript. It follows that this article gives no direct quotation from the corpus at all. Everything the corpus is reported as saying is paraphrase, drawn from the modern scholarly literature named in the Sources — Kraus's analysis above all, with Haq's systematic exposition and Lory's studies — and paraphrases are written as paraphrases. Where a formula is the corpus's own technical shorthand — the proportion 1:3:5:8, the paired terms ẓāhir and bāṭin, the name ʿilm al-mīzān itself — it is given as terminology, not dressed as quotation. Readers who meet flowing English “quotations from Jābir” in popular books are almost always meeting a modern author's paraphrase, a Latin pseudo-Geber text in disguise, or an invention; the archive's rule is to make the join visible.

// HOW TO READ THE DATES IN THIS ARTICLE //

Three dates recur and must not be fused. c. 815 is the traditional death-date of the biographical Jābir. c. 850–950 is the dating of the corpus in its surviving form on Kraus's thesis, the dating this archive uses. c. 1310 is the outer limit for the Latin pseudo-Geber's Summa perfectionis, a different body of writing altogether (Section XVI). An account that slides between the first and the second, or attributes the third's contents to either, has lost the thread of the whole subject.

What, then, is the corpus for? Read on its own terms, it is nothing less than a complete science of nature built on a single instrument: the balance. Where Greek alchemy — the world of the Greco-Roman hub's Wing II — worked by recipe, sympathy, and apparatus, and left its theory largely implicit in its operations, the corpus ascribed to Jābir builds the theory first and derives the operations from it. Metals are constituted of sulphur and mercury in proportions the balance can state (Section X). The elixir is not a wonder-substance stumbled on by adepts but a composition whose nature- proportion can be computed before it is compounded (Section XI). Language itself is drawn into the system: the letters of a name are a quantitative signature of the thing named, so that analysis of a word and analysis of a substance are two entries to one table (Section VIII). And the ambition does not stop at the laboratory door: the same balance is applied to souls, to the heavens, and to the structure of religious knowledge itself (Section IX). No earlier alchemical literature attempts anything on this scale. Whether the attempt is science, arithmology, theology, or a fusion for which modern categories have no single word is a question the corpus forces on every reader; Sections VII–IX give the system in enough detail for the reader to feel the force of the question.

The corpus also matters historically in the plainest way: it was read. Al-Rāzī worked in a world the Jābirian treatises had already furnished (see Al-Rāzī); the later commentators — al-Ṭughrāʾī, Abū al-Qāsim al-ʿIrāqī, above all al-Jildakī — preserved, in their quotations, treatises otherwise lost (Section XV); one collection crossed into Latin in the twelfth century (Section XVI); and the sulphur–mercury constitution of metals, in the corpus's systematised form, remained the art's default matter-theory into the eighteenth century (see The Sulphur–Mercury Theory). A corpus that founds a science's literature for eight hundred years is not a footnote to the history of alchemy but one of its main chapters, and this article treats it at that length.

II. The Traditional Biography: Kufa, Jaʿfar, and the Caliph's Court

The tradition's Jābir is a vivid figure, and the tradition tells his story with a completeness that is itself one of the problems. In the account preserved by the bibliographer Ibn al-Nadīm in the Fihrist (completed 987) and elaborated by later biographers, Jābir ibn Ḥayyān was a client or scion of the Azd tribe, associated with Kufa in Iraq and, in some reports, with Ṭūs in Khurasan, where his father Ḥayyān is said to have been a druggist or perfumer executed for supporting the ʿAbbāsid cause before its triumph. The son, formed in Kufa — in the tradition's telling, the great Shiʿi city of early Islam — became the disciple and intimate of the imam Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq (d. 765), the sixth imam of the Shiʿa and a figure of immense religious authority, from whom, the corpus itself insists on nearly every page, all of its teaching derives. Drawn to Baghdad, the traditional Jābir found patrons in the Barmakid viziers and stood, in the story's fullest form, at the court of the caliph Hārūn al-Rashīd (r. 786–809); when the Barmakids fell in 803 he withdrew, and he died — the conventional date is c. 815 — in Kufa, in concealment or in retirement, leaving behind the ocean of books the tradition numbers in the thousands of treatises.

Every element of that paragraph must carry its label: it is the tradition's account, assembled from sources written a century and more after the events they describe, and from the corpus's own presentation of itself. That is not a dismissal. Medieval biographical traditions often preserve real kernels, and Section IV gives the scholars who defend this one's kernel their full hearing. But three features of the account belong to the biography of a legend-forming name as much as to the life of a man, and the reader should see them plainly. First, the master: attachment to Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq gives the corpus the highest religious warrant available in its milieu — the teaching is not the author's invention but the imam's disclosed knowledge — and that warrant functions identically whether or not any historical discipleship occurred. Second, the court: attachment to Hārūn's Baghdad places the art at the summit of the age's patronage, in the city and generation of the great translation movement (see The Translation Movement), and again the placement is exactly the placement a later corpus would choose for its founder. Third, the productivity: a single lifetime's output of hundreds of systematic treatises, cross-referring to one another as parts of one design, is a literary fact about the corpus before it is a biographical fact about any writer, and it is the fact Kraus's thesis is built to explain.

KUFA

The tradition's home city and the corpus's recurring setting: a garrison city become a centre of Shiʿi learning, grammar, and speculation in the eighth and ninth centuries. Kufa's milieu — imam-centred teaching circles, disputation, esoteric exegesis — is the soil in which both the biographical Jābir and, on Kraus's reading, the school that wrote under his name are equally at home.

JAʿFAR AL-ṢĀDIQ (d. 765)

The corpus's named master, addressed in its pages with a devotion no other authority receives: the teaching is repeatedly presented, in paraphrase, as what Jaʿfar disclosed, permitted, or commanded to be written. Alchemical and occult works were ascribed to Jaʿfar himself in the same centuries — the imam's name anchors a whole literature, of which the Jābirian corpus is the largest part.

THE BARMAKIDS AND HĀRŪN'S COURT

The tradition's patronage setting: the Barmakid viziers, patrons of translation and of the sciences at Baghdad until their fall in 803. The association explains, within the story, both the corpus's Greek learning and its author's disappearance from public life — a narrative joint that does double duty, and is flagged here for doing so.

THE FIHIRST'S EARLY DOUBT

Decisive for the debate's shape: Ibn al-Nadīm, our earliest full witness (987), does not transmit the biography as uncontested fact. He reports, in paraphrase, that a party of scholars denied that Jābir had ever existed and held the books to be the work of others writing under his name, while the Shiʿa claimed him as one of their great ones and the philosophers claimed the books. The doubt is as old as the record.

That last card deserves its paragraph, because it disciplines everything that follows. The question of this article is sometimes presented as a modern scepticism imposed on a trusting tradition. The Fihrist shows the opposite: by the late tenth century the existence of Jābir, the attribution of the corpus, and the sectarian ownership of both were already live disputes among scholars who stood far closer to the material than we do. Ibn al-Nadīm himself comes down, in paraphrase of his discussion, on the side of a real Jābir and real authorship — while faithfully preserving the denial he disagrees with, together with a catalogue of the works that is one of the foundations of all later counting (Section V). The debate of Sections III–IV is therefore not anachronistic. It is the continuation, with better tools, of an argument the Arabic tradition was already having about its own foundation corpus within a century of that corpus's completion on the dating this archive accepts.

// THE BIOGRAPHY'S EVIDENTIARY STATUS, STATED ONCE //

No contemporary document — no dated manuscript, no inscription, no citation by a securely dated eighth-century author — attests the biographical Jābir. The earliest witnesses are the corpus itself, whose testimony is the thing in question, and the tenth-century bibliographical tradition, which already reports the dispute. This does not prove the man did not live; eighth-century Iraq leaves many real scholars nearly as shadowy. It fixes the burden of the argument: the biography is a tradition to be assessed, not a datum to be assumed, and Sections III–IV assess it on the internal evidence of the books themselves, which is where Kraus located the decisive testimony.

III. Kraus's Thesis: A School of the Later Ninth and Tenth Centuries

The modern debate begins with one scholar and two volumes. Paul Kraus (1904–1944), a Prague-born orientalist working in Cairo, devoted the last years of a short life to the corpus ascribed to Jābir, and his Jābir ibn Ḥayyān: Contribution à l'histoire des idées scientifiques dans l'Islam — volume II, on Jābir and Greek science, in 1942; volume I, presenting the corpus and its catalogue, in 1943 — remains the foundation on which all subsequent study stands, including the work of his critics. He had prepared the ground with an edition of selected treatises, the Mukhtār rasāʾil Jābir ibn Ḥayyān (Cairo, 1935), and the Contribution brought to the corpus, for the first time, the full apparatus of source-criticism: a catalogue raisonné of the works, an analysis of their Greek sources, and a reconstruction of the system from the inside. Its conclusion about authorship was stated without equivocation and has never been displaced as the position to beat: in paraphrase of Kraus's argument, the corpus is not the work of an eighth-century author but the collective production of a school, composed and redacted in the later ninth and the tenth century — in the dating this archive adopts, c. 850–950 — within a Shiʿi, specifically Ismāʿīlī-linked, intellectual milieu.

The Arguments: Anachronism, System, and Cross-Reference

Kraus's case is cumulative, and its strands should be distinguished, because his critics answer them with unequal success. The first strand is doctrinal anachronism. The corpus's intellectual world — its Neoplatonic emanation schemes, its developed cosmology of intellect and soul, its handling of Aristotelian logic and categories, its religious vocabulary of outer and inner, its parallels with the encyclopaedia of the Brethren of Purity and with early Ismāʿīlī thought — belongs, on Kraus's analysis, to the Arabic philosophy of the ninth and tenth centuries, not to the eighth. A disciple of Jaʿfar who died about 815 would have had to anticipate, by two generations and more, systems that the dated record shows being built, piece by piece, by the translation movement and its successors across exactly the decades to which Kraus reassigns the books. The second strand is the corpus's Greek science, the subject of Kraus's second volume: the treatises know Greek medicine, physics, and alchemy in forms mediated by the Arabic translations, and their citations of Greek authorities run through the channels — and sometimes through the specific renderings — of the ninth-century translators, a dependence hard to square with an author who died before most of those translations existed. The third strand is literary: the corpus cross-refers to itself as a planned whole, distributes one teaching across hundreds of treatises by an avowed method (Section VI), and speaks, beneath the changes of genre and addressee, in a recognisably single doctrinal voice over an output no plausible biography can contain. A school, writing and revising across generations under one master-name, explains all three strands at once; a single eighth-century author explains none of them without strain.

// WHAT KRAUS DID NOT CLAIM //

Kraus's thesis is often vulgarised into “Jābir never existed”, and the vulgarisation should be refused. Kraus demonstrated, to his own satisfaction and largely to the field's, that the corpus as we have it is a school production of c. 850–950. That demonstration is compatible with several positions on the man: that no historical Jābir stood behind the name at all; that a real eighth-century alchemist's name and perhaps a small core of writings were taken up and vastly expanded by the school; or that the school's master-figure is a deliberate construction around Jaʿfar's authority. Section IV keeps these positions distinct, because the scholars who hold them do.

The milieu Kraus proposed for the school gives the thesis its second, still-debated half. The corpus's religious architecture — its imam-centred authority, its hermeneutic of outer (ẓāhir) and inner (bāṭin) senses applied to nature as to scripture, its cyclical and hierarchical cosmology — struck Kraus as specifically Ismāʿīlī, and he connected the school's activity with the Ismāʿīlī missionary movement of Iraq and western Persia in its Qarmaṭian phase, the decades around 900 when that movement was building states and armies as well as doctrines. The connection, if it holds, would make the corpus a document of one of the most consequential religious movements of the medieval Islamic world, and its science an arm of that movement's teaching. Section XIV examines the claim at the length it deserves and with the caution later scholarship has counselled; here it is enough to register that Kraus advanced it as an inference from doctrinal parallels and historical fit, not as a documented affiliation — no colophon names a mission, no treatise preaches a rising — and that the inference has been received accordingly: as the most interesting unproven proposition in Jābirian studies.

One further element of Kraus's work must be named here, because Sections V and XVII both rest on it: the catalogue — a numbered catalogue of 298 items, extant, lost, and doubtful together, each with its manuscripts and its place in the corpus's own collection-scheme. The number 298 is therefore a count of catalogued titles, not of surviving books; its relation to the archive's working figure of some 215 extant treatises is a matter of counting units, addressed head-on in Section V. Kraus died in Cairo in 1944, and the field he founded has spent eighty years inside the house he built — working from his catalogue, his collection-scheme, and his statement of the problem.

IV. The Debate After Kraus: Sezgin, Haq, Lory, and an Open Question

Kraus's thesis did not pass unchallenged, and the challenge came from the scholar best equipped to make it. Fuat Sezgin, in the fourth volume of his Geschichte des arabischen Schrifttums (1971), devoted to alchemy and the sciences, mounted the systematic defence of the traditional position: a historical Jābir, an eighth-century floruit, and a corpus whose core is genuinely his. Sezgin's method was bibliographical mass brought to bear on Kraus's anachronisms one by one. Where Kraus saw a doctrine that could not predate the ninth century, Sezgin argued, in case after case, for earlier channels: Greek material available in Syriac before the great Baghdad translations, ideas circulating in the eighth century that the surviving dated record happens to attest only later, citations that need not depend on the specific translations Kraus matched them to. The defence also pressed a methodological point with real force: arguments from the silence or lateness of surviving parallels are fragile in a field where most eighth- and ninth-century Arabic scientific writing is lost, and Kraus — Sezgin held — had converted that fragility into certainty. Sezgin's Jābir is the tradition's Jābir restored by erudition: the disciple of Jaʿfar, the contemporary of the first translations, the founder writing at the fountainhead rather than a school writing at the delta.

Between the poles, the field's working positions have settled into a spectrum rather than a verdict, and the two names that mark its most influential middle positions are Haq and Lory. S. Nomanul Haq, in Names, Natures and Things (1994) — the fullest modern exposition of the balance system in English, and one of this article's principal guides to Sections VII–IX — accepts the essential shape of Kraus's redating while refusing to let the authorship question swallow the philosophy. Haq's corpus is layered: treatises of differing date and hand, gathered into a system whose coherence is a fact about the school's work of redaction as much as about any originating mind; and his book's energy goes not into the biography but into demonstrating that the system, so produced, is a serious and internally articulated philosophy of nature, to be analysed with the same care as any Greek or Latin system. Pierre Lory, in Alchimie et mystique en terre d'Islam (1989) and in a continuing body of editions and studies, reads the corpus from its religious side: a Shiʿi-inflected spiritual alchemy in which the laboratory work and the soul's transformation are one teaching in two registers, its Ismāʿīlī colouring real but to be handled — Lory is explicit — as resonance and milieu rather than as a membership card. Lory's editions of individual treatises have also done the unglamorous work on which all positions depend: putting reliable Arabic texts, with translation and commentary, into scholars' hands.

An honest article must end this survey by saying what would settle the question and why nothing has. The question would be settled, or radically narrowed, by evidence of three kinds. A dated early witness — a manuscript of a Jābirian treatise copied in the eighth or very early ninth century, or a quotation of a distinctive Jābirian doctrine in a work securely dated to those decades — would vindicate the early dating at a stroke; none has appeared, and the surviving manuscripts are centuries later than either proposed date. A demonstrable dependence of a core treatise on a source itself securely dated after about 850 — a specific translation, a datable doctrine in its developed form — would close the case for Kraus treatise by treatise; Kraus offered many such dependencies, and Sezgin's rejoinders show that most can be individually contested even where their cumulative weight impresses. Finally, stylometric and redactional analysis across the whole corpus, of a kind the still-unedited state of most treatises makes impossible (Section XVII), could map the school's growth directly. The evidence has not settled the question because the corpus is vast, mostly unedited, preserved in late manuscripts, and internally layered — a body of evidence on which a cumulative case can be built for more than one conclusion. This archive's position — Kraus's dating, c. 850–950, a school behind the name — is adopted because the cumulative anachronism and cross-reference arguments remain, on balance, the strongest reading of the evidence available. It is adopted as a position, stated here with its holders and its opponents named, and nowhere in this hub is it allowed to harden, silently, into the pretence that the eighth-century Jābir has been disproved as a man rather than displaced as an author.

// THE ARCHIVE'S RULE FOR CITING JĀBIR //

Throughout this hub: doctrines are cited to “the corpus ascribed to Jābir ibn Ḥayyān (c. 850–950)”; the biography is cited to “the tradition”; the Latin Summa perfectionis is cited to the pseudo-Geber and never to Jābir. A citation that follows the rule cannot mislead; one that breaks it — “Jābir wrote in 800 that” — smuggles a contested biography, a contested date, and sometimes a Latin forgery into a single clause.

V. Counting the Corpus: Catalogues and the Great Collections

How many works ascribed to Jābir are there? The literature offers numbers that differ by an order of magnitude — the tradition's thousands, Kraus's 298, this archive's working figure of about 215 — and the differences are not errors. They are counts of different things, and reconciling them honestly is a small model of how to handle every number in Jābirian studies. The tradition's thousands — the Fihrist already reports attributions running to thousands of treatises, and later biographers repeat and swell them — count attributions as rumoured: every short tract, excerpt, and recipe-scroll that ever carried the name, swollen by the medieval habit of crediting a famous master with a whole literature. Kraus's 298 counts catalogued titles: every distinct work his catalogue could identify from the corpus's own lists, the bibliographers, and the manuscripts — including works known only by title, works surviving in fragments or in other authors' quotations, and works whose attribution Kraus himself flagged as doubtful. The archive's c. 215 extant treatises, the figure used in the Egyptology hub's treatment and retained here, counts surviving treatises: works of which a text, complete or substantially complete, is preserved in at least one manuscript. Subtract the lost and the merely titular from 298 and the figures converge; the residue of difference is the genuinely uncertain margin — excerpts that may be chapters, chapters that may be treatises, double titles for single works — on which cataloguers of an unedited corpus will always differ by a handful. The honest sentence is the compound one: 298 titles catalogued by Kraus, of which some 215 treatises survive — and any source that gives a single bare number for “the works of Jābir”, without saying what it is counting, should be read accordingly.

The Great Collections

The corpus does not present itself as a heap of tracts. It presents itself as a set of collections, each with a name, a place in the teaching, and — characteristically — a number for a title. Kraus's reconstruction of the collection-scheme, from the corpus's own lists and cross-references, distinguishes the following great gatherings. The 112 Books, the earliest stratum on Kraus's ordering, dedicated in the corpus's framing to the Barmakid patrons of the traditional biography. The Seventy Books (Kitāb al-Sabʿīn) — seventy treatises of doctrine and practice, and the one collection with a great Latin future (Section XVI). The Ten Books of Rectification, short works of correction and supplement. The Books of the Balances (Kutub al-Mawāzīn) — the theoretical heart of the whole corpus, the collection in which ʿilm al-mīzān is built, and the collection this article's subtitle honours. And the Five Hundred (al-Kutub al-Khamsamiʾa), the vast late gathering whose title, like the tradition's thousands, is a programme rather than an inventory: a claim that the teaching, fully dispersed, comprehends everything. Around and beneath these stand treatises the scheme does not capture — independent works, fragments, and the pseudepigrapha within the pseudepigraphon, later attributions to Jābir that even the medieval cataloguers doubted.

THE SEVENTY BOOKS — KITĀB AL-SABʿĪN

Seventy treatises moving between theory and workshop practice: the constitution of metals, the elixir, the operations. Translated into Latin by Gerard of Cremona as the Liber de septuaginta, and thereby the corpus's principal ambassador to Europe (Section XVI).

THE BOOKS OF THE BALANCES — KUTUB AL-MAWĀZĪN

The corpus's theoretical summit: the treatises — among them the Greater and Lesser Books of the Balance — in which the science of the balance is stated as a universal method. Sections VII–IX are, in substance, an exposition of this collection's teaching.

THE FIVE HUNDRED

The great late collection, gathering treatises on every part of the art and its neighbouring sciences. Its scale is the school's monument: whatever the exact count of its contents in any recension, the Five Hundred is where the corpus's ambition to be a complete library under one name stands most fully realised.

THE CROSS-REFERENCE SYSTEM

The collections are bound by the corpus's own citations: treatise refers to treatise by title, doctrine is deferred to its proper book, and the reader is sent forward and back across the whole. The system is the school's signature — no heap of independent tracts cites itself into a curriculum — and a principal strand of Kraus's case (Section III).

The cross-reference system deserves a final emphasis, because it changes what kind of object the corpus is. A recipe-scroll does not refer its reader to a theoretical treatise in another collection for the grounds of its procedure; a curriculum does. The treatises behave, persistently, like chapters of a dispersed encyclopaedia that knows its own table of contents: the practical books presuppose the Balances, the Balances remit applications to the practical books. That scattering has a name and a doctrine of its own, and it is the subject of the next section.

VI. Tabdīd al-ʿIlm: Dispersion as the Corpus's Own Method

The corpus explains itself, and its explanation is one of the most remarkable pieces of methodological self-consciousness in medieval literature. The teaching, it states in paraphrase across many prefaces and asides, has been deliberately dispersed: tabdīd al-ʿilm, the scattering of knowledge, is the corpus's own term for its own form. No single treatise contains the whole doctrine. A principle is stated in one book, its proof deferred to a second, its application exemplified in a third, and its key term defined — if anywhere — in a fourth; the reader who would possess the science must gather it, treatise by treatise, in the order the cross-references prescribe, and the gathering is itself the initiation. The corpus is explicit, again in paraphrase, about the reasons. Knowledge of this power must not fall entire into unfit hands: dispersion is a lock, and only the reader who has done the work of reassembly — under a teacher, within the discipline the books demand — holds the key. And dispersion is also a preservative: a teaching scattered across hundreds of books in many cities cannot be destroyed by the loss, suppression, or burning of any one of them.

Modern readers divide over how to take the doctrine, and the division maps onto the authorship debate without coinciding with it. On one reading, tabdīd al-ʿilm is sincere pedagogy and sincere secrecy: the school's genuine method, of a piece with the esoteric hermeneutics of its milieu, in which truth is by nature bāṭin — inner, veiled — and a book that handed it over entire would betray it. On a second reading, it is redactional candour: a school that had in fact accumulated, across generations, a vast and not perfectly consistent literature, and that elevated the fact into a principle — the inconsistencies become dispersions, the repetitions become reinforcements placed at calculated distances, the gaps become veils. On a third reading, the two are the same: a milieu for which esotericism was the native theory of knowledge would naturally organise even its accidents esoterically, and the doctrine's sincerity is proved precisely by its convenience. This article does not adjudicate the motive. It registers the effect, which is a matter of record: the corpus cannot be read in extracts. Every modern exposition — Kraus's, Haq's, and this article's Sections VII–IX included — is a reassembly performed against the corpus's design, gathering into one place what the books deliberately put asunder, and the reader should know that the tidy system of the next three sections is tidy because its expositors made it so, out of materials their author or authors scattered on purpose.

// DISPERSION AND THE WORKED EXAMPLE //

The dispersion doctrine is why Section VIII's worked example of the letter-balance is labelled a reconstruction of the method. No single treatise states the whole procedure in one place, in a form a modern reader can simply transcribe; the procedure must be gathered from the Balances collection and its commentators in the secondary literature. An exposition that presented the gathered procedure as a quotation would be falsifying the corpus's form as well as its words.

Dispersion has a companion doctrine: the distinction, applied to books as to nature, between outer (ẓāhir) and inner (bāṭin). A treatise's outer sense is its surface teaching — the recipe, the procedure, the plain doctrine; its inner sense is what the surface veils: the balance-proportions behind the recipe, the teaching about the soul behind the teaching about metals. The pair comes from the Qurʾānic exegesis of the corpus's Shiʿi milieu, where scripture has its ẓāhir and its bāṭin and the imam is the bāṭin's authorised interpreter; the corpus's audacity is to extend the pair from scripture to nature and art. A metal, too, has its outer and inner constitution (Section VII), and a book about a metal does too: text and world are written in the same two registers, and the same master-key, the balance, reads both.

One practical consequence closes the section. Because the teaching is dispersed and double-registered, the corpus is difficult by design, and its difficulty has governed its reception at every stage: the medieval commentators exist because the books need gathering (Section XV); the Latin translators took the most self-contained collection and left the Balances largely alone (Section XVI); and the modern field advances treatise by edited treatise, because there is no shortcut through a library built to have none (Section XVII). To read the corpus ascribed to Jābir is to accept employment in the school's own workshop: the reassembly of a scattered teaching. The next three sections perform that reassembly for the teaching's centre — the balance itself.

VII. The Science of the Balance I: The Four Natures, Outer and Inner

The science of the balance — ʿilm al-mīzān — is the corpus's claim to have founded a science where its predecessors had an art. Its founding postulate can be stated in one sentence, in paraphrase of the Balances treatises as the modern literature expounds them: every substance carries the four natures — the hot, the cold, the wet, and the dry — in a determinate proportion, and that proportion is what the substance is. The four natures are the Aristotelian elementary qualities, inherited through the Greek tradition this archive's Greco-Roman hub lays out (Fire hot and dry, Air hot and wet, Water cold and wet, Earth cold and dry — the quality square in its canonical form), but the corpus puts them to a use Aristotle never envisaged. For Aristotle the qualities define the elements and explain their transformations qualitatively: a body is hotter or colder, wetter or drier, by degree and by dominance. For the corpus ascribed to Jābir, the qualities in a body are quantities: each nature is present in a substance in an exact, statable amount, the four amounts stand in a ratio, and the ratio — not the stuff, not the appearance — is the substance's identity, its diagnosis, and its prescription in one. To know a thing, in this science, is to know its balance.

Ẓāhir and Bāṭin: Two Constitutions in Every Body

The postulate is immediately doubled, and the doubling is the system's most original move. Every substance has two constitutions. Its outer constitution (ẓāhir) is the proportion of the natures as they manifest: what the senses and the simple tests report — the metal's colour, fusibility, weight, taste and smell in the period's diagnostic repertory, the manifest heat or coldness of a drug, the visible moisture or dryness of a body. Its inner constitution (bāṭin) is the proportion of the natures as they are hidden within: the latent disposition that does not appear until the substance is acted on — until it is heated, dissolved, calcined, fermented, or compounded — and that governs what it will become under those actions. A body's outer and inner proportions are not, in general, the same, and the science's whole practical content lives in the difference. The alchemist who works by the outer constitution alone is a cook following appearances; the master of the balance computes the inner constitution — by the method of Section VIII — and works on that: supplying what the inner proportion lacks, subduing what it has in excess, until the hidden ratio is brought to the perfected form and the body's outer manifestation follows its corrected inner truth. Transmutation, in this system, is not the imposition of a new nature on a body. It is the correction of a body's own hidden arithmetic.

THE OUTER — ẒĀHIR

The manifest proportion: the natures as they show themselves to sense and simple test. Real, but superficial — a body's ẓāhir is its symptom, and a medicine or an art that treats symptoms alone leaves the inner disposition, and so the body's future behaviour, untouched.

THE INNER — BĀṬIN

The hidden proportion: the natures as they lie latent within, disclosed by operation and computed by the letter-balance. The true object of the science — and the reason the science needs a method of computation, not merely of observation (Section VIII).

DEGREES AND RANKS

Each nature in a body is present not only in an amount but at a degree of intensity: the corpus's analysis distinguishes the nature, its degree (martaba), and its subdivisions, in a multi-level scheme the modern literature reconstructs from the Balances treatises. Quantity and intensity together fix the proportion the balance states.

THE BALANCE AS INSTRUMENT

The mīzān is at once a metaphor, a method, and — in the corpus's cosmological extension — a structure of the world itself (Section IX). Its laboratory meaning is precise: a proportion is a thing that can be weighed, compared against a standard, and adjusted. The standard is given in Section VIII.

The system's relation to its Greek inheritance should be marked exactly, because the corpus is neither a copy of Greek theory nor a rejection of it. The four natures, the elements they qualify, the humoral applications in medicine, the very idea that a body's state is a mixture (the Greek krasis, the Arabic mizāj — a word from the same root as mīzān, balance) capable of balance and imbalance: all of this is the common property of the Greek-Arabic scientific world, expounded for the Greek side in the Greco-Roman hub's Foundations articles. The corpus's novelties are three, and they are the novelties of a quantifier. First, exactness: proportions are not “more hot than cold” but ratios of integers, statable and comparable across all substances in a single arithmetic. Second, the inner constitution: a second, hidden ratio behind the manifest one, making the science a theory of latent structure a millennium before that phrase had a home. Third, the method of access: the inner ratio, being hidden, cannot be read off the body — it must be computed, and the corpus computes it from the one quantitative signature every substance openly carries: its name. To that computation — the most famous, most debated, and most easily caricatured part of the whole system — the next section is devoted.

// NOT A METAPHOR, IN THE CORPUS'S OWN TERMS //

It is tempting, and it is a temptation the older literature did not resist, to read the balance as a picturesque metaphor for qualitative mixture — a vivid way of saying that bodies differ in their qualities. The corpus's texts, as Kraus and Haq expound them, resist the reading: the proportions are given as numbers, the numbers are operated on arithmetically, and practical conclusions — how much elixir, of what composition, for which metal — are drawn from the operations. Whether the arithmetic was ever successfully applied at a bench is a separate question, and an open one. That it was meant literally, as the science's method, is the scholarly consensus this article follows.

VIII. The Science of the Balance II: Letters, Numbers, and 1:3:5:8

How is a hidden proportion to be known? The corpus's answer is its most distinctive doctrine: from the letters of the substance's Arabic name. The doctrine rests on a premise the corpus shares with its whole intellectual world and states with unusual rigour: a true name is not an arbitrary label. In the science of the balance, the name of a thing — its Arabic name, in the language the corpus treats as the measure of things — is a quantitative signature of the thing named, because its letters stand, in fixed order and fixed values, in the same kind of determinate structure as the natures within the body. The analysis of the name and the analysis of the substance are therefore not analogy but identity of method: parse the word, and you have parsed the body. This is the doctrine Haq's title commemorates — Names, Natures and Things — and it is expounded here in the form the modern literature reconstructs from the dispersed treatises (Section VI): first the machinery, then the canonical proportion, then a worked example labelled for exactly what it is.

The Machinery: Abjad Values, Nature-Classes, Degrees

The machinery has three parts. First, the letters and their values: Arabic letters carry numerical values in the abjad scheme — the old Semitic letter-order in which alif is 1, bāʾ 2, jīm 3, dāl 4, hāʾ 5, wāw 6, zāy 7, ḥāʾ 8, ṭāʾ 9, yāʾ 10, kāf 20, lām 30, mīm 40, nūn 50, sīn 60, ʿayn 70, fāʾ 80, ṣād 90, qāf 100, rāʾ 200, shīn 300, tāʾ 400, and onward by hundreds to ghayn at 1000. The abjad values are the alphabet's public arithmetic, used across the culture for chronograms, magic squares, and letter-speculation of every kind; the corpus takes the scheme up as its raw material. Second, the classification of the letters by nature: in the Balances treatises' tables, the twenty-eight letters of Arabic are distributed among the four natures, each letter belonging to one nature and standing at a determinate degree within it — the alphabet becomes a periodic table of qualitative atoms, seven letters to a nature, ranked by intensity. Third, the analysis of the name: the substance's name is resolved into its letters; each letter contributes its nature, at its degree, in its position; and the contributions are summed, level by level — the corpus distinguishes levels of analysis, from the letter's manifest nature down to the finest subdivision of quantity — until the body's inner proportion stands stated as four numbers. Those numbers, reduced to their simplest ratio, are the substance's bāṭin: the hidden truth the senses could not give.

The Canonical Proportion: 1:3:5:8

Against what standard is a computed proportion judged? The corpus's answer is the most famous number-sequence in Arabic alchemy: 1 : 3 : 5 : 8. In a perfected substance — paradigmatically, in the elixir, and by reflection in the gold the elixir makes — the four natures stand to one another in the proportion of one to three to five to eight. The sequence sums to 17, and seventeen recurs through the corpus's arithmology as the number of the completed balance: the total of parts in a perfected whole, against which imperfect totals and imperfect distributions are measured. The Egyptology hub's summary states the doctrine in its compressed form — the hidden natures, computed from the letters of the name, fall in the proportion 1:3:5:8 — and this article retains that statement as the doctrine's headline, with two precisions the headline needs. The proportion is a proportion of the inner constitution in the first instance: it is the bāṭin's perfected form, which the work then realises outwardly. And the assignment of the four terms to the four natures, and the level of analysis at which the proportion is to be read, are matters the treatises handle with variations the dispersion of the teaching makes laborious to reconcile — one of the many points at which the corpus's expositors must report a system with ragged edges rather than ironing them flat. What does not vary is the sequence itself and its sum: where the balance is perfected, the natures stand as 1, 3, 5, and 8 stand.

// THE WORKED EXAMPLE BELOW IS A RECONSTRUCTION //

The example that follows is a schematic reconstruction of the method, composed for this article from the procedure as Kraus and Haq expound it. It is not a quotation, not a translation, and not a transcription of any single treatise's worked case — the corpus, true to its dispersion, nowhere hands the modern reader a complete end-to-end computation in one place, and its own letter-tables assign natures and degrees in detail that varies between treatises. The abjad values in the first table are the standard scheme's, and are factual. The nature-totals in the second table are demonstration figures, chosen so the arithmetic can be followed and checked; the operations performed on them — reduction against the canonical 1:3:5:8, computation of excess and deficit, derivation of the elixir's task — are the corpus's method as the scholarship reconstructs it.

Take, for the demonstration, the Arabic name of gold: dhahab (ذهب). Resolved into letters, and valued by the abjad scheme, the name gives:

The abjad values themselves are not the proportion — a point caricatures of the system habitually miss. The values place each letter in the scheme; the corpus's tables then read off, for each letter, its nature and its degree, and the analysis proceeds through its levels — nature, degree, rank, and quantity in the modern literature's summary — summing the contributions nature by nature. Suppose, now, the completed analysis of two substances, stated as nature-totals in parts, with the four natures in the order hot, dry, wet, cold. Read the table as the corpus's practitioner was meant to read it, and the whole theory's practicality stands revealed. The lead's analysis does not merely say the metal is imperfect; it says in what, and by how much — and those are the only two things a corrective art needs to know. The excesses (+2 hot, +2 dry) are to be drawn off or subdued by the operations whose qualitative action runs the other way; the deficits (−1 wet, −3 cold) are to be supplied, and the vehicle of supply is the elixir: a substance itself constituted at the canon, compounded — Section XI takes this up — so that its application delivers exactly the natures the patient metal lacks, in exactly the amounts the analysis prescribes, while its own perfected balance withstands the fire that would dissipate a cruder medicine. Diagnosis, prescription, and pharmacology in a single table of small integers: that is what the corpus means by a science of the balance, and it is why the theory cannot be dismissed as decoration on a practical art. In the corpus's own economy, the theory is the practice's instructions, written in the only language — number — that the hidden constitution speaks.

Two honest limits close the exposition. First, the demonstration's figures are schematic, as flagged: the corpus's real analyses run through more levels, larger numbers, and letter-tables whose reconciliation across treatises remains scholars' work, and no modern expositor can hand the reader a verified end-to-end Jābirian computation of a real substance in the way the table above performs one for a demonstration case. Second, the method's premise — that an Arabic name encodes a nature — binds the science to one language's lexicon: substances whose Arabic names are loanwords, synonyms with different letters, or names of disputed form pose problems the corpus itself worries at, in discussions of true and conventional naming that Haq's exposition follows in detail. A universal science computed from the vocabulary of a single language carries its paradox on its face, and the corpus, to its credit, seems to have known it: the question of which name is a thing's true name — the name whose letters balance — is one of its own recurring problems, not a modern objection sprung on it from outside.

IX. The Science of the Balance III: One Science of Language, Souls, and Cosmos

A method that reads hidden proportions from letters will not stay inside a laboratory, and in the corpus it does not try. The Books of the Balances extend ʿilm al-mīzān, by deliberate stages, into a universal quantitative science of correspondences — the corpus's largest claim, and the one that most decisively separates it from every earlier alchemical literature. The extension runs in three directions, each of which the modern literature documents from the treatises. The balance is applied to language itself: not only the names of substances but speech as such is analysed by letter, nature, and degree, so that grammar, rhetoric, and the revealed text all become balance-phenomena — words have constitutions, and constitutions can be weighed. It is applied to souls: the human soul, and the hierarchy of souls and intellects above it, carry the natures in proportion, whence character, capacity, and spiritual rank are balance-states, and the soul's perfection is — in the strict vocabulary of the system — the bringing of its inner proportion to the canon, by the same logic that perfects a metal. And it is applied to the cosmos: the heavens' motions, the spheres' order, the cycles of generation and corruption in the world below are the balance written at the largest scale, the whole creation a single weighed composition whose Maker is, in the corpus's theology, the supreme Keeper of the measure.

THE BALANCE OF LETTERS

Language analysed quantitatively: letters as qualitative atoms, names as constitutions, discourse as compounded proportion. The entry point of the whole science (Section VIII) and, in the corpus's ordering, also one of its provinces — the science studies its own instrument.

THE BALANCE OF SOULS

Psychology as proportion: the soul's natures weighed, its rank and its disorders stated as balance and imbalance, its cure — ethical, intellectual, spiritual — as rebalancing. The bridge between the corpus's alchemy and its mysticism, on which Lory's reading (Section IV) lays its weight.

THE BALANCE OF THE COSMOS

Cosmology as proportion: spheres, cycles, and the sublunary world of generation ordered by the same arithmetic that orders a name. The corpus's Neoplatonism is here put to work — emanation gives the hierarchy, the balance gives its measures.

THE BALANCE OF RELIGION

Revealed knowledge itself weighed: prophecy, law, and esoteric teaching as proportions of outer and inner, the imam as the living standard of the measure. The province where the science touches its milieu's theology most closely — and where the Ismāʿīlī question of Section XIV is sharpest.

The extension is sometimes read by moderns as megalomania — a laboratory trick inflated into a metaphysics. The corpus's own logic runs the other way. If the natures and their proportions are truly the constitution of everything — if a metal, a drug, a word, and a soul are all, at bottom, weighed mixtures of the same four terms — then a science of the weighing is not one science among others but the science of which the others are chapters: the parallel is arithmetic, which no one accuses of megalomania for counting sheep, stars, and syllables with the same numbers. Whether the claim is true is not a question this archive adjudicates. That it is coherent, built patiently province by province, is the fact the caricatures miss, and the reason Haq's exposition treats the system as a philosophy of nature in full standing.

One consequence of the universal balance returns the exposition to the bench, and it is the consequence Section XI develops: the elixir's composition is computed, not found. If every substance's inner proportion can be stated, then the substance that corrects all proportions — the elixir, whose own constitution stands at the canon — can be specified in advance of its making, as an engineer specifies a bridge in advance of its building. The corpus's elixir-doctrine, and its still bolder doctrine of artificial generation, are the universal science's laboratory corollaries. First, though, the metals themselves: the domain where the balance met the art's oldest theory, and remade it.

X. Metals in the Corpus: Sulphur and Mercury in Jābirian Form

The corpus's teaching on metals is the point at which its system joins the main line of alchemical theory — and this article treats it, by design, as a junction rather than a destination. The full exposition of the doctrine belongs to this hub's dedicated article, The Sulphur–Mercury Theory of Metals, and is not duplicated here. What belongs here is the doctrine's Jābirian form: what the corpus, specifically, did with the theory, and where that form sits inside the science of the balance.

In paraphrase of the corpus's teaching as the modern literature expounds it: the metals are generated in the earth from two proximate constituents, sulphur and mercury — not the brimstone and quicksilver of the market, but the principles those substances most nearly embody: a hot, dry, combustible, colouring and fixing principle, and a cold, wet, fusible, metallic and volatile one. Every metal is the two principles compounded; the metals differ — gold from silver, silver from copper, copper from lead and tin and iron — by the purity of their sulphur and mercury, by the proportion in which the two are compounded, and by the completeness of their subterranean cooking. Gold is the compound in which the purest principles stand in the most perfect proportion, fully digested; the base metals are the same two principles in impurer states, worse ratios, arrested digestion. The theory in this form is the corpus's systematisation of materials the Greek tradition had left scattered — Aristotle's two exhalations in the Meteorology, the Alexandrian workshop's sulphur-waters and mercurial spirits — and its systematisation is what history kept: stated thus, with the differences among metals reduced to purity, proportion, and digestion of two common constituents, the doctrine both explained the metals' family resemblance and licensed the art's programme, since what differs only in proportion and purity can, in principle, be re-proportioned and purified.

// THE CORPUS'S VERSION, IN ONE SENTENCE //

Within the balance system, sulphur and mercury are themselves nature-compounds — each analysable, each with its outer and inner proportion — and a metal's sulphur–mercury ratio is the proximate form of its deeper four-nature balance: the two-principle theory is the balance read at the level of the metals' immediate ingredients. See the hub's Sulphur–Mercury Theory article for the doctrine's full career, Greek roots, and long afterlife.

Two further Jābirian contributions to the theory of substances belong in this section's brief compass, because the Egyptology hub's summary already credits the corpus with both and both are regularly confirmed by the modern literature. The first is classification: the corpus offers what is regularly described as the earliest surviving systematic classification of chemical substances — bodies sorted into spirits, metals, and the ranked orders of stones, salts, boraces, and vitriols, by their behaviour under fire and their balance-characters — a taxonomy that made the workshop's materia medica arguable-about in general terms for the first time. The second is procedure stated as knowledge: detailed, testable directions, dispersed through the practical collections, for the purification and testing of metals, for alloying, and — a recipe the summaries single out — for deriving sal ammoniac from organic materials, with fractional distillation used to separate a substance's natures for analysis. The procedures matter to this article for a historiographical reason: they are the corpus's bench-level deposit, the stratum in which the school handled real materials with real skill, and they forbid the opposite caricature to Section IX's — the corpus as pure arithmological reverie. The school that computed the balance of a name also knew how its furnaces behaved. Its theory and its practice were one literature, cross-referenced like everything else it wrote.

XI. The Elixir and Takwīn: What the Balance Is For

Every part of the system converges on a single substance and a single ambition. The substance is the elixir — al-iksīr, the word that passes through Latin into every European language, its own remoter etymology debated behind the Arabic — and the corpus's doctrine of it is the balance turned into pharmacology. The elixir is a substance whose own inner constitution stands at the canonical proportion, 1:3:5:8, in the highest attainable degree of intensity and purity. Projected — the corpus's technical picture is of a medicine cast upon a patient — onto an imperfect metal, it does not coat or colour the metal. It corrects it: the elixir's perfected balance overpowers and re-orders the metal's defective one, supplying deficits and subduing excesses on the scale Section VIII's table displays, until the patient's inner proportion stands at the canon and the patient is, in constitution and therefore in manifest nature, gold or silver according to the elixir's kind and degree. Elixirs are graded, in the corpus's scheme, as the analyses are graded: an elixir for a stated metal, of a stated strength, in a stated quantity, is a prescription the balance writes — which is why the theory of Sections VII–IX is, in the corpus's own economy, not speculation surrounding a practice but the practice's dosage chart.

The elixir's own making is therefore the system's crucial operation, and the corpus treats it with corresponding gravity: a substance at the canon is not found in nature ready-made, since nature's compounds all stand at their own imperfect proportions, but must be composed — chosen materials, analysed by name and by test, purified until their own balances are known exactly, and compounded in the computed amounts, under the computed regimen, until the compound's inner proportion is the canonical one. The composition of the elixir is the point where the letter-balance earns its keep most directly: the ingredients' hidden constitutions are known from their names before the furnace is lit, and the compound is arithmetic before it is chemistry. Modern readers who find the procedure incredible as chemistry should nonetheless register its form: it is a rational pharmacology, in which the medicine is designed from the diagnosis by a stated rule — a form no earlier alchemical literature achieves, and one whose rationality is independent of its premises' truth.

Takwīn: The Generation of Beings

Beyond the elixir stands the corpus's boldest doctrine: takwīn, the artificial generation of beings. If the balance of a natural thing can be known completely, the corpus reasons — in paraphrase of the treatises on generation, among the most discussed in the modern literature — then the thing can be made: not imitated in appearance, but generated in constitution, by compounding its natures in its true proportion under the conditions that govern natural generation. The doctrine is developed, in the corpus's dispersed manner, across the generation of minerals and metals, of plants, of animals, and — at the summit of the claim — of a rational being: an artificial human, grown by art, whose making the corpus treats as the balance's supreme demonstration and surrounds with its densest secrecy. The modern literature handles takwīn with the seriousness the corpus claims for it: Lory's studies place it at the centre of the corpus's religious anthropology, where the adept who generates life re-enacts, at laboratory scale, the Creator's own weighing and compounding of the world; Haq's exposition follows its logic as the balance system's necessary completion, the point where a complete quantitative knowledge of natures either issues in generation or confesses itself incomplete.

// TAKWĪN: CLAIM, DOCTRINE, AND EVIDENCE //

Takwīn is a doctrine of the corpus, and it is reported here as one. No evidence independent of the corpus attests a successful artificial generation by its school or any other, and the corpus's own accounts of the work are veiled, dispersed, and prescriptive rather than reportorial — they tell the reader how generation is to be done, in the corpus's idiom, not that it was done, when, and with what witnessed result. The doctrine's historical importance does not need the stronger claim: as the most uncompromising statement in medieval literature of the thesis that life is a proportion and proportion is makable, takwīn shaped Arabic and, through the tradition's channels, Latin thinking about generation for centuries, whatever any furnace ever held.

Elixir and takwīn together fix the corpus's place in the long history this archive traces. The Greek art, at its most ambitious, sought a tincture that would perfect metals; the corpus ascribed to Jābir rebuilt that ambition as a theorem — perfection is a proportion, proportions are computable, therefore perfection is composable — and then followed the theorem past the metals to life itself. The theorem's premises are the four natures and the letter-balance; its method is ʿilm al-mīzān; its mood is one of complete, systematic, almost serene confidence that the world is weighed and that the weights can be read. Later Arabic alchemy would keep the confidence and quietly drop the arithmetic; Latin alchemy would keep the elixir and lose the balance that designed it. The corpus's integrity — theory, computation, and operation in one dispersed design — was never again assembled whole. That, more than any single doctrine, is what the school under Jābir's name achieved, and what its dissolution into excerpts and commentaries (Sections XV–XVI) cost the tradition.

XII. The Corpus's Authorities: A Claimed Lineage, Read as Framing

A school that writes under a borrowed name will also borrow ancestors, and the corpus ascribed to Jābir assembles its lineage with a connoisseur's care. Its pages cite, invoke, and claim descent from the whole available pantheon of the art's authorities: Hermes at the fountainhead, the thrice-great founder whose name warranted the entire Hermetic tradition; Balīnās — Apollonius of Tyana under his Arabic name — the sage of the Book of the Secret of Creation, in whose frame-story the Emerald Tablet is found beneath a statue of Hermes in a vault (the Tablet itself, wherever this archive states its extent, runs to thirteen sentences in the usual segmentation, fourteen in editions that divide the closing sentences); Democritus, the Greek art's own founding pseudonym, whose name had headed the recipe-tradition since Bolos of Mendes; Plato and Aristotle, the philosophers, cited for the physics the balance rebuilds; Zosimos of Panopolis and Maria, the Alexandrian practitioners; and, presiding over all in a category of his own, Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq, not cited as an author among authors but present as the living voice from which the writing flows. The corpus's Greek debts are real — Kraus's second volume traced them in detail, through the Arabic translations — but the lineage is a construction, and this article reads it as the corpus's pseudepigraphic framing: a genealogy composed to place the school's new system in the oldest available succession, exactly as the master's own biography (Section II) places the school in Jaʿfar's circle and Hārūn's court.

HERMES

The fountainhead: in the Arabic tradition Hermes is also Idrīs, the Qurʾānic prophet, and the founder of the sciences in Egypt (see the Egyptology hub's treatment). The corpus's Hermes-citations claim for the balance the antiquity of the whole Hermetic succession — a claim of framing, over a debt to Hermetic materials that is genuine but general.

BALĪNĀS — APOLLONIUS OF TYANA

The sage of the Secret of Creation, the great Arabic cosmology of correspondences in whose vault the Emerald Tablet is discovered. The corpus's Balīnās is its link to that cosmology — the macrocosm–microcosm architecture the balance quantifies — and to the Tablet tradition this archive treats in its Greco-Roman wing.

DEMOCRITUS AND THE GREEKS

The recipe-tradition's founder-name, with Plato, Aristotle, and the Alexandrian practitioners behind him. Kraus showed the corpus's Greek science arriving largely through intermediaries and translations rather than by direct reading — the citations name the fountainheads; the channels were the ninth century's (see The Translation Movement).

JAʿFAR AL-ṢĀDIQ

The imam as master and warrant: not one authority in the list but the list's guarantor, the named source from whom — the corpus insists — the teaching, its dispersion, and its secrecy all descend. Read as framing, the Jaʿfar-relation is the corpus's charter; read as biography, it is the tradition assessed in Sections II–IV.

Reading a lineage as framing is not a debunking exercise, and two distinctions keep the reading honest. First, framing is not fraudulence in any simple sense: pseudepigraphy was the period's normal technology of authority, practised by the Greek art (Democritus, Hermes) centuries before the corpus and by the Latin West (the pseudo-Geber of Section XVI) centuries after; a school that believed it taught what Jaʿfar's circle had known would experience attribution to his disciple not as invention but as restoration. Second, the debts beneath the framing are itemisable and real: the corpus demonstrably works with Greek physics and medicine in Arabic dress, with the Alexandrian recipe-tradition, with late-antique cosmology of the Secret of Creation type, and with the exegetical methods of its Shiʿi milieu — and Kraus's source-analysis, which established the channels, is the same work that dissolved the lineage's literal form. The school's originality lies precisely where its genealogy is silent: no cited authority teaches the science of the balance. Hermes did not compute the 1:3:5:8 proportion from the letters of Arabic names, because the doctrine is the corpus's own — the one thing in it that needed no ancestor, and the thing its authors, characteristically, never claimed as their own invention.

XIII. The Corpus as Literature: Forms, Secrecy, and a School's Voice

The corpus's doctrines have occupied this article; its writing deserves a section of its own, because the writing is where the school — as against the solitary author of the tradition — is most plainly visible, and where the corpus's hold on its later readers was actually exerted. The treatises work in the standard literary forms of Arabic learned prose, bent to the school's purposes. The epistle (risāla) carries doctrine from master to named or unnamed disciple, its intimacy a warrant of authenticity. The dialogue — the master questioned, the master answering, very often with Jaʿfar's authority ventriloquised behind the master's voice — dramatises the teaching as transmission, enacting in form the discipleship the biography claims. The book (kitāb) in its systematic mood builds the Balances' architecture: definitions, divisions, tables of letters and degrees, the apparatus of a science presenting its credentials. And across all forms runs the corpus's distinctive rhetoric, the idiom any reader of the treatises learns to hear: oaths of secrecy sworn and exacted; addresses to the “brother” who alone will understand; warnings against the unworthy reader, the mocking philosopher, the ruler who would seize the art; cover-names for substances and operations, varied — the dispersion again, at the scale of the word — so that no glossary compiled from one treatise unlocks another; and the recurring, haunting gesture of the withheld conclusion, the sentence that breaks off where the bāṭin begins and refers the brother to another book, another degree, another year of study.

The secrecy rhetoric repays the same double reading as the dispersion doctrine of Section VI. As protection, it is functional: a literature that teaches the making of wealth and the generation of life (Section XI) has worldly reasons, in any century, to control its readers, and the corpus's milieu added religious ones — esoteric teaching, in its Shiʿi theology, is a trust (amāna) whose betrayal is a sin, not a publishing decision. As literature, the rhetoric is the school's bond with its audience: the reader who persists through oath, cover-name, and deferral is being formed by the reading, turned from a consumer of recipes into the kind of person to whom the balance can be taught — the pedagogy of Section VI operating at sentence scale. And as evidence, the rhetoric cuts, characteristically, both ways in the authorship debate: its uniformity across hundreds of treatises is a strand of Kraus's case for a single redacting school, while its very conventionality — every esoteric literature of the age swears the same oaths — lets the defence reply that the voice is a genre's, not a committee's. The article leaves the strand where the scholarship leaves it: probative as part of a cumulative case, decisive as nothing on its own.

// A SCHOOL'S VOICE //

The corpus's most quietly extraordinary literary fact is its consistency of personality: across collections, genres, and — on the dating adopted here — generations of writers, “Jābir” sounds like one man: imperious, intimate, digressive, systematic, wounded by his contemporaries' incomprehension, and utterly certain of the balance. Maintaining a persona at that fidelity is itself an authorial achievement — the school's truest work of art may be the author it composed to have written it.

The corpus's literary afterlife within Arabic begins with its difficulty. A dispersed, veiled library of two hundred treatises does not circulate like a handbook; it circulates like a scripture — with commentators, epitomists, and quarrelling schools of interpretation — and Section XV follows that circulation. The corpus, moreover, expects its commentators: its deferrals build the commentary tradition into the design, and al-Jildakī, expounding Jābir line by line, is not an accident that befell the corpus but a reader it ordered in advance.

XIV. Ismāʿīlī Resonances: Milieu, Parallel, and the Limits of the Claim

No section of this article requires more careful statement than this one, because its subject — the corpus's relation to Ismāʿīlī Shiʿism — is the point where Kraus's thesis reaches furthest beyond its evidence and where careless summary does the most damage. The claim, in its strong form, is Kraus's: that the school behind the corpus was not merely Shiʿi in sympathy but Ismāʿīlī in affiliation, connected with the missionary movement whose Qarmaṭian branch was, in exactly the corpus's decades (c. 850–950), convulsing Iraq, Arabia, and Persia; and that the corpus's science is, among other things, that movement's intellectual instrument — a teaching for initiates, graded like the mission's own hierarchy, promising a hidden knowledge of nature's government to match the movement's hidden knowledge of history's. The parallels Kraus marshalled remain, as parallels, impressive. The ẓāhir/bāṭin hermeneutic — outer text, inner truth, authorised interpreter — is Ismāʿīlī exegesis exactly, extended by the corpus from scripture to nature (Section VI). The graded disclosure of the dispersion doctrine mirrors the mission's graded initiation. The cosmology — emanated hierarchies of intellect and soul, cyclical sacred history, the imam as the age's locus of divine guidance — is the common architecture of the corpus and of the Ismāʿīlī thought being built in the same decades, not least in the encyclopaedia of the Brethren of Purity, whose milieu and the corpus's visibly interpenetrate. And the master himself — Jaʿfar, the imam as the source of all the teaching — places the corpus's authority where Ismāʿīlī authority also lives: in the imam, against the caliph, the jurist, and the philosopher alike.

The caution, which later scholarship — Lory prominently, and the broad run of subsequent work — has counselled, is equally definite, and this article adopts it as its position. Parallel is not affiliation. Every parallel named above is also, and perhaps more economically, explained by a shared milieu: the Shiʿi intellectual world of ninth- and tenth-century Iraq and Persia, in which Ismāʿīlīs, Twelvers, Muʿtazilites, translators, and courtiers read the same Greek books, breathed the same Neoplatonism, and built rival systems from common materials. No Jābirian treatise names the mission, preaches its politics, dates by its calendar, or instructs its missionaries; the Qarmaṭian states rose and fell without, so far as any surviving record shows, citing the corpus as theirs. Kraus himself advanced the affiliation as historical inference — from doctrinal fit and chronological fit — not as documented fact, and the inference's strength has not grown with time: what has grown is the appreciation of how much ninth-century Shiʿi thought the parallels can be made from, without the organisational claim. The archive's statement of the matter, accordingly: the corpus ascribed to Jābir is a product of the Shiʿi esoteric milieu of its century, saturated with the hermeneutics and cosmology that milieu shared with early Ismāʿīlism, plausibly composed by men in sympathy or contact with the mission, and not demonstrably an Ismāʿīlī institutional production. Anything stronger outruns the evidence; anything weaker ignores the parallels that made Kraus's inference worth a century's argument.

// WHY THE DISTINCTION MATTERS //

The affiliation question is not antiquarian. If the corpus is a movement's instrument, its science reads as ideology — the balance as the mission's cosmology with a laboratory attached — and its secrecy reads as security. If it is a milieu's product, the same science reads as what its pages claim to be: a school of adepts using the intellectual materials at hand, its secrecy the period's normal esotericism. The texts support the second reading without strain and the first only by inference; this article's Sections VII–XI therefore expound the system as a school's science, and this section exists so that the stronger claim is on the record, attributed to its author, at its proper strength — neither adopted in silence nor suppressed.

XV. The Arabic Afterlife: Commentators and Carriers

The corpus's first afterlife was Arabic, and it lasted half a millennium. Its shape was set by the corpus's own form (Section XIII): a dispersed and veiled library is received by commentary, and the commentary tradition on the writings ascribed to Jābir is one of the main channels through which Arabic alchemy itself flows. The carriers can be followed in a line. In the tenth century, Muḥammad ibn Umayl — whose symbolic masterpiece, the Book of the Silvery Water and the Starry Earth, expounds the art through an ancient sage's tablet — already works in a world furnished with Jābirian doctrine, quoting and absorbing the school's authorities; the great al-Rāzī, his older contemporary, engages the Jābirian inheritance critically from the side of a rival, bench-centred system (see Al-Rāzī). In the eleventh and twelfth centuries the corpus is a classic: al-Ṭughrāʾī (d. 1121) — vizier, poet of the famous Lāmiyyat al-ʿAjam, and practising alchemist — writes treatises, among them the Keys of Mercy, that take the Jābirian framework as their foundation and defend the art, balance and elixir included, against its philosophical critics. The tradition's most pictorial witness is Abū al-Qāsim al-ʿIrāqī (13th–14th century), whose Book of Knowledge Acquired Concerning the Cultivation of Gold illustrates the sages and their symbols in some of the most famous images in all alchemical literature — the corpus's authorities, Hermes and the rest of Section XII's lineage, now a painted pantheon presiding over the art's practice.

The afterlife culminates in Aydamur al-Jildakī (d. 1342), the most prolific commentator Arabic alchemy produced, and the corpus's single most important medieval reader. Al-Jildakī's great commentaries — the End of the Search among them, an exposition in many volumes of an earlier master's poem — quote the writings ascribed to Jābir constantly, at length, and with a scholar's care for the scattered whole: gathering the dispersed teaching (Section VI's programme, performed by a reader six centuries downstream), reconciling its registers, and defending its system as the art's foundation against the epitomes and simplifications of the intervening tradition. His quotations have a documentary value beyond their exegesis: al-Jildakī read manuscripts that have since been lost, and Jābirian treatises survive today, in part, because his commentaries carry their fragments — Kraus's catalogue (Section V) registers the works known only through such quotation, and the modern editor's task (Section XVII) still includes mining the commentators for the corpus's lost members. The Arabic afterlife thus ends where the modern study begins: with the corpus already an object of philology, its text already partly a reconstruction from its readers.

AL-ṬUGHRĀʾĪ (d. 1121)

The corpus as classic: a statesman-poet's alchemical treatises assume the Jābirian framework and argue the art's possibility on its grounds — evidence that by the twelfth century the balance system, or at least its vocabulary and elixir-doctrine, is the tradition's common property.

ABŪ AL-QĀSIM AL-ʿIRĀQĪ (13th–14th c.)

The corpus as iconography: the Cultivation of Gold's painted sages fix the lineage of Section XII in images, and its text — part commentary, part compilation — shows the Jābirian authorities fully absorbed into the art's symbolic language. He is also the Egyptology hub's witness for the medieval decipherment attempts (Islamic Egyptology, §IV).

AL-JILDAKĪ (d. 1342)

The corpus as scripture: the great commentator whose works are at once the medieval reception's summit and a primary source for the corpus's own text, preserving quotations of treatises now lost. No account of the corpus's survival is complete without him.

WHAT THE AFTERLIFE SELECTED

Reception is also filtration: the commentators transmit the elixir, the sulphur–mercury theory, the authorities, and the symbols at full strength, while the letter-balance's full arithmetic — the system's hardest and most distinctive part — is increasingly honoured as doctrine and less practised as method. The corpus that later centuries inherit is its own epitome.

That last card's point explains the corpus's strange double presence in the later tradition — everywhere as authority, almost nowhere as method. The balance as a computation (Sections VII–VIII) demanded the whole dispersed library and a faith in the name-doctrine that even sympathetic readers found easier to praise than to practise. The balance as a picture — substances have hidden proportions; the elixir corrects them; the wise weigh before they work — demanded nothing but assent, and it travelled accordingly, into the general stock of alchemical commonplaces, where it sits to this day, usually unattributed. Recovering the idea in its exact form is the modern scholarship's work, and this article's Sections VII–IX are its local performance.

XVI. Two Latin Afterlives: Gerard's Jābir and the Pseudo-Geber

Latin Europe received the name Jābir twice, and the two receptions must be kept as rigorously separate as the Arabic corpus and its biography. The full narrative of the art's passage into Latin belongs to this hub's The Latin Door; this section states the Jābirian facts and draws the line that protects them. The first reception is genuine. In twelfth-century Toledo, Gerard of Cremona (d. 1187), the most prolific of the Arabic–Latin translators, rendered the Seventy Books into Latin as the Liber de septuaginta — the Book of the Seventy — giving the Latin world its one substantial, direct portion of the corpus ascribed to Jābir: the school's doctrine and practice in the collection (Section V) best fitted to travel, its systematic frame intact enough for Latin readers to meet, in it, an alchemy of theory and not of recipes alone. Through the Liber de septuaginta and the scattering of shorter Jābirian translations around it, “Geber” entered Latin as an authority of the first rank — the name under which the Latin art would file its highest ambitions.

// THE FIRM LINE: THE SUMMA PERFECTIONIS IS NOT THIS CORPUS //

The famous Latin Summa perfectionis magisterii — the Summa of “Geber”, the most influential alchemical treatise of the Latin Middle Ages — is not a translation of any Jābirian work, not a member of the Arabic corpus, and not by its author or school. It is an original Latin composition of the late thirteenth century (before c. 1310), written under the borrowed name by the author modern scholarship calls the pseudo-Geber — identified by William Newman's research with the Franciscan Paul of Taranto — together with its companion treatises (the De inventione veritatis, the Liber fornacum, the Testamentum). Its matter-theory is a brilliant Latin development from the sulphur–mercury tradition, not the Arabic corpus's doctrine; its strong mineral acids and its laboratory programme belong to the Latin workshop treated in Mineral Acids and Practice. Any sentence that attributes the Summa's contents to “Jābir” without qualification is false, and the error is the single most common corruption in popular accounts of this whole subject.

The line matters in both directions. It protects the Arabic corpus from the Summa's contents — the Latin work's corpuscular matter-theory, its acid technology, its anti-arithmological temper are not Jābirian, and the school's actual system (Sections VII–IX) is in several respects the Summa's opposite: where the corpus computes perfection from names, the pseudo-Geber argues from furnace behaviour and explicitly distances his art from proportion-mysticism. And it protects the pseudo-Geber from dissolution into his pseudonym: the Summa is a masterpiece in its own right, the Latin art's theoretical summit, and Newman's attribution gives it back an author, a milieu, and a date of its own. Between the two stands the genuine, narrower bridge: Gerard's Liber de septuaginta, the Seventy Books in Latin dress — proof that the corpus itself did cross, in part, and a measure of how much of it, the Balances above all, did not. Latin alchemy inherited the name Geber entire and the corpus in excerpt; the science of the balance, as a working system, never crossed the language frontier at all. Its recovery waited for the Arabic manuscripts and the scholars of Section XVII — among them, fittingly, the tradition this archive's Greco-Roman hub follows to the same door from the other side (see Transmission).

XVII. Manuscripts and Modern Study: An Edition Still in Progress

The corpus ascribed to Jābir survives where Arabic scientific literature survives: in the manuscript libraries of Istanbul and Cairo, of Tehran and the Deccan, of Rampur and Hyderabad, and in the great European collections at Paris, Leiden, London, and Berlin — in copies, for the most part, many centuries younger than the works they carry, copied and recopied inside the commentary culture of Section XV. No autograph exists; no early codex anchors the text; the corpus's wording, at the level where doctrine can turn on a number in a table, rests on the collation of late witnesses — the material condition beneath the open question of Section IV. The modern study of the corpus is, accordingly, young relative to its size, and its history is a history of foundations laid one at a time. The first editors — Holmyard, whose Arabic Works of Jābir ibn Ḥayyān (1928) printed Arabic texts of individual treatises from the manuscripts; Stapleton and his collaborators, whose studies and editions of the 1920s opened the corpus's Indian-manuscript riches to scholarship; Ruska, whose source-criticism prepared the ground — established that the corpus could be edited at all. Kraus then gave the field its constitution: the catalogue of 298 titles (Section V), the collection- scheme, the edition of selected treatises in the Mukhtār rasāʾil (1935), and the Contribution's analysis (1942–43), on which every subsequent shelf is built. Sezgin's bibliography (1971) enlarged the manuscript record and re-argued the dating; Lory's editions and Haq's exposition (Section IV) carried the text and the system, respectively, to their current states of availability.

The honest statement of the present position is that most of the corpus remains unedited. Of some 215 extant treatises, a minority exist in modern critical editions; a smaller minority in translation; the Books of the Balances, the system's heart, are known to most scholars through Kraus's and Haq's expositions rather than through a complete text in print. Every large statement about the corpus — including this article's — is therefore a statement over a partially read archive. The work continues treatise by treatise, manuscript by manuscript, and it is also the final reason the authorship question stays open (Section IV): the school that dispersed its teaching across two hundred books has dispersed its evidence across two hundred unedited manuscripts, and the reassembly — of doctrine, text, and history — is a single task, still in progress.

// HOW TO GO FURTHER //

The reader who would meet the corpus itself, and not its summaries, should begin where the Sources begin: with Haq for the system in English, with Lory for the religious reading and for edited texts in French, with Holmyard's and Kraus's editions for the Arabic of individual treatises, and with Kraus's catalogue as the map of the whole — in the knowledge, stated plainly by every one of those guides, that the map's territory is still being surveyed. Within this archive, the companion articles named in the related links carry the story outward: to the sulphur–mercury theory the corpus systematised, to al-Rāzī who inherited and contested its world, to the translation movement that fed it, and to the Latin door through which its name, and one of its collections, passed into Europe.
