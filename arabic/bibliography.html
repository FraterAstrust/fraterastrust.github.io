I. How This Bibliography Is Organised

This page is a hub utility page, and it carries no wing badge of its own: it spans both of the hub's wings at once — Wing I, Transmission, and Wing II, The Art — and a single badge would mislabel half of what it lists. Instead, the wing discipline is applied at the level of the sections. The Antiquity division's bibliography sorts its sources by its three evidence tiers; this hub has no tiers, and its natural sort is different. It sorts by wing and by kind: first the primary texts themselves, in the editions and translations the hub reads them in (§II), and then the modern scholarship, divided between the wing whose questions it answers (§III for Transmission, §IV for The Art). A work that serves both wings — Manfred Ullmann's handbook is the standing example — is entered where its weight in this hub chiefly falls and cross-noted in its grounds line, not duplicated.

Primary texts come first for a reason that is specific to this field, and the reader should understand it before using the list. Arabic alchemy is read, even by specialists, through a narrow gate. Of the Jābirian corpus — some two hundred surviving treatises — only a small fraction exists in a modern critical edition, and a smaller fraction still in any European-language translation. Al-Rāzī's great alchemical summa is accessible in a German rendering of 1937 and in piecemeal English studies, not in a complete modern English edition. Ibn Umayl's major work waited until the twenty-first century for its full critical presentation. A bibliography of this hub that listed “Jābir, works” or “al-Rāzī, Secret of Secrets” without naming which edition, whose translation, and how much of the text that edition actually covers would be concealing the single most important fact about the evidence: its partiality. Section II therefore names editions and translations as precisely as the articles use them, and its grounds lines say, entry by entry, what remains unedited and untranslated. Where the hub says a work “remains untranslated”, that is a statement about the whole work; excerpts, extracts, and paraphrases in the secondary literature do not contradict it, and the entries distinguish them.

The governing rule of the page is the archive's honesty rule, stated on the hub index and applied here without exception: an entry appears only if the hub's articles actually rest on it. Nothing is listed for decoration, for completeness of the field, or because a bibliography of Arabic alchemy is “supposed” to contain it. Every entry below is harvested from an article's Sources section or from the hub index's own method and sources discussion, and every entry carries a tag line naming the article or articles it grounds. Where several articles rest on the same work — Paul Kraus's Jābir ibn Ḥayyān stands behind the Jābir article, the sulphur–mercury article, and the hub's dating practice throughout — the work is consolidated into a single entry in its home section and every article that uses it is tagged. Within each section the entries run in a deliberate order: §II follows the hub's own reading order, from the catalogue tools through the great corpora to the reference works; §§III–IV run alphabetically by author, as a working shelf should.

// CONSULTED, READ, RESTED ON //

Three verbs do different work in this hub, and the bibliography keeps them apart. A work is rested on when an article's claim would fall without it: it is tagged to that article. A work is read when it was worked through in full for an article, whether or not a single claim finally cites it. A work is consulted when it was opened for a check — a date, a catalogue number, a manuscript shelfmark — and used at exactly that strength. Where an article's Sources section records the weaker relation (“as cited in”, “via the discussions in”, “consulted for the catalogue numbers”), the entry below preserves the limitation rather than upgrading it. A citation here is a measurement, not a compliment.

Each entry gives the citation as the scholarship standardly gives it — author, title, year, and journal or publisher where those are certain — followed by one grounds line on what the work grounds in this hub: the text it makes readable, the claim it carries, the dating it fixes, or the account for which it is the cited authority. The grounds lines are written to be checked. A reader who wants to test any claim in this hub should be able to walk from the claim, to the article's Sources section, to this page, and out to the work itself without a gap — and, just as important, should be able to see at each step how much of the work the hub is standing on: the whole monograph, one chapter, a catalogue entry, a translated excerpt. In a field where a famous name can conceal a three-page excerpt, that visibility is the bibliography's main service.

§§III–IV — THE MODERN FIELD

The scholarship the articles argue with and build on, sorted by wing. Wing I's shelf is a history-of-transmission shelf — translation movements, bridge cities, the Latin door. Wing II's shelf is a history-of-the-art shelf — the corpus studies, the laboratory histories, the editions' companions. Several names stand on both shelves; each is entered once.

How a reader uses the page follows from how it is built. Start from the claim, not from the shelf: find the article, read its Sources section for the strength at which each work is cited there, and then find the work here for its full citation and its grounds. A reader who starts from the shelf instead — who wants to know, before reading an article, what kind of evidence it stands on — can read the tag lines in reverse: collect every entry tagged to an article and the article's evidentiary skeleton is on the table, editions first and interpretations after, each at its named strength. Both directions are deliberate. A bibliography that only supports the first is a footnote apparatus; one that only supports the second is a reading list. This page is built to be the first, and to be usable, with its limits visible, as the second.

A last word on transliteration, because a bibliography is where inconsistent spelling does its quiet damage. Arabic names and titles are given throughout in the hub's scholarly transliteration, with macrons and diacritics retained: Jābir ibn Ḥayyān, al-Rāzī, Ibn Umayl, Kitāb sirr al-asrār, Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ. Where a cited work's own title page uses an older or simpler spelling — Holmyard's “Geber”, the Memoirs' “Muhammad ibn Umail” — the entry gives the work's title as published and the hub's transliteration in the grounds line, so that a catalogue search under either form will find the book. Latinised names (Geber, Rhazes, Senior Zadith) are treated as what they are: separate publication identities, cross-referenced, never silently merged with the Arabic names they descend from. The fullest statement of why that separation matters is §V's standing-corrections note; the bibliography enforces it entry by entry.

II. Primary Texts — Editions and Translations

The entries in this section are not “the primary sources” in the abstract. They are the material forms in which the primary sources reach this hub: a catalogue raisonné that functions as an edition-tool for a corpus no complete edition exists for; a 1928 Paris edition that is still the printed Arabic text of the Jābirian treatises it covers; a 1937 German rendering that remains the fullest modern access to al-Rāzī's summa; a 1933 journal memoir that gave the scholarly world its first substantial Ibn Umayl in English; a twenty-first-century edition series that has finally superseded it. Read the dates as part of the evidence. Much of this hub's primary shelf was built between 1928 and 1948, by a small generation of scholar-editors — Holmyard, Ruska, Stapleton, Garbers, Kraus — whose work has been extended but, in several cases, never replaced. The hub says so where it is so, because a claim resting on a 1933 excerpt is a different kind of claim from one resting on a 2009 critical edition, and the reader is entitled to know which kind they are holding.

Two conventions govern the section. First, coverage is stated, not implied: where an edition covers part of a work, the entry names the part; where a translation is of excerpts, the entry says excerpts. Second, the Latin witnesses are filed here, with the Arabic ones, when the hub reads them as primary evidence — the Turba Philosophorum in Ruska's edition, Morienus in Stavenhagen's translation, and pseudo-Geber's Summa in Newman's — because the hub's threshold articles treat the Latin texts as primary documents of the transmission, not as secondary literature about it. They are labelled Latin at every mention. Nothing in this section is a modern paraphrase dressed as a text: where the hub's knowledge of a work comes through a modern study rather than an edition, the study is the entry, in §§III–IV, and the grounds line there says so.

One cluster on this shelf deserves a collective note, because its members are easily mistaken for three unrelated accidents of survival. The Bengal Memoirs of 1927 and 1933 (§IV and above) and the editions of 1928, 1937, and 1948 belong to a single scholarly moment: the generation between the wars in which Arabic alchemy first became an edited, translatable literature rather than a manuscript rumour. Stapleton worked from India, Holmyard from London and Paris, Ruska and Garbers from the German orientalist institutes, Kraus from Cairo and Paris. They corresponded, reviewed one another, and built on one another's manuscripts, and the hub's primary shelf is, in large part, their collective work — extended since by the CALA project and the Oxford Epistles, but nowhere yet replaced as a whole. The articles cite that generation with respect and with its dates attached, which in this field are the same gesture.

The absences in this section are as deliberate as its contents, and two of them deserve naming. There is no entry for a complete Jābir in English, because there is none: the reader who wants the corpus whole must go to the Arabic, largely in manuscript, by way of Kraus's catalogue — which is why the catalogue stands at the head of the section, in the position a complete edition would occupy in a better-supplied field. And there is no entry for the Ghāyat al-ḥakīm (the Latin Picatrix) or for the Kitāb sirr al-khalīqa (the Book of the Secret of Creation ascribed to Balīnās): both are named, and used at paraphrase strength, inside the articles that treat them, and both will be entered here, with their editions named, when an article comes to rest on either text directly. The rule of §I admits no exceptions in either direction: what the hub rests on is listed, and what is listed is what the hub rests on.

III. Wing I — Transmission: Modern Scholarship

Wing I asks how the art travelled: by what roads, in whose hands, under whose patronage, and at what cost in vocabulary and meaning. Its scholarship is therefore a shelf of histories — of a translation movement, of two bridge cities, of a Hermetic tradition's Arabic career, of a second translation movement, three centuries later, that carried the rebuilt art into Latin. The shelf's centre of gravity is Baghdad. Dimitri Gutas's account of the Graeco-Arabic translation movement as a social phenomenon — funded, staffed, and argued over, not a caliph's whim — is the wing's model and its most-tagged entry; George Saliba's revision of the same movement's origins and afterlife is its standing interlocutor; and Franz Rosenthal's older survey remains the shelf's orientation volume, the form in which two generations of readers first met the classical heritage in Islam. Around that centre stand the city studies (Tamara Green on Harran), the prophet-of-science study (Kevin van Bladel on the Arabic Hermes), the handbook that covers both wings (Ullmann), the Latin-door studies (Charles Burnett), and the medical-institution literature (Michael Dols, Vivian Nutton) on which the hub's Gundeshapur account is held at its real evidentiary strength.

A discipline note, because Wing I's questions invite a characteristic error. Transmission history is written from survivals: translators' prefaces, catalogues like the Fihrist (§II), colophons, and the manuscripts that happen to remain. Every work below is cited in this hub for what its evidence can carry, and the hub's own rule — stated on the index and repeated in the Gundeshapur article — is that a late source's institutional splendour is reported as that source's claim, not as the institution's history. Where the scholarship itself divides — on the scale of the Bayt al-Ḥikma, on the depth of Gundeshapur's academy, on how much of Harran's Hermetica is Harranian — the articles state the division with the positions named, and this bibliography tags the works on each side. The reader will find the standing form of those cautions collected in §V.

A transmission history is only ever as good as its catalogues; this one names its catalogue in §II and its historians here, and lets the reader watch the two shelves lean on each other.

IV. Wing II — The Art: Modern Scholarship

Wing II asks what the Arabic world made of the art it received: the Jābirian system of the balance, the sulphur–mercury theory, al-Rāzī's laboratory order, al-Kindī's perfumery, the Brethren's filing of the art inside a complete philosophy, Ibn Umayl's allegorical commentary. Its scholarship is accordingly a shelf of corpus studies, laboratory histories, and field surveys, and it has a clear patriarch. Paul Kraus's two volumes of 1942–43 — the catalogue entered as a tool in §II, the monograph entered here — redated the Jābirian corpus, reconstructed its system, and set the questions the field still argues: the shelf's other Jābir entries (Sezgin's counter-dating, Haq's systematic exposition, Lory's studies) are all, in one way or another, answers to Kraus. Around the corpus studies stand the bench histories — Stapleton's papers, Levey's pharmacological and perfumery work, Multhauf's and Forbes's histories of chemistry and distillation — and the two modern syntheses, Newman's and Principe's, in which the Arabic art takes its argued place in the longer history of chymistry. Holmyard's Alchemy of 1957 stands on the shelf as what it is: the field's classic English survey, by the editor of §II's 1928 texts, read in this hub as a document of the scholarship's own history as much as an authority for its claims.

The wing's discipline is the index's workshop rule, and it shapes what these entries are cited for. A claim about doctrine is admitted in Wing II alongside the evidence of practice; the scholarship below is therefore tagged not only to the articles it informs but, in its grounds lines, to the kind of evidence it supplies — system, bench, or both. Kraus and Haq supply the system; Stapleton and Levey supply the bench; Newman supplies the bridge between them, in the Latin Summa (§II) and in his account of corpuscular theory. Where a work on this shelf has been corrected by later work — Forbes on distillation is the named case, in its entry and in §V — the hub keeps the work, keeps the correction attached to it, and never cites the corrected claim at its original strength.

The Ikhwān shelf (Netton; de Callataÿ) sits in this section rather than in Wing I's, and the placement is a small lesson in the hub's sorting rule. The Brethren of Purity were transmitters too — their encyclopedia is one of the great relay stations of Greek science into Arabic high culture — but this hub rests on them for what they filed: the place their system assigns to minerals, metals, and the art itself. That is a Wing II question, asked of a Wing II kind of evidence, and the scholarship that answers it is shelved here, beside the Oxford editions (§II) through which the hub reads the epistles themselves. Sorting by wing means sorting by the question an article asks of a work, not by the work's own century or genre; where a work answers two wings' questions, §I's cross-noting rule, not duplication, keeps both debts visible.

Read as a whole, the shelf tells the wing's own history in miniature. Its first layer is documentary: Stapleton's generation printing texts and cataloguing apparatus (§II; Stapleton and Levey here). The hub's Wing II articles are written from all three layers at once — a doctrinal claim from the second layer is checked against a procedure from the first and, where one exists, a reconstruction from the third — and the tag lines, collected article by article, show that triple footing more honestly than any methodological preface could.

V. A Note on Access, Editions, and Standing Corrections

The first fact about access to this literature is a linguistic one, and the hub states it plainly, here and wherever it matters: much of the field's foundation literature is in German and French. Kraus wrote in French; Ruska, Ullmann, Sezgin, and Garbers wrote in German; Lory writes in French. The editions on which the hub's primary shelf rests — the 1928 Paris Jābir, the 1937 Berlin al-Rāzī, the 1948 Leipzig al-Kindī — carry their introductions, commentaries, and apparatuses in those languages, and no English equivalents have replaced them. A reader confined to English can read Haq on the balance, Newman and Principe on the art's long history, Gutas and Saliba on the transmission, and the growing Oxford Epistles — a real shelf, and this bibliography marks it — but cannot yet read the Jābirian corpus whole in any language but Arabic, nor Kraus's reconstruction in any language but French. The hub does not apologise for resting on the German and French literature; it insists on naming it, so that no reader mistakes an English summary's confidence for the field's actual state of access.

Editions matter for the same reason they matter in the Antiquity division, with one sharpening. There, a text might be read in several translations and the bibliography refused to collapse them. Here, the commoner case is the opposite: a text read in one ageing edition, or in excerpts, or — for most of the Jābirian corpus — in no edition at all. Consolidation on this page therefore runs under a standing warning. A single entry can stand behind several articles (Kraus's monograph is tagged for the Jābir article, the sulphur–mercury article, and the hub index) without any of those articles having read the corpus the entry is about at first hand in full: they have read Kraus, and the bibliography's honesty consists in making that the visible fact. Conversely, where an article does rest on a text directly — Ibn Umayl in the CALA edition, al-Kindī in Garbers, the Summa in Newman — the edition is the §II entry and the scholarship that expounds it is entered separately, so that text and interpretation are never fused into a single undifferentiated citation.

// STANDING CORRECTIONS OBSERVED THROUGHOUT THIS HUB //

Four corrections recur across this hub's articles, and they are collected here, once, in their standing form. (1) The Jābir corpus's date. The corpus ascribed to Jābir ibn Ḥayyān is dated, in the form in which it survives, to c. 850–950, on Kraus's demonstration (§IV), and is read as a school's work gathered under a master's name. The traditional biography — the disciple of Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq who died c. 815 — is the corpus's own account of itself, reported as such and never silently used as the corpus's date; Sezgin's earlier dating (§IV) is the named counter-position. (2) Pseudo-Geber is not Jābir. The Latin Summa perfectionis (c. 1300; §II, Newman) is a Latin composition by a Latin author who borrowed the name Geber; it is not a translation of any Arabic work, and its doctrines are Latin developments of Arabic foundations, in that order. (3) The mineral acids' horizon is Latin. The strong mineral acids — nitric acid, aqua regia, and their kin — are first clearly and systematically described in the Latin pseudo-Geber tradition, not in the Arabic Jābirian corpus; accounts that credit “Jābir” with their discovery have fused correction (2) into a bench claim, and this hub separates them every time, Forbes's older datings (§IV) included. (4) The Bayt al-Ḥikma at its real strength. Baghdad's House of Wisdom is held throughout this hub as Gutas (§III) documents it: a caliphal library and translation bureau of the first importance under al-Maʾmūn and his successors — not a university, academy, or research institute in the modern institutional sense, and not the translation movement's sole engine, which was a society-wide patronage economy of caliphs, viziers, physicians, and private families. Popular accounts that inflate any of these four points are not repeated in this hub, even in paraphrase, without the correction attached.

A note on what “correction” means here, because the box above could be misread as a list of other people's errors. Each of the four standings was once the field's own default: an eighth-century Jābir was the catalogue tradition's plain implication; Geber's identity with Jābir was the Latin tradition's own claim, inherited by the early moderns and by Holmyard's generation in part; the acids were assigned to the Arabic corpus by historians working before Newman's edition; the Bayt al-Ḥikma's splendour is the medieval sources' own presentation, amplified since. The hub's corrections are therefore dated scholarly positions, held at the strength of their evidence — Kraus's dating is an argument, and Sezgin's dissent is on this page precisely because the argument is not a measurement — and they will be revised if the literature moves. That is not a weakness of the standings. In a field whose primary shelf is still being edited, it is the only honest form a standing can take.

A word on revision, since a bibliography in a living archive is a dated document whether it admits it or not. This page's standings — Kraus's dating canonical, Sezgin's dissent named, the CALA editions standard for Ibn Umayl, the Oxford Epistles in progress — describe the field as the hub's articles found it at their writing. Editions in progress will complete; catalogues will be supplemented; a full English al-Rāzī, if the field produces one, will change §II's shape more than any single new monograph could change §§III–IV. When any of that happens, the change is made here from the revised article's Sources section outward, the superseded entry is kept with its supersession stated, and the standing corrections above are re-argued rather than re-asserted. The reader who returns to this page in five years should find a different bibliography — and should be able to tell, from the grounds lines, exactly what changed and why.

Finally: this bibliography is complete for the hub as it stands — the articles built, the index's method and sources discussion, and the threshold and companion pieces the articles rest on — and incomplete by design for everything else. It is not a general reading list for Arabic alchemy, and its silences are not judgements. A work absent here is simply a work no article in this hub yet rests on. When an article is added or revised, its Sources section is written first and this page is updated from it, never the other way round — and when an edition supersedes one listed here, as the CALA Ibn Umayl superseded the 1933 excerpts without erasing their historical place, both entries stay, with the supersession stated in the grounds lines, so that the bibliography remains what it is meant to be: not only a shelf-list, but a record of how the hub's access to its own subject has been built.
