I. The Claim and the Correction: What This Article Adjudicates

Open almost any older popular history of chemistry, any school textbook written before about 1980, and a fair number of encyclopaedia entries still in circulation, and the same sentence is waiting. In paraphrase of its usual form: the Arab alchemist Geber — Jābir ibn Ḥayyān, eighth or ninth century — discovered or first described the great mineral acids: nitric acid (aqua fortis, “strong water”), sulphuric acid (oil of vitriol), hydrochloric acid (spirit of salt), and their mixture aqua regia (“royal water”), the one common solvent that attacks gold itself. The sentence is tidy, memorable, and — on the surviving documentary record — wrong. Not wrong in detail, in the way a date can be wrong by a decade. Wrong in its attribution, its language, and its century. The first clear, repeatable descriptions of the strong mineral acids occur in a Latin corpus, written in Latin, in the Latin West, in the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries: the works ascribed to “Geber” that modern scholarship therefore calls the Latin pseudo-Geber corpus, whose centrepiece is the Summa perfectionis magisterii — the Summa perfectionis, the “Summa of Perfection”. Those works are not translations of the Arabic Jābir corpus. No Arabic original for them has ever been found, and their contents — the acids above all — have no counterpart in the Arabic texts that do survive.

That distinction is this article's reason to exist, and every section of it serves the distinction. But the correction must not be allowed to do the opposite injustice. Stripping the acids from the Arabic record does not strip the Arabic workshop of its achievement; it restores the achievement to its real shape. What the Arabic texts of the ninth to fourteenth centuries demonstrably did — in the corpus ascribed to Jābir ibn Ḥayyān (the Jābir corpus, dated by Paul Kraus and the scholarship following him to c. 850–950, a dating stated throughout this hub as debated), and in the surviving works of Abū Bakr Muḥammad ibn Zakarīyā al-Rāzī (865–925) — is something arguably more consequential than any single reagent: they systematised the operations of the art into a canonical, teachable list; they classified the substances of the workshop — spirits, bodies, stones, salts, boraxes, vitriols — into a working taxonomy; they stocked, sourced, traded, and characterised a reagent shelf of salts, alums, vitriols, and fluxes that Latin chemistry would inherit entire; and they carried the Greek assay tradition — touchstone, cupellation, cementation — into the mints and markets of the Islamic world, where al-Bīrūnī raised one branch of it, hydrostatic weighing, to a precision Europe would not match for centuries. The acids, when they come, come on top of that system, in a Latin workshop culture that had learned its operations, its apparatus, and much of its shelf through Arabic transmission. Credit where the documents put it cuts both ways, and this hub's standing rule is to follow it in both directions.

THE POPULAR CLAIM — MISATTRIBUTED

“Geber (Jābir) discovered nitric, sulphuric, and hydrochloric acids and aqua regia.” No passage of the surviving Arabic Jābir corpus, and no passage of al-Rāzī, gives a preparation of any of the four that a reader could repeat at a bench. Graded misattributed throughout this article (Sections II, IX–XI, XIII).

THE DOCUMENTARY POSITION — ATTESTED

The first clear mineral-acid preparations — aqua fortis from vitriol, saltpetre, and alum; aqua regia by the addition of sal ammoniac; oil of vitriol from vitriol driven hard — are in the Latin Summa perfectionis tradition, late 13th–early 14th century. Graded attested, in Latin, at that date (Section XI).

THE REAL ARABIC ACHIEVEMENT — ATTESTED

The canonical operation-list, the substance taxonomy, the reagent shelf, and the assay practice of Sections IV–VII and XIV–XV: documented in the Jābir corpus, in al-Rāzī's Book of the Secret of Secrets (Kitāb Sirr al-asrār), and in the mint and market literature. This, not the acids, is the Arabic workshop's title to fame.

THE MIDDLE GROUND — CLAIMED

The Arabic texts do describe corrosive and solvent “waters” — distilled vinegar, sal ammoniac solutions, sulphur and arsenic waters — and some of them claim, in words, to dissolve metals including gold. A claim in words is not a recipe. Each is graded claimed and assessed at its real chemical strength in Sections VIII–X.

A note on method, in the form this archive uses wherever a famous name invites invention. No direct quotation is given in this article. Every ancient and medieval text in it — Jābir corpus, al-Rāzī, pseudo-Geber, al-Rammāḥ, al-Bīrūnī — is rendered in paraphrase, labelled as paraphrase. The discipline is deliberate: the mineral-acid legend grew precisely by unlabelled paraphrase hardening into supposed quotation and then into supposed fact. Where scholarship disputes a point — the Jābir corpus's dating, the Summa's author, a saltpetre term — the dispute is stated as a dispute. Procedures assembled from a text's hints plus the later workshop literature are labelled reconstruction. And every acid claim carries one of Section II's three grades — attested, claimed, misattributed — in so many words.

// TWO GEBERS, ONE NAME //

Jābir ibn Ḥayyān is the name on a large Arabic corpus, composed — on Kraus's dating, followed here with the debate flagged — in the later ninth and early tenth centuries, probably by a circle rather than one man. Geber is the Latin form of the name. Under it, Latin Europe read both genuine translations from the Arabic and original Latin works falsely ascribed to the Arabic master — the pseudo-Geber corpus included. Same name, two bodies of writing, four centuries apart.

II. How to Grade an Acid Claim: Attested, Claimed, Misattributed

Before any text is opened, the instrument for reading it must be calibrated. Claims about early acids fail, almost without exception, in one of three ways: a text says a water dissolves a metal but gives no way to make the water; a text gives a way to make a water but the water it makes is not the acid later readers name; or a text that does give the acid is silently moved — in date, in language, in authorship — from the corpus where it stands to a more prestigious one where it does not. The three grades used throughout this article are built to catch exactly those three failures.

ATTESTED

A surviving text gives a preparation — named materials, in workable relation, subjected to a named operation — from which a competent reader can produce the substance in question, and whose described behaviour matches what that substance does. Attestation is a property of a text at a date, not of a name.

CLAIMED

A surviving text asserts the effect — a water “dissolves gold”, a solvent “opens all bodies” — without a repeatable preparation, or with a preparation whose product, on modern chemical assessment, cannot do what is asserted, though it may do something real and weaker. Claims are evidence of ambition and of vocabulary, not of possession.

MISATTRIBUTED

The preparation is attested — somewhere — but the credit is assigned to a text, author, language, or century where it does not occur. The mineral acids' assignment to the Arabic Jābir and to al-Rāzī is this article's type case: real recipes, wrong shelf, wrong century.

The Four Tests a Recipe Must Pass

First, the materials test. A mineral-acid recipe needs the acid's parents on the bench. Nitric acid, in every pre-modern preparation, is born from a nitrate — saltpetre — driven by heat with vitriol or alum. Sulphuric acid is born from a vitriol or from sulphur burned under conditions the Arabic texts do not describe. Aqua regia is not born at all until nitric acid exists to be married to a chloride. A text whose shelf contains no purified saltpetre cannot be making aqua fortis, whatever its waters are said to do — which is why Section VI's shelf and Section XII's saltpetre horizon are not background colour but part of the proof.

Second, the operation test. The strong acids are distillation products. They are not made by soaking, steeping, or dissolving, however long or however hot. A “water” made by dissolving salts in vinegar is a salt solution in a weak organic acid: it can pickle, clean, corrode a surface, and feed a cement — real workshop powers, graded as such in Section VIII — and it is not, and cannot concentrate itself into, a mineral acid. When a claimed acid's stated operation is solution rather than distillation, the claim fails at the operation test before its ingredients are even examined.

Third, the behaviour test. Each acid has a signature behaviour no other common water shares, and a genuine description betrays it. Aqua fortis dissolves silver eagerly and leaves gold untouched — the parting behaviour of Section XVI. Aqua regia does the one thing aqua fortis cannot: it dissolves gold. Oil of vitriol is oily, heavy, thirsty for water, and chars organic matter. A text that describes its water's behaviour in terms matching a signature — as the Summa perfectionis does, in paraphrase, for aqua fortis and silver — earns its identification. A text whose “gold-dissolving water” is never shown sparing silver, or whose “oil” is never shown doing anything oily, has given the reader a name without a behaviour, and names are the cheapest thing in alchemical writing.

Fourth, the silence test — used with care. An argument from silence is weak where survival is patchy, and strong where the corpus is large, practical, and loquacious about exactly the relevant matters. The Arabic alchemical corpus is the second case. Al-Rāzī's Sirr al-asrār lists apparatus piece by piece, classifies substances group by group, and gives operation after operation in bench detail. The Jābir corpus runs to hundreds of treatises on the working of metals and the making of waters. That neither corpus, in all that procedural abundance, anywhere sets down the distillation of vitriol with saltpetre — while a Latin text of c. 1300 sets it down in a paragraph — is a silence with evidentiary weight. It is not proof that no Arabic practitioner ever saw a drop of nitric acid form in a retort; stray phenomena leave no texts, and this article claims nothing about unrecorded accidents. It is proof of what this archive grades: the described, repeatable preparation is Latin and late, and the Arabic attribution is a later construction. Section XIII shows the construction being built.

// A WORD THAT HIDES THE WHOLE PROBLEM: “WATER” //

Arabic māʾ (plural miyāh), like Latin aqua, names any liquid preparation the workshop makes: a solution, a distillate, an infusion, a corrosive liquor. “Sharp water”, “divine water”, “water of sal ammoniac” — the phrases are everywhere in the Arabic texts, and every one has been Englished by an eager historian as if it named a mineral acid. It does not, until the four tests above are passed. This article keeps “water” in its period sense throughout.

One more calibration, of the opposite kind. Grading the acid claims down is not grading the Arabic workshop down: a sal ammoniac solution that bites copper, a distilled vinegar that eats a pearl, a cement that strips silver out of gold at red heat are real chemical powers, described at full strength in Sections VIII–X. The discipline is symmetrical — no inflation of a claim into an acid, and no deflation of a genuine surface chemistry into nothing.

III. The Apparatus Inheritance: From Maria to the Arabic Workshop

The Arabic workshop did not invent its glassware. It inherited the apparatus tradition of Hellenistic Egypt — the tradition this archive's Greco-Roman division treats in Maria the Jewess and the Apparatus Tradition — preserved it, standardised its Arabic names, extended its sublimation furniture, and, most importantly for this article, wrote it down as lists: named inventories of vessels and furnaces against which any reader could equip a bench. Al-Rāzī's apparatus chapters in the Sirr al-asrār are the type case, and their full treatment is in this division's Al-Rāzī dossier. The present section states the inheritance at the strength the operations of Sections IV–V need, and no stronger: it treats what the vessels do in an operation, not their catalogued forms, which belong to the companion articles.

The Three Heat Regimes Every Operation Chooses Among

Arabic workshop writing is precise about heat in the way that matters at a bench: not in degrees — no pre-modern text has them — but in regimes, each with its apparatus, its fuel discipline, and its characteristic failures. The water bath — Maria's balneum, the bain-marie that still carries her name — holds a vessel in boiling water and so caps its charge at the gentlest sustained heat the workshop commands: the regime for distilling delicate aromatics without scorching, for digestions, and for the slow solution of salts. Its failure mode is its virtue inverted: it cannot drive a stubborn distillation, and a worker who asks it to will boil his bath dry and crack his vessel. The ash bath and sand bath interpose a bed of sifted ash or sand between fire and vessel: slower to heat, slower to cool, proof against the sudden flare that shatters glass, and the standard regime for sublimations and for distillations that must be held steady for hours. The open fire and the muffle furnace give the workshop its top heats — calcination, cupellation, the roasting of ores and vitriols — at the price of control: the charge is at the fuel's mercy, volatile products are lost to the flue unless the vessel is closed and luted, and the endpoint must be read from colour, because nothing else survives to be read. Every operation in Sections IV–V is specified against these three regimes, because the Arabic texts specify them so: the same nominal operation at the wrong regime is a different operation, with a different product.

The Vessels the Operations Run In

The still — cucurbit below, alembic head above, delivery tube to a receiver — is the distillation engine, treated in its perfume-workshop form in Al-Kindī and the Perfume Distillers, whose inventory this article does not duplicate. What matters here is the still's one decisive property: it separates by volatility, returning a condensed liquid that was never in the pot as a liquid. That property is why distillation, and only distillation, can concentrate an acid — and why the operation test of Section II is an apparatus test in disguise. The sublimation vessel — in al-Rāzī's lists the al-uthāl family, a lower pot and an upper vessel or cone on which the risen solid deposits — does the same separation for solids that rise as vapour and fall as flower: sal ammoniac, sulphur, the arsenic sulphides, mercury compounds. The crucible and the cupel serve the open-fire operations, calcination and cupellation (Sections IV and XIV). Luting — the clay seals usually Englished as “philosophers' clay” — is the unglamorous technology on which every closed operation depends: a failed lute is a failed sublimation, a lost distillate, and, with mercury in the vessel, a poisoned workshop. The texts' attention to luting recipes indexes how much closed-vessel work they actually did — which was a great deal.

// READING AN OPERATION LIST AS EVIDENCE //

An apparatus list is a claim about practice, and it can be graded like any other claim here. A list that names a vessel, its material, its lute, and its failure — as al-Rāzī's lists do — is the deposit of a working tradition: attested practice. The Arabic corpus passes this test for stills, sublimation vessels, crucibles, cupels, baths, and furnaces — the full furniture of Sections IV–V. Judged by the same standard, it offers no apparatus arrangement for making or handling a strong mineral acid: no specially luted still for a corrosive distillate, no receiver discipline for a fuming product. The furniture and the silence belong to the same record.

The inheritance, then, in one sentence: Maria's Egypt gave the art the still, the water bath, and the reflux idea; the Arabic workshop received, named, and listed them, and built around them the most explicit operational literature the art had yet possessed. What it built is the subject of the next two sections.

IV. The Operations Systematised I: Distillation, Sublimation, Calcination

The canonical list varies in ordering and membership from text to text — the Jābir corpus and al-Rāzī do not file the art identically — but a stable core of eight operations recurs across the Arabic workshop literature and passes from it into the Latin lists: distillation, sublimation, calcination, solution, coagulation, filtration, ceration, and fixation. This section and the next give each operation as the texts organise it and as the bench requires it: purpose, apparatus, heat regime, endpoint, and failure modes. Procedural connective detail that the texts assume and the later workshop literature spells out is labelled reconstruction, per Section I's rule.

Distillation (Taqṭīr): Separation by Rising and Falling

Purpose. Distillation separates the volatile part of a charge from its fixed residue and delivers it, condensed, as a liquid — a “water” or an oil — cleaner than anything solution and filtration can produce. In the Arabic workshop its great documented employments are the perfume and drug waters of the al-Kindī tradition, the preparation of distilled vinegar (Section VIII), the winning of mercury from cinnabar in its retort form, and the concentration of liquids by repeated redistillation — cohabation, in the later Latin term, where the distillate is poured back on fresh or residual matter and driven over again.

Apparatus and procedure (reconstruction of the standard sequence). The charge — liquid, or solid matter moistened or mixed with a liquid — goes into the cucurbit, filled no more than about two-thirds, so that boiling cannot throw crude matter into the head. The alembic head is set and every joint luted. Heat is raised slowly: the first regime is the water bath for delicate charges, the ash or sand bath for stubborn ones. The worker watches the head, not the fire: when vapour reaches the head and the first drops gather in the delivery tube, the heat is held, not increased — the still is now working, and the whole art of the operation is to keep it exactly there. Distillate is collected in fractions where the texts direct it: the first runnings, the heart, and the tail are different goods, and a perfume distiller or a vinegar maker who mixes them has wasted the operation. The run ends when the drops slow and change character — thinner, weaker in smell, or, in a dry charge, when the residue begins to scorch and the distillate takes a burnt note. The residue in the cucurbit, the caput mortuum of the later Latin lists, is not necessarily waste: calcined, dissolved, and filtered, it yields the fixed salt of the charge in the standard later work-up.

Endpoints and failure modes. The endpoint is read in the receiver: volume against expectation, smell, taste where the texts permit it, and behaviour — a distilled vinegar that does not bite, a rose water that smells of the pot, are failed goods with named causes. The failures are four. Too fierce a fire: the charge boils over into the head, the distillate comes crude and coloured, and a sealed still can burst its lute — or its glass. Too weak a fire: nothing rises, the bath boils dry, the vessel cracks. A leaking lute: the volatile part — the very thing the operation exists to catch — escapes as invisible loss, and the worker blames his materials for his seal. Scorching: an organic charge heated dry past its water gives an empyreumatic, burnt-smelling oil that no redistillation fully cures. Every one of these failures is a failure the Arabic perfume literature knows by name and corrects — which is the practical background against which Section XI's point must be heard: this was a workshop culture fully capable of distilling a corrosive acid had the recipe been in its texts. The capability is attested. The recipe is not.

Sublimation (Taṣʿīd): Separation by Rising and Settling

Purpose. Sublimation is distillation's solid twin: a volatile solid is driven up as vapour by heat and caught, re-solidified, on a cooler upper surface, leaving fixed impurities below. It is the Arabic workshop's great purifying operation for the volatile “spirits” and their kin — above all sal ammoniac (nūshādir), whose commercial purification is a sublimation (Section VI), and for sulphur, the arsenic sulphides, and mercury preparations. Repeated sublimation — three times, seven times, in the texts' characteristic counts — is the standard idiom for “purified to the working standard”.

Apparatus and procedure. The ground charge goes into the lower vessel of the sublimation apparatus — the al-uthāl family of al-Rāzī's lists — often mixed with a fixed helper: common salt, alum, iron filings, or ash, which holds back impurities, prevents the charge from melting into an unworkable pool, and gives the vapour a cleaner road up (the helper varies by charge and text; the principle is constant). The upper vessel or cone is set and luted. The regime is the ash or sand bath, heat raised by degrees: too sudden a fire melts what should rise, or drives it past the cool zone and out of the lute. The sublimate gathers above as a crust, cake, or flower — sal ammoniac in dense white crusts, sulphur in pale flowers — and the operation is judged finished when no more rises at a heat that has been held steady, and the upper vessel, cooled before opening, shows its full harvest. The vessel is always cooled before opening: a hot sublimation vessel opened early exhales its product — and, with arsenic or mercury in the charge, its poison — into the worker's face.

Endpoints and failure modes. The endpoint is the clean separation itself: white, light, friable sublimate above; dark, fixed residue below. Failures: fusion instead of sublimation (heat too fierce or too direct; the charge melts and seals its own vapour in); sooting (an organic or sulphurous charge chars and blackens the product — the fire was raised before the volatile part had risen clear); loss at the lute, the standing failure of every closed operation; and false completion, where a first thin crust persuades the worker to stop while half his charge still sits below. The texts' insistence on repetition is the correction for the last two: what one sublimation misses, the third catches.

// SPIRITS AND BODIES //

The operation-list presupposes the corpus's master classification, stated here at the strength this article needs and treated in full in The Jābir Corpus and Al-Rāzī. The spirits are the volatile substances — sulphur, mercury, sal ammoniac, and the arsenic sulphides in the usual lists — which fire drives off as vapour. The bodies are the fixed, fusible metals. Sublimation purifies spirits; calcination breaks bodies down; fixation (Section V) is the attempt to make a spirit behave like a body. The taxonomy is a bench taxonomy: a substance's class is what the fire does to it.

Calcination (Taklīs): Breaking a Body Down to Its Calx

Purpose. Calcination subjects a body to sustained strong heat, usually in open air, until it loses its metallic or stony character and becomes a powdery, earthy calx — in modern terms, usually its oxide, though the period texts know it only as the body's ash, its opened and mortified form. The calx is the workshop's intermediate of choice: a metal that will not dissolve as metal will often dissolve as calx; a stone that will not powder under the pestle will powder after the fire. Calcination is also the first stage of the vitriol work of Section VII: green vitriol calcined to red colcothar is a calcination with a colour endpoint every worker could read.

Apparatus and procedure. The charge — metal in thin plates, filings, or small pieces, to maximise the surface the air can work on; mineral in lumps no larger than the heat can penetrate — goes into an open crucible or a shallow dish, in the muffle or the open furnace, at the workshop's top regime. The charge is stirred or turned where the texts direct, because a calcination crusts: the surface oxidises, seals, and protects the unburnt core beneath, and an unturned charge can roast for hours and emerge as a shell of calx around living metal. Time is the operation's real measure — hours, and for stubborn bodies repeated days of firing, cooling, grinding, and refiring, the grinding breaking the crust the fire made. Lead and tin calcine readily and their calces — the litharge family — feed cupellation (Section XIV). Iron calcines to a red scale. Copper blackens and reddens by stages the texts read as progress marks.

Endpoints and failure modes. The endpoint is constancy: the calx no longer gains the character of burning, no longer shows metallic glints when ground, and — in the careful workshops — no longer changes weight between firings. Failures: incomplete calcination under a sealed crust (corrective: grind and refire); vitrification, where too fierce a heat or a fluxing impurity melts the calx into a glass that no solvent will open (the charge is lost as a calx and kept, if at all, as a coloured glass); volatilisation loss, where a body with a volatile component — a mercury or arsenic preparation mistaken for a fixed body — simply leaves by the flue; and contamination from the vessel, a clay crucible at top heat contributing its own substance to a charge that will later be weighed or assayed. The operation looks like the crudest on the list — put it in the fire and wait — and its literature is correspondingly full of the corrections experience teaches: thin charges, turning, grinding between firings, and patience as a specified ingredient.

V. The Operations Systematised II: Solution, Coagulation, Filtration, Ceration, Fixation

Solution (Ḥall): Opening a Body in a Water

Purpose and procedure. Solution brings a solid into a liquid — a salt into water, a calx into vinegar, a metal into a sharp water — so that it can be filtered clean, mixed intimately with another dissolved body, or recovered pure by coagulation. The bench form is unglamorous and exactly specified in the working texts: the solid is ground fine first (surface is everything), added to the solvent in portions, worked in a mortar or a warmed vessel — the water bath's gentle regime speeds most solutions — and the mixture is left to digest, warm and covered, for the hours or days the recipe names. The endpoint is clarity: a solution that has stopped taking up solid and stands clear over its residue is saturated or complete, and the residue goes to filtration. Failure modes: passivation, where the first action coats the solid with an insoluble skin and the solution stops with the body barely touched (the pickle-maker's standing problem, Section VIII); false solution, a cloudy suspension mistaken for a dissolved body, which filtration exposes; and solvent exhaustion, charged to the material when the measure of solvent was simply too small. Solution is the operation on which every Arabic “sharp water” claim of Sections VIII–X is executed — which is why its limits matter: solution in a salt water is a real operation with real effects, and it is not distillation, and its product is not an acid concentrate.

Coagulation (ʿAqd): Bringing the Dissolved Back to a Body

Purpose and procedure. Coagulation is solution's reverse and partner: the dissolved or liquid body is made solid again — a salt recovered by evaporating its solution, a dissolved metal thrown down by a reagent or by a baser metal laid in the liquor, and, in the corpus's most ambitious idiom, mercury “coagulated” into a fixed, solid body. The bench forms are three. Evaporation: the solution is boiled down gently — water bath, then ash bath — until crystals form on cooling or the whole sets to a salt; the endpoint is the crystal habit the texts describe, and the failure is scorching a charge driven dry over direct fire. Cooling: a hot saturated solution deposits its salt as it cools, the standard purification rhythm — dissolve hot, filter hot, cool, collect — by which saltpetre itself would later be purified (Section XII). Precipitation and cementing-out: a body in solution is displaced by a stronger claimant — copper thrown down from its vitriol water by iron laid in it is the workshop's classic, known since antiquity and described across the Arabic literature — and the thrown-down body is filtered, washed, and dried. The failure modes are impurity carried down with the precipitate (corrective: wash, redissolve, repeat) and the coagulated product that redissolves in its own mother liquor when the worker is slow to separate them.

Filtration (Tarshīḥ): The Workshop's Sieve of Cloth and Felt

Purpose and procedure. Filtration separates the clear liquor from its sediment — the operation that turns a turbid solution into a workable water and a precipitate into a collectable solid. The Arabic bench filters through cloth — linen, in folds, laid in a funnel or strained across a vessel's mouth — through felt cones and sleeves for liquors that would eat or clog cloth, and by simple decantation and settling where the sediment is heavy and the liquor patient. Hot filtration, for solutions that crystallise on cooling, is done fast, with the funnel and receiver warmed. Endpoints are visual and absolute: the filtrate runs clear, drop by drop, and a second passing through fresh cloth changes nothing. Failures: blinding, where a fine precipitate seals the cloth at once (corrective: let it settle, decant the bulk, filter the dregs last); passage, where the precipitate is finer than the filter and the “clear” liquor is a suspension in disguise; and loss, the liquor a thirsty felt cone drinks and does not return. Filtration makes no one's reputation and ruins no one's legend, and it is in every serious recipe in the corpus — a reliable marker, again, of texts written from benches.

Ceration (Tashmīʿ): Making a Body Flow Like Wax

Purpose and procedure. Ceration is the list's most specialised operation: a hard, refractory powder — a calx, an earth, a fixed salt — is brought, by repeated imbibition with a liquid and gentle heating, to a soft, wax-like consistency in which it will fuse and flow at a heat it previously endured unchanged. The bench rhythm is cyclical: the powder is moistened with its directed water or oil, worked to a paste, dried gently, ground, and moistened again, cycle on cycle, until the mass softens on a warm plate like wax rather than sitting like sand. The endpoint is tactile and the texts name it so: the body cerates — it takes an impression, it flows at gentle heat, it has become workable. Its purpose in the system is preparatory: a cerated body fuses evenly with its partners instead of segregating in the melt, and several of the corpus's elixir procedures route their fixed ingredients through ceration before the final conjunction. Failure is under-cycling — a mass that looks waxen wet and reverts to powder dry — and over-wetting, where the imbibing liquid's own salts contaminate a charge whose purity was the point.

Fixation (Tathbīt): Making the Volatile Stay

Purpose and procedure. Fixation is the operation the whole system strains toward: a spirit — mercury above all, but also sulphur, sal ammoniac, the arsenics — which the fire naturally drives off is so treated that it stays: endures the furnace, enters a body, and does its work there instead of leaving by the flue. The bench strategies the corpus deploys are combinations of the operations already given: repeated sublimation of the spirit with a fixed body, so that each rising carries less of it away; prolonged digestion and coagulation with salts, calces, and oils; calcination of the spirit's compounds into their fixed calces — mercury to its red calx is the type case — and slow, sealed heating in which the spirit is given nowhere to go until it combines. The endpoint is a test, not an appearance: the fixed product is put to strong fire and weighed before and after — what loses nothing has been fixed; what smokes and lightens has not, whatever it looks like. Failure is therefore quantitative and unforgiving, and the corpus's fixation literature is its most repetitive for a reason: partial fixation is the normal result, and the texts' cycles of sublime–imbibe–calcine are the record of workers closing the gap by iteration. Fixation graded honestly is attested as an operation with real partial successes — mercury calces and sulphur compounds that genuinely endure heat their parents could not — and claimed in its perfected, elixir-bearing form. Both grades stand in this article, as they stand in the texts.

// THE LIST ITSELF IS THE ACHIEVEMENT //

Stand back from the eight operations and notice what kind of thing the list is: not a theory of matter but a complete grammar of bench actions — every transformation a pre-modern workshop can perform is a sequence drawn from these eight verbs, each with its apparatus, regime, endpoint, and failures, teachable to an apprentice innocent of all theory. That grammar, Arabic in its systematised form, is what Latin alchemy learned first and kept longest: the acids, when a Latin author finally writes them down, are written down in it — a distillation, of named shelf reagents, to a named endpoint, with named behaviour tests.

VI. The Reagent Shelf I: Salts, Alkalis, and Natron

An operation is only as good as what it operates on. The Arabic workshop's second great deposit — after the operation-list — is its reagent shelf: a stock of salts, alkalis, alums, vitriols, and fluxes, each with known sources, preparations, and bench behaviours, classified in al-Rāzī's taxonomy among the salts and boraxes. This section and the next walk the shelf — source, preparation, bench behaviour, and purpose for each reagent — with the full shelf set out as a table at the end of Section VII. Note, as the walk proceeds, what is not on it: no purified saltpetre in working stock, and no bottle of anything that chars, fumes, and eats metal on contact. The shelf is rich, and its richness is what makes that absence informative rather than accidental.

Common Salt (Milḥ): The Ubiquitous Helper

Source and preparation. Sea salt from coastal pans and rock salt from mines — the Arabic world commanded both, from the salt flats of the Maghrib to the rock-salt districts of Persia — cleaned for bench use by solution, filtration, and coagulation (Section V's rhythm): dissolve, filter off the sand and clay, boil down, collect. Bench behaviour. Salt is the workshop's great assistant rather than its actor: it fluxes a melt, covers a charge against the air, carries the cement of cementation parting (Section XIV), and in solution with vinegar makes the pickle of Section VIII. At red heat with silver present, its chlorine does real work — the cementation mechanism, stated in modern terms in the Greco-Roman division's Fire and Assay article and carried forward in Section XIV. What it was for: fluxing, cementing, pickling, and a dozen preparatory solutions. What it was never for, in any Arabic text graded here: no solution of common salt, at any strength the bench can make, is hydrochloric acid — the operation test of Section II settles that in one line.

Sal Ammoniac (Nūshādir): The Volcanic Salt

Source. Nūshādir is the shelf's traveller. Its prized natural source was Central Asia: sal ammoniac encrusts the ground and the rock around volcanic fumaroles and burning coal seams in the Turkestan districts, whence it moved west on the trade routes through Persia into the Arabic world as a recognised, priced commodity — the geographers and the drug literature name its eastern origin plainly. A second, humbler source was soot: the salt was also gathered and prepared from the soot of dung-fired baths and furnaces, and the drug trade distinguished the kinds by origin and quality. Either way, what arrived at the bench was crude and needed the operation it would then serve. Preparation: sublimation (Section IV) — the crude salt, mixed with its helpers, is sublimed once and again until it rises in clean white crusts; the purified cake is the workshop's standard. Bench behaviour. Nūshādir is a spirit by the taxonomy: heat drives it wholly into vapour, which is its glory as a purifiable salt and its danger in a sealed vessel. In solution it is the sharpest of the common salt waters: with vinegar it bites copper and its alloys, cleans metal surfaces, feeds the verdigris makers, and serves the solderer as a flux that strips oxide at the joint. Heated with metals and their calces it chloridises surfaces — real, useful, surface chemistry, whose full grading is Section VIII's business. What it was for: soldering flux, metal cleaning, the making of sharp waters and cements, the sublimation work of the spirits, and medicine. Hold its career in mind for Section XI: when aqua regia finally is described, it is described as aqua fortis plus this salt — the Arabic shelf supplied the regia's second parent centuries before the Latin workshop supplied its first.

Sal Alkali and Qilī: The Ash of Salt-Loving Plants

Source and preparation. Qilī — the word that gives European languages, through al-qilī, their very word alkali — is the ash of salt-tolerant plants of the saltwort kind, burned in the Syrian and Egyptian districts where the glass and soap industries consumed it by the camel-load. The ash is lixiviated — water percolated through it, filtered, and the liquor boiled down (solution, filtration, coagulation in sequence) — to a hard, grey-white alkaline salt, in modern terms a crude potassium and sodium carbonate. Bench behaviour. Its waters are caustic to the touch, cut grease on contact, and turn the workshop's indicators in the alkaline direction the period read by feel and by effect: a qilī liquor feels soapy because it is making soap of the skin's own oils. With oils and fats it makes soap outright; with sand and heat it makes glass; in the alchemical workshop it opens, softens, and dissolves by the alkaline road what the sharp waters attack by the acid one. What it was for: glass, soap, washing and scouring preparations, alkaline solutions and fusions, and the fixation work where a mild, fusible, solvent salt was wanted. Its documentary importance outruns its bench role: qilī is the proof that the Arabic workshop commanded strong reagents in the alkaline direction — genuinely caustic liquors, honestly described. The asymmetry is the point. Caustic alkali: attested. Caustic mineral acid: not in the corpus. The shelf's silence is selective, and selective silence is evidence.

Natron (Naṭrūn): Egypt's Carbonate

Source. Natron is the natural soda of the Wādī Naṭrūn lakes west of the Nile Delta — a sodium carbonate and bicarbonate mixture harvested from the lake crusts as it had been since pharaonic times, and exported across the Mediterranean world for glassmaking. Preparation: gathering, sorting, and solution–filtration cleaning for fine work; the glasshouses took it as it came. Bench behaviour: a milder, cleaner alkali than qilī, fusible, glass-forming, and in solution a gentle scouring and softening water. What it was for: glass above all, then washing, medicine, and the mummifier's ancient trade that the Islamic period inherited as history rather than practice. In the alchemical texts natron appears among the salts and borax-adjacent fluxes, valued where a clean alkaline flux was wanted without qilī's impurities. A terminological caution the sources themselves enforce: naṭrūn and the Latin nitrum name this carbonate — not saltpetre. The confusion of natron with nitre in later European reading is one of the seedbeds of the saltpetre muddle Section XII untangles.

VII. The Reagent Shelf II: Alums, Vitriols, Borax, and the Sulphur–Arsenic Family

The Alums (Al-Shabb): Astringency in a Crystal

Source. Alum — potash alum in modern terms, with its ammonium and soda kin imperfectly distinguished by the period — came to the Arabic bench from named districts whose product the trade graded: Yemenite alum stood at the head of the drug literature's lists for purity and strength, with Egyptian alum — from the Western Desert oases — the other great commercial source, and further kinds from Syria and beyond ranked below them. Preparation: the crude alum stone or earth was lixiviated, the liquor filtered and crystallised by cooling and evaporation — the standard salt rhythm — to the translucent octahedral crystals of the shops. Bench behaviour. Alum is the shelf's astringent: its solution puckers, staunches, and bites mildly; it mordants cloth for dye — its largest industrial employment, the dyer's indispensable bridge between fibre and colour — preserves and taws skins in the alum-tawing that complements oil and vegetable tanning, and in medicine staunches blood and treats the eye. In the alchemical workshop it appears in sublimation mixtures as a helper, in sharp waters as a sharpening salt, and — hold this for Section XI — in the Latin Summa's aqua fortis recipe as the third ingredient beside vitriol and saltpetre. What it was for: mordanting, tawing, medicine, and bench assistance. Alum alone, distilled, yields no strong acid — a point Section XI needs, because the Summa's recipe works by its saltpetre, not by its alum.

The Vitriols (Zāj): The Shelf's Most Consequential Family

The word first. Arabic zāj covers the vitriols — the crystalline metal sulphates of the period's mines and weathering heaps — and the texts distinguish kinds by colour and origin: green vitriol, the iron vitriol (in modern terms, ferrous sulphate), the commonest and the workshop's standard; blue vitriol, the copper vitriol, known and named by its colour; white kinds; and prized geographical sorts, Cypriot among them, whose exact modern identities the commentators still argue — a caution this article keeps, because zāj in a recipe is a family name, not a formula. Source: natural efflorescences in mines and on pyritic ground, gathered and crystallised from lixiviated liquors; also made by weathering iron pyrite and crystallising the run-off — the boundary between mined and manufactured vitriol was as porous in the Arabic world as in Europe's. Bench behaviour. Green vitriol dissolves to a pale green liquor, inks black with gall — the iron-gall ink of the chanceries is vitriol's most durable monument — dyes, tans, and, calcined, performs the shelf's most legible transformation: heated in the crucible it whitens as its water leaves, then reddens stage by stage to colcothar, the red iron oxide (in modern terms, ferric oxide), a pigment, a polishing powder, and a medicine. Driven harder still, in a closed vessel, vitriol gives off sharp, suffocating vapours — the phenomenon from which, in the Latin centuries and only there, oil of vitriol would be caught and kept (Section XI). What it was for: ink, dye, medicine, calcination work, and the general sulphate chemistry of the bench. The vitriols are the acids' true ancestors on the Arabic shelf — sulphur already married to a metal and to water, waiting for an operation the Arabic texts do not direct at them. The ancestry is real; the birth certificate is Latin.

Borax (Tinkār): The Goldsmith's Flux

Source. Tinkār — the word travels with the goods, from the Persian and ultimately the Central Asian trade — is natural borax, gathered from the dried salt lakes of Tibet and Central Asia and imported westward as a costly, recognised commodity; the drug and workshop literature also knows artificial or refined grades and warns, in the usual way, against sophistications. Preparation: solution, filtration, and recrystallisation to a clean white salt. Bench behaviour. Borax is the shelf's perfect flux: it swells and froths as its water leaves, then melts to a clear glass that dissolves metal oxides on contact — which is to say, it cleans a joint while the solder runs. What it was for: the soldering and welding of gold and silver above all — the goldsmith's flux, named as such across the literature — then glassmaking assistance and medicine. Its presence on the shelf is a small lesson in the workshop's real geography: a Baghdadi or Cairene bench routinely consumed a mineral harvested on the Tibetan plateau. The Arabic workshop was a node in a Eurasian reagent trade, and its texts' sourcing talk — this alum from Yemen, that nūshādir from Turkestan, tinkār from the lakes — is trade knowledge, graded attested wherever the geographers confirm it.

Marcasite, the Arsenic Sulphides, and Cinnabar

Marcasite (markhashītā) — iron pyrite, the brassy, glittering sulphide — stood on the shelf as a sulphur ore and a medicine, roasted to drive its sulphur into preparations and calcined in the sulphur work of the spirits; its bench treachery is that it weathers to vitriol liquors, so that a marcasite district and a vitriol district are often the same district at two dates. Realgar and orpiment — the red and yellow arsenic sulphides, the zarnīkh family — were pigments first (the illuminator's red and gold-yellow), medicines in minute and feared doses, and alchemical spirits: sublimed, calcined to the white arsenic of the later lists, and deployed in the colouring and whitening operations where an arsenical surface on copper imitates silver — real surface chemistry again, of the kind Section VIII grades. Their hazard was known in kind if not in mechanism: the corpus's warnings about arsenical fumes belong with the Greco-Roman record this archive keeps of workshop poisoning, and the modern reader should take the texts' caution at full strength. Cinnabar (zunjufr) — red mercury sulphide, mined from Spain to Central Asia and also made by uniting mercury and sulphur — was the pigment vermilion and, decisively, the ore of mercury: ground with iron or lime and driven in a retort or a cooled still, it yields the living metal, which condenses and runs to the receiver. Mercury so won feeds the whole amalgamation economy — gilding, mirror work, the coagulation and fixation operations of Section V — and cinnabar's red calx form, made by the sealed sublimation of the same two elements, closes the sulphur–mercury circle on the shelf itself.

VIII. Sharp Waters in the Arabic Record I: Vinegar and the Salt Waters

The Arabic texts do describe corrosive and solvent preparations — a shelf of “sharp waters” in the period sense of Section II — and this section and the two following give them their full, exact due: each water with its preparation as the texts give it (in paraphrase), its real chemical strength as modern assessment reads it (labelled as modern assessment), and its grade. The pattern can be stated in advance. The Arabic sharp waters are solutions — of salts, sulphur compounds, and ashes, in water or vinegar, sometimes concentrated where the solvent itself is distilled. Their powers are real and almost entirely surface powers: pickling, cleaning, etching, cementing, amalgamating, corroding a skin. None is made by distilling a nitrate or a vitriol into a receiver. None is a mineral acid.

Vinegar (Khall) and Distilled Vinegar: The One True Acid on the Shelf

Vinegar — wine or date vinegar, soured and matured — is a genuine acid: a dilute solution of acetic acid, in modern terms typically some four to eight parts in the hundred. The Arabic workshop knew its powers precisely and used them daily. In paraphrase of the working literature's standard employments: vinegar dissolves lime, chalk, eggshell, and pearl — slowly, with effervescence the texts note — softens and opens calces that water will not touch, pickles oxide and tarnish off copper and iron, and, left on copper with air and sal ammoniac, feeds the slow growth of verdigris, the green pigment-corrosion the painters consumed. Distilled vinegar — vinegar driven over in the still of Section IV and collected in fractions — is stronger: the operation concentrates the acetic acid in the early fractions, and the texts' praise of distilled vinegar's sharpness over the common kind is, in modern assessment, chemically justified. Grade: attested, as a weak organic acid with real solvent powers over carbonates, oxides, and reactive metal surfaces. And the limit is as exact as the power: distilled vinegar does not dissolve silver, does not dissolve gold, does not part the two, and chars nothing. A workshop whose strongest acid is distilled vinegar is a workshop with an acid — and without the mineral acids. Both halves of that sentence are the record.

The Salt Waters: Sal Ammoniac, Alum, and Salt in Solution

The second family is the salt waters: solutions — most often in vinegar, sometimes in water, sour wine, or urine in the older Mediterranean manner — of nūshādir, of alum, of common salt, singly and in the mixtures the recipes favour. In modern assessment, a vinegar solution of sal ammoniac is a genuinely aggressive bench liquor: the ammonium and chloride ions, in a weakly acid bath, attack copper and its alloys, strip oxides, and keep a surface chemically awake in a way plain vinegar does not — which is why the solderers, the verdigris makers, and the metal colourists lived by it. Alum waters bite more mildly and astringently. Salt waters clean, pickle lightly, and preserve. Applied hot, worked with abrasion, renewed over days, these waters corrode: they will eat a copper vessel thin in time, pit iron, and dissolve the calces and carbonates set in them. Grade: attested, as corrosive salt solutions with real metal-attacking power at surfaces. The grading line is the same as vinegar's, drawn at the same place: attack at a surface, over time, on the reactive metals, is not solution of a noble metal in bulk, and no salt water on this shelf parts silver from gold in the wet way. Where a text moves from describing such a water's bite to claiming it dissolves a noble body outright, the grade changes — and Section IX takes up exactly those moves, in the corpus that makes them most grandly.

The Three Real Surface Chemistries the Claims Rest On

AMALGAMATION — REAL

Mercury wets gold and silver on contact and dissolves their surfaces into amalgam — a genuine, room-temperature “dissolution” of noble metals, exploited in fire gilding and in the winning of gold from its ores. A text that says its mercury preparation “dissolves gold” may be reporting this, truly. Amalgamation is not acid action, and it parts nothing: the gold is recovered by driving the mercury off, whole.

CEMENTATION — REAL

Gold alloyed with silver, packed in a salt cement and held at red heat, loses its silver to the cement as silver chloride while the gold stands untouched (Section XIV). A hot, dry, vapour-phase chemistry — the ancient parting — easily misread, at a distance of centuries, as the work of a dissolving water. It is the work of chlorine at red heat, and it needs no acid.

PICKLING AND ETCHING — REAL

Vinegar and salt waters strip oxides, tarnish, and fire-scale, brighten a surface, and bite a design into copper. To a reader summarising centuries later, a water that “eats” metal and “opens” bodies sounds like a solvent of metals. At the bench it is a cleaner of surfaces — indispensable, and a different thing.

Those three chemistries — amalgamation, cementation, pickling — are the factual seedbed of nearly every inflated acid claim in the later literature. Each is real; each involves a noble metal being acted on, disappearing into a medium, or emerging transformed; and each can be described, in the loose vocabulary of “waters” and “dissolving”, in sentences that a nineteenth-century historian, reading fast and knowing what acids he possessed, could mistake for aqua regia. The mistake is understandable. This archive's business is not to repeat it. With the real waters graded, the article now turns to the grand claims themselves — the waters of the Jābir corpus that are said to dissolve all bodies, gold included — and grades them one by one.

IX. Sharp Waters in the Arabic Record II: The Jābir Corpus, Claim by Claim

The Jābir corpus — the large body of Arabic alchemical writings ascribed to Jābir ibn Ḥayyān, dated on Kraus's analysis to c. 850–950 and treated in full, dating debate included, in The Jābir Corpus — is where the acid legend goes hunting for its proof-texts. The corpus is vast, theoretically ambitious, and rich in waters: waters of sulphur, waters of sal ammoniac, divine waters, sharp waters, and, in its most famous reaches, solvents credited with opening every body set in them. This section assesses the solvent claims at their real strength, group by group. Everything here is paraphrase; the corpus's own words, in the standard editions and translations the Sources name, are the court of appeal, and the reader is directed to them.

The Book of Seventy and the Universal-Solvent Idiom

The Book of Seventy (Kitāb al-Sabʿīn) — one of the corpus's major treatises, known also through its medieval Latin career — handles the elixir work in which powerful waters figure as agents that soften, open, and dissolve the bodies they are to perfect. In paraphrase of the treatise's characteristic claims: its prepared waters act on the metals not as a pickle acts on a surface but as a solvent acts on a solute — the body is taken up, held, and re-coagulated in an improved state. Assessed at real strength, the preparations behind the idiom, where ingredients are named, are the shelf of Sections VI–VII at work: sal ammoniac and salt waters, sulphur and arsenic preparations, vinegar and its distillates, alkaline liquors — the full armamentarium of Section VIII, deployed with heat, with repetition, and inside sealed digestions where a modern chemist would expect slow surface attack, cementation-like chloridising, amalgamation where mercury is present, and the gradual corrosion of the baser metals. On gold and silver in bulk, in modern assessment, such waters do — separately and together — not what the idiom asserts. Grade for the Book of Seventy's dissolving waters: claimed. The claim itself is historically precious: it shows the corpus wanting a universal solvent, theorising one, and naming the want in water-language. A want is not a possession, and the history of chemistry is full of both; the archive files each under its own grade.

“A Water That Dissolves Gold”: The Sentence and the Recipe

Here the article states its central teaching distinction as plainly as it can, because the whole legend turns on blurring it. A text that says a water dissolves gold, and a text that gives a repeatable preparation of aqua regia, are different kinds of object, and the distance between them is the distance between a programme and a product. The Jābir corpus contains the first kind of object, repeatedly: assertions, in paraphrase, that such-and-such a water — its composition variously hinted, veiled in the corpus's celebrated dispersion of knowledge across treatises, or referred to another book — dissolves gold, dissolves all bodies, opens what fire cannot. The corpus does not contain the second kind: nowhere in the surviving Arabic treatises is there a sequence of the form — take vitriol and saltpetre in stated relation, distil, collect a water; prove it on silver, which it dissolves, and on gold, which it spares; add sal ammoniac and it will take the gold — which is the form the Summa perfectionis gives, in Latin, four centuries later (Section XI). The corpus's dispersion technique — its deliberate scattering of a procedure's parts across books, so that only the initiated reader can assemble them — is sometimes offered as the explanation: the recipe is there, but dispersed. The explanation fails the materials test of Section II. Dispersion can hide an order of operations; it cannot hide the shelf. Across hundreds of treatises of bench talk, purified saltpetre never takes its place as a stock reagent with a preparation, a source, and a behaviour — because, Section XII argues, it was not yet a workshop commodity in that world. What is dispersed in the corpus is real workshop knowledge of the Section VIII kind. What is absent is absent whole: ingredient, operation, and behaviour together.

// THE MERCURY–SULPHUR FRAME, AND WHY IT MATTERS HERE //

The Jābir corpus's great theoretical deposit — the sulphur–mercury theory of the metals, in which the metals are compounded of a sulphur principle and a mercury principle in varying purity and proportion — is treated in full in the companion dossier and is not re-argued here. Its bearing on the acid question is one sentence: a theory in which perfection is purifying and re-proportioning two principles directs ambition toward waters that purify, open, and re-coagulate — elixir-waters — not toward an acid valued, as the Latin assayers valued aqua fortis, for what it destroys and separates. The corpus's solvent claims live inside that theory, as elixir claims; reading them as assay-acid claims is a category error the nineteenth century made and the Latin centuries did not.

What the Corpus's Waters Demonstrably Do

The claim-by-claim audit has a credit side, and it should be entered as carefully as the debit. Where the corpus's waters are given with ingredients from the attested shelf and operations from the attested list, their described effects match Section VIII's real chemistries with a fidelity that marks them as bench reports: copper and its alloys coloured, whitened by arsenical treatment, cleaned and bitten by the salt waters; mercury coagulated toward solidity by sulphur and by the salts; calces opened by vinegar; bodies “dissolved” in the period's own weaker sense — taken into a liquor as calx, as corrosion product, as amalgam — and recovered by coagulation. Grade for this stratum: attested, as the working record of the Section VIII chemistries, theoretically dressed. The corpus, in short, is exactly what its best modern readers — Kraus, and the scholarship in the Sources — take it for: a vast, uneven, genuinely practical literature whose real contents need no borrowed acids to be remarkable. Borrowing them for it, as the popular tradition does, does the corpus the disservice of praising it for the one thing it does not contain while passing over the system it does.

X. Sharp Waters in the Arabic Record III: Al-Rāzī's Waters at Their Real Strength

If the acids were anywhere in the Arabic record, they would be in al-Rāzī. The case deserves stating at full strength, because it is the strongest form of the silence argument and this article rests its adjudication on it. Abū Bakr al-Rāzī (865–925) — physician, philosopher, and the most uncompromisingly practical writer in the Arabic alchemical tradition — left in the Kitāb Sirr al-asrār, the Book of the Secret of Secrets, the corpus's most systematic workshop manual: substances classified group by group, apparatus listed piece by piece, operations given in sequence, procedures written to be followed. Julius Ruska's edition and study of 1937 — Al-Rāzī's Buch Geheimnis der Geheimnisse — established the text's modern scholarly footing, and the full dossier is in this division's Al-Rāzī article. Al-Rāzī is also, not coincidentally, the author to whom popular tradition assigns sulphuric acid most confidently after “Geber” himself — oil of vitriol is “Rāzī's discovery” in a hundred retellings. The manual itself is the refutation, and it is worth watching it refute in detail.

What the Sirr al-Asrār's Waters Are

In paraphrase of the manual's solvent and water preparations, as Ruska's edition presents them: al-Rāzī's working waters are the Section VIII families, organised with his characteristic tidiness — waters of sal ammoniac and of the salts, vinegar and sharp vinegar preparations, alkaline waters from the ashes, sulphur waters, and compound liquors for softening, opening, colouring, and corroding the bodies and stones of his classification. Their operations are solution, digestion, distillation of the solvent kind, and coagulation — the attested list, exactly. Their targets are the attainable ones: calces, stones, the baser metals' surfaces, the colouring and whitening work, the preparation of bodies for the elixir operations that the book, like the corpus it criticises and continues, keeps in view. Of a distillation of vitriol with a nitrate to a corrosive water; of a water that dissolves silver and spares gold; of an oil from vitriol that chars and burns — of any of the Section II signatures — the manual, in all its procedural abundance, says nothing, because there was nothing in its author's workshop to say. Grade for al-Rāzī's waters: attested as Section VIII preparations; the sulphuric-acid attribution misattributed, against the evidence of his own book.

// WHY AL-RĀZĪ'S SILENCE WEIGHS MORE THAN JĀBIR'S //

The Jābir corpus is veiled by design, so its silences can always be pleaded as concealment, however unconvincingly (Section IX). Al-Rāzī's manner is the opposite: he writes against obscurity and lists his substances in the open. A mineral acid in his workshop would have been classified, listed, given a preparation and uses, and wielded in an argument. The plea of concealment cannot be entered for the least concealing author in the tradition.

Two smaller witnesses close the Arabic record. Al-Kindī (c. 801–873), whose perfume book — the Book of the Chemistry of Perfume and Distillations — is the Arabic distillation literature's elegant foundation, distils everything his art can distil: scented waters, oils, compounded perfumes, in apparatus this division treats in Al-Kindī and the Perfume Distillers. His stills run on flowers, woods, resins, and waters — never on vitriol and a nitrate, because his art has no use for their product and his shelf no such pairing. And the later Arabic centuries — workshop literature, pharmacopoeias, ḥisba manuals — continue the same shelf and the same waters without an acid appearing, through the very decades in which the acids enter the Latin record. The Arabic record is not a fragment with the acid pages torn out. It is a complete, continuous, talkative record in which the acids do not occur — because the preparation that produces them had not yet been written down anywhere, by anyone. The next section shows it being written down.

XI. The Contrast Case: The Latin Pseudo-Geber and What a Mineral-Acid Recipe Looks Like

The Summa perfectionis magisterii — the Summa perfectionis — is the most accomplished alchemical treatise of the Latin Middle Ages: a systematic, argumentative, bench-literate work in two books, setting out a theory of the metals, a doctrine of their perfection and corruption, and a working apparatus of operations and tests that no earlier Latin text matches. It presents itself as the work of Geber. It is, on the modern scholarly consensus established by William R. Newman's critical edition and study — The Summa Perfectionis of Pseudo-Geber (1991), the edition this article follows and names wherever the Summa's contents are in question — an original Latin composition of the late thirteenth century, by a Latin author writing under the borrowed name. Newman argued, on textual and doctrinal evidence set out in the edition, for the Franciscan Paul of Taranto as that author; the attribution is Newman's, is widely though not universally accepted, and is flagged here as an attribution, not a datum. What is not in dispute is the corpus's Latinity, its date horizon, and its independence of any Arabic original. And it is in this corpus — the Summa and its companion pseudo-Geber treatises — that the mineral acids are first clearly described. The descriptions follow, in paraphrase throughout, from Newman's edition. They are given at length for one reason: so that the reader can see, feature by feature, what a genuine mineral-acid recipe looks like as a text — and can thereafter never mistake an Arabic sharp-water claim for one.

Aqua Fortis: Vitriol, Saltpetre, Alum — Distilled

In paraphrase of the Summa tradition's preparation, as Newman presents it: take vitriol, saltpetre, and alum, in stated proportion; subject the mixture to distillation, with the heat raised in the strong regime the operation demands; collect the water that comes over. Then — and this is the passage's evidentiary heart — the text proves its product by its behaviour, in paraphrase: the water is of marvellous dissolving power; it dissolves silver, and the other metals set in it, readily; gold it does not dissolve. Every Section II test is passed in a paragraph. Materials: a nitrate, present, named, and proportioned. Operation: distillation, not solution. Behaviour: the exact signature of nitric acid — silver yes, gold no — stated as the product's defining test, because the author's intended use is the test: this water parts silver from gold in the wet way, the subject of Section XVI. In modern terms, the distillation drives nitric acid out of the saltpetre by the vitriol's sulphuric strength, generated in the hot mixture itself; the alum assists the traditional recipe as a drying, heat-moderating partner. Grade: attested — Latin, late 13th century, in the pseudo-Geber corpus. Hydrochloric acid enters the same Latin horizon by the parallel road — a spirit driven from common salt with vitriol, described in the pseudo-Geber tradition and its successors — and is graded attested on the same footing and at the same date, a full documentary generation after aqua fortis and never in the Arabic corpus.

Aqua Regia: Aqua Fortis Learns to Take Gold

In paraphrase of the same tradition: to the aqua fortis, add sal ammoniac — nūshādir, the Arabic shelf's own salt, in Latin dress — and the water gains the one power its parent lacked: it now dissolves gold itself, whence its name, the royal water, the water that masters the king of metals. In modern terms, the chloride from the sal ammoniac converts the nitric acid's attack into the mixed aqua regia chemistry — free chlorine and nitrosyl compounds doing together what neither parent does alone — and gold passes into solution as a chloroaurate. Grade: attested, in the same Latin corpus and horizon. Pause on the genealogy, because it is this article in miniature. Aqua regia has two parents of different nations: the operation and the nitrate are the Latin workshop's new contribution; the chloride salt is the Arabic shelf's old stock, known, sourced, sublimed, and used for centuries — for everything except this. Transmission supplied the reagent. Invention, at a datable Latin moment, supplied the marriage. An honest history credits both, in that order, and confuses neither with the other.

Oil of Vitriol: The Vitriol Driven to Its End

In paraphrase of the pseudo-Geber tradition's vitriol work: vitriol — the green zāj of Section VII, in its Latin form — calcined and then distilled under the strongest heat the furnace gives, in a retort arrangement that can catch a heavy, reluctant product, yields a dense, oily liquor: oil of vitriol, in modern terms sulphuric acid, known to its first describers by its behaviour — oily in body, corrosive beyond any water on the older shelf, thirsty and destructive toward organic matter. The preparation is the hardest of the three: the product comes over late, at top heat, in small yield, and eats at the apparatus that makes it, which is one reason its description is the tradition's most laboured. Grade: attested, Latin, same corpus and horizon — and the grade carries the article's recurring moral in its sharpest form. The Arabic workshop roasted vitriol for centuries, read its colour ladder to colcothar like a book, and stopped — as its texts direct — at the calx. The Latin text drives the same mineral one operation further, in a closed vessel, and catches what the open crucible had always let escape to the flue. The difference between the records is not intelligence, not theory, not even equipment. It is one directed operation, written down in one place at one date. That is what a discovery looks like in a documentary archive, and it is why the archive, not the legend, gets the credit.

// WHY THE ABSENCE IS A MEANINGFUL SILENCE, NOT A GAP IN SURVIVAL //

Manuscript survival is uneven. But the acid case rests not on a single missing manuscript but on four converging records: the Arabic corpus's vast surviving bulk, without the recipes (Sections IX–X); the shelf's inventory, without purified saltpetre in stock (Sections VI–VII, XII); the Latin corpus's sudden, fully formed possession of the recipes, under a borrowed name (Section XIII); and the acids' later career, running forward from the Latin texts with no Arabic tributary joining it. A gap in survival explains a missing leaf, not a missing river flowing visibly from a Latin spring.

XII. The Saltpetre Horizon: Purified Nitre and Al-Rammāḥ

One ingredient governs the whole chronology, and it deserves its own section: saltpetre — potassium nitrate, in modern terms — the parent of nitric acid in every pre-modern preparation and the feedstock of gunpowder. The acids could not be described before saltpetre could be had: not as a crust, a curiosity, or a contested word, but as a purified, recognisable, stockable commodity with a preparation of its own. That horizon — in the Arabic world as in the Latin — is late, and its lateness is an independent check on Section XI's dating, arrived at from the materials side.

The Word Problem: Three Salts in One Name

The pre-modern vocabulary conspires against the historian here. Latin nitrum and its Arabic counterparts name, in different texts and centuries, natron (Section VI's carbonate), borax-adjacent salts, efflorescent salts scraped from walls and soils — the “wall salt” of the Arabic phrases — and, eventually, true saltpetre. An efflorescence on a stable wall in a dry climate is in fact often nitrate-bearing, which is why the scraping trade existed; it is also a filthy mixture of nitrates, carbonates, chlorides, and earth, useless for either powder or acid until it is purified. Every early “saltpetre” citation must therefore pass a purification test before it passes anything else: does the text do anything with its salt that only the purified nitrate can do — deflagrate in a powder, yield an acid on distillation — or does it merely name a white salt from a wall? Graded by that test, the Arabic alchemical corpus's salt-words resolve, in the judgement this article follows, into natron, the borax family, and crude mixed efflorescences: real salts, honestly used, none of them the purified nitrate. The purification itself — solution in hot water, filtration, crystallisation by cooling, the nitrate's steep temperature-solubility doing the separating, in the Section V rhythm applied to a new material — is a datable technology, and its texts are datable with it.

Al-Rammāḥ: The Arabic World's Saltpetre Witness, at Its Real Date

The Arabic world's unambiguous saltpetre witness is military, not alchemical, and it stands exactly where the horizon predicts: al-Ḥasan al-Rammāḥ, the Syrian military engineer whose Book of Horsemanship and Military Stratagems (Kitāb al-Furūsiyya wa'l-manāsib al-ḥarbiyya), written in the later thirteenth century — the 1270s–1280s in the usual dating — gives, in paraphrase, the purification of saltpetre by solution and crystallisation and a series of gunpowder compositions in stated proportions, rockets and incendiaries among them. Grade: attested — Arabic, late 13th century, in the pyrotechnic literature. The witness cuts both ways, and this article takes both cuts. For the Arabic record: it proves the Islamic world possessed purified saltpetre and its defining technology — powder — by the later 1200s, in the same decades as the Latin acid texts, on the Eurasian horizon (Chinese powder knowledge travelling west) where both belong. Against the acid legend: it proves the date of that possession. Al-Rammāḥ writes four centuries after the Jābir corpus's horizon and three and a half after al-Rāzī. Had purified saltpetre been a stock reagent of the ninth-century workshop, the powder and the acid would not both have waited until the thirteenth century to be written down, on opposite ends of the same world, within a generation of each other. The horizon is one horizon: nitrate purification, gunpowder, and the mineral acids are a single late-medieval package, and the documents — Arabic military, Latin alchemical — date the package, not the legend.

// A NOTE ON THE LATIN DOOR //

How Arabic alchemy passed into Latin, and how original Latin writing grew up inside the translated frame, is the subject of this division's The Latin Door. The pseudo-Geber corpus is that story's most consequential chapter: a Latin author who had learned from the translations so thoroughly that he could write as the tradition — and published his own advances, the acids among them, under the tradition's greatest name. Section XIII follows what that choice did.

XIII. Why “Geber” Happened: Ascription, Translation, and Popular History

A misattribution this durable is not an accident; it is a machine with working parts, and the parts can be named. Four of them did the work. First, the name itself. Latin Europe received Jābir as Geber — a king of the art, in the translators' prefaces and the tradition's imagination, the supposed master of Gerard of Cremona's century and of every alchemical authority-list thereafter. A name of that weight attracts attributions the way a great mint's stamp attracts imitations: whatever circulated as Geber's was read, copied, and cited as the master's. Second, the Summa's own ascription. The pseudo-Geber author did not have his work stolen into the corpus; he put it there. Writing as Geber was a deliberate act of self-authorisation — the new Latin science presenting its advances in the voice of the received master — and it worked completely: for five centuries the Summa was read, printed, and revered as the Arabic Geber's own summa, the acids included in his credit. Third, the genuine translations standing beside the false ones. Latin readers possessed real Jābir, in real translation, in the same bindings and the same author-lists as the pseudo-Geber treatises; nothing in the medieval book trade obliged a reader to sort them, and the sorting tools — Arabic manuscripts, collated, in Latin hands — barely existed. Fourth, the popular histories. When the nineteenth and twentieth centuries wrote chemistry's pedigree for schools and encyclopaedias, they took the printed Geber corpus at its title page, compressed “the Geber corpus describes the acids” into “Geber discovered the acids”, and filed the discovery under the Arabic master's conventional early dates. Each retelling dropped a qualifier. By the mid-twentieth century the sentence of Section I stood in textbooks as unadorned fact — attested nowhere, repeated everywhere.

The Corrective Scholarship

The correction was built, piece by piece, across the same century. Julius Ruska and then Paul Kraus, working on the Arabic corpus itself — Ruska's al-Rāzī edition of 1937 among their instruments — established that the Arabic Jābir was a corpus with a history, datable and analysable, and that its contents did not include the Latin works' signature preparations. Robert Multhauf, in The Origins of Chemistry (1966) and his studies of distillation, gave the correction its technological frame: the acids belong to the history of an operation and its apparatus, and that history runs late. William R. Newman's 1991 critical edition of the Summa perfectionis — text, translation, and a demonstration of the work's Latinity, its scholastic argumentation, its sources, and its author's milieu — is the correction's keystone: after Newman, “the Latin pseudo-Geber” is not a conjecture but a shelf-mark. Lawrence Principe, in The Secrets of Alchemy (2013) and across his work, carried the corrected account to the general reader the legend had owned, pseudo-Geber's acids restored to their Latin workshop and their real, considerable brilliance. Earlier authorities are used here with their corrections attached: Forbes's Short History of the Art of Distillation (1948), a mine of apparatus history, is cited with corrections noted — its acid datings run early against the Newman consensus, and Section XI follows Newman and Principe where they differ. The Stapleton school's work, foundational for reading the corpus as bench literature, stands in the Sources under the same rule.

Credit where the documents put it. The rule sounds austere and is in fact the most generous rule available: it gives the Arabic workshop its operations, its shelf, its taxonomy, and its assay practice — an achievement large enough to need no garnish — and it gives an anonymous Latin master of c. 1300 the acids that are, textually, his. What it refuses is only the transfer: the moving of credit from the shelf where the documents keep it to the shelf where a famous name would look better holding it. Every article in this division is written under that rule. This article, whose subject is a transfer seven centuries old, is where the rule earns its keep.

One question remains before the closing movement, and it is the fair one: if the acids are Latin and late, does anything in the Arabic record point at them — any practice whose logic the acids would complete? The answer is yes, and it is the assay tradition. The Arabic world inherited from Greece a complete technology for asking a piece of metal what it was — and lived for centuries with that technology's one great gap: fire assay could count the precious metal in a sample, but parting gold from silver still meant the slow, hot, dry cement. A water that parted them in the wet way was, in that world, a thinkable, desirable, missing thing. The next two sections give the assay tradition its Arabic career; Section XVI then shows the Latin acids arriving, precisely, as the missing instrument — which is the deepest reason their true date matters.

XIV. Assay Carried Forward I: The Miḥakk, Cupellation, and the Mints

This article's final movement continues a story told in the Greco-Roman division's Fire and Assay: Testing in the Greek World — the touchstone that reads a streak, the cupel that counts by destruction, the cement that parts by chlorine at red heat, the public tester with his chisel. That article ends in the recipe papyri of Roman Egypt. The tradition did not end there. It passed, with the rest of the Greek workshop inheritance, into the Arabic world, where it was practised at a scale the Greek cities never commanded: an empire of mints, striking gold dīnārs and silver dirhams from Iberia to Central Asia, whose every issue was a standing promise about fineness, and whose assay practice is documented in mint administration, in the market-inspection (ḥisba) manuals, and in the technical literature. This section follows the Greek instruments into that world; Section XV follows the one instrument the Arabic world raised to a new power.

The Miḥakk: The Touchstone in Arabic Practice

The touchstone in Arabic is the miḥakk — the word names the stone and, in the moral vocabulary that follows the Greek basanos exactly, a test of character: what the stone does to metal, life does to men. The instrument is the Greek one unchanged in specification — the dark, fine, hard basanite that takes a metal streak and holds it for the eye — and the procedure is the Greek one of the fire-assay article's Section III: draw the streak of the suspect piece, draw beside it the streaks of needles or standards of known fineness, read the colours side by side in the same light. Its Arabic career runs at every level the Greek one did, widened by empire. At the mint, the streak is the receiving screen for bullion and old coin coming in for recoinage: fast, non-destructive, and sufficient to route a parcel to acceptance, to the cupel, or to refusal. At the money-changer's table — the ṣarrāf, whose bench is the ḥisba manuals' standing anxiety — the miḥakk is the day's first instrument, backed, as in Athens, by the balance for weight and by the ring and heft of the hand. At the goldsmith's bench, it grades the workshop's own product before the hallmark of reputation is spent on it. And its Greek failure modes travel with it, unaltered: it reads the skin and not the core, it cannot count three metals at once, and its verdict lives in the operator's discipline. The ḥisba literature's fraud catalogue — plated dirhams, surface-enriched pieces, clipped and sweated coin — is the Greek counterfeiter's catalogue in Arabic dress, hunted by the Greek counter-measures: the streak for the surface, the cut for the core, the balance for the weight, and the cupel for the count.

Cupellation and Parting in the Mint Tradition

Behind the streak stands the count. Cupellation — the sample alloyed with lead, held in moving air at bright heat on a porous calcareous cupel while the litharge carries every base metal away and leaves the precious bead to be weighed, exactly as the fire-assay article's Section V sets it out — was the Islamic mints' quantitative court of appeal, as it had been the Greek refineries'. The mint context added one discipline the Greek record shows only in embryo: routine. A mint striking to a standard fineness — the reformed dīnār of the Umayyad reform at the end of the seventh century set the type: a gold coin of defined weight and high, maintained fineness, with the dirham reformed beside it — must assay its melts as a matter of course, charge by charge, and the documentary traces of that routine survive in the administrative and ḥisba record's assumption that fineness is a checkable, enforceable fact about a coin, with named officials answerable for it. Parting and refining complete the mint's bench: silver won from lead ores by the cupellation hearth at production scale; gold parted from its silver by the salt cement — the Lydian process of the fire-assay article, foil or granulated alloy packed with salt, brick dust, and its helpers, held at red heat for its long hours while the silver chloridises into the cement and the gold stands — practised in the Islamic refining tradition as it had been at Sardis, because until the Latin acids there was, in all the world, no other way to part them. Hold that last clause. The whole of Section XVI hangs on it.

// TESTING AS THE WORKSHOP'S CONSCIENCE //

The ḥisba manuals — the market inspectors' handbooks, al-Shayzarī's and Ibn al-Ukhuwwa's among the surviving type — treat the assay instruments as moral furniture: the changer must keep a true miḥakk, true weights, and a true balance, and the inspector must know the frauds that defeat each. It is the Greek dokimasia ethic in an Islamic idiom: a market is a system of promises about invisible interiors, and testing keeps the promises honest. The Arabic alchemical literature breathes the same air — its elixir claims are, in form, dokimasia claims: this product is gold, and here is the fire it will stand. The acids, when they come, will be received into this culture of the test, and valued first of all as tests.

XV. Assay Carried Forward II: Al-Bīrūnī's Balance — Hydrostatics as Assay

The Greek fire-assay tradition contained one instrument that was not a workshop procedure at all but a piece of natural philosophy: Archimedes' hydrostatic weighing, the comparison of a body's weight in air and in water, from which its density — and, for an alloy of two known metals, its composition — can be computed. The fire-assay article's Section VIII gives the crown anecdote and its limits in full. In the Arabic world that instrument found its master. Abū Rayḥān al-Bīrūnī (973–after 1050) — polymath of Khwarizm and Ghazna, astronomer, geographer, and the most exact measurer of his millennium — made hydrostatic weighing a precision assay method and published its results in his Kitāb al-Jamāhir fī maʿrifat al-jawāhir, the Book Most Comprehensive in Knowledge on Precious Stones — the Book of Stones of the Hakim Mohammed Said translation (1989) named in the Sources — where the densities of the metals, the stones, and the workshop's materials are determined, tabulated, and used as instruments of identification and assay.

The Method: A Vessel That Measures Volume by Overflow

In paraphrase of al-Bīrūnī's described procedure, as the Said translation presents it: the body to be measured is weighed in air on a fine balance. It is then weighed against the water it displaces — in the apparatus associated with his name, a vessel filled to a spout, into which the body is lowered so that the overflow, caught and weighed, gives the weight of a volume of water exactly equal to the body's volume. The ratio of the two weighings is the body's specific gravity: its density against water's, dimensionless, comparable across substances and across centuries. For a pure metal the number is an identity card. For a gold–silver alloy it is a composition: given the two pure densities, the alloy's density fixes the proportion of its parts by the mixture rule Archimedes' crown problem first posed — non-destructively, without cupel, cement, or fire. Al-Bīrūnī understood the method's sensitivities as a modern metrologist would: the purity of his reference samples, the temperature and purity of his water, the fineness of his balance, the exclusion of bubbles and of the meniscus's tricks — the treatise's care on these points is the care of a man who knew his third decimal place was only as honest as his procedure.

The Kitāb al-Jamāhir identifies its gems by hardness, cleavage, and colour — and by the balance, the one identifier a polish cannot flatter. Its gem densities sit in the same tables as its metal densities: a lapidary that weighs is an assay manual in disguise.

XVI. From Fire Assay to Acid Parting: Why the True Date Matters

The two stories this article has told — the Arabic workshop's operations and shelf, and the testing tradition it carried — meet in a single Latin moment. Recall the assay system's standing gap (Section XIV): cupellation counts the precious metal in a sample only by destroying it, and cannot separate the gold from the silver in the bead it leaves. For that separation — parting — the whole pre-modern world possessed exactly one process, the salt cement: slow, hot, fuel-hungry, days at red heat in sealed pots, the silver recovered afterwards by smelting and cupellation. Every refiner who packed a cement pot knew what he wished for: a water that would do in a vessel, in hours, what the cement did in a furnace in days.

Aqua fortis is that water. A gold–silver alloy, granulated or filed to open its surface, set in aqua fortis and warmed, gives up its silver to the liquor — and its copper, and every baser partner — while the gold stands untouched, to be filtered, washed, and weighed: parting in the wet way, quantitative, bench-scale, and repeatable by any worker who can run Section IV's distillation and Section V's filtration. The Latin assay literature takes the process up from the pseudo-Geber horizon forward; the cement pot begins its long retirement; and the touchstone itself gains, in the centuries after, the acid drop of the modern kit — a drop of aqua fortis across the streaks, turning the Greek colour judgement into a survival race the fire-assay article was careful to call an anachronism for any Greek bench. That is why the acids' true date is a load-bearing fact and not a pedant's trophy. Date the acids to ninth-century Baghdad, and the entire subsequent history of testing becomes unintelligible: nine centuries of cement pots, packed and fired by men who supposedly possessed the water that would have emptied them; a hydrostatic assay raised to genius by al-Bīrūnī to solve, non-destructively, a problem his workshop's acids could have solved in an afternoon; a Latin literature of c. 1300 presenting, as hard-won novelty, preparations its supposed Arabic originals had owned for four hundred years. Date them where the documents date them, and every one of those absurdities dissolves: the cement's long reign, the balance's brilliance, and the Summa's pride in its waters all become, at once, exactly what they are — the record of a world that needed the acids, lacked them, worked magnificently without them, and then, in one Latin generation, got them.

WHAT THE ARABIC WORKSHOP GAVE

The operation-list as a teachable grammar; the classified shelf, nūshādir to tinkār; the sharp waters at their real, surface-chemical strength; the assay tradition practised at imperial scale; and, in al-Bīrūnī, hydrostatics raised to precision. Attested, 9th–11th centuries and after — the foundation on which the Latin centuries built.

WHAT THE LATIN WORKSHOP ADDED

One directed operation — the distillation of vitriol with purified saltpetre, and its kin — producing aqua fortis, aqua regia, oil of vitriol, and the spirit of salt: the strong mineral acids, with their behaviour signatures stated as tests. Attested, Latin pseudo-Geber corpus, late 13th–early 14th century — the addition that turned parting wet and remade the assay.

The workshop record, graded whole, is not a story of a theft corrected but of a relay honestly run. Egypt and Greece built the still, the stone, the cupel, and the cement. The Arabic world systematised the operations, stocked the shelf from a Eurasian trade, classified the substances, ran the mints, and weighed with al-Bīrūnī's balance. The Latin world, standing on all of it — through the translations this division's Latin Door describes — added the acids, under a borrowed name that hid the addition for seven centuries. This article has unborrowed the name. What remains is not less than the legend promised: it is more, and it is true — a thousand years of workshops, each adding what the documents show it adding, and the sharp waters flowing at last from the still they always required, in the century, the language, and the corpus where a reader can still watch them being made.
