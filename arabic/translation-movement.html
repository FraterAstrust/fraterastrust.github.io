Baghdad · The Abbasid Court

// GREEK INTO SYRIAC, SYRIAC INTO ARABIC, ARABIC INTO A SCIENCE //

Between about 750 and 1000 CE, Baghdad rendered the Greek scientific inheritance — medicine, philosophy, astronomy, mathematics, and with them the alchemical corpus — into Arabic, on a scale and with a philological discipline no earlier translation movement matches. This article is about how that was done, who paid for it, who did the work, and what the famous House of Wisdom actually was.

I. A Movement, Not a Whim: The Phenomenon

Sometime around the middle of the eighth century CE, scholars working in and around Baghdad began, systematically and at someone else's expense, to translate Greek books into Arabic. They did not stop for roughly two hundred and fifty years. By the time the effort wound down, around the year 1000, the Arabic language possessed — in translation — the greater part of the Greek scientific and philosophical inheritance: Galen's medicine nearly entire, Hippocrates with his commentators, Aristotle nearly whole, Euclid, Ptolemy's Almagest, Archimedes and Apollonius in part, Dioscorides on drugs, Plato in part and often at one remove, and, travelling on the same roads and sometimes in the same baggage, the technical Hermetica and the Greek alchemical corpus. Nothing on this scale had been attempted before. The Roman world, which ruled Greek speakers for centuries, had translated remarkably little Greek science into Latin. The Syriac churches had translated a great deal, but for their own schools and within a theological frame. Baghdad's movement was different in kind: it was a sustained, funded, socially broad campaign to move an entire foreign literature of knowledge into a language that, at the campaign's opening, did not yet possess the vocabulary to receive it.

The framing this article follows — and states at the front, because the older framing still dominates popular accounts — is Dimitri Gutas's, in Greek Thought, Arabic Culture (1998). On the older telling, the movement is a caliph's whim enlarged: al-Maʾmūn dreams of Aristotle, founds a House of Wisdom, and Greek wisdom pours into Arabic at his command. Gutas dismantled that picture piece by piece. The movement, he showed, was a social movement: it began before al-Maʾmūn, continued after him, was funded by caliphs but also by viziers, generals, court physicians, merchants, and scholars themselves, and it drew its energy from the ideology of the early Abbasid state — a new empire, ruling from a new capital, presenting itself as the heir and restorer of the sciences of the peoples it had succeeded, Persian and Greek alike, against its Byzantine rival, which possessed the Greek books and, in Baghdad's polemical view, was letting them rot. Translation was cultural policy, social ambition, professional strategy, and genuine intellectual hunger at once, distributed across hundreds of patrons and practitioners over ten generations. A movement of that shape cannot be switched on by one man's dream, and it was not switched off by one man's death.

THE DATES

c. 750–1000 CE. Isolated translation under the Umayyads and the first Abbasids; organised caliphal patronage from the reign of al-Manṣūr (r. 754–775); the great age of the Ḥunayn circle in the ninth century; translation continuing, in changed forms, through the tenth.

THE SCALE

Hundreds of works, by the catalogues' count: the medical corpus of Galen and Hippocrates, the Aristotelian school corpus, the mathematical and astronomical canon, and the technical and occult shelf beside them (Sections XII–XIII).

THE DISCIPLINE

Collation of Greek manuscripts, revision of earlier versions, team translation, and the deliberate creation of an Arabic technical vocabulary — a philology, not a copying exercise (Sections VII–IX).

THE CONSEQUENCE

Arabic becomes a language of original science. By c. 900 the heirs of the translators are no longer only translating Greek knowledge; they are extending, correcting, and superseding it (Section XV).

One framing caution, because it governs everything that follows. The translation movement was not a rescue operation mounted for posterity's benefit, and the translators were not librarians of a burning world. They were working scholars in a competitive court economy, translating the books their patrons wanted, for uses their patrons understood: medicine for the caliph's body and the court's prestige, astronomy and astrology for the calendar, the horoscope, and the empire's ideological astronomy, mathematics for administration, inheritance law, and the exact sciences' own sake, philosophy for the theological and intellectual contests of a capital arguing about reason and revelation. Alchemy travelled inside that economy — never at its centre, as the Greco-Roman Transmission article already established, but persistently at its working edge, carried by the same translators, the same patrons, and the same Syriac bridge as Galen and Ptolemy. To understand how the alchemical corpus reached Arabic, there is no shortcut around understanding how everything else did. That is the work of the sections that follow.

II. Before Baghdad: The Syriac Bridge and the Roads In

Baghdad did not invent Greek translation. It inherited a going concern, some three centuries old, staffed by the Syriac-speaking Christians of Mesopotamia and Syria — and the first precondition of the Arabic movement is that the concern existed. The archive's full treatment of that inheritance is the companion article Harran and Gundeshapur in this division, which follows the Syriac schools, the Harranian Sabians, and the medical tradition of Gundeshapur in detail. This section states the bridge at the strength the present article needs, and no stronger.

Syriac — the literary Aramaic of the Christian East — had been receiving Greek texts since the fifth century: first theology, then, decisively, medicine, logic, and natural philosophy. The founding figure of the scientific line is Sergius of Reshaina (died 536), an Alexandria-trained physician-priest who rendered some thirty works of Galen into Syriac, translated Aristotle's Categories and Porphyry's Introduction, and wrote the first Aristotelian commentary in the language. After him the practice never lapsed. The West Syrian (Jacobite) and East Syrian (Nestorian) schools — Edessa, Nisibis, Reshaina, the monasteries of northern Mesopotamia — taught a curriculum in which Greek logic and Galenic medicine, in Syriac dress, were the higher studies; the translators of the seventh and eighth centuries, Athanasius of Balad and Jacob of Edessa among them, revised the older versions toward ever closer fidelity to the Greek. By the time the Abbasids founded Baghdad in 762, there existed, within a few days' journey of the new capital, a bilingual scholarly class that had been translating exactly the books Baghdad would want, into exactly the intermediate language Baghdad would start from, for two hundred years.

Gundeshapur and Harran: Two Nodes of the Bridge

Two places name the bridge's ends. Gundeshapur, in Khuzistan in south-western Persia, was a Sasanian royal foundation with a famous medical tradition: a hospital and teaching centre, staffed heavily by East Syrian Christians, where Greek medicine in Syriac transmission met Persian and Indian practice. Its great family, the Bakhtīshūʿ, supplied court physicians to the early Abbasid caliphs — Jibrīl ibn Bakhtīshūʿ was summoned to Baghdad by Hārūn al-Rashīd — and thereby carried the Syriac medical tradition physically into the caliphal palace, where it became one of the translation movement's engines: a court physician who reads his Galen in Syriac is a standing argument for Galen in Arabic. Harran, in the north, was the bridge's other kind of node: a pagan Hellenophone city — its Sabians, as the Arabic sources call them, kept a Greek astral religion and a Greek philosophical curriculum alive under Islamic rule — and the home of Thābit ibn Qurra (836–901), the movement's greatest mathematical translator, whose family carried the Harranian line into the tenth century (Section X). The companion article Harran and Gundeshapur gives both nodes their full dossiers, including the cautions: Gundeshapur's later legend of a full-blown university is, like the Bayt al-Ḥikma's, an inflation the sources do not support, and it is filed there at its real strength.

// THE BRIDGE, STATED AT CATALOGUE STRENGTH //

The Syriac bridge is documented for medicine, logic, and philosophy by surviving translations and by Ḥunayn's own catalogue of his Syriac predecessors. For alchemy specifically, the Syriac evidence is narrower — a documented body of Syriac alchemical treatises, Zosimos among them, in late manuscripts — and this archive states it at that strength in the Greco-Roman Transmission article (§X) and the Egyptology division's Zosimos dossier. Nothing in the present article requires the alchemical bridge to have been wider than the evidence shows. The movement's general machinery — Syriac first, Arabic second — is what alchemy demonstrably travelled on; the size of its own baggage is a separate question, answered elsewhere in the archive and not enlarged here.

There were other roads in, and a complete account names them so that the Syriac bridge is not mistaken for the whole map. Persian material — astronomical tables, astrological doctrine, courtly and administrative literature — entered Arabic from Middle Persian, sometimes through the same court circles; the early Abbasid translation effort under al-Manṣūr drew heavily on Persian as well as Greek exemplars, and the caliph's astrologers, Nawbakht and Māshāʾallāh among them, worked in that Persianate tradition. Indian astronomy and medicine arrived too, famously in the Sindhind tradition of Brahmagupta's astronomy, rendered into Arabic in the 770s. And Greek books themselves were physically obtainable: the Abbasid world bordered Byzantium along a frontier that was crossed by embassies, ransomed prisoners, merchants, and — Section IV's subject — deliberate manuscript-buying missions. But for the Greek scientific corpus proper, the road that mattered ran through Syriac. It ran through Syriac because the men who could walk it were already there.

III. Paper: The Material Precondition

A translation movement is, among other things, a manufacturing problem. Hundreds of long technical books had to be copied, collated, corrected, re-copied, and shelved — and the writing material of the eighth-century world was equal to that problem only in one place. Parchment, the Mediterranean standard, is prepared animal skin: a single large codex consumes a flock, and its cost sets a hard ceiling on how many books a society can afford to make. Papyrus, the Egyptian standard, is cheap but brittle, disliked for codices, and a regional monopoly. Paper — rag paper, made from macerated linen and hemp fibre, sized and burnished — broke the ceiling. It was cheap enough to copy on speculatively, durable enough for a working library, and manufacturable anywhere water and rags were available. The translation movement and the paper economy grew up together in Baghdad, and neither is fully intelligible without the other.

The traditional account of paper's arrival runs through Central Asia. Papermaking was a Chinese technology, centuries old by the time it moved west; the Arabic sources connect its transmission to the Muslim victory at Talas in 751, after which Chinese papermakers — prisoners, in the usual telling — are said to have established the craft at Samarkand, whose paper became a famous export. The modern caution belongs in the text, not the margin: paper was known in Central Asia before Talas, and the prisoner story has the shape of a foundation anecdote; what is not in dispute is the sequence that matters. Samarkand paper circulated in the eastern Islamic lands in the later eighth century, and in 794–95 a paper mill was established at Baghdad itself, an initiative the sources associate with the Barmakid vizier al-Faḍl ibn Yaḥyā. Within a generation the Abbasid administration was shifting its own paperwork onto paper; within two, Baghdad had a booksellers' and copyists' quarter — the market of the warrāqūn, the stationer-copyists — whose trade is the physical substrate of everything the Fihrist catalogues.

WHAT PAPER MADE CHEAP

Drafts and working copies. Collation — the movement's core method (Section VIII) — requires several copies of the same work open at once, corrected against each other and discarded or re-copied as the text improves. On parchment that workflow is a luxury. On paper it is a routine.

WHAT PAPER MADE POSSIBLE

A book market. Private patrons (Section IV) could commission translations because the finished product — a paper codex — cost a fraction of its parchment equivalent; scholars could own working libraries; a bookseller like Ibn al-Nadīm could spend a lifetime cataloguing a literature that physically existed in thousands of copies.

The point deserves one more turn, because it is easy to file paper as background colour. It is not background; it is a load-bearing precondition, and the comparison proves it. Byzantium possessed the Greek books, the Greek language, and a continuous scholarly tradition — and produced, in these same centuries, a respectable but modest copying culture, parchment-bound and court-centred. Baghdad possessed fewer Greek books and no native Greek scholarship at all — and out-copied, out-translated, and within two centuries out-composed the empire that owned the originals. The difference was not talent. It was demand organised as patronage (Section IV) multiplied by a writing material cheap enough to meet the demand. When the Latin West's turn came in the twelfth century, it too would wait on its material base: the Toledo translations circulated first on parchment, in a thin stream, and flooded only as paper mills spread through Christian Spain and Italy. Translation movements run on paper the way armies run on roads.

// A DATING CAUTION //

The Baghdad mill's date is given as 794–95 in the standard literature, on the Arabic sources' association of the foundation with al-Faḍl ibn Yaḥyā's governorship and the Barmakid ascendancy. Like the Talas story, it is a reported date, not a document-dated one, and this article gives it as the tradition's date. The archaeology of early Islamic paper — surviving dated documents and codices — supports the broader claim beyond serious dispute: paper was in administrative and book use in Iraq by the early ninth century.

Paper also changed what a translation was. A parchment culture copies monuments; a paper culture copies working texts, and expects to copy them again. The Arabic versions of Galen and Aristotle were not made once and frozen: they were made, revised, re-made by a second translator, epitomised, commented, and re-copied in each form — the version-history that Section XI traces for whole corpora. That restless textual economy, in which no version is final because another copy is always affordable, is the movement's native element. It is also, not incidentally, the economy in which the Arabic alchemical corpus would grow: the Jābir corpus's hundreds of treatises, endlessly re-collected and re-attributed, are a paper phenomenon as surely as the Galen translations are.

IV. Patronage: Caliphs, Viziers, Physicians, and Private Money

Translation is skilled labour, and skilled labour is paid. The second precondition of the movement — after the Syriac bridge and beside the paper — is a patronage market deep and various enough to employ translators for ten generations. Gutas's social-movement framing (Section I) is, in large part, a claim about this market: that it was never a single caliphal budget line, but a whole economy of buyers, each purchasing translation for their own reasons. This section walks the market's tiers.

The Caliphs

Caliphal patronage opens the story, on the standard account, under al-Manṣūr (r. 754–775), the capital's founder, whose court imported Persian and Indian astronomy and the astrologers to work it, and under whom the first state-sponsored renderings of Greek material — much of it arriving through Persian and Syriac channels — were made. Under Hārūn al-Rashīd (r. 786–809) the court's medical establishment, headed by the Bakhtīshūʿ physicians from Gundeshapur, deepened the demand for Galen. Under al-Maʾmūn (r. 813–833) caliphal patronage reached its celebrated height: translation was folded into the caliph's broader ideological programme — the promotion of rational inquiry, the contest with Byzantium for the Greek inheritance, embassies and missions after Greek manuscripts — and the Bayt al-Ḥikma of Section V belongs to his reign and his successors'. The famous story that al-Maʾmūn was set on this course by a dream in which Aristotle appeared to him and defined the good is preserved in the Arabic sources and repeated in the Fihrist; it is filed here as what it is — the court's own foundation story for a policy, evidence for how the policy wished to be remembered, not for how it began. The policy itself predates the dreamer and outlasts him: al-Mutawakkil (r. 847–861), no one's candidate for a philosopher-caliph, was Ḥunayn's patron and employer, and translation continued under caliphs who cared for it and under caliphs who merely paid the court physicians who did.

The Viziers: The Barmakids

Between caliph and translator stood the administrators, and the first great ministerial patrons were the Barmakids, the Persian vizieral family who ran the empire under Hārūn al-Rashīd until their fall in 803. Yaḥyā ibn Khālid al-Barmakī and his sons al-Faḍl and Jaʿfar sponsored translation from Persian and Indian sources, patronised the court's physicians and astrologers, and are credited by the tradition with the Baghdad paper mill of Section III — a fitting emblem of their role: the Barmakids built infrastructure. Their patronage also set the pattern that outlived them. In a court where advancement ran through the vizier's salon, sponsoring a translation was a legible act of magnificence and ideological alignment; every subsequent ministerial household — the Ṭāhirids, the Banū al-Furāt, the Buyid-era viziers of the tenth century — inherited the expectation that a great man's court contained translators the way it contained poets.

The Court Physicians

The market's most reliable buyers were its doctors. Medicine was the one Greek science whose utility no patron needed explained, and the great medical households — the Bakhtīshūʿ above all, Nestorian Christians from Gundeshapur who served as caliphal physicians across seven generations — were patrons, translators, and commissioners of translation in one. A court physician's standing depended on his Galen; his Galen depended on the Syriac versions he inherited and the Arabic versions he caused to be made; his rivals at court were other physicians whose Galen might be better. The translation of medicine therefore had what no other field quite matched: a professional guild with money, court access, and a permanent competitive motive to fund better versions. Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq's career (Section VI) was made inside this economy — he was a physician first, the caliph al-Mutawakkil's doctor, and his translation enterprise was the medical profession's own infrastructure project.

Private Patrons: The Banū Mūsā

The market's most instructive buyers were private. The Banū Mūsā — the brothers Muḥammad, Aḥmad, and al-Ḥasan, sons of Mūsā ibn Shākir — were ninth-century Baghdad's model scholarly patrons: themselves accomplished mathematicians and engineers (their Book of Ingenious Devices is a landmark of Arabic mechanics), wealthy at court, and systematic purchasers of translation. The Fihrist reports — and the report is given here as a report, at the strength of a catalogue's testimony — that the brothers spent on the order of five hundred dinars a month on translation, maintained translators including Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq, Ḥubaysh, and Thābit ibn Qurra on salary, and sent manuscript-buying missions into Byzantine territory to acquire Greek books their library lacked. Whether the figure is exact matters less than what its preservation shows: contemporaries understood translation as a salaried industry with running costs, foreign procurement, and competing employers, and they kept the accounts in round, boastful numbers because the spending itself was the prestige.

A single patron's death, disgrace, or change of taste — the Barmakids' fall in 803 is the type case — closed one purse and left the others open; translators moved between employers, taking their skills and their half-finished versions with them. The demand, moreover, was self-renewing: every translated book created readers who wanted the books it cited. The curriculum itself was a subscription that renewed by reading. What finally wound the movement down (Section XV) was not a failure of patronage but the demand's satisfaction — a different ending, with a different moral.

V. The Bayt al-Ḥikma at Its Real Evidentiary Strength

Every account of the translation movement must sooner or later walk through a famous door: the Bayt al-Ḥikma, the House of Wisdom at Baghdad. Popular accounts walk through it into a vast research institute — a university before universities, with departments, an observatory, a faculty of translators working in organised schools, and al-Maʾmūn presiding like a chancellor. This section walks through the same door at the speed the evidence permits. What stands on the other side is smaller, later-attested, and — stated plainly — more interesting: a caliphal library and translation bureau of a type the Islamic world inherited from Sasanian Persia, whose real history shows how the movement actually organised its centre, and whose modern inflation shows how easily a famous name becomes a container for everything a period is admired for.

What Is Attested

The attestations, assembled in Françoise Balty-Guesdon's study of the Bayt al-Ḥikma in the Arabic sources (Arabica 39, 1992) — the corrective investigation on which modern caution rests — are these. An institution called the Bayt al-Ḥikma, or described as the caliph's khizāna (treasury, storehouse) of books of wisdom, existed at the Abbasid court. It functioned as a library: a place where books — Persian books first, on the earlier evidence, and the Greek scientific translations as the movement produced them — were kept, copied, and consulted. Translation work was associated with it: it served as a translation bureau in the modest, accurate sense that translators worked under its auspices and its holdings supplied their exemplars. And it had named heads. The sources name Salm — a Persian courtier of al-Maʾmūn's reign, associated with translation from Persian and with the library's direction — and after him Sahl ibn Hārūn (died 830), a Persian secretary and man of letters, as the institution's director under al-Maʾmūn. An institution with a library, a bureau's function, and a named director in the sources is a real institution. That much, the record carries.

What Is Not Attested

The record does not carry the rest of the popular picture, and the corrective scholarship — Balty-Guesdon's source-study, and Gutas's treatment in Greek Thought, Arabic Culture, which places the Bayt al-Ḥikma in its Sasanian institutional lineage — is plain about the gap. No source describes lecture halls, a curriculum, a student body, degrees, or departments. No source makes the Bayt al-Ḥikma the movement's headquarters in the sense that the great translators worked there as its staff: Ḥunayn's enterprise (Sections VI–VIII) was his own, funded by the patrons of Section IV, and its association with the House is a later tidiness, not a documented employment. The observatory sometimes attached to the institution belongs to al-Maʾmūn's separate astronomical programme — the observations at Baghdad's Shammāsiyya quarter and on Mount Qāsiyūn at Damascus — which the sources treat as its own undertaking. And the very phrase's grandeur misleads: a bayt al-ḥikma is a house of the books of wisdom — a royal library on the Sasanian model, where the Persian kings had kept their dynastic and scientific archives — before it is anything else. The early Abbasid institution was, on the evidence, substantially a Persian-style court library that the Greek translation movement grew around and through.

// THE DISPUTE, STATED WITH POSITIONS NAMED //

The maximal picture — House of Wisdom as research institute and proto-university, coordinating the movement — is the picture of older popular and some older scholarly literature, built by aggregating everything al-Maʾmūn's court did with books under the institution's one famous name. The corrective picture (Balty-Guesdon 1992; Gutas 1998): the sources attest a caliphal library with translation activity and named directors — Salm, then Sahl ibn Hārūn — modelled on the Sasanian royal book-store; the institute picture is an inflation not supported by the sources. This article's position follows the corrective, and states the institution at the corrective's strength throughout. The movement does not need the inflation: a translation economy distributed across the whole patronage market of Section IV is a larger and truer marvel than any single building.

Why did the inflation happen? Three causes, each instructive. First, the name is magnificent, and magnificent names attract contents the way the name of Hermes attracted treatises. Second, later Arabic usage itself broadened the term — other courts and other centuries founded their own houses of wisdom, most famously the Fatimid Dār al-Ḥikma at Cairo (founded 1005), which did have something closer to a teaching function, and the Cairo institution's profile has been read back into Baghdad's. Third, modern admiration wanted an address. A social movement is hard to put on a postcard; a House can be drawn, modelled, and opened by a caliph in a single scene. The archive's rule applies to institutions as to texts: give the evidence at its real strength, and let the real thing be interesting enough. A palace library whose shelves filled, within two generations, with the translated science of three civilisations — Persian statecraft and astronomy, Indian mathematics, and the Greek corpus arriving volume by volume from the translators' workshops — whose director was a Persian man of letters and whose readers included the men remaking Arabic thought: that house needs no departments invented for it.

// THE DIRECTORS, AS NAMED //

Salm (fl. under al-Maʾmūn): courtier associated in the sources with the Bayt al-Ḥikma's direction and with translation from Persian; the Fihrist connects his name with versions of Aristotelian material as well. Sahl ibn Hārūn (died 830): Persian secretary, author of works on statecraft and the famous Book of the Tigress and the Fox, named as the House's director under al-Maʾmūn. The succession of two Persian secretaries at the head of the caliph's book-store is itself the Sasanian lineage made visible.

One last precision. To deflate the Bayt al-Ḥikma is not to deflate al-Maʾmūn's court, whose patronage of astronomy, geography, and translation was real and lavish — the world map commissioned under him, the meridian measurements, the embassies after Greek books. It is to locate that patronage where the sources locate it: in the court as a whole, its library among its instruments, its translators mostly employed elsewhere. The movement's centre of gravity was never a building. It was a labour market and a library network, and its greatest single enterprise was not the caliph's at all. It was a Christian physician's from al-Ḥīra, and it is time to meet him.

VI. Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq: The Man and the Circle

Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq (809–873) is the translation movement's centre of gravity: the most productive translator of the age, the methodologist who described the work from inside, and the head of a family workshop whose versions of Galen and Aristotle set the standard every later translator measured himself against. The biographical tradition on Ḥunayn is rich — he is a hero of the Arabic biographical dictionaries — and correspondingly embroidered; this section gives the documented outline, flags the embroidery where it is famous, and reserves the method for Sections VII–VIII, where Ḥunayn's own document carries the weight.

He was born in 809 at al-Ḥīra, the old Arab Christian city of southern Iraq, an East Syrian — Nestorian — Christian whose first languages were Arabic and Syriac and whose Greek had to be acquired as a deliberate professional act. The biographers tell the acquiring as a story: the young Ḥunayn, come to Baghdad to study medicine under the great physician Yūḥannā ibn Māsawayh, asks too many questions, is dismissed by his exasperated teacher — in the anecdote's sharpest form, with a contemptuous word about the money-changers' sons of al-Ḥīra — disappears for years, and returns with perfect Greek, reciting Homer to his astonished former master. The story is an anecdote, shaped like a parable of the translator's vocation, and it is filed here as one. What stands behind it is plausible and sufficient: Ḥunayn spent years acquiring Greek to a standard no contemporary matched — the biographical tradition sends him to the lands of the Byzantines, and his own Risāla shows him travelling in search of Greek manuscripts through Mesopotamia, Syria, Palestine, and Egypt — and he built his career on the conjunction nobody else could offer: a physician's training, a Syriac schoolman's formation, and a Hellenist's Greek.

The career that followed made him the age's indispensable man. He served as physician to the caliph al-Mutawakkil; he survived the court intrigues that destroyed lesser favourites — the biographical dictionaries relish his refusal, at mortal risk, to compound a poison for the caliph, and his imprisonment and restoration — and he died in 873, by the tradition's account the most honoured translator of his century. The honours were also financial, and here the archive's discipline is needed again: the dictionaries report that Ḥunayn was paid the weight of his translated books in gold by his patrons — that the finished codices were laid in one pan of a balance and gold in the other. The story is famous, picturesque, and flagged here, plainly, as anecdote: it belongs to the biographical tradition's portrait of a translator valued beyond measure, it is not corroborated by Ḥunayn's own writings, and no argument in this article rests on it. What the Risāla does document — fees, commissions, named patrons, a working enterprise — is impressive without the balance.

The Circle: A Family Workshop

Ḥunayn did not work alone, and the movement's mature form is his workshop's organisation. Three names define it. Isḥāq ibn Ḥunayn (died 910 or 911), the son, was a translator of the first rank in his own right — his Arabic versions, many made directly from Greek or from his father's Syriac, carried above all the philosophical corpus: Aristotle's Categories, Physics, On Generation and Corruption, On the Soul in part, and the mathematical tradition including the standard revision of Euclid. Ḥubaysh ibn al-Ḥasan, the nephew, was the workshop's Arabic arm: many of the Galen versions that circulate under Ḥunayn's name were completed in Arabic by Ḥubaysh, working from his uncle's Syriac — a division of labour the Risāla itself records, and a standing warning against reading the name on a version as a single pair of hands. Around the family stood associates and pupils — ʿĪsā ibn Yaḥyā, Isṭafan ibn Bāsīl and others — revising, copying, and carrying commissions of their own. The circle's output, across two generations, is the ninth century's central scholarly fact: a hereditary, multi-confessional, quality-controlled translation house, organised like the great medical families it resembled, and like them turning a craft into a dynasty.

What the circle changed can be stated in one sentence, and Sections VII–IX unpack it: before Ḥunayn, Arabic translation was largely the work of individuals rendering what they had, as they could; after him, it was a method — collated exemplars, revised predecessors, divided labour, created terminology — that could be taught, commissioned, and judged. The method's inventor left its description in his own hand. It is the movement's single most valuable document, and the next section reads it.

VII. The Risāla: The Movement Describes Its Own Method

Movements rarely write their own manuals. This one did. Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq's Risāla — his Letter to ʿAlī ibn Yaḥyā, a patron, on the books of Galen that had been translated and those that had not — is the translation movement's methodological charter, written from inside the workshop by its master. Composed first in the 850s and updated by its author toward the end of his life, the Risāla works through the Galenic corpus title by title — 129 works in the standard count — and for each records, with a bibliographer's dry precision, what existed in Syriac and by whom, what he himself had translated, revised, or re-translated, for which patron, at what age, and from what quality of Greek exemplar. Gotthelf Bergsträsser's edition of the Arabic text (1925) and his study of the new material (1932), with Gotthard Strohmaier's subsequent work on the Ḥunayn tradition, made the Risāla the foundation document of modern scholarship on the movement — the text every account in this article's Sources ultimately leans on.

Read as a method, the Risāla teaches four lessons, and each is a rebuke to the picture of translation as inspired dictation. First: the predecessors are audited, not worshipped. Ḥunayn assesses the old Syriac Galen — Sergius of Reshaina's versions above all — with professional candour, in paraphrase: Sergius translated as well as the Syriac of his day allowed, but his Greek was limited and his versions want revision; later hands, Job of Edessa and Ibn Māsawayh among them, are graded as plainly. The movement knew its own version-history and kept score. Second: the exemplar is everything. Entry after entry turns on the Greek manuscript available — complete or defective, single or collated — and Ḥunayn records travelling for better copies and postponing works for which no sound Greek text could be found. Third: translation is staged. Greek into Syriac first, in the workshop's practice, then Syriac into Arabic — frequently by different hands (Section VIII) — with the Syriac stage serving as the philological control. Fourth: the work is commissioned and accounted. Patrons are named, versions are dated by the translator's age — a youthful version confessed and later replaced by a mature one — and the whole letter reads, beneath its courtesies, as what it also is: a master craftsman's report to a paying client on work done, work in progress, and work honestly declared impossible for want of a Greek text.

// HOW TO READ THE RISĀLA //

Ask of each entry the questions Ḥunayn asks: Does a Greek text survive to the translator, and in what state? Who translated it before, and how well? Into which language, for which patron, and has the author since revised his own work? The answers locate every version in the movement's economy — and they are the questions Section XVI will ask, in turn, of the Risāla itself, which is also a document with a patron, a purpose, and a case to make.

The Risāla's famous number deserves its own sentence. The 129 Galenic works it catalogues are not 129 translations by Ḥunayn's hand: they are the works of Galen known to him, across which his circle's labour — new Syriac versions, Arabic versions, revisions of Sergius and of his own youth, works sought and not found, works found too late or in too damaged a state — is distributed entry by entry. Popular accounts that credit Ḥunayn personally with translating 129 books of Galen flatten the document they cite. What the Risāla actually claims is more credible and more impressive: that one workshop, over one lifetime, took systematic responsibility for the entire Galenic inheritance in two languages — and left the audit trail.

The document's limits are the obverse of its virtues, and Section XVI states them in full; two belong here, at the point of use. The Risāla is Ḥunayn's account of Ḥunayn: a letter to a patron, written in a competitive market, by a man whose rivals — the older translators he grades so coolly, the court physicians who intrigued against him — do not get to reply. And it covers Galen: the movement's other shelves must be reconstructed from other witnesses. Neither limit diminishes it. A self-interested audit is still an audit, and its entries can be checked — against surviving versions, against the Fihrist, against the manuscripts — as modern scholarship has spent a century doing. The movement that produced the Risāla produced, in it, the instrument of its own verification.

VIII. How Translation Worked: Collation, Revision, and the Team

This section assembles the workshop practice that the Risāla documents entry by entry and that the surviving versions themselves display. It has four operations. Together they amount to a philology — a disciplined technology for moving a text across languages without, so far as human care could manage it, losing or inventing content on the way.

1. Collation of Greek Manuscripts

The mature method began before translation, with the Greek book itself. Where several Greek copies of a work could be had, they were compared — collated — and a corrected exemplar established, because a translator who works from a corrupt copy manufactures a corrupt version at industrial scale. Ḥunayn's Risāla records the practice as routine and its absence as a reason to wait: works were deferred, sometimes for years, until a sound Greek text arrived — by purchase, by a patron's mission to Byzantium, by the translator's own travels. The Greek exemplars behind the great Arabic versions were, in consequence, often better than the Greek manuscripts that happen to survive today, some of which descend from later, thinner copies. That is why the Arabic versions remain, to this day, witnesses that editors of the Greek text must cite: a ninth-century Arabic translation can preserve the reading of a Greek codex older and sounder than any Greek codex now on a shelf — the surplus phenomenon the Greco-Roman Transmission article documented for the alchemical corpus, operating here across the whole scientific shelf.

2. Revision of Earlier Versions

Almost nothing was translated once. The movement's characteristic product is the revision: an existing Syriac version — Sergius's Galen, say — corrected against the Greek by a later hand; an existing Arabic version — one of the early, literal renderings of the eighth century — re-worked or replaced by the Ḥunayn circle's freer, sense-for-sense standard. Version succeeded version the way editions succeed editions, and the catalogues preserve the stratigraphy: for Euclid's Elements the tradition knows the version of al-Ḥajjāj ibn Yūsuf ibn Maṭar, then Isḥāq ibn Ḥunayn's, then Thābit ibn Qurra's revision of Isḥāq's; for the Almagest, an old version, al-Ḥajjāj's, and the Isḥāq–Thābit line again. A reader of Arabic science in the year 950 stood where a reader of a much-edited classic stands today: before a text with a documented history of improvement, choosing — as the commentators who cite their versions by translator's name show they did — among named editions of differing authority.

3. The Two Languages, Staged

The Ḥunayn workshop's standard route ran Greek into Syriac, then Syriac into Arabic. The Syriac stage was not a detour but a control: Syriac possessed, from two centuries of school use, a settled technical vocabulary calqued on Greek — a ready-made terminological bridge — and Ḥunayn's Syriac versions fixed the sense with a philologist's care before the Arabic stage clothed it for its readers. Section XI traces how the balance between this route and direct Greek-to-Arabic work shifted across the movement's life; here the point is the staging itself, which made each translation two acts of scholarship, separately checkable.

4. The Team

The labour was divided, and the Risāla names the divisions without embarrassment: Ḥunayn translates into Syriac; Ḥubaysh renders the Syriac into Arabic; Isḥāq works in both directions and revises; pupils copy, collate, and draft. A “Ḥunayn version” is therefore a workshop product under a master's quality control — closer to a modern translation institute's output than to a solitary scholar's — and its authority rests on the process, not on a legend of one man's bilingual genius. The genius was real. It was organisational as much as linguistic: Ḥunayn built the machine that made Ḥunayn versions.

Philology of this order is not a Greek or an Arabic virtue. It is what happens when patrons pay for accuracy, professionals compete on it, and paper makes the drafts affordable. Baghdad, for two centuries, had all three.

IX. The Arabic Scientific Lexicon: Words Had to Be Made

Every translator of the movement faced a problem no patron's money could dissolve: Arabic did not yet have the words. The language of the Qurʾān, of poetry, law, and administration possessed no settled terms for Galen's faculties, Aristotle's categories, Euclid's constructions, or Ptolemy's circles — because the things themselves had never been discussed in Arabic. The movement therefore had to do something more fundamental than translate books. It had to build, inside Arabic, the technical lexicon that would make Arabic a language in which science could be thought. That construction — largely complete by the year 900 — is arguably the movement's most permanent achievement: the books could be superseded, and were; the words remained, and a millennium of Arabic, Persian, and Turkish science was written in them. Traces reached Latin too: the Greco-Roman Transmission article noted that Latin alchimia is an Arabic loan; the traffic in terms ran, for centuries, overwhelmingly in that direction.

The lexicon was built by three techniques, all visible in the surviving versions. Transliteration carried a Greek term across as an Arabic-shaped foreign word — the route of falsafa itself, philosophy wearing its Greek name (philosophia) in Arabic dress, and of a host of drug, disease, and instrument names where Dioscorides' Greek plant-names, having no Arabic referents, simply emigrated. Calque — loan-translation — built an Arabic compound mirroring the Greek term's structure, as the Syriac versions had done before: the bridge language's solutions were inherited wholesale wherever they served, which is one more reason the Syriac stage of Section VIII mattered. And semantic extension conscripted ordinary Arabic words into technical service: jawhar, a jewel or essence, becomes substance; ʿaraḍ becomes the logical accident; qiyās, a measuring, becomes the syllogism; mizāj, a mixing, becomes the temperament — Galen's krasis in Arabic, and with it the whole humoral medicine of the following centuries. Each choice was a small act of theory: to name the Greek thing with this Arabic word was to decide what the Greek thing was.

THE OLD VERSIONS: WORD FOR WORD

The movement's earliest stratum — the versions associated with Yaḥyā ibn al-Biṭrīq and his generation (early 9th c.) — translated literally, Greek syntax and all, producing Arabic that later readers found crabbed to the point of obscurity. The old versions are historically precious and were, already in the ninth century, being replaced.

THE ḤUNAYN STANDARD: SENSE FOR SENSE

The circle's mature practice rendered the meaning in idiomatic Arabic, sentence by sentence — the standard the Risāla embodies and later translators cite. Fidelity migrated from the word to the sense, and Arabic science got a prose it could actually think in.

The shift between those two cards is the lexicon's biography in miniature. A literal version can borrow its terminology word by word, at the price of unreadability; a sense-for-sense version must possess its terminology — must know, before the sentence begins, what Arabic says for faculty, temperament, element, and proof. The Ḥunayn standard was therefore possible only because the lexicon had been substantially built, and the standard's triumph completed the building: once the great versions circulated, their usage was the terminology, fixed by the authority of the texts every student read. Terminology creation of this order is collective and cumulative — Sergius's Syriac calques, the old versions' experiments, the circle's settlements, the commentators' refinements — and no single translator, Ḥunayn included, should be credited with it entire. What can be credited to the circle is the standard that let the accumulation converge.

Alchemy's vocabulary travelled the same three roads, with results the Jābir corpus would systematise: Greek substance-names transliterated and naturalised, workshop terms calqued through Syriac, and ordinary words — spirit (rūḥ), body (jasad), soul (nafs) — extended into the technical triad in which Arabic alchemy describes what rises from a body under fire and what remains. The companion article on the Jābir Corpus treats that vocabulary at work. The point here is the precondition: when the Jābir authors sat down, around 850–950, to compose an original Arabic science of the art, the language on their desk already contained — courtesy of two generations of translators — the words for everything they needed to say. Original science is written in a lexicon somebody else built. In Arabic's case, the builders' names are known, and the next section musters them.

X. Who the Translators Were: A Multi-Confessional Economy

Ask who translated Greek science into Arabic and the honest answer is a census, not a name. The translators were, in their great majority, Syriac Christians — East Syrian (Nestorian) and West Syrian, with Melkite Christians of the Byzantine-rite Arabic-speaking churches beside them — because the bridge of Section II was theirs: they held the Greek, the Syriac, and increasingly the Arabic, and their schools had kept the curriculum. Beside them worked the Sabians of Harran, pagans by the old Greek religion, whose mathematical-astronomical line runs from Thābit ibn Qurra through his descendants. A smaller number were Persians, heirs of the Sasanian translation culture, working from Persian as well as Greek; and as the ninth century advanced, Muslim scholars entered the ranks in growing numbers — first as patrons and students, then as translators and revisers in their own right. The movement was, in the accurate modern phrase, a multi-confessional scholarly economy: a labour market in which the caliph's money bought a Nestorian's Galen, a Sabian's Archimedes, and a Melkite's Diophantus, and in which confessional identity predicted a translator's languages and schooling far better than it predicted his employment.

Translation at this level required a rare triple competence — scholarly Greek, a school language, and literary Arabic — and in the eighth and ninth centuries that competence was concentrated, by the accidents of educational history, in the Christian schools and the Harranian tradition. The Abbasid state's role was not to create the competence but to price it: to make a bilingual schoolman's skill worth a court salary, a vizier's pension, a private patron's retainer. Confession, in this economy, functioned as a guild marker rather than a barrier — the sources record friction, competition, and the occasional intrigue (Ḥunayn's troubles under al-Mutawakkil had confessional threads), but the market cleared across the lines for two centuries, because every party to it needed what the others had. The caliphs needed the science; the translators needed the salaries; the physicians needed the Galen; and Arabic needed the words.

It is worth pausing on the Harranian line, because it carries this hub's particular freight. Thābit ibn Qurra was recruited — the biographical tradition tells it as a chance meeting with Muḥammad ibn Mūsā, eldest of the Banū Mūsā, who recognised the young money-changer's brilliance on the road from Harran — into the brothers' salaried circle, and became the age's second great translation enterprise: the mathematical shelf as Ḥunayn's was the medical. His descendants translated and composed into the later tenth century. The Sabians' Greek was not a school survival only: Harran kept Greek as a learned and liturgical inheritance of its own, and its scholars brought to Baghdad a directness of access to Greek mathematical texts that the Syriac bridge, strong in medicine and logic, did not always match. The companion article Harran and Gundeshapur gives the Sabian dossier in full, including the cautions on Harran's later legend. For the movement's census, the point stands: its second city of translators was a pagan enclave, and nobody at the caliph's court found that a reason not to buy its Archimedes.

// A NOTE ON “NESTORIAN” //

The Arabic sources and the older scholarship call Ḥunayn's church Nestorian; its own name is the Church of the East, and its scholars' Syriac is the East Syrian tradition. This article uses East Syrian (Nestorian) at first mention and East Syrian thereafter. The label matters only as a schooling marker: the Church of the East's schools, from Nisibis onward, are the institutions that kept Greek medicine and logic in Syriac dress — the bridge's piers were schoolrooms.

By the movement's later decades the census was changing, and the change is the ending's first sign (Section XV). Muslim scholars formed in the translated curriculum — al-Khwārizmī in mathematics, the Banū Mūsā themselves in mechanics, al-Kindī commissioning and revising philosophy — no longer needed the bridge generation to read Greek science for them, because the science now existed in their own language, in versions their own teachers had made. The translators had translated themselves out of indispensability. It is the most honourable obsolescence in the history of scholarship, and the movement's structure — Christians and Sabians building the lexicon and the library in which Muslim, Christian, and Jewish scholars of the next centuries would all work — is its monument.

XI. The Two Roads: Greek into Syriac into Arabic, and Direct

The movement's texts travelled by two roads, and the balance between them shifted across its life. The older road — the bridge road — ran Greek into Syriac, then Syriac into Arabic. The newer ran directly from Greek into Arabic. Getting the roads and their chronology right matters beyond pedantry: a version's road is part of its evidence, since each staging is a filter through which the Greek had to pass, and the archive's judgements elsewhere — on the Arabic Zosimos, on the Jābir corpus's Greek debts — depend on knowing which road a given text could have travelled, and when.

The Bridge Road, and Why It Led

In the movement's first generations the bridge road was not a preference but a necessity. The translators available held Greek through Syriac: their schooling was Syriac, their terminological resources were the Syriac versions' calques, and for the medical corpus a Syriac Galen already existed — Sergius's and its revisions — waiting to be carried the second step. The Ḥunayn workshop institutionalised the road (Section VIII): the master's Syriac as the controlled intermediate, the nephew's Arabic as the published form. Nor was the road a mark of second-rateness. A staged translation through a terminologically settled intermediate could be more accurate than a direct leap into a language still inventing its terms — the Syriac stage was where the philology happened, and the Arabic stage inherited its results. The Risāla's entries, which track Syriac and Arabic versions as separate assets with separate histories, show a movement that understood its intermediate texts as capital, not scaffolding.

The Direct Road, and Its Growth

Direct Greek-to-Arabic translation existed from early — al-Ḥajjāj's Euclid and Almagest versions were made in the movement's first decades, and the Harranian line, Thābit above all, worked from Greek exemplars with a directness the medical tradition took longer to reach — and it grew as the century turned. Isḥāq ibn Ḥunayn's Aristotelian versions mark the maturity: a translator whose Arabic lexicon was now built (Section IX) could face the Greek without the Syriac crib, and his versions' authority rested on exactly that directness. By the tenth century the balance had inverted in the philosophical tradition: the Baghdad Aristotelians — al-Fārābī's heirs, Yaḥyā ibn ʿAdī's circle — worked in an Arabic philosophical language of their own, translating the remaining logical corpus from Syriac where Syriac versions existed and commenting in Arabic as a first language of philosophy. The bridge had been crossed so often that the far bank had become a country.

WHAT THE BRIDGE ROAD PRESERVES

Terminological discipline: two centuries of Syriac calques standing between the Greek term and the Arabic one. What it risks: double filtration — a Syriac misreading carried faithfully into Arabic, invisible to any reader who cannot check the Greek.

WHAT THE DIRECT ROAD PRESERVES

The Greek itself, at one remove only. What it requires: an Arabic technical lexicon already built — which is why the direct road's great age comes after the bridge road has done its work, and not before.

For alchemy, the roads question has a specific edge, and the archive states it consistently across its divisions. Greek alchemy reached the Islamic world in the eighth- to ninth-century translation movement largely via Syriac — the formulation of the Egyptology division, adopted in the Greco-Roman Transmission article (§XI) and repeated here. The Syriac alchemical manuscripts are late and narrow (Section II's caution), but the route they document is the route the whole corpus's machinery supplied; and some Arabic alchemical material demonstrably rests on Greek exemplars of a fullness the surviving Greek codices do not match, which is the surplus phenomenon again — evidence that the translators' Greek books were, in places, better than ours. What the roads cannot tell us, and what no responsible account claims they do, is the precise itinerary of every alchemical text: for much of the Arabic occult shelf (Section XIII) the choice between translation proper and Arabic composition on Greek models remains, in the scholarship's own verdict, undeterminable — and is stated as undeterminable wherever this hub meets it.

XII. What Got Translated I: Medicine, Philosophy, and the Exact Sciences

The movement's selectivity is its signature, and the next three sections take the inventory in three passes: what was translated in bulk, what was translated at the shelf's occult edge, and — Section XIV — what was not translated at all. The bulk pass can be organised by the patrons' demands of Section IV, because the inventory follows the money with embarrassing fidelity.

Medicine: The Near-Complete Corpus

Medicine came first, came biggest, and came nearest to completeness. The Galenic corpus — the Risāla's 129 works — was rendered into Syriac and Arabic across the Ḥunayn circle's lifetime, new versions and revisions together, until Arabic possessed Galen more completely than any other language of the age, including, in places, Greek itself: a number of Galenic works survive whole only in Arabic, their Greek lost or fragmentary. Hippocrates travelled with him — the Aphorisms, Prognostics, On Airs Waters Places, the surgical and regimen works, characteristically accompanied by Galen's commentaries, so that the Arabic Hippocrates is a commented school text from the start. Dioscorides' On Medical Material — the pharmacopoeia of the ancient world — was translated by Isṭafan ibn Bāsīl and revised by Ḥunayn, its Greek plant-names transliterated where Arabic had no equivalents (Section IX), and went on to a long Arabic afterlife of illustrated copies and commentaries. Around the great names stood the profession's working shelf: Rufus of Ephesus, Paul of Aegina's surgery, Oribasius in part, and the late-antique medical encyclopaedists. The result, by c. 900, was that an Arabic-reading physician commanded the Greek medical inheritance entire — the platform from which al-Rāzī and Ibn Sīnā would presently criticise Galen in Galen's own translated terms.

Philosophy: Aristotle Nearly Whole, Plato at One Remove

Philosophy's inventory is asymmetric, and the asymmetry is famous. Aristotle was translated nearly whole: the Organon entire — Categories, On Interpretation, the Analytics, Topics, Sophistical Refutations, with the Rhetoric and Poetics filed, in the Arabic tradition, as logic's outer books — the Physics, On the Heavens, On Generation and Corruption, the Meteorology, On the Soul, the zoological works in the great version associated with Ibn al-Biṭrīq's circle, the Metaphysics in several partial versions, the Nicomachean Ethics. With Aristotle came his Greek commentators — Alexander of Aphrodisias, Themistius, John Philoponus — in translation and excerpt, so that Arabic philosophy inherited the school tradition, not the master alone. Plato, by contrast, arrived partial and often at one remove: the Timaeus in the old versions and in Galen's compendium-tradition, the Republic and Laws known substantially through summaries, paraphrases, and the doxographical tradition — al-Fārābī's Plato is a philosopher reconstructed from such mediations, and a remarkable reconstruction it is. The asymmetry had causes: Aristotle was the school author, embedded in the Syriac curriculum the bridge already carried; Plato's dialogues, literary to their marrow, resisted the movement's methods and its purposes alike. Section XIV returns to what the asymmetry meant.

The Exact Sciences: Euclid, Ptolemy, and the Mathematical Shelf

The mathematical-astronomical inventory is the movement's most consequential after medicine. Euclid's Elements existed in the versions traced in Section VIII — al-Ḥajjāj's, then Isḥāq's, then Thābit's revision — and became the foundation text of Arabic mathematics. Ptolemy's Almagest travelled the same stratigraphy, its Arabic title itself a souvenir of the road: al-Majisṭī, from the Greek megistē, the greatest (treatise), transliterated through Syriac. Around the two pillars: Apollonius's Conics in substantial part — books of it survive only in Arabic — Archimedes in a selection that Arabic geometers would extend, Diophantus's Arithmetic in Qusṭā ibn Lūqā's version, Nicomachus, Heron of Alexandria's mechanics and pneumatics, Theodosius, Menelaus, and the astronomical handbooks and tables through which Greek astronomy met its Persian and Indian counterparts at the Baghdad observatories. Astronomy was the caliphs' own science — calendar, prayer-times, astrology, and imperial ideology in one — and its shelf shows the funding.

Two features of the inventory deserve emphasis before the occult shelf complicates it. First, the movement translated curricula, not curiosities: what crossed was, field by field, the late-antique school canon — the books a professor at Alexandria in 600 would have taught from — because the bridge had preserved the canon as a canon and the patrons bought it as one. Second, the inventory's gaps within its own fields are as telling as its coverage: Plato partial, the commentators excerpted, whole stretches of Greek natural history and paradoxography ignored. The movement was not vacuuming up Greek literature. It was executing a selection, made by school tradition and patron demand together, and the selection's outer edge — where the school canon shades into the technical and the occult — is the next section's ground.

XIII. What Got Translated II: The Occult and Technical Shelf

Beside the school canon, and largely through the same hands, crossed a second literature: the technical Hermetica, the pseudo-Aristotelian occult corpus, and the Greek alchemical writings themselves. This shelf was never the movement's centre — no caliph founded a bureau for it, and its documentation is thinner than Galen's by an order of magnitude — but its crossing is the Arabic hub's founding fact, and it is treated here at the documentation's real strength, following Manfred Ullmann's survey of the Arabic occult and natural sciences (Die Natur- und Geheimwissenschaften im Islam, 1972) and the archive's adjudicated positions elsewhere.

The Hermetica and Pseudo-Aristotle

The technical Hermetica — the astrological, alchemical, medical-magical, and lapidary writings under Hermes' name, as distinct from the philosophical Corpus Hermeticum, which did not travel this road — entered Arabic early and deep: Hermes in Arabic is Idrīs, the prophet, and the Hermetic shelf of astrology and occult properties grew, in Arabic, far beyond its Greek exemplars, by composition under the great name as much as by translation. The pseudo-Aristotelian corpus made the same crossing with even greater consequence: above all the Secret of Secrets (Sirr al-asrār), the mirror-for-princes framed as Aristotle's secret instruction to Alexander — statecraft, physiognomy, astrology, medicine, and occult properties in one compilation — rendered into Arabic in the version associated with Yaḥyā ibn al-Biṭrīq in the ninth century, from materials whose Greek and possibly Middle Persian strata scholarship still disentangles. The Secret's roots are exactly the kind this hub must state carefully: an Arabic compilation with Greek sources and Greek pretensions, not a translation of a Greek book that ever existed in its Arabic shape. Its Latin career, through the door of Section XV's successor article, The Latin Door, would make it one of the most-read books of the Latin Middle Ages.

The Alchemical Corpus

The Greek alchemists crossed within this shelf. The Fihrist lists works under Hermes' name, names the translators who carried Greek and Syriac material into Arabic, and mentions — the detail the Greco-Roman Transmission article (§XI) already put on record — translations of four books of Zosimos, scattered by inconsistent transliteration under garbled forms of his name. A large pseudepigraphic production formed in Arabic under Greek names — Plato, Aristotle, Hermes, Apollonius of Tyana (Balīnās, to whom the Book of the Secret of Creation, with its Emerald Tablet, is ascribed), Zosimos — and Persian ones, Jamāsp and Ostanes among them; and for much of it, as Pierre Lory's summary for the Encyclopaedia Iranica puts the scholarly consensus, the line between translation proper and Arabic composition on a Greek model cannot now be drawn, and is not pretended to here. What can be said — and the Arabic Zosimos dossiers in the Egyptology division say it in detail — is that genuine translation occurred, that some of it preserves Greek material lost in Greek, and that by the tenth century a Baghdad bookseller could catalogue an Arabic alchemical literature at scale.

The shelf's destination was the Jābir corpus. The treatises composed in Iraq, c. 850–950, by the anonymous school writing under Jābir ibn Ḥayyān's name — the subject of this hub's companion article, The Jābir Corpus — cite this inheritance constantly: Democritus and the Greek ancients, often through intermediaries; Hermes; Balīnās; the Zosimos tradition. The debts are real, and the corpus is not a translation of anything: it is the occult shelf, plus the school canon's natural philosophy, rebuilt as an original Arabic system — the sulfur–mercury theory, the science of the balance, the classification of substances. The translation movement's role in that creation is precisely the role this article has documented for the sciences at large. The translators supplied the books, the vocabulary, and the Greek problem-set. The Jābir authors supplied everything else.

For the school canon, the question is usually answerable: versions survive, colophons name translators, the Risāla and the Fihrist audit the work. For the occult shelf it usually is not. Arabic Hermetica and pseudo-Aristotelica grew by accretion under authoritative names; a work “of Hermes” in the Fihrist may be a translation, a reworking, or an Arabic composition wearing the name, and the manuscripts rarely say which. This article follows Ullmann's and Lory's discipline: the shelf's Greek debts are asserted where the texts show them; the shelf's translations are asserted only where a version's documentary trail — catalogue, colophon, surviving Greek parallel — carries the claim. The distinction is not pedantry. It is the difference between the archive's evidence tiers, applied to the hub's own founding shelf.

One consequence of the shelf's crossing deserves a paragraph of its own, because it reverses the usual direction of loss. Because the Arabic translators and their Syriac intermediaries worked from Greek exemplars older, in some lines, than the ancestors of the surviving Byzantine codices, the Arabic occult shelf — like the Arabic Galen — preserves Greek material the Greek tradition lost. The Arabic Zosimos is the type case: works with no complete Greek counterpart, versions resting on differing Greek originals, a master's voice more continuous in Arabic than in the language he wrote. The movement did not merely receive the Greek inheritance. In places, it is now the only place the inheritance survives — a fact the Latin translators of the twelfth century, rendering Arabic versions of Greek books back toward the West, understood perfectly well, and profited from without embarrassment.

XIV. What Did Not Get Translated: The Deliberate Absences

The inventory's negative space is as sharply drawn as its contents, and it is the strongest single proof that the movement was a selection executed by a culture that knew what it wanted. Greek literature did not cross. Homer was not translated — the Iliad and Odyssey existed for Arabic readers only as names, a handful of quotations inside translated philosophical works, and the figure of a sage in the wisdom tradition. The tragedians and comedians were not translated: no Aeschylus, no Sophocles, no Euripides, no Aristophanes entered Arabic — Aristotle's Poetics crossed, famously, as a book of logic, its tragedy and comedy re-read as praise and blame by translators working in a culture without the theatre the treatise describes. Lyric and elegiac poetry did not cross. And Greek history did not cross: no Herodotus, no Thucydides, no Xenophon's histories, no Plutarch's Lives as history — the past of Greece interested Baghdad hardly at all, even as the past of Persia, in translation from Middle Persian, interested it greatly. What crossed, field by field, was the school canon of knowledge: medicine, philosophy, the exact sciences, and their occult penumbra. What stayed behind was the Greek imagination of itself.

The absences were not accidents of supply. Greek literary manuscripts were obtainable — the same Byzantine channels that supplied Galen could have supplied Homer, and the Banū Mūsā's missions bought what their patrons asked for. Nor were they accidents of capacity: the translators who could render the Analytics could have rendered a tragedy. The selectivity was structural, and Gutas's account names its drivers. The Abbasid programme wanted the sciences of the ancients — the knowledge an empire could use and a court could glory in — and it wanted them as a weapon in the cultural contest with Byzantium: to possess Greek science in Arabic was to argue that the Greeks' true heirs sat in Baghdad, not Constantinople. Greek poetry and history served neither purpose. Arabic, moreover, possessed in its own poetry a classical tradition of overwhelming prestige, against which translated Greek verse — untranslatable in its metres, alien in its gods — offered no competition anyone at court wished to stage; and Greek myth, as narrative, arrived anyway by other roads: through the Hermetica, the Alexander romance tradition, the wisdom literature, and the alchemical corpus itself, whose Greek gods and heroes walk through Arabic pages in Arabic dress. The movement's Greece was a Greece of doctors, geometers, and philosophers. It was a deliberate Greece.

The consequences ran for a thousand years, in both directions. For the Islamic world, the selection fixed the meaning of the Greek inheritance: Greek wisdom was falsafa and the sciences, a body of demonstrable knowledge to be mastered, criticised, and surpassed — and the Arabic philosophical tradition's Plato, reconstructed from summaries (Section XII), is a political philosopher and a sage, not the dramatist of the dialogues, because the dialogues as literature never arrived. For Europe, the selection fixed what the Latin door would open onto: when the twelfth-century translators rendered Arabic into Latin, they received the movement's Greece — Galen, Aristotle, Ptolemy, and the occult shelf — and the Greek imagination had to reach the West by the other road, in Greek, from Byzantium, in the Renaissance. The archive keeps those routes separate as a standing rule, and nowhere does the rule matter more than here: the Baghdad movement's absences are half the reason the Renaissance had to happen as a re-discovery. A culture is known by what it translates. It is known just as well — Baghdad is the standing proof — by what it looks at, understands, and leaves on the shelf.

// THE POETICS, AS A TEST CASE //

Aristotle's Poetics was translated — Abū Bishr Mattā's Arabic version, from Syriac, in the tenth century — and its career proves the section's point from inside the inventory. The treatise crossed because the Arabic tradition filed it in the Organon, as logic's final book: an art of poetic argument, not a criticism of Athenian drama. Tragedy, having no Arabic institution to refer to, was glossed as panegyric and comedy as satire. The book arrived; the literature it describes did not; and the translators, working exactly as their movement taught them, made the book mean what their culture could use.

XV. Why It Ended: Success, Patronage, and Fragmentation

The translation movement did not collapse. It completed itself, and then its world changed around the completion. Three causes, in the order of their weight in the modern literature, account for the winding-down between roughly 900 and 1000 — and the first of them is the one popular accounts, addicted to decline, most often miss.

1. Success: The Demand Was Satisfied

By about 900 the school canon existed in Arabic, in good versions, with a built lexicon and a growing commentary tradition. The marginal value of the next translation had fallen accordingly: what remained untranslated was, in the main, what the selection had always passed over (Section XIV) or what the versions already covered adequately. At the same moment, Arabic original science came of age, and the talent that would once have translated went to compose. The chronology overlaps the movement itself — al-Khwārizmī (died c. 850) was writing original algebra and astronomy while Ḥunayn still worked — but the tenth century is the succession's open season: al-Rāzī (865–925) criticising Galen from inside the translated corpus; the Banū Mūsā's heirs and the Baghdad astronomers improving Ptolemy's parameters; al-Fārābī (c. 870–950) rebuilding philosophy as an Arabic discipline; and beyond the century's edge, Ibn Sīnā and Ibn al-Haytham, for whom the Greek books are a foundation to be corrected, in a language that no longer needs translators to argue with them. George Saliba's account (Islamic Science and the Making of the European Renaissance, 2007) presses the point hardest: the translation movement's product was not a warehouse of Greek knowledge but a scientific culture that began competing with its sources almost immediately, and whose later history — including its transmission to Latin Europe — is unintelligible if the translations are treated as the story's end rather than its beginning.

2. Patronage Shifted

The market of Section IV did not vanish, but it changed its tastes and its addresses. Court demand moved from acquiring the canon to using it — the astrologer, the physician, and the philosopher were now expected to produce, and a patron's magnificence was as well displayed by an original dedication as by a commissioned version. Translation itself continued where a living need remained: the tenth-century Baghdad Aristotelians still rendered Syriac logic into Arabic, Yaḥyā ibn ʿAdī's circle worked on, and individual versions were made wherever a text was wanted badly enough. But the organised, multi-patron campaign — salaries, missions, workshops running in parallel — belonged to the canon-building century, and when the canon stood built, the campaign stood down.

3. Baghdad Fragmented

The political floor gave way beneath the whole economy. The later ninth century had already strained the caliphate — the Anarchy at Samarra, the Zanj revolt, the Ṭūlūnid and Ṣaffārid secessions — and in 945 the Buyids, Daylamite warlords ruling as amirs, took Baghdad itself, reducing the caliph to a pensioned figurehead. Patronage did not die — Buyid viziers were among the age's great book-men, and the tenth-century Baghdad of al-Fārābī's successors, the Aristotelian school, and the great libraries was intellectually brilliant — but it dispersed: to Buyid courts at Rayy and Shiraz, to the Hamdanid court at Aleppo, to Fatimid Cairo, to Umayyad Cordoba, where al-Ḥakam II's library became the western pole of Arabic book culture. The movement had always been a market rather than an institution (Section V's lesson); a market survives its capital's decline by moving. What ended around 1000 was therefore not Arabic translation, and certainly not Arabic science, but the specific Baghdad-centred campaign that had rendered the Greek canon into Arabic — ended, in the main, because its work was done and its centre of gravity had moved, as this hub's own architecture now moves, from transmission to original composition: from Wing I to Wing II.

XVI. How We Know: The Fihrist, the Risāla, and the Biographers

Everything asserted in this article rests, at one or two removes, on three kinds of Arabic witness — and a transmission article owes its readers an audit of its own evidence to close. The witnesses are contemporary or near-contemporary, they are unusually rich by the standards of pre-modern science, and each has a bias that can be named. Naming them is not a ritual of doubt. It is how the reader knows which of this article's sentences are load-bearing.

Ibn al-Nadīm's Fihrist (c. 987)

The Fihrist — the Catalogue — of Ibn al-Nadīm, a Baghdad bookseller and copyist, completed about 987, is the movement's own inventory of itself: a catalogue, in ten discourses, of the books known to its author in the Arabic world, with the translators' names, the patrons' commissions, the versions' histories, and a bookseller's gossip about all three. Its seventh discourse, on philosophy and the ancient sciences, is the single richest source for who translated what, for whom, and in which order of versions; its notices on the Banū Mūsā's spending, on the Bayt al-Ḥikma's directors, and on the alchemical shelf's Hermes and Zosimos titles have all been used above, at the strength flagged at each use. Bayard Dodge's English translation (The Fihrist of al-Nadīm, 1970) is the standard access. The biases are a bookseller's: the Fihrist knows the Baghdad trade's stock better than the provinces'; it repeats informants' claims — round numbers, famous anecdotes — with a cataloguer's inclusiveness rather than a critic's sieve; and its titles, above all on the occult shelf, record what books called themselves, which for pseudepigrapha is precisely what cannot be trusted. Used as a catalogue — evidence that a book existed, under a title, in a milieu — it is superb. Used as a history of the events its titles allege, it must be checked, and this article has checked it against the other witnesses wherever a claim carried weight.

Ḥunayn's Risāla

The second witness has been this article's companion since Section VII: Ḥunayn's Risāla on the Galen translations, in Bergsträsser's edition and the Strohmaier tradition of its study. Its strength is contemporaneity — a participant's audit, entry by entry, against which surviving versions can be collated — and its bias is the participant's: it is a report to a patron in a competitive market, by the market's leading firm, grading its predecessors and rivals. Its judgements on Sergius are a successor's judgements. Its silences — on costs, on failures that were not later redeemed, on collaborators' independent work — are a principal's silences. None of this disqualifies it; all of it prices it. Where the Risāla and the surviving manuscripts agree, the movement's history is as firm as the ninth century allows. Where the Risāla stands alone — a youthful version's confession, a journey's itinerary — this article has written “the Risāla records”, and the reader should hear the attribution.

The Biographical Dictionaries

The third witness is the latest and the most seductive: the biographical tradition, crowned by Ibn Abī Uṣaybiʿa's History of Physicians (13th century), with Ibn Juljul, al-Qifṭī, and their fellows — collective portraits of the translators, physicians, and philosophers, rich in anecdote, chronology, and book-lists, compiled three to five centuries after the movement's great age from sources now partly lost. This tradition preserves genuine documents — letters, wills, catalogues — inside its narratives, and its narratives are literature: the payment of Ḥunayn's books' weight in gold (Section VI), the dream of al-Maʾmūn (Section IV), the dismissed student returning with Homer on his tongue (Section VI) all live here, shaped to the biographical genre's love of the revealing scene. The archive's rule for this witness is the rule this article has followed throughout: anecdotes are told as anecdotes, with their genre named; dates and book-lists are used where the earlier witnesses or the manuscripts corroborate them; and no structural claim — about the Bayt al-Ḥikma, the patronage economy, or the method — rests on the dictionaries alone. The movement is fortunate in its evidence. It deserves readers who grade it.
