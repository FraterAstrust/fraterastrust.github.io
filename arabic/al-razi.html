I. A Physician at the Bench: Why al-Rāzī Reads Differently

Open the Kitāb Sirr al-asrār — the Book of the Secret of Secrets — beside any text of the Jābir corpus, and the difference is felt before it can be argued. The Jābirian books, treated in this division in The Jābir Corpus, speak in the voice of a master veiling doctrine for initiates: the science of the Balance weighs letters as well as substances, authorities are layered on authorities, and the imam stands behind the teaching as its guarantor. Al-Rāzī's book speaks in a different voice entirely. It begins by sorting the materials of the workshop into classes. It goes on to describe the furnaces, vessels, and hand tools the work requires. Only then does it give procedures, in numbered succession, with the order of operations stated and the signs of success and failure noted. There is no science of letters here. There is no imam. There is a list, a bench, and a sequence — and the list comes first.

That order is the article's subject. Abū Bakr Muḥammad ibn Zakarīyāʾ al-Rāzī (865–925) — Rhazes, Rasis, and Albubator in the Latin spellings — was by profession a physician, and by the testimony of the Arabic biographical tradition one of the most successful of his age: director of the hospital of his native Rayy, then of a hospital in Baghdad, author of the vast medical notebook-encyclopedia that Latin Europe would read as the Continens. His alchemy carries the habits of that profession into the workshop. A physician of the Galenic school lived by classification: diseases sorted by kind, drugs sorted by power and degree, cases recorded whether the outcome flattered the doctor or not. Al-Rāzī's Secret of Secrets does to the mineral workshop what his medicine did to the clinic. Substances are sorted by what they observably do under treatment. Instruments are inventoried before they are used. Procedures are written so that another worker can repeat them — and can tell, when the result fails, at which step it failed.

THE JĀBIRIAN MANNER

Doctrine dispersed and veiled by design; the Balance of letters; esoteric exegesis; authority descending from the imam Jaʿfar al-Ṣādiq. Knowledge is guarded, and the guarding is part of the teaching. See The Jābir Corpus.

THE RĀZIAN MANNER

Doctrine inventoried and ordered for use; classification by behaviour in fire; apparatus described before procedures; failure assigned to method. Knowledge is arranged, and the arrangement is the teaching. The present article.

None of this makes al-Rāzī a modern chemist before his time, and this article will not call him one. He believed in the elixir. He believed base metals could be transmuted into silver and gold, he organised his book around that belief, and the third part of the Secret of Secrets is a repertoire of elixir procedures. The modernity that older popular writing claimed for him — the laboratory rationalist who had quietly stopped believing what his contemporaries believed — is a mirage, and Section XVII returns to it. What distinguishes al-Rāzī is not disbelief. It is discipline about belief: transmutation, for him, is not a wonder to be credited on a master's word but a result to be produced by a stated method, from classified materials, in described apparatus — and if the result does not come, his own preface argues, in paraphrase, that the fault lies in the worker's procedure and not in the Art's principle. That sentence, paraphrased again at its proper place in Section XIV, is the most consequential sentence in Arabic alchemy. It converts failure from a refutation into a diagnosis.

A note on method, in the form this archive uses wherever a text invites invention. No direct quotation from al-Rāzī is given in this article. Everything the Secret of Secrets says is given in paraphrase, labelled as paraphrase, and the paraphrases follow the standard modern access to the work: Julius Ruska's edition and German translation, Al-Rāzī's Buch Geheimnis der Geheimnisse (Berlin, 1937), cited throughout simply as Ruska's edition. Where a famous saying circulates in popular writing under al-Rāzī's name and cannot be verified in the edited text or in a named scholarly translation, it is not printed here — not even to be refuted at length. The discipline is the same one the Greco-Roman division applies in Fire and Assay: what a text states, what a later tradition reports, and what a modern writer wishes it had stated are three different kinds of evidence, and they are not interchangeable.

II. The Man and the Record: Rayy, Baghdad, and the Hospitals

Al-Rāzī was born in Rayy — ancient Rhages, on the southern edge of the Elburz foothills, near modern Tehran — in 865 by the dating this archive follows, and he died there in 925. The biographical tradition, preserved in Ibn al-Nadīm's Fihrist, in al-Bīrūnī's bibliography of his works, and in the later medical biographers, gives a life in two careers. The young al-Rāzī, on the tradition's telling, worked first in his father's world of exchange — he is remembered as having been a money-changer — and came to medicine and philosophy as an adult, studying at Rayy and then in Baghdad. The anecdotal colour of the early life (the lute-playing youth, the turn to study) belongs to the biographers' stock of conversion stories and is filed here as anecdote. What is firm is the second career's outline, because it is corroborated by the works themselves and by the institutions they name.

He directed the hospital of Rayy, and then a hospital in Baghdad — the biographical sources associate the Baghdad post with the great hospital foundation of the caliph al-Muʿtaḍid's era — and he wrote medicine on a scale that still staggers. The Kitāb al-Ḥāwī, the Comprehensive Book on medicine, is less a composed treatise than a lifetime's clinical notebook, assembled after his death from his papers: case upon case, excerpt upon excerpt from Greek, Syriac, Persian, and Indian authorities, each tagged with its source and often with al-Rāzī's own dissent recorded beside it. Latin Europe, which received it in the thirteenth century as the Liber Continens, treated it as an authority of the first rank for three hundred years. The Kitāb al-Manṣūrī, dedicated to the same Samanid governor of Rayy who received the Secret of Secrets (Section IV), is the opposite kind of book: a compact, ordered handbook of medicine in ten treatises, whose Latin form, the Liber ad Almansorem, became a European textbook. And the treatise on smallpox and measles — the first clinical differentiation of the two diseases to survive — shows the same mind at the bedside that the Secret of Secrets shows at the bench: observe the distinguishing signs, record them in order, let the classification do the work.

PHYSICIAN FIRST

Hospital director at Rayy and at Baghdad; the al-Ḥāwī (Latin Continens) as a career's clinical archive; the al-Manṣūrī as its ordered handbook. The alchemy was written by a man whose day work was diagnosis, regimen, and the hospital ward.

THE CASE-HISTORY HABIT

Al-Rāzī's medical writing is famous for its case records — presentations, treatments, outcomes, including outcomes that failed. The Secret of Secrets' habit of noting the signs by which a procedure's success or failure is known is the same habit, moved from ward to workshop.

THE DISSENTING READER

His Doubts About Galen (al-Shukūk ʿalā Jālīnūs) argues, respectfully and in detail, that the greatest medical authority of antiquity was wrong on stated points — on evidence al-Rāzī says he observed himself. Deference to authority was not in his method, in any field.

THE RECORD'S KEEPERS

What is known of the life rests chiefly on Ibn al-Nadīm's Fihrist (tenth century), on al-Bīrūnī's catalogue of al-Rāzī's writings (Section XV), and on the medical biographers. The catalogue counts well over a hundred works across medicine, philosophy, and the sciences — most now lost, the alchemical books among the survivors.

Two cautions govern the biography. Chronologically, al-Rāzī (865–925) stands a generation after the translation movement's great age and roughly contemporary with the Jābir corpus's later strata: he is not Jābir's pupil, in any sense the evidence supports, but a reader of the Jābirian books who chose to write otherwise. And the tradition's report of his sight failing late in life — cataract is the usual account — is widely attested as fact, while the speeches attached to it vary by source and are literature, not record.

Why does the biography matter to a bench manual? Because the Secret of Secrets is unintelligible as a courtly or initiatory text and fully intelligible as a professional one. Its author earned his living by a learned art practised in institutions with stores, instruments, assistants, and records, and the book's opening moves — inventory the materials, describe the instruments, then and only then prescribe — are those of a man who had run a dispensary. Section III takes up the other half of his independence, the philosophical half: al-Rāzī held, on stated grounds, that no inherited authority was entitled to his assent without examination. The workshop book and the heretical metaphysics are one mind at two benches.

III. The Five Eternals: Philosophical Independence and Its Price

Al-Rāzī's philosophy survives mostly in the reports of men who opposed it — a fact that must be stated before any of it is summarised, because it sets the evidence's strength. His own philosophical books are largely lost. What remains is the doxographical record: summaries, refutations, and debate-reports by Ismaili, Muʿtazilite, and later writers, from which modern scholarship — Shlomo Pines's studies and Lenn E. Goodman's work are the standard modern accesses named in the Sources — has reconstructed a system startling in its independence. At its centre stands the doctrine by which al-Rāzī is still known to historians of philosophy: the five eternals. In paraphrase of the doctrine as the reports agree in giving it: there are five eternal, uncreated principles — God, the Soul, Matter, Time, and Place. God did not create the world from nothing at a moment of His choosing. The Soul, eternal but unwise, desired embodiment and entangled itself in eternal Matter; God, in wisdom and mercy, fashioned the ordered world from that pre-existent matter as the theatre in which souls could learn, suffer, and finally be released from embodiment altogether.

Every term of that paraphrase was a provocation. An eternal Matter beside an eternal God limits creation in a way no orthodox theology, Muslim, Christian, or Jewish, could accept. An absolute Time and an absolute Place — durations and extensions that exist whether or not any world fills them — cut against the Aristotelian physics the Baghdad schools were then absorbing, in which time is the measure of motion and place the boundary of a containing body. And the Soul's fall through desire, with release through knowledge, owes more to the Platonist and gnostic currents of late antiquity than to the Qurʾān. Al-Rāzī held these positions, on the reports, knowingly and in public disputation. The Ismaili missionary Abū Ḥātim al-Rāzī — a namesake, not a relative — debated him at Rayy, on the reports preserved in Abū Ḥātim's own Aʿlām al-nubuwwa, over prophecy itself: al-Rāzī is there made to argue, in paraphrase, that reason is given to all men equally and that a God who singled out prophets would set men at each other's throats — an argument whose exact wording is Abū Ḥātim's report of an opponent he aimed to refute, and which this article therefore gives only as a reported position, not as a quotation and not as a transcript.

// HOSTILE WITNESSES: HOW THE PHILOSOPHY SURVIVES //

The five eternals are known chiefly through opponents — Abū Ḥātim's debate-report, the refutations of later theologians, and doxographical summaries such as those Pines and Goodman analyse. Hostile transmission cuts both ways, and the article states both. It can distort: a refuter sharpens the position he refutes. But it also corroborates the doctrine's outline, because writers who agree on nothing else agree that al-Rāzī taught five eternals and denied creation from nothing. The outline is firm; the fine wording is lost with the books. Later charges that al-Rāzī denied prophecy outright, or mocked revelation, belong to the polemical layer and are flagged again in Section XV: they tell the historian what his enemies needed him to have said, which is evidence about them as much as about him.

What has this to do with alchemy? Three things. First, matter is eternal and therefore lawful: in a world fashioned from pre-existent matter, the Art's transformations are rearrangements of what eternally is — the conservation instinct the Greco-Roman division traces from Empedocles onward, here held as metaphysics. Second, no authority closes a question: the man who wrote Doubts About Galen was not going to accept an elixir procedure because Jābir's name stood over it. The Secret of Secrets' silence about the Jābirian veiling — no Balance of letters, no imam, no dispersed doctrine — is a philosophical position enacted as an editorial one. Third, knowledge releases: in the five-eternals myth, in paraphrase, souls are freed by learning what they are and what matter is. His alchemy is humbler — it promises gold, not salvation — but it shares the premise that liberation, of a metal or of a soul, comes by knowledge of natures, methodically acquired.

Atomism, Briefly and Carefully

The reports also credit al-Rāzī with a form of atomism: matter, in paraphrase of the doxography, composed of indivisible particles with void between them, defended against Aristotelian continuum physics and opposed after him by al-Bīrūnī and Ibn Sīnā (Section XV). The doctrine reaches us entirely through critics' summaries, its details vary between reports, and Pines's reconstructions differ on its originality and centrality. It is stated here at report strength only. Nothing in the Secret of Secrets' procedures requires it, and the procedures are not argued from it in the text: the metaphysics explains the man's independence, not the recipes.

IV. The Book and Its Patron: Sirr al-asrār and Kitāb al-Asrār

The Kitāb Sirr al-asrār, the Book of the Secret of Secrets, was written for a patron, and the patron matters because he fixes the book's kind. The dedication, as Ruska's edition gives it, is to Abū Ṣāliḥ al-Manṣūr ibn Isḥāq, the Samanid governor of Rayy — the same al-Manṣūr who received the medical Kitāb al-Manṣūrī. Al-Manṣūr was no initiate of an alchemical circle. He was a provincial governor of the Samanid amirate, a man of the court world, who wanted — on the preface's own presentation, in paraphrase — the Art set down for him completely and intelligibly: its materials, its instruments, and its procedures, without the deliberate obscurity in which, the preface complains, earlier writers had wrapped it. A book commissioned by a governor from his physician is a commission for usable knowledge. The Secret of Secrets' famous clarity is not an accident of temperament alone. It is what the patron paid for.

That preface argument deserves its own statement, because Section XIV builds on it. In paraphrase of Ruska's text: earlier masters spoke of the Art in riddles and dispersed fragments, so that a reader could spend a life assembling what any one of them knew; he will instead set down the whole in order — substances, apparatus, procedures — and he defends the Art against its despisers on a single ground: that men who failed at it failed in the doing, through ignorance of the materials, the measures, or the order of operations, and that a failure of procedure is not a disproof of principle. The argument's shape — completeness promised, obscurity blamed, failure reassigned to method — is not in dispute among the book's modern readers (Section XVII).

Two Books, Not One: al-Asrār and Sirr al-asrār

The bibliography must now be untangled, because al-Rāzī wrote two closely related alchemical books and the tradition — Arabic, Latin, and modern — has repeatedly tangled them. The Kitāb al-Asrār, the Book of Secrets, is the shorter and, on the usual scholarly account, the earlier of the two: a compact presentation of the same classification, apparatus, and procedure material. The Kitāb Sirr al-asrār, the Book of the Secret of Secrets, is the fuller work: it carries the dedication to al-Manṣūr, expands the apparatus and procedure sections, and presents itself as the complete statement. The two overlap extensively — whole stretches of substance-description and procedure run parallel — and manuscripts and catalogues have not always kept their titles apart, a confusion Ullmann's catalogue of the Arabic scientific literature (1972) does much to sort. The distinction this article observes is therefore stated plainly: where the fuller, dedicated text is meant, Sirr al-asrār is meant, and Ruska's edition is the access; where the shorter companion is meant, it is named Kitāb al-Asrār in so many words. Stapleton and Husain's pioneering English study of 1927 worked principally from the Asrār material, which is one reason the shorter book is disproportionately familiar to English readers.

It is not al-Rāzī's book, contains no laboratory, and must never be cited as if it did. The confusion is old, common, and refused throughout this archive (see Section XVI).

A word on the title itself. Sirr al-asrār, "the secret of secrets", sounds like the deepest veiling of all — and the book then proceeds, with almost comic directness, to inventory the workshop. Al-Rāzī keeps the genre's title and declines the genre's method: the secret, his preface argues in paraphrase, was never a riddle. It was a procedure nobody had yet written down in order.

V. Three Parts: Substances, Apparatus, Procedures

The Secret of Secrets, in Ruska's edition, is built in three parts, and the build is the most quoted fact about it — rightly, because no earlier surviving Arabic alchemical book is built so. Part One treats the substances: the complete materia of the Art, classified and described kind by kind — the six mineral classes of Sections VII–VIII, then the vegetable and animal substances of Section IX — with the marks by which each kind is known, chosen, and tested. Part Two treats the apparatus: the furnaces, vessels, and tools of Section X, described as instruments with specifications, before any procedure is allowed to presuppose them. Part Three treats the procedures: the operations of Section XI applied, in the worked sequences of Sections XII–XIII, to the preparation of spirits and bodies and finally to the composition of elixirs. Substances, apparatus, procedures: the order of a pharmacopoeia, of a surgical manual, of a hospital formulary — of every genre, that is, in which al-Rāzī's medical profession had already learned to write — applied for the first time, at this completeness, to the alchemical workshop.

Why does the structure make this the most laboratory-facing Arabic alchemical text that survives? The claim needs its qualifiers. It is about surviving texts: earlier Arabic alchemy survives in fragments too broken to compare as books. It is about structure, not truth: a book can be perfectly laboratory-facing and its elixir procedures still unworkable, as Section XVII weighs. And it is comparative within the Arabic tradition: the Jābir corpus contains vastly more procedure, but dispersed and veiled by design across hundreds of treatises, where al-Rāzī concentrates his whole art between two covers in bench order. With those qualifiers, the claim stands on the structure alone: no other surviving Arabic alchemical book lets a reader answer, from the book itself, what a laboratory asks every morning — what have we got, what do we work with, and what do we do, in what order?

VI. Classification by Bench Behaviour: The Analytical Point

Part One of the Secret of Secrets divides the mineral substances of the Art into six classes. The division is usually quoted as a list — spirits, bodies, stones, vitriols, boraces, salts — and left there, as if it were a piece of medieval tidiness. It is considerably more than that, and this section states the article's analytical point before the classes themselves are given, so that the detail of Sections VII–VIII can be read as evidence for it. Al-Rāzī's classes are defined by bench behaviour: by what the substances do when the workshop treats them. A spirit is not a substance with a volatile essence contemplated in the abstract. It is a substance that rises — that sublimes or evaporates under fire and can be caught above. A body is a substance that stands — that endures the furnace, melts without fleeing, and can be cast, hammered, and weighed after firing as it was before. A stone is a mineral that does neither: it neither flees nor properly melts, and the Art must break, calcine, or dissolve it by indirection. The salts dissolve in water; the boraces melt and make other things melt with them; the vitriols bite, colour, and yield sharp waters on distillation. Each class is a prediction: treat a member so, and it will answer so. Classification, in this book, is compressed procedure.

The contrast that proves the point is with the classifications al-Rāzī did not use. He did not classify by the four elements or the four qualities, though that scheme was available to him from the whole Greek tradition this archive's Greco-Roman division describes — a scheme that sorts by essence, by a body's supposed inner constitution of hot, cold, wet, and dry. He did not classify by the Jābirian Balance, by letters and their numerical weights. He classified the way his own medicine classified drugs in the Galenic manner: by powers known through effects, graded by observation. A Galenic simple is hot in the second degree because of what it observably does to a body. A Rāzian substance is a spirit because of what it observably does in a furnace. The epistemology is one and the same, and it is why Stapleton, himself a chemist, could recognise so many of al-Rāzī's substances from their described behaviours in 1927: behaviour-descriptions survive translation in a way that essence-descriptions do not. A text that says a substance is phlegmatic tells a modern reader nothing testable. A text that says it rises in the aludel, melts in the crucible, or dissolves in water and recrystallises on evaporation has told him where to look.

// A CLASS IS A PROMISE ABOUT FIRE //

Read the six classes as six fire-verdicts. Spirits flee the fire upward. Bodies endure it and melt in place. Stones resist it and must be broken down. Vitriols decompose in it, colouring and corroding. Boraces flow in it and carry other things into flow. Salts mostly fear water more than fire: they dissolve, and can be recovered by drying. The scheme is not modern chemistry — sulphur and mercury share a class with sal ammoniac, which no modern table would allow — but it is a workshop truth: those four really are the things in the store-room that will leave by the chimney if the vessel is not sealed.

Two limits must be entered in fairness. The classes are not perfectly behavioural: the bodies are the seven metals of the ancient canonical list, a traditional roster, and the spirits carry theoretical freight from the sulphur–mercury doctrine (Section XIV), mercury and sulphur being the Art's principles as well as two volatile store-room substances. The classification is behaviour organising tradition, not replacing it; and within the classes the descriptions mix bench marks with lore, as every pre-modern materia medica does. The analytical point survives both limits: the sorting principle is operational — volatility, fusibility, solubility, behaviour in fire. Sections VII–VIII now give the classes in his order.

VII. The Six Mineral Classes I: Spirits and Bodies

The Spirits (arwāḥ): The Volatile Four

The spirits, arwāḥ (singular rūḥ), are four in number, and the fourfold list is one of the most stable in the whole book: mercury, sulphur, the arsenic sulphides, and sal ammoniac. In paraphrase of Ruska's text, what unifies them is stated operationally: they are the substances that cannot be held in open fire — they rise, evaporate, or sublime away, and the whole technology of sealed vessels, alembics, and aludels (Section X) exists in large part to catch them. Mercury (zaybaq) flees as vapour and amalgamates the bodies it touches. Sulphur (kibrīt) burns and rises, and carries the Art's colouring and hardening powers. The arsenics (zarnīkh) — the yellow sulphide, orpiment, and the red sulphide, realgar, counted as kinds of one spirit — sublime to a deadly, whitening vapour: the workshop's great whitening agent and its great poison, in one. Sal ammoniac (nūshādir) sublimes clean, without residue when pure, and serves the Art as a volatile salt that can carry other substances up with it and bite into metals' surfaces. Four substances, one behaviour, and — not coincidentally — the four most dangerous things in the store-room. The class is a safety warning as well as a taxonomy.

The Bodies (ajsād): The Seven Metals

The bodies, ajsād (singular jasad), are the seven metals of the ancient canon, in the order the text's descriptions presuppose: gold, silver, copper, iron, lead, tin — and the seventh, khārṣīnī, the metal the Arabic tradition associated with China (Ṣīn), identified in the modern literature, following Stapleton and Ruska, with zinc or a zinc-rich alloy. The identification deserves its caution stated: metallic zinc was not isolated as a European laboratory product until the early modern period, but zinc-rich metals and zinc ores were traded from the East far earlier, and al-Rāzī's descriptions of khārṣīnī — in paraphrase, a body resembling tin in some marks and iron in others, brittle where tin is soft — fit a zinc-bearing metal better than any other candidate the commentators have offered. This article gives the identification as the standard one, flagged as an identification, not as a certainty the text itself states.

What unifies the bodies, in paraphrase of the class's treatment, is fixity with fusibility: a body can be melted in the crucible, cast into a mould, and taken out the same metal it went in — which no spirit survives and no stone achieves. Each body is described by its bench marks: colour, weight in the hand, ring and hardness, behaviour at file and hammer, its melting heat relative to its companions, and the company it keeps — which spirits attack it, which salts calcine it, which fluxes clean it. Lead and tin anchor the scale's easy end; iron, which the period's furnaces could barely melt and which the Art worked mostly by calcination and solution, anchors the refractory end — "fusible" in this book means fusible in its furnace, a practical term. Gold and silver are described with a refiner's eye, by the testing discipline the Greco-Roman division documents in Fire and Assay: colour under heat, behaviour in the melt, and the verdict of the balance.

// LEAD AND TIN: A TERMINOLOGY THAT MOVES //

The Arabic words for the soft white metals shift between authors and manuscripts — raṣāṣ and usrub for lead, qalʿī and related forms for tin — and al-Rāzī's usage, as Ruska's commentary notes, is not everywhere the usage a modern reader expects from later Arabic chemistry. Where this article says "lead" and "tin" for the bodies, it follows Ruska's identifications of the passages in question. The general lesson is the classification section's own: in this literature, a metal is safest identified by its described behaviour — its melting, its calx, its companions — and only then by its name.

VIII. The Six Mineral Classes II: Stones, Vitriols, Boraces, Salts

The Stones (aḥjār): What Neither Flees nor Melts

The stones, aḥjār (singular ḥajar), are the classification's residual and most populous class: the minerals that are neither spirits nor bodies nor any of the three soluble or fluxing families. In paraphrase of Ruska's text, the class gathers, among others: marcasite (marqashīthā), the brassy pyritic stone in its golden, silvery, and coppery kinds; magnesia (maghnīsiyā), a name covering dark manganese-bearing earths and stones, not the modern element or its oxide; malachite (duhnaj), the green copper stone; tutty (tūtiyā), the furnace-soot zinc oxide collected from the roofs and flues of brass and copper works — a manufactured mineral, gathered where zinc vapour had burned in air, and prized in medicine as in the Art; talc (ṭalq), the splitting, fire-enduring sheet mineral; haematite (shādhana), the blood-red iron stone; lapis lazuli (lāzaward); gypsum, glass, crystal, and the coloured ornamental stones in their kinds. The list matters less than the class-logic. A stone, for the bench, is a promise of difficulty: its useful content — copper in malachite, iron in haematite, zinc in tutty — is locked in a matrix that fire alone will not open. Stones enter procedures pre-treated: roasted, calcined, ground to impalpable powder, dissolved in sharp waters, or fluxed with boraces and salts. Part One's descriptions therefore dwell, for the stones, on the marks that distinguish the good specimen from the worthless look-alike — colour, streak, weight, fracture — because the workshop buys stones by the eye and pays for its errors at the furnace.

The Vitriols (zājāt): The Biting Earths

The vitriols, zājāt (singular zāj), are the sulphate earths of the ancient dye, ink, and tanning trades, sorted in the text principally by colour: green vitriol, the iron vitriol of the ink-makers; white vitriol; yellow vitriol; red vitriol; and black kinds beside them. The Arabic nomenclature here is the least stable in the whole classification — the names qalqand, qalqadīs, qulquṭār, and sūrī are distributed among the colour-kinds differently by different authors and copyists, a confusion already old in al-Rāzī's time from the Greek vitriol vocabulary it translates — and Ruska's commentary tracks the variants rather than forcing them into one scheme. This article follows that restraint: the colour-kinds are firm, the one-to-one mapping of every Arabic name to a modern sulphate is not, and no mapping is asserted here beyond the green kind's standard identification with iron vitriol (ferrous sulphate in modern terms). What unifies the class at the bench is behaviour, once again: vitriols dissolve in water to biting, ink-darkening liquors; they decompose under fire; and on distillation they yield the sharp waters that Part Three uses as solvents. That last property is the point at which this article must police its canonical boundary, and the caution-note below does so in full.

// SHARP WATERS ARE NOT MINERAL ACIDS: THE BOUNDARY, STATED PRECISELY //

Al-Rāzī's procedures distil vitriols, salts, and sal ammoniac and use the distilled "sharp waters" (in paraphrase of Ruska's text) to dissolve metals and stones that water alone will not touch. Popular writing repeatedly promotes those waters into "the discovery of the mineral acids" and credits al-Rāzī — or Jābir — with nitric or sulphuric acid. The archive's canonical position, stated here without qualification, is that this is wrong. What the Secret of Secrets describes is the preparation and solvent use of sharp distilled liquors from vitriol-and-salt mixtures. It does not describe the isolation, concentration, or characterisation of a mineral acid as a distinct reagent, and it nowhere claims the defining acid behaviours — the parting of gold from silver by an acid that dissolves the one and spares the other — on which the later acid technology rests. The unambiguous description of the mineral acids belongs to the Latin pseudo-Geber, above all the Summa perfectionis of the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries, four hundred years after al-Rāzī. The full argument, with the Greek antecedents, is this division's Mineral Acids and Practice. Al-Rāzī's sharp waters are real solvents and real evidence of bench sophistication. They are not aqua fortis, and this article does not call them so.

The Boraces (bawāriq) and the Salts (amlāḥ): The Helpers

The last two classes are the workshop's auxiliaries — substances the Art rarely seeks for themselves and constantly uses on others. The boraces, bawāriq (singular bawraq), gather natron (naṭrūn), the natural soda of the Egyptian lakes, known to every glass-maker and fuller; tinkār, the borax of the Eastern trade, the goldsmith's soldering flux; Armenian borax; and the humbler kinds, down to the borax called "of bread", a baker's soda pressed into the Art's service. In paraphrase, their unifying behaviour is fluxing: a borax melts early, wets the surfaces of metals, dissolves the skins of oxide that keep metals from joining, and carries refractory mixtures into flow — which is why Part Three reaches for a borax wherever two bodies must be united or a stubborn charge persuaded to melt. The salts, amlāḥ (singular milḥ), gather common salt, the salt of the table; sal alkali (qilī), the strong plant-ash alkali of the soap- and glass-makers, won by burning salt-tolerant plants and lixiviating their ashes; salt of lime and salt of ashes; bitter salt; salt of urine; and their companions. Their unifying behaviour is the water-side mirror of the spirits' fire-side one: salts dissolve, penetrate, bite gently, and can be recovered whole by evaporation — and in calcination mixtures, ground with a body and fired, they help break the body's metallic form down to calx (Section XI). Between them, boraces and salts are the Art's reagents in the strict sense: the substances whose job is to act on the substances whose job is to become something else.

Across all six classes, Part One's descriptions follow, in paraphrase of Ruska's text, one working pattern: the kind's names and kinds; where it comes from; the marks of the good specimen against the bad; and what fire, water, and the common reagents do to it. Provenance and marks come before virtues — the buyer is served before the theorist. It is that behaviour-first pattern that let Stapleton and Husain (1927) identify the great majority of the substances with modern species: the founding demonstration that the classification describes a real store-room.

IX. The Shorter Shelf: Vegetable and Animal Substances

After the six mineral classes, Part One treats — much more briefly, and the brevity is itself a datum — the substances the Art takes from living things. The vegetable substances enter the book principally as sources and servants rather than as principals: plant ashes, whose lixiviation yields the sal alkali of Section VIII; plant juices and gums that serve as binders, coatings, and gentle reagents; and the fuels themselves, charcoal above all, without which no furnace section makes sense. The animal substances, in paraphrase of Ruska's text, are listed with the same classificatory intent and a visibly thinner bench knowledge behind them: hair, blood, milk, urine, eggs — shell, white, and yolk distinguished — bile, fat, and bone, and the curious sub-class the tradition calls the animal stones: the hard concretions found in animal bodies, gall-stones and their kin, filed among the stones by form though animal by origin. Hair and eggs do real work in Part Three — hair as a source of volatile alkali on distillation, egg as a medium, a coating, and a gentle sulphur-bearer — and urine supplies its salt to the salts class. But no reader of Ruska's edition can miss the proportion. The mineral classes run to scores of kinds, each with provenance and marks. The living shelf is a page or two of names with brief treatments.

The proportion is worth interpreting, carefully. In the Greek workshop tradition — the recipe papyri, the pseudo-Democritean Physika — eggs, milk, bile, and urine are headline reagents. Al-Rāzī demotes them, and the demotion fits a physician's pharmacology: no two batches of bile are the same reagent twice, where a vitriol can be bought, tested, and stored to a standard. A classification built on repeatable bench behaviour starves the living shelf because living substances do not repeat. This is stated as an interpretation of the book's proportions, not as a sentence the book pronounces — and with one caution: in Part Three's elixir and ceration procedures the organic materials return, as media and imbibition waters. The shelf is short because it is hard to classify, not because it is unused.

X. The Laboratory Inventoried: Furnaces, Vessels, and Tools

Part Two of the Secret of Secrets is, so far as this archive knows, the earliest surviving Arabic description of a complete alchemical instrumentarium written as an inventory — each instrument named, its construction given, and its use stated, before the procedures are allowed to begin. This section follows Ruska's text in paraphrase, grouping the inventory as the bench itself groups it: first the fires, then the vessels that stand in them, then the vessels that stand over them, then the hand tools. The Greco-Roman reader will recognise the ancestry at every shelf. The alembic and cucurbit descend from Alexandrian distillation as the hub's article on Maria the Jewess describes them; the water bath still carries, in the European languages, Maria's name. What al-Rāzī adds to the inheritance is not a new instrument so much as a new completeness: the whole kit, in one place, specified well enough to build from.

The Fires: Furnaces and Baths

In paraphrase of Ruska's apparatus chapters, the furnace stock runs as follows. The athanor (al-uthāl in one usage, the word doing double duty in the tradition with the sublimation vessel of the next heading — the commentary in Ruska tracks the ambiguity) is the slow, tower-like furnace for long, even, gentle heating: the digestion and fixation furnace, built to hold a moderate fire for days without attendance swings. The blast furnace (kūr) is its opposite: a forge furnace driven by bellows (minfākh) to the heats at which bodies melt and calces form. Between them stands the book's most remarked device, the self-feeding furnace: in paraphrase, a furnace with a fuel reservoir arranged so that charcoal feeds down into the fire as the fire consumes it, holding a charge at heat through the night without a stoker — the athanor principle mechanised, and the clearest single proof in Part Two that its author had personally lost work to a fire that died at the wrong hour. For the gentlest heats the inventory gives the two baths: the water bath, a vessel set in heated water so that its charge can never exceed water's boiling gentleness, and the dung bath, a vessel buried in fermenting dung, whose slow biological heat holds a digestion warm for weeks at a temperature no flame can be trusted to keep. Fire, in this inventory, is not one thing. It is a graded series of heats — dung, water, athanor, bellows-blast — and Part Three's procedures specify their operations by grade, as a modern text specifies by temperature.

Luting: The Inventory's Unlisted Instrument

One material runs through Part Two without heading a chapter: the lute — the clay mixtures, in paraphrase of Ruska's recipes for them, compounded of clay with hair, dung, salt, and other binders in stated kinds, with which every joint is sealed. A spirit worked in an unluted vessel is a spirit lost to the chimney; a sublimation attempted with a cracked lute is a poisoning attempted on the worker. The attention to lute recipes — composition, drying, repair — is bench knowledge of the purest kind, the kind no literary compiler invents: the furnaces give the heat, the vessels give the shapes, and the lute makes the assembly one closed world in which volatile things can be persuaded to stay.

XI. The Operations: The Verbs of the Art

Part Three presupposes a small vocabulary of operations — the verbs of the Art — and the Secret of Secrets, in paraphrase of Ruska's text, defines them by doing them, in sequence, on named substances. This section states the seven principal operations as the book uses them, each as a sequence, because a Rāzian operation is not a word but a procedure in miniature. Two companion operations, descension and liquefaction, are added at the end. The worked examples of Sections XII–XIII then show the verbs conjugated in full.

1. Calcination (taklīs, the making of a calx). Sequence, in paraphrase: the body is prepared — filed, cut small, or ground as the procedure directs — mixed where directed with its calcining companions (salts, sal ammoniac, sulphur, or sharp waters in their kinds), charged into a crucible or an earthen vessel, and held in strong fire until the metallic form is destroyed and a powdery, earth-like calx remains. The signs of completion are negative and exact: the charge no longer melts as a metal, no longer rings, no longer shows bright particles at the fracture. Calcination is the Art's controlled killing of a body — its reduction from the metallic state, in which few reagents can reach it, to an open, reactive powder in which anything can.

2. Sublimation (taṣʿīd, the causing to rise). Sequence, in paraphrase: the spirit, or the mixture from which a spirit is to be raised, is charged into the pot of the aludel or beneath the blind alembic; the joints are luted; fire is applied below, gently at first and then to the grade the procedure states; the volatile part rises and gathers, pure and flower-like, on the cool upper surfaces; the fire is let die; the vessel is opened cold; the sublimate is scraped out and the residue — the "dead" remainder — inspected, since its weight and look are the operation's report on itself. Sublimation purifies by the sharpest of all bench distinctions: what rises is taken, what will not rise is left.

3. Distillation (taqṭīr, the causing to drip). Sequence, in paraphrase: the liquid or moist charge is set in the cucurbit; the alembic is luted on; heat is applied by the grade stated — bath for the tender charges, direct fire for the robust; vapour rises, condenses in the head, and runs by the spout into the receiver, fraction answering to fraction as the fire is raised. By distillation the book wins its waters: the sharp waters of Section VIII, the waters of sal ammoniac, of vitriols, of hair and eggs — the solvent arsenal of Part Three.

4. Solution (ḥall). The calx, the stone, or the body prepared by filing is set in its stated water — common water for the salts, sharp water for the bodies and stones — warmed or digested as directed, until the solid has passed into the liquid and the liquid stands clear or coloured as the procedure promises. Solution is calcination's complement: the calx opened the body to attack; the water completes the opening by taking the body up entirely.

5. Coagulation (ʿaqd). The solution is evaporated, cooled, or treated with its stated coagulant until the dissolved substance sets again — as crystals, as a curd, as a solid salt — purified by its passage through the liquid state and recoverable to the hand. Solution and coagulation together are the Art's washing machine: what enters mixed can be made to leave in parts, the soluble from the insoluble, the crystallising from the deliquescent.

6. Fixation (tathbīt). The volatile is made to stay. In paraphrase of the book's usage: a spirit is combined with a body or a calx that can hold it, the combination is heated by grades, and the operation is repeated — imbibe, dry, fire gently, fire stronger — until the spirit no longer flees a heat that would once have driven it off entire. Fixation is the operation on which the whole elixir doctrine turns: a tincture that evaporates tinctures nothing. Its test is stated as plainly as its method: the fixed thing is put in the fire, and the fire is watched.

7. Ceration (tashmīʿ, the making wax-like). A hard, dry, refractory calx is brought, by repeated imbibition with its stated waters or oils and gentle drying between, to the consistency its name promises: soft as wax, fusible at a gentle heat, penetrating when melted as wax penetrates. Ceration is the elixir's final preparation in many of Part Three's sequences — the fixed tincture made applicable, so that when it is projected on a molten base body it spreads, sinks, and works through the whole melt instead of riding on its surface.

DESCENSION (ISTINZĀL)

The companion operation of the descensory (Section X): a charge is melted in a perforated upper vessel and made to descend, drop by drop, through fire or through a filtering bed into the receiver — separation by flowing, for mixtures whose parts melt at different heats or not at all.

REPETITION AS METHOD

Across all seven operations, Part Three's characteristic instruction is repetition to a sign: grind and imbibe and fire until the calx is white, until the spirit no longer rises, until the melt stands clear. The endpoint is an observation, not a count — the physician's "until the fever breaks", written for metals.

Read as a set, the seven verbs describe a complete strategy for matter: calcine to open, dissolve to free, coagulate to recover, sublime to purify, distil to arm, fix to hold, cerate to apply. Every procedure in Part Three is a sentence in this grammar, and al-Rāzī's claim, in paraphrase, is that the grammar suffices — that there is no operation in the Art that is not one of these, on one of Part One's substances, in one of Part Two's vessels. The next two sections follow three procedures through, step by step.

XII. Worked Procedures I: Mercury Purified, a Body Calcined

The two procedures of this section and the one of the next are given as labelled paraphrases of Ruska's text: the steps are the book's, in the book's order; the wording is this article's; and where the manuscripts or the parallel passages give proportions differently, the paraphrase says so rather than electing one figure into false authority. They are chosen, as the task of this article requires, for documentary clarity — for how completely each shows the classification, the inventory, and the operations working together.

Procedure One: The Purification of Mercury (Paraphrase)

The problem. Mercury as bought is foul: it carries lead and tin in solution, earth and oxide on its skin, and it runs dull, tailing, and slow. Every procedure that uses mercury — every amalgam, every sublimation of its compounds, every "killing" and fixing of the spirit — presupposes it bright. Part Three therefore purifies it first, and the purification, in paraphrase, runs in three stages: washing, straining, and firing with companions.

Stage one — wash and knead. The mercury is worked, in paraphrase of Ruska's directions, with vinegar and salt — in some passages with sal alkali or ashes beside them — kneaded and ground against the vessel's side until the liquor comes away black with the filth the mercury carried. The washing is repeated with fresh liquor until the washings no longer blacken: the endpoint, characteristically, is an observation. The mercury is then washed in clean water and dried. What the stage does, in modern terms the text does not use, is strip the base-metal oxides and surface dross that dull the spirit's skin.

Stage two — strain. The washed mercury is pressed through cloth or soft leather, in paraphrase, squeezed so that it falls in bright rain through the pores while the coarser amalgam-dross and unpassed filth stay in the cloth. A mercury that will not pass, or that passes leaving a heavy tail of residue, is sent back to stage one. The test of the stage is the eye: bright, quick, undivided running, a mirror that moves.

Stage three — fire with companions, where the procedure requires the highest purity. For mercury destined for sublimation work, the paraphrase continues: the strained spirit is ground with its stated companions — sulphur, salts, or vinegar-preparations in the kinds specified — and sublimed or distilled in the luted apparatus of Section X, recovered bright from the vapour, its base companions left in the residue: a spirit, classified by its flight, purified by being made to fly under control. Proportions differ between Ruska's several passages; the paraphrase therefore reports the structure — wash to a clean sign, strain to a bright sign, sublime to a weighed recovery — and refers the worker to Ruska's edition for the figures. That restraint is the difference between a paraphrase and a forgery.

Procedure Two: The Calcination of a Body (Paraphrase)

The problem. A body in its metallic state is closed: compact, shining, and proof against almost every water the Art commands. To dissolve it, colour it, or wed it to a spirit, it must first be killed — reduced to calx. The book gives calcinations for the bodies severally; the paraphrase here follows the general form they share, with lead — the easiest body, and the Greek assay tradition's own calcination subject — as the running example, and copper's harder case noted at the end.

Step one — divide the body. In paraphrase: the metal is brought to the greatest surface the worker can give it — beaten to leaf, cut to clippings, or filed to filings — because calcination works inward from surfaces, and a lump calcines in days what filings calcine in hours. Step two — charge with companions. The divided metal is ground or layered with its stated companions: common salt or sal ammoniac for the biting calcinations, sulphur for the burning ones, vinegar or a sharp water sprinkled through for the moist kinds. Step three — fire by grade. The charge goes into its crucible or earthen pot, luted or open as the passage directs, and into the furnace at the stated grade, there to be held — stirred, in the open calcinations, so that fresh surface keeps meeting the air — until the transformation is complete. Step four — read the signs. Completion, in paraphrase, is known when the charge is wholly powder or scale, when no bright metallic particle shows at a fresh fracture, when a sample refuses to gather into a bead in the assay fire. What remains uncalcined is separated and returned to step two. Step five — wash the calx. Where salts have served, the calx is washed in water until the washings lose their salt taste and bite, and dried: a clean, open powder, ready for solution.

// LEAD EASY, COPPER HARD: THE SCALE INSIDE THE PROCEDURE //

Lead calcines almost willingly — its calx, the yellow-red oxide earth the painters knew, forms at heats the dung bath's hotter cousins can reach, and the Greek cupellation tradition of Fire and Assay is built on the same willingness. Copper resists: its calcination in the book's manner wants stronger fire, sharper companions, and more repetitions, and its calx keeps a core of metal that must be picked out and returned. The single procedure-form thus contains a scale of the bodies themselves, easiest to hardest — lead, tin, silver, copper, gold, iron in their several orders of obstinacy — and Part Three's separate calcinations are that scale, written out body by body.

XIII. Worked Procedures II: Sublimation — Sal Ammoniac and Arsenic

Procedure Three: The Sublimation of Sal Ammoniac (Paraphrase)

The problem. Sal ammoniac as traded is mixed with soot, earth, and common salt. The Art needs it pure, because it is the Art's volatile hand: ground with a calx it bites the calx open; sublimed with a spirit it can carry the spirit up; dissolved, it is among the sharpest of the common waters. Purification is by its own defining behaviour — it rises — and the procedure, in paraphrase of Ruska's text, is the aludel operation of Section XI performed with a pharmacist's care.

Step one — prepare the charge. The crude salt is dissolved in water, the solution settled or strained from its earth and soot, and coagulated back to dryness (operations four and five of Section XI, doing preparatory duty): what will not dissolve is already discarded. In passages where the dry route is directed, the crude salt is instead ground with its stated companions — ashes or common salt in their kinds — which serve to keep the charge open and to hold back what should not rise. Step two — charge and lute. The prepared salt goes into the pot of the aludel, or into the lower vessel beneath the blind alembic; the rings or head are set; every joint is luted and the lute dried before fire is mentioned. Step three — fire by grade. Fire is applied below, gentle at first — a too-hasty fire drives the salt up mixed with its moisture and its companions, and cracks cold lutes with steam — then raised to the working grade and held even. The salt rises invisibly and gathers above, in the cool rings, as a white, feathery, crystalline flower. Step four — cool, open, gather. The fire is let die completely; the vessel is opened cold — a warm-opened aludel exhales its product back into the room and into the worker — and the sublimate is scraped from the rings into its jar. Step five — judge and repeat. The residue in the pot is weighed and looked at: earth, soot, and unrisen salt, in proportions that report the crude material's quality. The sublimate that is grey, damp, or heavy with companions is ground and sublimed again. Purity's sign, in paraphrase, is total: the finished salt rises in the test fire and leaves nothing behind.

The Arsenic Sublimation: The Same Form, a Deadlier Spirit

The arsenic sulphides are sublimed in the same apparatus and the same form — charge prepared, vessel luted, fire graded, product gathered cold — and the paraphrase can therefore be brief where the form is identical and full where it differs. It differs in three respects, all of them instructive. First, the stakes: what rises from orpiment or realgar is the Art's great whitener, the vapour that will later be fixed into copper to whiten it toward silver's likeness — and it is a poison of the first order, whose fume the text's handling directions, in paraphrase, treat with a respect the modern reader should share and not re-enact: sealed vessels, cold opening, and no breath over an open charge are the period's own precautions, stated here as history and not as instruction. Second, the preparation: the sulphide is commonly ground with iron filings, salt, or other stated companions whose office is to hold back its sulphur and moderate its rise, so that the white sublimate — the arsenious flower of the later pharmacopoeias — gathers clean. Third, the product's career: arsenic's sublimate is not an end but a weapon, destined for the whitening procedures in which a fixed arsenic tincture enters copper. The sublimation is therefore judged by a downstream sign the sal ammoniac procedure does not know: not only does it rise clean? but does it whiten? — tested, in paraphrase, on a small charge of the body it is meant to serve, before the batch is committed.

These paraphrases are documentary exposition: they exist so that a historian can see what kind of text the Secret of Secrets is. They are abbreviated, they omit the figures and the repetitions on which any real attempt would turn, and the substances they name — mercury vapour, arsenical fumes, lead calces — are grave poisons whose handling killed practitioners in al-Rāzī's century and would kill in this one. The archive prints procedures as evidence, in the same spirit in which Fire and Assay prints cupellation: to be understood, not to be performed. Nothing in this article is a recipe for use.

Step back from the three procedures and their common shape is the book's whole epistemology in miniature. Every stage ends in a sign — washings that no longer blacken, a mirror that runs bright, a fracture without bright particles, a sublimate that leaves no residue — and every sign is a place where the worker can stop, judge, and correct. Failure is therefore localisable: a bad elixir can be traced down the chain of signs to the first stage whose sign was misread or skipped. That is what the preface's argument (Sections IV and XIV) means in practice. Whether the chain, perfectly followed, ends in gold is the question doctrine must answer. The next section takes it up.

XIV. Doctrine: Sulphur–Mercury Held to Method

Al-Rāzī's theory of metals is the theory he inherited, and this section's first duty is to say plainly that he did not invent it. The sulphur–mercury theory — in the form this archive treats in full in The Sulphur–Mercury Theory — holds that the metals are compounded underground, and can be re-compounded at the bench, from two principles: a mercury, the moist, fusible, metallic bearer, and a sulphur, the hot, colouring, hardening agent whose purity and proportion fix the metal's kind. Gold is the perfect balance of the purest mercury with the purest, rightly-proportioned sulphur, fully digested by subterranean heat; the base metals are the same two principles in impure kinds or wrong proportions or incomplete digestion. Al-Rāzī receives this doctrine from the Jābirian tradition and uses it throughout Part Three: the spirits mercury and sulphur head his classification precisely because they are also the Art's principles, and his elixir procedures are, in theoretical dress, operations for purifying a body's mercury, correcting its sulphur, and completing its digestion at the bench in days instead of in the earth in ages.

His modifications are those of a working physician, and they are best stated as shifts of emphasis that the text, in paraphrase, sustains. First, the principles are handled as substances. Where Jābirian theory can treat sulphur and mercury as natures, balances, and letter-weighted qualities, al-Rāzī's sulphur and mercury are first of all the yellow stone and the bright liquid in Part One's store-room, with grades, provenances, and adulterations. Theory, in his hands, keeps being pulled back to inventory. Second, arsenic stands beside sulphur as a colouring and hardening spirit — the whitening procedures of Section XIII have no place in a strict two-principle scheme, and the book gives them a large place anyway, because the bench says arsenic whitens. Practice amends theory, on the record. Third, and most consequentially, the elixir is a product of method, not of grace. In paraphrase of the preface's and Part Three's sustained position: given the classified substances, the described apparatus, and the operations performed in order to their stated signs, the elixir follows as a drug follows from a correct prescription — not certainly in every hands, but reliably in skilled ones, and improvably in all. The comparison to pharmacy is this article's, offered as the reading that best fits a book written by the author of the al-Manṣūrī; the position paraphrased is the book's.

Transmutation defended by method rather than credulity: the Art's failures, in al-Rāzī's stated standard, are failures of procedure — of substance, measure, order, or fire — and not failures of principle. That standard deserves a cold eye, and Section XVII gives it one: a doctrine that explains every failure as a fault of procedure has made itself very difficult to falsify, and the historian must note the shelter as well as the discipline. But its historical work is the opposite of credulity's. Credulity says: believe, because the master achieved it. Al-Rāzī's standard says: the master achieved it, if he did, by steps that can be written, repeated, and checked at their signs — and your failure assigns you a findable error. It is the standard of a teaching hospital applied to the elixir, and no earlier Arabic alchemical writer states it so completely.

XV. The Critics: al-Bīrūnī, Ibn Sīnā, and the Later Polemic

Al-Rāzī's alchemy was argued with, and the argument begins with a chronological correction this article is obliged to make in so many words. Al-Bīrūnī (973–after 1050) never met al-Rāzī, and no correspondence between them exists or could exist: al-Bīrūnī was born nearly half a century after al-Rāzī died. What exists is a posthumous engagement: the next age's greatest bibliographer took al-Rāzī as a principal subject, writing the Epistle on the catalogue of al-Rāzī's books — a bibliography compiled, in paraphrase of its framing, because the output was already too large and disputed to navigate without one — and reporting the alchemical claims, in the Kitāb al-Jamāhir and elsewhere, with a scepticism that never quite becomes dismissal. In paraphrase of his reported position: he had investigated the Art's claims himself, found its practitioners' transmutations unproven and often fraudulent, and held the strong elixir claim at arm's length — while treating al-Rāzī's substance-knowledge and industry with open respect. The doubleness is the tenth century's most honest verdict on the book: the inventory is trusted; the summit is not.

The famous exchange on atomism belongs, correctly assigned, to the correspondence between al-Bīrūnī and Ibn Sīnā — the Questions and Answers in which the young al-Bīrūnī pressed the young Ibn Sīnā on Aristotelian physics — where al-Rāzī's reported positions on atoms, void, absolute time, and absolute place enter as positions to be refuted, cited from his lost books. Ibn Sīnā's own verdicts on al-Rāzī are two, and both are posthumous in the same way. On metaphysics, in paraphrase: the five eternals are rejected, creation and the soul's nature re-argued on Aristotelian-Avicennian lines, and al-Rāzī's independent philosophy treated as the error of a man who, in the famous Avicennian gibe as the tradition reports it, should have kept to medicine — a gibe this article reports as tradition, in paraphrase, and does not quote. On alchemy, the Kitāb al-Shifāʾ's mineralogical section delivers the most consequential anti-transmutation argument of the Arabic Middle Ages, in paraphrase: the species of metals are true species, fixed by their specific forms; the artificer can produce likenesses — colour, weight, and surface deceiving every common test — but he cannot transmute one species into another, because he cannot confer a substantial form. The argument is aimed at the Art generally, but its target-species is the Rāzian claim in particular: method, however perfect, cannot do what Ibn Sīnā's metaphysics says no method can. Between al-Rāzī's "the procedure failed" and Ibn Sīnā's "the species forbids", Arabic alchemy's central argument is fully stated — and it is still, recognisably, the argument the Latin Middle Ages will inherit.

// HERESY CHARGES: LATER POLEMIC, FLAGGED AS SUCH //

From the eleventh century onward, a polemical literature fixed al-Rāzī's image as the arch-heretic: denier of prophecy, mocker of revelation, author of blasphemies the polemicists quote with a freedom his lost books cannot check. Modern scholarship — Pines, Goodman, and the editors of the surviving ethical works — treats that image as a construction: built from the genuine five-eternals doctrine and the reported Rayy debate, sharpened by Ismaili and orthodox refuters who needed a refutable opponent, and unusable as biography without the discount this article has applied throughout. The charges are evidence — for the history of al-Rāzī's reception, for what his independence cost his name, and for the climate in which his books were copied or left to perish. They are not evidence for what he wrote. The Secret of Secrets, notably, contains nothing for the polemic to feed on: it is a workshop book, and it survived, in part, because a workshop book gives heresy- hunters nothing to hold.

XVI. The Double Afterlife: Rhazes the Physician, the Liber Secretorum

Al-Rāzī's reputation lived two lives in Latin Europe, and they barely touched. The first and greater life is the physician's. The Continens and the Liber ad Almansorem, translated in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, made Rhazes — with Galen, Hippocrates, and Avicenna — a pillar of the European medical curriculum, printed among the earliest medical folios and lectured on into the sixteenth century. That Rhazes is a clinician: case-histories, regimens, the smallpox treatise's distinctions. Generations of European physicians learned his name without learning that he had written on alchemy at all. The second life is the alchemist's, and it is narrower, later, and bibliographically treacherous. An Arabic alchemical Secret of Secrets under al-Rāzī's name passed into Latin in the translation movement's later phase as a Liber Secretorum — the Latin career this division's The Latin Door treats in its full context — circulating in a manuscript world already crowded with secrets: above all with the pseudo-Aristotelian Secretum secretorum, the mirror for princes that shares the title and nothing else (Section IV). Citations of "Rasis on the secret" in Latin alchemical writing must therefore be audited one by one: some reach the genuine Rāzian laboratory tradition, some reach the pseudo-Aristotelian court book wearing a similar name, and some reach neither — reaching instead the large Latin pseudo-Rāzī penumbra of attributed treatises that a famous name attracts. This article asserts the Latin transmission of the alchemical Secret at that audited strength, and no stronger.

The doubleness has a moral for the archive. In Arabic, the physician and the alchemist were one author, catalogued together by al-Bīrūnī and Ibn al-Nadīm. In Latin, the author split, and European alchemy received al-Rāzī's procedures largely detached from the professional discipline that had produced them — the inventory without the hospital, the elixir without the case-history habit — circulating for centuries as "secrets" of exactly the veiled kind their author had promised his patron to stop writing. The Latin door let the recipes through more faithfully than the method. Section XVII begins from that irony.

XVII. Assessment: Workshop Manual or Literary Collection?

What kind of evidence is the Secret of Secrets? The modern debate, stated fairly, has three positions, and this article closes by giving each its strength. The first is the workshop reading, founded by Stapleton and Husain in 1927 and carried furthest by the historians of chemistry who followed them. On this reading, the classification identifies real substances by real behaviours at a rate no armchair compilation could achieve; the apparatus describes a buildable, coherent instrumentarium; the operations are the actual operations of the early medieval laboratory, several of them — sublimation in the aludel, distillation of sharp waters, fluxing with boraces — continuous with later documented practice. The book, on this reading, is a working manual's fair copy: not a notebook, but a master's considered statement of a real workshop's real art, elixir chapters included as the workshop's own research programme. Lawrence Principe's modern assessment (2013) stands broadly in this line, with a historian's hedges: al-Rāzī emerges as among the most practically grounded of the early alchemical authors, his systematisation itself a form of laboratory knowledge.

The second position is the editorial reading, of which Ruska himself is the measured representative. Ruska edited the book as a scholar edits a book: a composed, literary artefact, drawing on sources — Greek, Syriac, Persian, and the Jābirian tradition — arranging inherited material with a strong systematising hand, and standing at a real distance from any single bench. On this reading, the manual's completeness is a literary achievement before it is a documentary one: real workshops are messier, more local, and less symmetrical than Part One's six classes, and the symmetry itself — four spirits, seven bodies, the tripartite build — advertises the study, not the store-room. The elixir procedures, on this reading, are included because the genre requires a summit, compiled from the tradition's stock with the same care as the calcinations and no more personal guarantee.

The third position — the one this article adopts, as an adjudication checkable against the sections above — is that the two readings divide the book where the book itself divides: the lower two-thirds are a workshop manual, the summit is a literary programme. The classification, inventory, operations, and preparatory procedures (Sections VI–XIII) carry the marks no compilation fakes well: failure-signs, lute recipes, self-feeding furnaces, residue inspections, endpoints stated as observations. The elixir procedures presuppose every lower operation perfectly performed and then ask a result no sign in the book can verify as the lower signs are verified — there is no bench test in Part Three that distinguishes a successful projection from a beautiful alloy, and Ibn Sīnā's likeness-argument (Section XV) exploits exactly that gap. As evidence for what an early tenth-century Arabic laboratory contained and did daily, the Secret of Secrets is the finest single document the tradition produced. As evidence that transmutation was achieved there, it is a programme and a promise — believed by its author, organised by his method, and unproven by his book. It is his own standard, applied to him: the procedure is documented; the principle remains where he left it, defended, systematised, and awaiting a demonstration his inventory makes imaginable and his text does not supply.
