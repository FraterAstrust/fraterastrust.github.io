I. What "Literature" Means Here — the Belles-Lettres Debate

This article surveys the Egyptian texts that modern readers shelve as stories and poems: tales, love songs, hymns read as poetry, and the mixed, miscellaneous writing of the schools. Its companion article treats the Instructions, the sbꜣyt [Gardiner: S29-D58-G1-Z4-X1-A2-Z2] or teaching literature, in full; the two articles divide the secular corpus between them and cross-refer throughout, rather than retelling each other's ground. See Wisdom Literature.

The first question is whether "literature" is an Egyptian category at all, or a modern box into which Egyptian texts have been sorted after the fact. Egypt had no single word that maps exactly onto the modern term. It had words for speech, for writing, for teaching, and for song, and it had scribes who plainly treated some compositions as special: copied for a thousand years, attributed to named sages, and praised in other texts. Whether that special treatment adds up to "literature" in our sense is a genuine scholarly debate, and this article states it rather than hiding it.

The framing used here follows Richard B. Parkinson, whose Poetry and Culture in Middle Kingdom Egypt (2002) and whose translations in The Tale of Sinuhe and Other Ancient Egyptian Poems (Oxford World's Classics, 1997) argue, in paraphrase, that a body of Middle Kingdom writing functioned as a differentiated, fictional, aesthetically valued discourse: texts composed to be experienced as crafted works, entertaining and unsettling at once, and not reducible to royal propaganda or school utility. Parkinson revived for this corpus the older scholarly term belles-lettres: beautiful writing, recognised as such by the culture that kept it.

The counter-considerations deserve equal space. Literacy was restricted to a small scribal elite, so any "literature" was elite culture, performed and copied inside institutions. Many supposed literary texts survive only as school copies, which can make a curriculum look like a canon. Genre labels such as "tale," "hymn," and "instruction" are modern conveniences laid over Egyptian forms that blend freely: a tale contains hymns, an instruction contains narrative, and a hymn can carry a story. The honest position, and the one this article works from, is Parkinson's with its limits stated: Egypt possessed a recognisable literary sphere, above all in the Middle Kingdom, but its borders were porous, its audience narrow, and its categories ours as much as theirs.

"Secular," in this article's title, needs the same care. It does not mean irreligious. Gods act in the tales, hymns praise gods, and the love songs invoke Hathor. It means only that these texts are not, in the first instance, ritual scripts, funerary corpora, or temple liturgy: they circulated as compositions to be read, heard, copied, and enjoyed, whatever sacred matter they contain. The funerary corpora have their own article; see Funerary Texts.

II. A Manuscript Culture — Writing, Voice, and Audience

Egyptian literature was a manuscript culture, and the material facts govern everything else in this article. There was no printing, no book trade, and no reading public in the modern sense. A composition existed as a handful of hand-written copies, in hieratic on papyrus for library copies and on limestone flakes and potsherds, ostraca, for school and draft copies. To publish a text was to have it copied; to preserve it was to keep copying it. Every work in this article survives because that chain of copying held, somewhere, for long enough. See Scribal Arts.

The audience question follows. If only a small percentage of Egyptians could read, who were tales and songs for? The evidence points two ways at once. The copies are scribal: library rolls from tombs and houses of life, school ostraca from the Theban west bank, the workmen's village of Deir el-Medina above all. But the texts themselves are built for the ear: verse points marking lines, refrains, dialogue, nested storytelling in which a character performs a tale for listeners inside the tale. The working model used by most scholars, stated here as a model and not a fact, is a small literate circle producing and performing texts that could be heard by more people than could read them.

The language itself is stratified. The Middle Kingdom classics are written in Middle Egyptian, which then became the classical language: later Egyptians learned it, copied it, and measured new writing against it, much as later Europeans treated Latin. New Kingdom tales and love songs shift toward Late Egyptian, closer to the spoken language, and the Demotic stories of the first millennium are written in the language and script of their own day. A literary history of Egypt is therefore also a history of its language stages, and dating a composition often means dating its language before its manuscript.

III. A Canon Egypt Named Itself — Chester Beatty IV

Egypt left one text that does the canon-maker's work explicitly. Papyrus Chester Beatty IV, a New Kingdom manuscript now in the British Museum, contains on its verso a celebration of famous writers of the past, known to scholarship as the Immortality of Writers. Miriam Lichtheim translates it in Ancient Egyptian Literature, Vol. II, among the school texts, and the companion article on the Instructions discusses its place in the scribal curriculum (Wisdom Literature, Sections XVIII and XXI).

The argument of the piece, in paraphrase throughout, is that the named sages of old, the authors of the Instructions and the classics, achieved the only durable immortality there is: not their tombs, which crumbled, nor their children, who died, but their books and the names the books carried. The text names its canon: among them Imhotep and Hardjedef from the deep past, and Middle Kingdom authors including Khety, the school classic's author, and the writers associated with the royal instructions. A culture that writes a text arguing that writers live forever in their writings has, whatever label we choose, a literature and knows it.

Two cautions keep the evidence honest. First, the Chester Beatty list is a scribal text praising scribes: it is the profession's own claim for itself, made in a school manuscript, and should be read as advocacy as well as testimony. Second, its canon is weighted toward the Instructions; the tale-tellers are less visible in it, which may reflect what schools taught rather than what the culture valued whole. With those cautions entered, the text remains the single strongest ancient witness that Egyptians themselves sorted some writings into a class apart, attached authors' names to them, and expected the sorting to last.

IV. Sinuhe — the Manuscripts

The Tale of Sinuhe (also printed Sanehat) is the most fully attested work of Egyptian literature, and the manuscripts are the place to start, because they show what survival meant in practice. UCL's Digital Egypt pages, which this archive follows for the manuscript census, name two principal sources. Papyrus Berlin 3022 is a Middle Kingdom papyrus preserving the tale from shortly after its opening, supplemented by fragments known as the Amherst fragments. Papyrus Berlin 10499, the Ramesseum papyrus, is a late Middle Kingdom copy found in a tomb beneath the Ramesseum precinct at Thebes, preserving the beginning Berlin 3022 lacks, with the Eloquent Peasant (Section VIII) on its other side.

Around those two pillars stands a crowd of lesser witnesses: a fragment from Harageh (Petrie Museum UC 32773), a Ramesside papyrus from Deir el-Medina, and a long list of New Kingdom ostraca, among them one of the largest Ramesside ostraca known, Ashmolean 1945.40, and copies in the British Museum, Berlin, and Cairo. Roland Koch's 1990 edition gathered the sources together across eighty-one pages of transcription. No other Egyptian tale approaches this density of attestation, and the reason is Section III's canon at work: Sinuhe was a school classic, copied as an exercise by centuries of students, and every classroom copy was another chance of survival.

The Ramesseum find deserves its own sentence, because it is the closest thing Egyptian literature has to a private library caught in the ground. In 1896 Quibell excavated, beneath the later store-rooms of the Ramesseum, a late Middle Kingdom shaft tomb containing a box of manuscripts: literary tales, rituals, medical and magical texts, and a word-list, buried with a man whose other grave goods suggest a lector priest and healer. UCL's summary of Gardiner's listing gives the flavour: Sinuhe and the Eloquent Peasant, a ritual for a statue of Senusret I, the Semna despatches, an onomasticon. Literature, in this tomb, sits in the same box as medicine and ritual, the working library of a professional reader.

V. Sinuhe — Structure and Telling

The tale, in paraphrase throughout this section, opens as a tomb autobiography: Sinuhe, a courtier in the service of a royal wife, tells his own life in the first person, in the voice Egyptians used for the inscriptions in which officials presented their careers to eternity. The fiction begins inside that respectable form and then breaks it. King Amenemhat I dies; Sinuhe, on campaign with the crown prince Senusret, overhears messengers bringing the news to the army, and panics. Fearing he will be implicated in a succession struggle, he flees, alone, northeast out of Egypt.

The middle of the tale is the exile. Sinuhe crosses into Asia, is found half-dead by herdsmen, and is taken in by a ruler of Upper Retjenu, Ammunenshi, who has heard of him. He marries into the ruler's family, receives land, leads troops, grows rich and honoured, and defeats a local champion in single combat, in a duel passage told with a craftsman's relish for equipment and preparation. Embedded in the narrative are set pieces in other genres: a hymn of praise to Senusret I, spoken to Ammunenshi; Sinuhe's own prayer in distress; and, late in the tale, the exchange of letters that turns the plot home.

The close is a return engineered by the king. Senusret, now reigning, sends a royal decree, quoted within the tale as a document, pardoning and recalling the exile. Sinuhe comes home, is received at court in a scene that plays his Asiatic appearance, his beard and foreign dress, for both comedy and pathos, is washed, shaved, and re-clothed as an Egyptian courtier, and is granted what the whole tale has been moving toward: a tomb, a funerary endowment, and burial in Egypt. The autobiography frame closes over the story like a lid: the man who tells the tale is, by the end, the man in the tomb the telling presupposes.

Structurally, the work is a lesson in what Egyptian narrative could do by the early 12th Dynasty. It sustains a single first-person voice across decades of story time; it embeds documents and hymns without breaking that voice; it marks its verse with red verse points in the manuscripts; and it builds its meaning by symmetry, the flight out mirroring the return home, the foreign bathos of the duel mirroring the courtly order of the close. Parkinson's literary reading, in the work cited in Section I, treats these not as ornaments but as the poem's argument: the form performs the reintegration the plot describes.

VI. Sinuhe — Exile, Return, and Burial

What is the tale about? The older reading made it royal propaganda: a hymn to Senusret I wrapped in a story, reassuring its audience that the succession was secure and the king merciful. The hymn is really there, and the reassurance is really in it. But the propaganda reading has to explain away the tale's centre of gravity, which is not the king but the exile: the long, lovingly detailed account of a good life lived outside Egypt, in a land the official ideology treated as wretched and chaotic.

Parkinson's counter-reading, which this archive follows while noting it is a reading and not a fact, takes the tale as an exploration of identity at the border. Sinuhe prospers in Asia; nothing there fails him; and yet the prosperity does not settle the question the flight opened, because what he cannot have abroad is the one thing his culture told him a life was for: an Egyptian death and burial, a tomb among his own people, a name kept by his own rites. His longing, in paraphrase, is not for power or even for home as a place, but for the proper ending. The king's decree grants it, and the tale can close.

Read this way, the tale holds two truths without resolving them, which is why it has outlasted the propaganda reading. Egypt is the only place a life can be completed, and a complete and honourable life can nonetheless be lived, for decades, outside it. The foreign ruler is generous, the foreign land fertile, the foreign family real. The tale's Egyptocentrism is total and its sympathy crosses the border anyway. That tension, sustained in accomplished verse by an author whose name we do not know, is the strongest single exhibit for Section I's claim that Middle Kingdom Egypt had a literature in the full sense.

For the tale in its historical setting, the accession of Senusret I and the court world of the early 12th Dynasty, see Middle Kingdom, which treats Sinuhe among the period's defining works. The Instruction of Amenemhat, the dead king's own warning, which forms the tale's dark companion piece, is treated in Wisdom Literature, Section VII.

VII. The Shipwrecked Sailor — a Tale inside a Tale

The Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor survives in a single manuscript, Papyrus Hermitage 1115 in St Petersburg, a Middle Kingdom hieratic copy, and it is the archive's standing reminder that survival could hang on one roll. Lichtheim translates it in Ancient Egyptian Literature, Vol. I, and Parkinson includes it among the Middle Kingdom poems. Where Sinuhe is attested everywhere, the Sailor is attested once: had that roll perished, a masterpiece of the same age would simply not exist for us, a point Section XXVI develops.

The structure, in paraphrase, is a set of nested boxes. A frame narrator, a courtier returning from a failed royal expedition, is being consoled by a follower, who tells him a story to steady him: how he himself once sailed for the king's mines, how his ship, with its picked crew, was lost in a storm, and how he alone was washed ashore on an island. On the island he met its lord, a gigantic serpent, golden and bearded, who told him, in turn, a story inside the story: how his own family of serpents had been destroyed by a falling star, leaving him alone. The serpent prophesied the sailor's rescue, loaded him with gifts, and vanished with his island when the rescue ship came.

The frame then closes with the tale's most discussed stroke. The consoled courtier, in paraphrase, is not consoled: he answers that there is no point telling him of rescue, since his own failure stands. The consolation fails inside the story that offers it. Scholars divide on whether that ending is irony, realism, or a damaged text's accident, and this archive records the division rather than settling it. What is not in doubt is the craft: a tale about the telling of tales, in which a story is offered as medicine and the patient declines it, composed at the same courtly moment as Sinuhe and with the same interest in what narrative can and cannot do for the person hearing it.

The serpent's island, stocked with every good thing and removed from the mapped world, is Egyptian literature's earliest fully realised otherworld of plenty, and the serpent himself, courteous, grieving, and prophetic, its earliest great non-human character. The tale's motifs, the sole survivor, the marvellous island, the grateful and gifted return, recur in world storytelling for millennia afterward; the Egyptian text is not thereby the source of the later ones, and this archive claims only the priority and the resemblance, not a descent.

VIII. The Eloquent Peasant — Eloquence on Trial

The Tale of the Eloquent Peasant is the Middle Kingdom's great work about speech itself. Its manuscripts are Berlin papyri: P. Berlin 3023 and 3025 and the Ramesseum copy, P. Berlin 10499, which shares a roll with Sinuhe, with a further copy in the British Museum (Papyrus Butler 527, BM 10274). Parkinson edited the text in 1991 and translated it in the 1997 volume cited above; Lichtheim's translation is in Ancient Egyptian Literature, Vol. I.

The story, in paraphrase, is deliberately slight. A peasant from the Wadi Natrun, Khuninpu, travelling to market with his donkeys loaded, is tricked and robbed by a subordinate official, Nemtynakht, who covets the goods. The peasant petitions the man's superior, the High Steward Rensi, and his petition is so eloquent that Rensi reports it to the king. The king, in paraphrase, orders the case prolonged: let the peasant be kept petitioning, his speeches written down, and his household fed meanwhile, without his knowing. Nine petitions follow, rising in rhetorical splendour and in frustration, until justice is finally done: the robber is punished and his property given to the peasant.

The petitions are the work. They are formal, figured speeches on justice, built from images of the balance, the rudder, the flooded field, and the honest measure, addressed upward across the whole height of the social order by a man with nothing but his tongue. The tale thus stages the central Egyptian claim about mꜣꜥt [Gardiner: Aa11-X1] [𓐙𓏏], that right order is not the property of rank: the lowest man in the story is its best speaker for truth, and the machinery of state, king included, exists in the tale to listen to him. The uncomfortable undertow is equally deliberate: justice is delayed, by royal command, so that the eloquence can continue, and the peasant's suffering is the price of the court's entertainment. Both readings are in the text.

The tale is this archive's bridge between literature and the administration of justice: the petition as an institution, the steward's court, and the written record of speech are treated in Law and Administration, and the theme of justice as universal right is taken up in Middle Kingdom and Ma'at and Isfet. Within this article, the Peasant stands as the proof that Egyptian narrative could make rhetoric itself a plot: nine speeches, one robbery, and the whole theory of the state carried by a voice.

IX. The Westcar Tales — Wonders at Khufu's Court

The Westcar Papyrus (Papyrus Berlin 3033) preserves the most charming frame in Egyptian literature. At the court of Khufu, builder of the Great Pyramid, the king's sons entertain their father with tales of wonders performed by magicians of earlier reigns, until the last son, Hardjedef, produces not a tale but a living magician, Djedi, who performs at court himself. The manuscript is a single, damaged copy, its beginning lost; its language places its composition in the Middle Kingdom or the Second Intermediate Period, centuries after the court it depicts. Lichtheim translates it in Ancient Egyptian Literature, Vol. I, as the Tales of Wonder.

The wonders, in paraphrase, escalate by design. A lector priest of an earlier reign fashions a wax crocodile that becomes real and seizes his wife's lover. Another parts the water of a lake so that a girl's lost pendant can be recovered from the bottom. Djedi, the living wonder, rejoins severed heads, makes a lion follow him, and knows, he says, the secret chambers of the sanctuary of Thoth, which Khufu covets for his own pyramid. Each tale is a performance inside a performance, and the frame keeps score: the court is an audience, and storytelling is what a court does with its past.

The final movement changes key. Djedi will not, or cannot, deliver the secret chambers; instead he prophesies the end of Khufu's line and the birth of three children to a woman named Reddjedet, children of the sun god Re, who will found the next dynasty. The closing tale, the only one set in the story's present rather than its past, tells that birth: the gods themselves, disguised as travelling musicians and dancers, attend the delivery, and the god Khnum, the potter who fashions children, is among them in his creator's role. Wonder tales that began as court entertainment end as dynastic prophecy, and the collection's real subject steps forward: kingship passes, houses fall, and the stories outlast them both.

Hardjedef's presence in the frame ties the Westcar collection to the wisdom canon: the same prince whom the Instructions name as their earliest author appears here as the impresario of the tales, the sage as the court's master of narrative. See Wisdom Literature, Section III. The Westcar tales are also the headwaters of a long Egyptian tradition of magician stories, running through the priest-magicians of the Late Period to Setne himself (Sections XX to XXII): the lector priest with his book and his wax figures is the ancestor of every magician in this article.

X. The Tale of the Two Brothers — Bata and Anubis

The Tale of the Two Brothers is the great New Kingdom folk tale, preserved complete on Papyrus D'Orbiney (British Museum EA 10183), a Ramesside manuscript whose scribe names himself in the colophon, the scribe Ennene, a point Section XXIV returns to. Lichtheim translates it in Ancient Egyptian Literature, Vol. II. Its heroes are gods in human dress: the elder brother Anubis and the younger, Bata, presented not as jackal and god of the necropolis but as a farmer and his herdsman brother on an Egyptian farm.

The plot, in paraphrase, runs through the great folk tale motifs in sequence. Bata, blameless, rejects the advances of his brother's wife; she accuses him of the very assault he refused; Anubis, deceived, comes to kill him, and is stopped when Bata's cattle warn their herdsman and a god puts a water of separation between the brothers. Bata proves his innocence by a terrible oath, exiles himself to the Valley of the Pine, and places his heart on the pine's flower. The gods fashion him a wife, who betrays his secret; the heart is cut down, Bata dies, and Anubis, warned by a sign in his beer, searches three years for the heart and revives him. Bata then takes forms in succession, a bull, and then trees felled for Pharaoh's palace, until, by a splinter swallowed, he is reborn as the king's son, and at last king himself, judging the wife who betrayed him.

Scholars have long noted the tale's kinship with the wider world's story stock: the falsely accused innocent, the external soul hidden in a tree, death and revival by a brother's search. The Egyptian text is an early and fully achieved telling, not a primitive draft of later ones, and claims of direct descent in either direction exceed the evidence. What can be said on the evidence is that by the Ramesside age Egyptian narrative commanded the whole folk tale register, metamorphosis, talking animals, divine intervention, and moral reversal, and could sustain it at length in Late Egyptian prose of great pace and economy.

The tale also marks a social widening of literature's world. Sinuhe's stage was the court and the foreign palace; Bata's is the farm, the byre, and the valley, and its moral weight falls on a herdsman's innocence against a household's lie. Between the Middle Kingdom classics and this Ramesside tale, the centre of Egyptian storytelling moved outward from the Residence, and the Deir el-Medina community that copied and kept such tales (Section XVIII) is part of that movement's evidence.

XI. Other New Kingdom Tales — Princes, Gods, and a Report

Around the Two Brothers stands a shelf of New Kingdom narrative that survives, characteristically, in single and often incomplete copies. The Tale of the Doomed Prince, on the verso of Papyrus Harris 500 in the British Museum, tells, in paraphrase, of a prince fated at birth by the Hathors to die by crocodile, snake, or dog, guarded in a tower, released to the world, married in Syria by winning a princess in her tower, and defended by his wife against the fates as they arrive. The manuscript breaks off before the ending: whether the third fate took him is, literally, lost. Lichtheim translates the fragment in Ancient Egyptian Literature, Vol. II.

Truth and Falsehood, on Papyrus Chester Beatty II, is an allegory wearing a tale's clothes: two brothers named for the abstractions dispute, blind, and pursue each other across a generation, until the son of the wronged Truth vindicates him before the gods. The Contendings of Horus and Seth, on Papyrus Chester Beatty I, is the New Kingdom's great mythological narrative, eighty years of divine lawsuit over Osiris's throne told with a bawdy, satirical energy that treats the gods as litigants in a very Egyptian court; the archive treats the underlying myth in its own articles, and cites the Contendings here for its narrative manner, prose storytelling at full stretch, rather than its theology.

At the shelf's edge stands the Report of Wenamun, on a Moscow papyrus, the account of an official robbed and humiliated on a mission to Byblos for cedar, at the moment of Egypt's imperial decline. Scholars still divide on whether Wenamun is a real administrative report that reads like literature, a literary tale built on a report's skeleton, or something between; Lichtheim prints it among the tales in Vol. II with the question open. This archive follows her: the text is catalogued here, its status flagged as debated, because its narrative power is a fact whatever its filing category, and because the debate itself teaches Section I's lesson, that Egypt's genres refuse our boxes.

XII. Hymns at the Boundary — Praise as Literature

Hymns stand at this article's border, and the border is worth patrolling carefully. A hymn carved in a temple or copied into a funerary papyrus is liturgy; the same hymn copied onto a school ostracon, anthologised, and studied for its language has entered literature's economy without leaving religion's. Egypt did not patrol this border, and neither, in practice, do the modern anthologies: Lichtheim's volumes give hymns and prayers a full part in each period, and Parkinson's Middle Kingdom corpus includes the great hymns to Senusret III and to the Nile.

The Middle Kingdom Hymns to Senusret III, preserved on a Lahun papyrus, praise the king in a cycle of songs of mounting intensity, in paraphrase celebrating him as the border, the herdsman, and the refuge of his people. Read as state theology they are royal doctrine; read as poetry they are among the most accomplished compositions of the age, built on refrain and parallel line. The Hymn to the Nile, the inundation hymn copied endlessly in the New Kingdom schools, praises the flood in the same doubled way, as god and as the year's bread. In both cases the literary reading does not cancel the religious one; it records that the texts were made, and kept, with a craftsman's care that mere utility does not explain.

XIII. Royal and Loyalist Texts at the Boundary

A second boundary runs between literature and the royal inscription. Kings' deeds were published as carved narrative, the annals, the battle accounts, the triumphal poems, and some of those inscriptions cross into literature by any formal test: the Poetical Stela of Thutmose III, in which Amun speaks the king's victories in verse, or the Qadesh Poem of Ramesses II, a sustained verse narrative of the king alone in the battle, copied on papyrus as well as carved on temple walls. The papyrus copies are the tell: a text the schools excerpted and the libraries kept had a life beyond its wall.

The Loyalist Teaching, treated in full in Wisdom Literature, Section XIX, stands exactly on this boundary, and is cross-referred rather than retold here: an instruction in form, it is also the Middle Kingdom's most direct piece of royal devotion, its first half a hymn to the king's life-giving power carved by an official on his own memorial stela. Its presence in both articles' territory is not an error to be resolved but the boundary's nature: the same text is instruction, hymn, and political literature at once, and any taxonomy that forces a single filing falsifies it.

Prophetic and discursive royal texts complete the boundary shelf. The Prophecy of Neferti, set at Khufu's court like the Westcar tales and composed in the 12th Dynasty, puts a vision of national disorder and a coming saviour king into a sage's mouth at the old king's table; the Admonitions of Ipuwer, surviving in a single Leiden papyrus, laments a world turned upside down in dialogue form. Both are translated by Lichtheim in Vol. I and by Parkinson among the Middle Kingdom poems. Both sit where lament, prophecy, political reflection, and entertainment meet, and modern attempts to file them as history, propaganda, or fiction alone have each failed on the texts' resistance.

XIV. Discursive Poems — Dialogue, Lament, and Harper's Songs

Between tale and instruction stands a group Parkinson's anthology calls discursive poems: compositions in which voices argue. The greatest is the Dialogue of a Man and His Soul (the Dispute Between a Man and His Ba), on Papyrus Berlin 3024, its beginning lost. In paraphrase, a man weary of life argues for death as release, and his soul, his Ba, argues back for life, reputation, and proper burial, in an exchange that moves through lyric passages of extraordinary beauty, the man's songs on death as a homecoming among them. Lichtheim translates it in Vol. I; no summary here attempts its poetry, and the dialogue's refusal of a settled winner is recorded as its most famous quality.

The Harper's Songs are the tradition's licensed scepticism. Sung, their rubrics say, by harpists at banquets and in tombs, the songs preserved in Papyrus Harris 500 and in Theban tombs counsel, in paraphrase, enjoyment of the day, since the dead sages' tombs have crumbled and no one returns to report. The song from the tomb of King Intef names Imhotep and Hardjedef, the very founders of the wisdom canon (Section III), as its evidence that monuments fail. That the tradition copied and kept its own refutation, in the same manuscripts as the love songs, is among the most attractive facts in this article: the canon contained its counter-voice.

XV. Love Poetry — the Manuscripts

The love songs are the New Kingdom's lyric achievement, and their survival is unusually well mapped. UCL's Digital Egypt summary, which this archive follows, puts it precisely: love songs survive from only one time and place, the Ramesside period, and in the main from the community of royal tomb craftsmen at Deir el-Medina in the 13th and 12th centuries BCE. There are three papyri carrying sets of longer songs, a pottery jar covered with another set, and some twenty ostraca bearing individual compositions.

The three papyri are the corpus's pillars. Papyrus Chester Beatty I, now in the Chester Beatty Library, Dublin, carries on its verso the most elaborate series, a cycle of seven stanzas, with further songs following. Papyrus Harris 500, in the British Museum, carries three groups of songs, and shares its roll with the Doomed Prince and a Harper's Song, an anthology in itself. A Turin papyrus (P. Turin 1966) carries the songs of the trees, in which garden trees speak in turn, inviting the lovers into their shade. The Cairo vase and the ostraca complete a corpus of more than fifty songs and fragments. Michael V. Fox's The Song of Songs and the Ancient Egyptian Love Songs (1985) is the standard scholarly study; Lichtheim's selection, from Chester Beatty I, Harris 500, and the Cairo vase, is in Ancient Egyptian Literature, Vol. II.

The Metropolitan Museum's exhibition publication Romance along the Nile (Allon and Patch, 2015 to 2016) sets the poems' themes plainly: ardent description of the beloved, the intoxication of love, the difficulty of pursuit, and illness caused by unrequited love, with the poems often invoking Hathor to secure the beloved's desire. The songs are written in Late Egyptian, in a formal version of the spoken language, and their speakers alternate, man and woman in turn, a feature the Chester Beatty cycle builds into its very structure (Section XVII). Who composed them is unknown; that the surviving copies are scribal work from a craftsmen's village, while the songs' settings suggest courts and great houses, is one of the corpus's open questions, stated here as open.

XVI. "Sister" and "Brother" — the Idiom of the Love Songs

Every reader of the love songs meets the same puzzle in the first lines: the lovers call each other brother and sister. The woman is the man's snt [Gardiner: S29-N35-X1] [𓋴𓈖𓏏], his "sister"; he is her sn [Gardiner: S29-N35] [𓋴𓈖], her "brother." The idiom needs explaining once, plainly, and then it explains the whole corpus: in the language of Egyptian love poetry, brother and sister are terms of affection between lovers, not statements of kinship. UCL's summary states it in so many words: as in many cultures, they call one another brother and sister.

The usage has a social root. Egyptian spouses could be addressed with sibling terms as marks of intimate equality, and the songs take the household word and make it the lyric word. Nothing in the poems suggests the lovers are kin; everything in them, the meetings at the door, the mother's house as obstacle, the longing across a river or a street, presupposes two households, not one. Translations that keep "sister" and "brother," as Lichtheim's and Fox's do, preserve the idiom's strangeness honestly; a reader who knows the convention reads past it to the tenderness it encodes, exactly as an Egyptian hearer would have done.

Around that central idiom the songs build their world from a small stock of images, handled with great freshness: the beloved's hair, neck, and hands described in catalogues of praise; the garden, the river, and the sycamore as meeting places; the door bolted or, in one famous song cycle's wish, persuadable; love as a sickness no physician can cure but the beloved's arrival; and over it all the Golden One, Hathor, invoked to tip the beloved's heart. The beloved in these songs is praised in the vocabulary of mrwt [Gardiner: G17-D21-G43-X1] [𓅓𓂋𓅱𓏏], love, a word the songs wear openly, whatever indirection their images practise.

One further honesty belongs in this section. The female voices of the songs are real voices in the text, speaking desire in the first person with a frankness that has struck every modern reader, but the manuscripts are scribal products, and whether women composed, performed, or only received these songs cannot be recovered from the copies. This archive records the voices as the texts give them, and the question of their makers as open, following the caution of the modern studies cited in Section XV.

XVII. Love Poetry — Performance, Music, and Hathor

Were the love songs sung? The corpus's own rubrics say that some were. Groups of songs carry headings, in Lichtheim's renderings, that present them as songs of entertainment, and the Chester Beatty cycle is built as performance: seven stanzas, introduced by the word for mansion, stanza by stanza, with the number of each stanza punned on in its opening or closing words, the man and the woman singing in alternation of their love in separation. UCL's stanza-by-stanza summary gives the arc: he praises her beauty; she, in her mother's house, longs for his coming; hope fails and revives on both sides; and the seventh stanza finds him sick after seven days apart, curable only by her. A text scored for two voices, numbered, and punned is a text made to be heard.

The performance setting most often proposed, stated here as a proposal and not a fact, is the banquet and the festival: the world of singers and harpists depicted on tomb walls, of drinking songs and Harper's Songs (Section XIV), and above all of Hathor's festivals, at which music, drink, and desire were the goddess's own liturgy. The songs' invocations of the Golden One fit that setting naturally. Against a purely festive reading stands the manuscripts' own context: these are written, collected, and copied texts, anthologised on papyri with tales and school pieces, and whatever their first performance life, their surviving life is a reader's and copyist's life, in the village of the tomb-makers.

Hathor's presence in the songs is the archive's reason for cross-linking this article to hers. The goddess of love, music, and drunkenness is the love songs' patron and their internal power: invoked to put desire into a mother's heart, to incline the beloved, to bless the meeting. The lovers' world, its gardens, sistra, and festival joy, is her world described from inside. See Hathor for the goddess, her festivals, and her musicians, and read the songs as that article's human-scale companion: what the temple celebrated, the songs feel.

XVIII. New Kingdom Miscellanies and School Texts

Beside the tales and songs stands the New Kingdom's most characteristic school genre, the miscellany: collections of model compositions, letters, hymns, admonitions, and praises of the scribal life, assembled by teachers as copybooks. Papyrus Lansing, Papyrus Anastasi, Papyrus Sallier, and the Chester Beatty papyri are the famous collections. The companion article treats the miscellanies as the Instructions' schoolroom (Wisdom Literature, Sections XVIII and XXII); this article claims them for narrative and secular literature on different grounds: they are where the period's occasional writing lives, and their model letters and satirical pieces are small literature in their own right.

The contents, in summary, mix the practical and the playful. Model letters rehearse the correspondence of officials: reports, requisitions, complaints, and one celebrated satirical letter, the letter of Hori (Papyrus Anastasi I), which mocks a rival scribe's pretensions by examining him, at merciless length, on the geography and logistics of the Levantine empire he claims to know. Hymns to the king, to Thoth, and to the scribe's palette stand beside pieces praising the scribal profession in the Satire of the Trades' tradition: every other calling miserable, the scribe's alone free. Lichtheim gathers a selection in Vol. II under her heading "Be a Scribe."

The miscellanies matter to this article for three reasons. First, they are transmission's engine room: the classics of Sections IV to IX survive in large part because miscellany culture kept boys copying. Second, they preserve the period's own literary criticism, the Immortality of Writers among them (Section III). Third, they show secular writing at its most various: Egypt's surviving letters, jokes, and professional boasts are here, in the schoolbooks, rather than in any archive of finished works. The village that produced so many of the copies is treated in Deir el-Medina.

The school texts also fix the social fact beneath the whole article. A sš [Gardiner: Y3], a scribe, was made, not born, and he was made by copying literature: the tales entertained him, the instructions formed him, the miscellanies drilled him, and the love songs, copied on his ostraca beside his exercises, suggest the curriculum had its pleasures. Egyptian literature's audience, its copyists, and in large part its authors were this one profession at different ages, which is why the scribal self-praise of Section III rings through the whole corpus like a ground note.

XIX. The Late Period Narrative World

After the New Kingdom, Egyptian storytelling changes its language, its script, and its centre of gravity. The surviving narrative of the first millennium BCE is written increasingly in Demotic, the cursive script and late stage of the language treated in Demotic, and its heroes are no longer Middle Kingdom courtiers but figures of a remembered, legendary past: warrior princes of the age of fragmentation, and above all the scholar-prince of Memphis whom Sections XX and XXI follow. Lichtheim's third volume, Ancient Egyptian Literature, Vol. III, covering the Late Period, is the standard anthology for everything in this part of the article.

The change is also one of scale and kind. Where the Middle Kingdom produced the polished, self-contained classic, the Demotic tradition produced the cycle: clusters of stories gathered around a hero or a family, copied and recopied across the Ptolemaic and Roman centuries, episodic, expansive, and comfortable with magic, violence, and the marvellous in proportions the classical tales rationed. Scholars compare the phenomenon, carefully and by analogy only, to the story cycles of other literatures that gather around a Charlemagne or an Arthur: a national past becoming a story-world. The comparison is a modern heuristic, flagged as such, and no Egyptian text states it.

Two great clusters dominate. Around the warrior heroes of a divided Egypt gather the stories modern scholarship calls the Inaros and Petubastis cycles, tales of princes and their armour, their feuds and single combats, set in the age after the empire and preserved in papyri of the Roman period, some of them very fragmentary. Around Prince Khaemwaset of the 13th Dynasty's great age gather the Setne tales, in which the hero is not a warrior but a reader: the first Egyptologist in fiction, a prince whose learning is his glory and his undoing. The next two sections take the Setne tales one at a time, as the Late Period's most completely surviving narrative works.

A note on period honesty closes the section. Much Demotic narrative is preserved in manuscripts of the Roman age copying compositions of uncertain, probably Ptolemaic or earlier, date, and much of it was published late and remains less read than the classical corpus. The picture of Late Period literature is therefore genuinely incomplete in a way the Middle Kingdom picture is not, and this article's proportions, two sections for Setne against six for the classical tales, reflect the state of survival and study, not a judgment of worth. See Late Period for the historical frame.

XX. Setne I — Setne Khaemwaset and the Book of Thoth

The historical seed is firm. Khaemwaset, fourth son of Ramesses II, high priest of Ptah at Memphis, was famous in his own afterlife for restoring ancient monuments and reading their inscriptions; later Egypt made him the type of the learned prince. In the Demotic tales he appears under his priestly title as Setne, and the First Tale, Setne I, survives on Papyrus Cairo CG 30646, an early Ptolemaic manuscript whose first two pages are lost. Lichtheim translates it in Ancient Egyptian Literature, Vol. III, and Robert Ritner's translation, in the Yale anthology The Literature of Ancient Egypt (3rd ed., 2003), is the other standard English version.

The tale, in paraphrase throughout, is built as boxes within boxes, like the nested chests in which its prize is kept. Setne hears of a book written by Thoth himself, buried with Prince Naneferkaptah in the Memphite necropolis. Entering the tomb, he finds the book shining between the mummies of Naneferkaptah and his wife Ahwere, whose spirits resist him; Ahwere tells, as a tale within the tale, how her husband stole the book from Thoth's keeping and how the god drowned her and their child in requital. Setne, undeterred, plays the dead prince at a board game for the book, loses game after game and is struck deeper into the ground with each loss, escapes by his brother's magic, and carries the book away.

The second half is the punishment, and it is a fiction inside the fiction. Setne is made to desire a beautiful and cruel woman, Tabubu, who draws from him, step by step, his property and, in the tale's telling, the lives of his children, before the whole scene dissolves: it was an illusion worked by Naneferkaptah, and Setne wakes naked and shamed before Pharaoh. He returns the book, and, at the dead prince's demand, finds and reunites the scattered family, bringing Ahwere and her child from Coptos to the Memphite tomb. The learned prince's arc is complete: knowledge seized by force is returned in humiliation, and the tale's scholarship ends as an act of restoration.

Ritner's introduction to the tale, in the Yale anthology, draws the literary moral this archive adopts: the Setne hero is intelligent but flawed, a reader whose desire for the book outruns his wisdom, in a line of such heroes running back to Sinuhe. The tale's sophistication is not in doubt: nested narration, symmetry between the theft in the inner tale and the theft in the outer, and a hero educated by a story told to him inside his own. A millennium and a half after the Shipwrecked Sailor, Egyptian narrative was still building tales about the power and danger of tales.

XXI. Setne II — Si-Osire and the Judgment of Rich and Poor

The Second Tale of Setne survives on a Roman-period Demotic manuscript, British Museum Papyrus EA 10822, written on the back of Greek documents, its beginning partly lost. Its hero is not Setne but his son: Si-Osire, a child prodigy of learning and magic, born, the tale's frame says, in answer to prayer. Lichtheim translates the tale in Ancient Egyptian Literature, Vol. III. Setne himself is demoted, affectionately, to the role of the admiring and sometimes baffled father, the flawed scholar of Setne I become the straight man of his son's brilliance.

The tale's famous centre, in paraphrase, is a lesson in judgment. Father and son watch two funerals: a rich man's, splendid with mourners and goods, and a poor man's, carried out without ceremony. Setne wishes his own fate to be the rich man's. Si-Osire takes him down into the netherworld and shows him the reality: the rich man in torment, the poor man enthroned in honour near Osiris, because the weighing of their deeds, not their funerals, decided. The episode states, in narrative form, the oldest doctrine in this archive, that the judgment weighs the life and not the monument, and states it as a guided tour: Egyptian literature's most literal case of showing rather than telling.

The tale's second movement is a magicians' contest. A Nubian challenger brings to Pharaoh's court a sealed letter that must be read without being opened; Egypt's honour rides on the reading, and Si-Osire reads it, revealing, in paraphrase, an ancient shame of Nubian sorcerers defeated by an Egyptian sage, and then reveals the challenger himself as that ancient enemy returned. The boy then unmasks his own identity in the tale's closing turn: he is himself a great sage of the deep past, returned for this need, and he vanishes. Setne is left, as Sinuhe was left, an old man whose story has been overtaken by the powers it invoked.

Setne II's afterlife in scholarship turns on its parallels: the rich and poor men and their reversed fates have been compared, since the tale's first publication, with later Mediterranean story traditions of the same motif. This archive records the comparison as scholars make it, a resemblance and a possible line of influence debated in the literature, and claims no settled descent. What the manuscript itself proves needs no parallel: in Roman Egypt, a thousand years after Sinuhe, Egyptian storytellers in Demotic were still composing at full stretch, and a scribe still thought the old prince of Memphis worth a new tale.

XXII. The Demotic Story Tradition — Cycles and Survivals

Setne is the best-preserved peak of a range. The Demotic story tradition, as Lichtheim's third volume and the modern editions present it, includes the Inaros and Petubastis cycles of Section XIX, heroic tales of a divided Egypt's warrior princes, preserved in papyri chiefly of the Roman period and often in tatters; the tale of the prophet and the king in the Oracle of the Potter, a prophetic composition in Greek and Demotic transmission; and a body of shorter tales, animal fables, and narrative fragments whose publication is still catching up with their excavation. The tradition's full shelf cannot yet be written, and this section is a map of a coastline still being surveyed.

One Demotic work deserves its sentence for scale alone: the Myth of the Sun's Eye, a long narrative in which Thoth, in baboon form, persuades the distant goddess to return to Egypt, telling animal fables along the way to keep her amused and moving. It is at once myth, frame tale, and fable collection, the Demotic tradition's most capacious single composition, and its storytelling god, coaxing a goddess home with stories, is the tradition's own emblem: narrative as the instrument by which the lost are brought back.

The Demotic tradition also closes a circle this article opened. The classical tales were preserved by schools copying Middle Egyptian as a learned language; the Demotic tales were composed in the living written language of their day and copied in temple and community libraries under foreign rule, Persian, Greek, and Roman in turn. Egyptian literature's last centuries are not a decline from its first: they are the same activity, storytelling in the current hand, carried on by the same profession in harder times, until the tradition passes, in Roman Egypt, into the Greek novel's world and the Coptic literature that follows. See Demotic and Coptic.

XXIII. Narrative Techniques — Frames, Nesting, and Verse

Gathered from the works above, Egyptian narrative's toolkit can be listed, and the list is a rebuttal to any notion of the tales as artless. Frame narration: Sinuhe's tomb autobiography, the Shipwrecked Sailor's consolation, Westcar's court of storytellers, Setne's tomb visit. Nested tales: the serpent's story inside the sailor's inside the follower's; Ahwere's tragedy inside Setne's quest. Embedded documents: the royal decree and the letter exchange in Sinuhe, the petitions in the Eloquent Peasant, the sealed letter in Setne II, each quoted as a document inside the prose that carries it, and each turning the plot at its hinge.

First-person voice is the classical tales' signature: Sinuhe, the sailor, and in its way the Peasant's petitions all speak as "I," borrowing the authority of the autobiographical inscription, Egypt's oldest prose form, and bending it to fiction. Verse in prose's clothing is the second signature: the classical tales are written as verse lines, marked with red verse points in careful manuscripts, organised in couplets and parallel members, so that what anthologies print as prose paragraphs was laid out by its makers as poetry. Parkinson's decision to title his anthology Poems is a statement of this fact, and this article follows it.

The later tradition adds its own devices: the Demotic tales' episodic quest structure, their contests of magic conducted as formal duels of knowledge, their taste for the recognition scene, the unmasking of Si-Osire and of the Nubian challenger, in which identity itself is the plot's last card. Across two thousand years the devices persist because the situations persist: a story is told to console, to entertain a king, to win a case, to coax a goddess home. Egyptian narrative theory, never written down as theory, is fully legible in its practice: the tale is an act performed on a listener, and the texts keep showing us the listener.

XXIV. Authorship and Anonymity

Who wrote these works? The honest answer divides by genre. The Instructions carry authors' names, Imhotep, Hardjedef, Ptahhotep, Khety, whether or not the named men composed the texts (see Wisdom Literature, Sections III and XIX, on attribution as tradition). The tales, by contrast, are anonymous almost without exception. No manuscript names the author of Sinuhe, the Sailor, the Peasant, or Westcar; the love songs name no poet; the Demotic tales name no storyteller. The canon of Chester Beatty IV (Section III) is a canon of instruction-writers, and its silence about tale-tellers matches the manuscripts.

What the manuscripts do name, sometimes, is the copyist. The D'Orbiney papyrus of the Two Brothers closes with a colophon naming the scribe Ennene, who made the copy, and school ostraca name their student copyists by their errors and their teachers' red corrections. The distinction is itself information about the culture: authority attached to the named sage whose voice a text borrowed, and to the named scribe whose hand preserved it, but the composing author of a tale, the person who first shaped Sinuhe's flight, stood outside the system of credit entirely, or inside a court and school world where the work, not the worker, was the property.

Modern scholarship's candidates and dates are therefore built from language, manuscript, and internal evidence alone, and this article has presented them as such: Sinuhe composed in the 12th Dynasty, by an author unknown; Westcar composed centuries after its setting, by an author unknown; the love songs composed in the Ramesside age, by authors unknown, possibly performed by singers unrecorded. Anonymity here is not a failure of the evidence to be lamented and filled with guesses. It is a fact about how this literature understood itself, and the archive preserves it unfilled.

XXV. Transmission and Canon Formation

The mechanisms of survival, gathered from the sections above, are three. The school preserved the classics: Sinuhe, the Peasant, the Instructions, and the hymns lived for a millennium as copy-texts, and their survival rate is the syllabus's survival rate. The library, the house of life and the private professional's box of rolls, preserved the working collections: the Ramesseum tomb's mixed library (Section IV) and the Chester Beatty family's papyri, tales, songs, and school texts together, are the type specimens. See House of Life. The single roll preserved, by luck, what neither school nor library multiplied: the Shipwrecked Sailor, the Westcar tales, the Doomed Prince, each in one copy.

Canon formation, on this evidence, was a by-product. No Egyptian authority decreed a canon; teachers chose copy-texts, librarians shelved rolls, families kept collections, and the aggregate of those choices, repeated for centuries, is what "the classics" means in Egypt. The Chester Beatty IV praise of writers (Section III) shows the process becoming conscious of itself, a scribal culture naming its own immortals, but the naming followed the copying, not the reverse. The canon was a syllabus before it was a pantheon.

Transmission also edited. School copies abbreviate, miscopy, and modernise; the two principal Sinuhe manuscripts differ in wording throughout, and Koch's edition exists because no single copy can simply be printed as the tale. Every translation cited in this article is therefore a reconstruction from witnesses, Lichtheim's and Parkinson's alike, and the reader who compares them will find small differences that are not errors but the manuscript tradition's grain. A literature transmitted by hand for two thousand years arrives with its history written into its variants.

XXVI. What Is Lost

This article has surveyed what survives. It closes its survey with the losses, because they are the larger part of the subject and the discipline's standing caution against every generalisation above. The losses can be counted in the survivals themselves. The Doomed Prince has no ending. Setne I has no beginning. The Dialogue of a Man and His Soul has no beginning. Westcar has no beginning. The Herdsman's Tale, copied with Sinuhe's family of manuscripts, is a fragment. Hardjedef's instruction, in the companion corpus, survives as an opening only. The classics arrive as torsos, and the torsos are the lucky ones.

Behind the damaged survivals stand the total losses, inferable but uncountable. Every tale attested in a single copy implies, by the mathematics of survival, tales attested in none. The Ramesseum box held one professional's library; Egypt had thousands of such readers across three thousand years. The love songs survive from one village in one period; nothing suggests Deir el-Medina invented the genre, and everything suggests the songs of other places and centuries are simply gone. Oral storytelling, the performances behind the performed texts, the songs sung and never written, are lost by definition, recoverable only as the written texts' shadows: the frame that shows a tale being told, the rubric that calls a poem a song.

The archive's rule for this section is the rule for the whole article: name the loss, do not fill it. No lost ending is reconstructed here, no lost corpus estimated into existence, no oral tradition ventriloquised. What Egypt wrote and kept is, even in fragments, one of the great literatures of the ancient world. What it wrote and lost is the dark around that light, and the honest survey ends by looking at both.

XXVII. Reading a Tale — a Student's Protocol

For the reader coming to these texts for the first time, this archive recommends a fixed order of operations. First, read the tale whole, in one sitting, in a single translation, for the story: Sinuhe in Parkinson or Lichtheim, the Sailor and the Peasant after it, the love songs aloud if possible. The classics were made to be experienced before they were made to be studied, and the first reading is the one the authors composed for.

Second, read the manuscript facts: which papyrus, how complete, how many copies, whose hands. Section IV's census for Sinuhe and Section XI's table for the New Kingdom tales are the models. A tale in sixty copies and a tale in one are different kinds of evidence, and the reader who knows which kind he is holding reads more honestly. Third, compare translations at one chosen passage: Lichtheim against Parkinson on Sinuhe's flight, or on a single love song, and watch two fine scholars divide the same Egyptian between them. The comparison teaches the language's grain faster than any grammar.

Fourth, keep the disciplines of this article: paraphrase is labelled paraphrase, and only short passages from named published translations are quoted as quotations, here or anywhere in the archive. Fifth and last, place the tale back in its shelf: read Sinuhe beside the Instruction of Amenemhat, its dark twin; the Peasant beside the wisdom corpus's teaching on speech; the love songs beside Hathor's festivals; Setne beside the Demotic world that made him. Egyptian literature is a conversation among its genres, and it is best read, as it was copied, in company.
