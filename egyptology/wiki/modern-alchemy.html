I. The Ontology of Modern Practice — Systematic Re-activation

Modern Alchemy is not a nostalgic revival of a dead pseudo-science, but the Systematic Re-activation of the Unbroken Chain connecting Ancient Kemet to the present. The work of the House of Life continues through dedicated circles of practitioners who utilize traditional laboratory methods alongside modern consciousness studies.

In this research framework, modern alchemy is viewed as High-Fidelity Frequency Management. The practitioner seeks to refine their own Composite Soul (Ba, Ka, Ib) while simultaneously working with material substances to manifest higher-dimensional data into the physical plane. Alchemy is the Science of Transmutation—the technology used to bridge the gap between the "Lead" of habitual, low-frequency existence and the "Gold" of transfigured, high-frequency awareness (Akh).

A note on evidence, because this article surveys a living practice and liveliness invites credulity in both directions. Where the article states a checkable fact — a date, an institution, a publication, a pharmacopoeial monograph — it follows the standard of the rest of the archive: documented, and checkable by the reader. Where it reports what practitioners believe, claim or experience, it says so, in those words, at the point of report (§XXII). And where the tradition's own account of itself is the only source — a lineage, a master's biography, an institution's inner history — the article attributes it to that account, as it does for Bartlett's Paralab years (§XII), rather than laundering testimony into fact. Modern alchemy deserves the same courtesy the archive extends to ancient Egypt: its real history, which is interesting enough, without invented antiquity or invented results.

II. Defining the Art — The Raising of Vibrations

Within the modern tradition's own vocabulary, to practise alchemy is to raise the vibration of a substance — and, by correspondence, of the practitioner. The phrase is the tradition's self-description, recorded here as such: a prepared medicine is said to carry a finer, more coherent pattern than the crude material it was made from, and the operator's task across the operations of §III is to refine matter and awareness together, each serving as the measure of the other.

The term belongs to the modern revival rather than to the older literature, whose authors spoke instead of purification, exaltation, and the separation of the subtle from the gross. It is not a quantity in physics, and this archive does not present it as one; it names how contemporary practitioners understand what they are doing, in the same way that Ma'at alignment (§X) names the ethical condition they hold the work to require.

III. The Wet Path — Laboratory Operativity

The Wet Path (Via Humida) operates through the physical laboratory. It utilizes glassware, heat, and solvents (menstrua) to perform operations on plants, minerals, and metals.

In the modern context, the laboratory acts as a Microcosmic Map of the practitioner’s internal process. Every distillation, calcination, and conjunction performed in the vessel is a physical demonstration of the practitioner’s ability to direct Heka. The Wet Path provides an Objective Feedback Loop: the physical changes in the substance (the color stages of Black, White, and Red) are indicators of the successfully applied theurgical intention. See Alchemy — The Sacred Art.

The Wet Path's humblest instrument carries its longest pedigree. The water bath — the bain-marie — descends from the balneum Mariae attributed to Maria Hebraea and transmitted by Zosimos: a vessel within a vessel of boiling water, capping its charge at the bath's temperature, for digestions and evaporations that direct fire would scorch. It is the one ancient alchemical technology that never left the laboratory or the kitchen; every double boiler and laboratory water bath in current use is Maria's device, uninterrupted. A modern spagyrist digesting a tincture in a water bath is therefore doing something more literal than 'following tradition': she is using the founding apparatus of her art, in its original form, for its original purpose — gentle, capped, patient heat.

IV. The Dry Path — Internalized Theurgy

Alongside the Wet Path of §III, the literature of the Work distinguishes a Dry Path (Via Secca, also called the short or royal way): work carried out in the crucible at high temperature, without the prolonged solvent circulations of the humid way, and correspondingly faster and harder to control. In the accounts transmitted by Fulcanelli and set out most openly by his pupil Eugène Canseliet, the dry way is bound up with the antimony work — stibnite and its regulus — and is treated as the more dangerous of the two routes precisely because its fires are literal and its stages compressed.

The contrast the texts draw is one of tempo and risk rather than of goal: the Wet Path works over months at gentle heats in glass, the Dry Path in days at furnace heat in the crucible, and both are presented as roads to the same Stone. This archive records the distinction as the literature states it; the internalised reading of the Dry Path as work within the practitioner's own vessel is taken up at §XVIII.

Canseliet is the dry way's chief witness, and his testimony should be characterised exactly. Eugène Canseliet (1899–1982) presented himself, from the prefaces to Fulcanelli's books onward, as the master's pupil and the tradition's continuator; his own works — on alchemy, on the two alchemical dwellings, on the work's stages — describe the dry way's antimony path with a fullness Fulcanelli never permitted himself, while withholding, by his own declaration, the one operative secret on which, he held, everything turned. Scholars of the revival treat Canseliet as a primary source for 20th-century French practice and as an interested one: the pupil who controls the master's posthumous meaning controls the lineage. The archive cites him in that double capacity — witness and gatekeeper — which is also, §XII suggests, the structural position of every modern lineage-holder, Bartlett included.

V. The Operations — Calcination, Distillation and the Rest of the Grammar

Modern laboratory alchemy speaks a grammar of named operations, inherited from the medieval and early modern laboratory and still used, in the same senses, in the schools of §XVI. The list below gives the core vocabulary as the tradition itself defines it — the verbs of the Wet Path (§III) — because a reader who cannot parse the operations cannot grade any claim made with them.

  • Calcination. Reduction of a body to ash or calx by strong heat — the fate of the plant marc in §VII, and of metals and salts in the mineral work of §VIII.
  • Distillation. Separation by vapour and condensation in the alembic line that descends, name and form, from Maria's apparatus (§III): the drawing-off of a plant's spirit, the rectification of solvents.
  • Fermentation and digestion. The slow work of warmth and time — a material sealed and held at gentle heat, in the bain-marie or the dung-bed of the older books, until it changes its nature: putrefaction as the gateway operation of the plant work.
  • Solution and filtration. Dissolving a calx or ash in water or spirit, and clearing it — the leaching step by which the Salt of a plant is drawn from its ashes.
  • Coagulation and cohobation. The fixing of the volatile: cohobation pours a distillate back over its residue and distils again, concentrating by repetition; coagulation is the general name for bringing a dissolved or volatilised body back to fixity.
  • Circulation. A sealed, self-returning digestion in a pelican or circulator vessel, vapour rising and condensate falling in a closed loop for weeks — the maturation stage to which practitioners attribute the 'exaltation' of a finished tincture.
  • Sublimation. The rising of a solid directly to vapour and its collection above — the operation of the volatile salts, and, with mercury, sulfur and arsenic compounds in the old books, among the most dangerous in the grammar (see §XX).

Two things about the list are worth noticing. Every operation in it is also an operation of classical chemistry — the continuity is the Albertus school's founding claim (§XIII). And none of them is exotic equipment: furnace, still, water bath, sealed vessel and balance exhaust the traditional bench. What the tradition adds to chemistry is not apparatus but interpretation and sequence — which operations, in which order, on which day (§XI), toward which of the three principles (§VI).

The operations also mark the boundary between the article's two modern alchemies. Every operation in the list above is performed, with the same names, by the laboratory schools; the internal school of §XVIII performs them in imagination, mapping calcination onto the burning-off of habit, distillation onto the refinement of attention, cohobation onto the return of insight to its residue. The shared grammar is why the two schools can talk to each other — and why their talk so easily confuses outsiders, for whom a 'calcination' may be a crucible or a crisis. This article disambiguates by context wherever the term does work: operations are laboratory operations unless the section says otherwise, and the internal senses are introduced, each time, as the internal school's reading.

VI. Plant Spagyrics — The Extraction of Essences

Spagyric medicine is the most accessible point of entry for modern laboratory work. It involves the separation and recombination of a plant's three principles:

SULFUR (SOUL)

The essential oils and active volatile principles. Represents the plant's unique Vibrational Signature. Corresponds to the Ba.

MERCURY (SPIRIT)

The alcohol produced through fermentation. Represents the Carrier Wave of the life-force. Corresponds to the Ka.

SALT (BODY)

The mineral salts extracted from the plant's calcined ash. Represents the Grounding Matrix. Corresponds to the Khat.

Recombining these purified principles creates an Integrated Medicine. Unlike standard herbal extracts, a spagyric preparation addresses the patient's entire composite being, restoring balance through "Vibrational Realignment."

The three principles should be read as the tradition reads them — as principles, not chemicals. Sulfur is not brimstone, Mercury is not quicksilver, and Salt is not table salt, though each principle borrows its name from the substance that best displays it: the combustible and oily, the volatile and spirituous, the fixed and earthy. Paracelsus's demonstration, repeated in every primer since, is a burning piece of wood: the flame is its Sulfur, the smoke and vapour its Mercury, the ash its Salt. The spagyric procedure of §VII is that bonfire slowed down and put in glass — fermentation and distillation for the volatile, calcination and leaching for the fixed — which is why the schools insist the method is the philosophy enacted, and not a metaphor laid over a tincture.

VII. Spagyric Procedure in Full — Separation, Purification, Recombination

The spagyric method of §VI, set out as a bench procedure, is the best-documented process in modern alchemy — described in the same terms by Paracelsian tradition, by Manfred Junius's Practical Handbook of Plant Alchemy, and by Robert Allen Bartlett in Real Alchemy (2007), from which the staging below is drawn in paraphrase. The word itself states the method: spao, to separate, and ageiro, to gather together — the coinage tradition assigns to Paracelsus.

THE STAGES, AS THE SCHOOLS TEACH THEM

  • 1. Preparation and fermentation. The plant — harvested, in the full traditional form, at its planetary election (§XI) — is crushed and set to ferment in water and its own juices. Fermentation generates the plant's own alcohol: its Mercury, the spirit, made by the plant itself rather than added to it, in the strict form of the method.
  • 2. Distillation of the Mercury. The fermented mass is distilled, and the spirit redistilled to purity and set aside. What the alcohol has carried with it — the plant's volatile oils and aromatics — constitutes its Sulfur, the soul; in procedures that separate them, the oil is drawn off as the Sulfur in its own right.
  • 3. Calcination of the body. The spent plant matter — the marc, the caput mortuum — is dried and burned in a crucible to ash, then heated on until the ash is white or grey-white: the blackening and whitening of the old colour-stages enacted on the plant's body.
  • 4. Extraction of the Salt. The white ash is leached with distilled water; the solution is filtered and the water evaporated, yielding the plant's mineral salts — chiefly potassium carbonate with the species' trace minerals. Dissolving, filtering and evaporating are repeated, in careful work several times, until the salt is clean and white.
  • 5. Cohobation — the reunion. The purified Salt is returned to the reserved Mercury and Sulfur — ground in, dissolved, and allowed to marry the tincture. This is the step an ordinary herbal tincture never takes, and the tradition's whole claim rests on it: body restored to spirit and soul, each purified in the interval.
  • 6. Circulation and maturation. The recombined preparation is sealed and circulated at gentle heat — traditionally for a philosophical month of forty days — and then aged, in the Albertus line's practice, with samples retained for later analysis (§XIII).

Safety, stated as procedure rather than decoration: the method works with flammable spirit over heat, with glass under thermal stress, and with crucible temperatures that take ash to whiteness. Its hazards at plant level are the ordinary serious hazards of a chemistry bench — fire, burns, fumes — and they are managed by the ordinary disciplines: no open flame near spirit, ventilation, eye protection, and the laboratory training the schools require before the mineral work (§XX) is opened at all.

VIII. Mineral Alchemy — The Purified Earth

Beyond the plant work of §VI, the modern curriculum takes up the mineral kingdom: salts, and then the metals and their ores, worked through the acetate route and kindred preparations transmitted in the Paracelsian literature. In the teaching sequence documented for the Albertus school (§XIII), mineral and metallic work forms the third and final stage of instruction, after plant and animal work, and its preparations — the oils and tinctures of the metals among them — are correspondingly the longest-aged and most closely held of the laboratory's products.

It is also the stage at which the tradition's safety discipline bites hardest. The ores in question include antimony and mercury compounds, and practitioners in the Albertus line insist on dried solvents and analytical checking for metallic work, since even the small water fraction in ordinary rectified spirit can dissolve a potentially toxic amount of a mineral salt into a preparation (see §XXI). Mineral work is therefore presented in the literature as advanced work, undertaken only after the plant operations are routine.

The acetate route, named in this section's introduction, deserves its sentence of explanation. In the Paracelsian mineral literature transmitted to the Albertus school, metals and their calces are opened with vinegar and its distilled and radical forms — the acetates being soluble, workable salts from which the tradition prepares its metallic tinctures and oils. The route is long (digestions and distillations counted in weeks), and its intermediates include the toxic compounds catalogued at §XX, which is why the curriculum places it last, behind years of plant work, and why this archive describes its existence and its place in the sequence without describing its bench detail. Description has a stopping point, and mineral alchemy is where this article observes it.

IX. Monatomic Elements — The White Powder Mystery

One of the most controversial and high-priority areas of modern alchemical research is the investigation of Monatomic Elements (also known as ORMUS or High-Spin state matter).

Modern promoters present these substances as the material correlates of an ancient Potable Gold. The Egyptian identification does not hold: mfkꜣt means turquoise, the product of the Sinai mining expeditions under Hathor, Lady of Turquoise; no Egyptological source attests it as a powder used by any priesthood (see Potable Gold & White Powder). Promoters claim that in this state metals like gold and iridium lose their metallic bonds and exhibit Superconductive Properties; those claims have never been confirmed in peer-reviewed literature and remain scientifically unverified. From a theurgical perspective within the modern tradition, monatomic elements are treated as High-Bandwidth Intermediaries for the soul — a belief of that tradition, recorded here as such.

The commercial form of the monatomic claim belongs in this survey, since for most contemporaries it is modern alchemy's public face. Powders and liquids sold as ORMUS — precipitated, by vendor-specific methods, from seawater, salt solutions or metal salts — are marketed in the vocabulary of Hudson's lectures (see the physics section of Potable Gold), with meditation, healing and perception claims attached. The archive's grading, applied there and repeated here, is threefold: the Egyptian identification is false (mfkꜣt is turquoise); the physical claims are unverified and unreplicated; and the products are uncharacterised in any published independent analysis. Within the laboratory tradition proper, the monatomic episode is generally regarded as a cautionary tale — the Albertus school's insistence on analysis (§XXII) exists, in part, so that its preparations can be told apart from exactly this. A survey that omitted the episode would flatter the field; a survey that adopted its claims would betray the archive.

X. Ma'at Alignment in Practical Work

Modern alchemy emphasizes that laboratory results are directly dependent on the Ethical Integrity of the operator.

A practitioner who works with a heart heavy with Isfet (greed, anger, or dishonesty) will find their experiments fail or produce unstable results. This is the Law of Resonance. In the Hall of Judgment, the heart was weighed against the feather of Ma'at; in the laboratory, the success of the transmutation is the weight. The "Integrated Alchemist" must maintain a state of ritual purity and ethical alignment to successfully "Command" (Hu) the spirits of the matter.

XI. Astrological Calibration — The Timing of the Stone

Modern laboratory practice in this tradition is electional: operations are begun at astrologically chosen times. The documented pattern is planetary — work on a plant or metal is commenced in the day and hour of the planet that tradition assigns to it, harvests are timed to the relevant planetary and lunar conditions, and long circulations are counted against the calendar of the heavens as the older texts prescribe. The scheme descends from the electional astrology of the medieval and Renaissance art, in which the timing of an operation was held to be part of its recipe rather than a decoration upon it.

Practitioners record the elections they used alongside the operations themselves, so that a preparation carries its timing as part of its laboratory record. Whether the heavens act on the vessel is the tradition's claim, held as such; that contemporary spagyric practice is in fact organised around planetary timing is a matter of its own documented routine, visible in the curricula and workbooks of the schools surveyed at §XVI.

The mechanics of election, for readers who have not met them, are determinate and computable — whatever one thinks of their efficacy. A planet's day runs from sunrise: Sunday to the Sun, Monday to the Moon, and so through the week in the Chaldean order. The daylight and the night are each divided by twelve into planetary hours of unequal, seasonal length, ruled in sequence from the day's ruler. To begin a solar work 'in the day and hour of the Sun' is therefore an instruction as precise as a temperature: Sunday, first hour after sunrise, or the eighth hour, and so on. Practitioners compute elections from ephemerides and record them in the laboratory notebook beside weights and durations (§V) — a survival, inside a working laboratory, of the Hellenistic electional astrology that the medieval art inherited entire. The archive records the system as the tradition's documented routine; the heavens' action on the vessel remains, as this section's main text says, the tradition's claim.

XII. Robert Allen Bartlett — The Paralab Years in His Own Account

The modern Albertus line has a primary source, and it is Bartlett's own. In the introduction to Real Alchemy: A Primer of Practical Alchemy (2007), Bartlett sets out his formation in the first person, and the dates in this section are his, as the task of this archive requires: precision about institutions begins with not confusing them.

  • 1974. Bartlett begins intensive study at the Paracelsus Research Society (PRS) in Salt Lake City — later Paracelsus College — under Albert Riedel, Frater Albertus. His first class, the Prima, opens the graded curriculum of §XIII: the classes ran, in his account, Monday to Saturday, nine to five, with homework and continuous laboratory work, for two weeks each year across seven years of returning study.
  • 1976. At Albertus's prompting he returns to university to finish the chemistry degree he had suspended — Albertus's condition, in Bartlett's telling, for the work he hoped to do: the laboratory of the Art staffed by a trained chemist.
  • 1979. On graduating, he is appointed Chief Chemist at Paralab — and the institution must be named exactly. Paralab was the commercial arm of the enterprise, its producing laboratory for herbal and mineral preparations made on alchemical principles for research and alternative-healthcare use. Bartlett was chief chemist of Paralab, not 'of the PRS': the Society taught; Paralab manufactured and analysed. The distinction is his own, and accounts that blur it misplace both the man and the institutions.
  • Late 1983. Paralab closes, and the college with it in the same unwinding; Albertus dies in 1984. Bartlett had been selected, he records, as a Director of Research for the projected Tristar complex — college, laboratory and healing-arts centre united — which Albertus's death left unbuilt.
  • 2003. Bartlett founds the Spagyricus Institute to teach the work in the PRS succession (§XVI), and publishes the graded method in Real Alchemy (2007) and The Way of the Crucible (2009).

Bartlett's account is a practitioner's testimony and is cited here as such — for institutional facts (dates, roles, the PRS/Paralab distinction) it is the best source there is, being the recollection of the office-holder, corroborated by the conference and publication record of the revival. His claims for the preparations themselves are a different category, and §XXII grades them accordingly.

Bartlett's later teaching completes the account his introduction begins. At the Spagyricus Institute (founded 2003) he taught the graded work in the PRS sequence — plant spagyrics first, in courses built around the bench operations of §V and §VII — and his conference workshops of the 2000s carried the same curriculum to the wider revival (§XVI). His published stance toward evidence is the one §XXII grades the field by: preparations described with their analyses, claims for his best-known work stated with its analytical limits attached, and the chemistry degree Albertus made him finish standing behind the whole enterprise as its entry condition. In a field whose public face is the ORMUS marketplace (§IX), that stance is the Albertus line's distinguishing mark, and this article has treated it as such throughout.

XIII. The Legacy of Frater Albertus — PRS and Beyond

Frater Albertus — Albert Richard Riedel (1911–1984) — is the pivotal figure of the 20th-century laboratory revival in the English-speaking world. Trained in the Rosicrucian milieu of the 1930s and 1940s, he founded the Paracelsus Research Society (PRS) in Salt Lake City in 1960 and taught alchemy there as a graded laboratory curriculum — Prima, Secunda, and Tertia classes moving from plant work through the animal kingdom to the mineral and metallic work of §VIII. Paralab, the Society's laboratory, was its commercial and analytical arm, producing spagyric preparations and cell salts and employing trained chemists; Robert Allen Bartlett, who took his first PRS class in 1974, served as its chief chemist from 1979 until the Society's closure in late 1983.

Albertus's Alchemist's Handbook (1960) carried the graded, hands-on method to readers the Salt Lake classes could not reach, and his insistence that students complete ordinary scientific training — he sent Bartlett back to finish the chemistry degree he had suspended — set the line's characteristic posture: laboratory alchemy practised with, not against, analytical chemistry. The schools and teachers of §XVI descend from PRS directly or by reaction to it.

The Tristar project, mentioned in Bartlett's account (§XII), is the school's might-have-been and belongs in the record: Albertus's plan to unite college, producing laboratory and healing-arts centre on one site — teaching, manufacture and clinic under one roof, the House of Life re-founded as a Utah campus. It died with him in 1984, unbuilt. The revival that actually happened was diasporic instead: students carrying the curriculum to their own benches and institutes (§XVI), the books carrying it further (§XV). The archive notes the pattern because it recurs in the tradition's history — Fulcanelli's master likewise vanished, leaving books and pupils — and because a lineage that survives by teaching and text, rather than by institution, is exactly the kind whose documents must be kept with the care §XVII describes.

XIV. Fulcanelli and the 20th Century Transition

Fulcanelli is the pen name on the two books that re-set the terms of French alchemy: Le Mystère des cathédrales (The Mystery of the Cathedrals), published in Paris in 1926, which reads the sculpture of the Gothic cathedrals as an alchemical programme in stone, and Les Demeures philosophales (1930). Both appeared with prefaces by the author's declared pupil, Eugène Canseliet, who maintained for the rest of his life that his master was a single, accomplished adept who had withdrawn from public view.

Who Fulcanelli was remains an open question. Candidates proposed in print include the artist Julien Champagne, who illustrated the books; René Schwaller de Lubicz; the bookseller Pierre Dujols; Canseliet himself; and a small committee of these men — and Canseliet rejected each of these identifications in turn. No identification has been established, and the archive records the question as unresolved: what is certain is the books' effect, which was to hand the 20th-century revival a textual authority for the dry way (§IV) and for the cathedral symbolism taken up across the modern literature.

The Fulcanelli literature's afterlife in English began with translation — The Mystery of the Cathedrals in Mary Sworder's rendering (1971) — and continued through the century's occult revival, in which the cathedral-reading became a genre of its own. Its scholarly assessment has since caught up with its popular one: historians of alchemy treat the two books as major documents of the 20th-century French revival — learned, serious in their command of the older literature, and evidentially worthless about the medieval cathedrals, whose sculptors left no sign of the programme Fulcanelli reads in them. That double verdict is the archive's: Fulcanelli is a primary source for modern alchemy (§IV, §XVII) and a secondary fantasy about the Middle Ages, and the sections that cite him cite him in the first capacity only.

XV. The Books of the Revival — Handbook, Primer and Curriculum

A laboratory tradition that teaches bench-side (§XVI) still lives by its books, and the revival's shelf is short enough to survey whole. Frater Albertus's The Alchemist's Handbook (1960) is the founding text of the Salt Lake school: a manual of graded laboratory practice — plant work first, with its tinctures, ens preparations and salt extractions in the manner of §VII — addressed to students Albertus expected to work, not merely to read. Its authority in the line is curricular: it is the book the Prima class put into hands.

Manfred M. Junius, an Ayurvedic physician and spagyrist working in India and Australia, published The Practical Handbook of Plant Alchemy in 1979 — the most systematic spagyric manual of the century, setting out the three-principles method, the operations of §V, and the preparation of plant quintessences with a pharmacist's completeness. Junius matters to this archive for a structural reason: his book demonstrates that by the 1970s the spagyric method was a documented, teachable procedure with a literature, independently of any single school's lineage claims — the same conclusion the German pharmacopoeial tradition (§XIX) supports from inside regulated pharmacy.

Bartlett's two books (§XII) complete the shelf: Real Alchemy (2007) as the primer — history, principles, and the plant work set out for a beginner's bench — and The Way of the Crucible (2009) carrying the method toward the mineral kingdom under the safety discipline of §XXI. Read together, the four books are the revival's canon: two generations, two continents, one method, argued over in detail and agreed in structure. What they do not contain, anywhere, is controlled clinical evidence for their preparations — the gap §XXII addresses.

XVI. Initiatic Orders and the Modern Chain

The modern work is carried by a loose ecology rather than a single church: teaching schools in the PRS succession, of which Bartlett's Spagyricus Institute (founded 2003) is the direct continuation of the Albertus line; practitioner associations such as the International Alchemy Guild, which archive the lineages' documents and conference record; and independent study circles and orders that combine laboratory instruction with the graded, initiatic framing their members inherit from the Rosicrucian and Hermetic orders of the previous century (see Esoteric Orders).

What these bodies share is transmission by instruction rather than by book alone: the operations of §§III–VIII are taught bench-side, in sequence, with the student's preparations examined before the next grade is opened. The chain they claim is therefore a chain of teachers — Albertus to his students, Canseliet to his — and it is as a documented succession of teaching, not as an unbroken antiquity, that this archive records it (see The Unbroken Chain).

The ecology has a documentary infrastructure worth naming. The International Alchemy Guild, founded in the revival's conference era, maintains archives of the lineages' papers and a journal; the Alchemy Conference circuit — at which Bartlett taught spagyrics workshops, in programmes that survive — gave the schools a common room in which PRS-descended, European and independent practitioners could compare preparations and pedigrees. University collections have begun to acquire the revival's papers, as they acquired Newton's (§XVII): the historian and the archivist now stand inside the tradition's transmission chain, preserving a movement that once preserved itself by secrecy. Whether that preservation is the chain's continuation or its museumification is a question practitioners debate among themselves; the archive records the debate as the field's own, unresolved.

XVII. The Scholars Arrive — Historians, Replication and the Making and Knowing Project

Modern alchemy has acquired, in the last half-century, a second literature besides its own: academic history of science, which arrived at the laboratory door with different questions. Lawrence Principe's The Secrets of Alchemy (2013) and William Newman's work — on Newton (§XVII's sibling case in the Newton article) and on the wider chymical tradition — established the scholarly frame this archive uses throughout: early modern chymistry as a real experimental culture, its cover-names decodable, its processes replicable, its transmutation claims unfulfilled. Principe replicates period processes at the bench as a historical method; the Leyden sulfur-water replication cited in Potable Gold is his.

The Making and Knowing Project at Columbia, under Pamela Smith, generalised the method: a 16th-century French practitioner's manuscript (BnF Ms. Fr. 640) reconstructed process by process — casting, pigment-making, imitation gems among them — on the principle that a recipe's silences are data, visible only to hands that have tried to follow it. The project's relevance to the modern schools is direct: it supplies the evidential standard — reconstruction, analysis, published failure — against which a living tradition's claims can be tested without caricature, and it is the standard Bartlett's own analytical instincts (§XIX) reach toward from inside the tradition.

The encounter cuts both ways, and the archive records it neutrally. Historians gain, from living practitioners, tacit knowledge no manuscript carries — how a regulus should look, when a circulation is 'right'. Practitioners gain a mirror in which their lineage claims are checked against documents, and not all of them survive the checking: the PRS succession is documented (§XII); successions claimed beyond it, into an unbroken antiquity, are not (§XVI). Modern alchemy in the 21st century is thus two cultures in one laboratory — a practice and its own historiography — and this article is written from the meeting point.

XVIII. The Internal Athanor — Psycho-Alchemical Integration

The Dry Path (Via Sicca) focuses on the Internal Athanor—the practitioner’s own body and awareness field.

By utilizing breath-control (the regulation of the Ka), visualization, and the vibration of divine names, the alchemist performs the transmutations within. This path seeks the Internalization of the Stone. The "Philosopher's Stone" is not an external object, but the fully realized and integrated state of the individual's own divinity. This path is faster but more dangerous, as the "Internal Heat" can cause systemic decoherence if the vessel (the character) is not sufficiently hardened through Ma'at.

The internal reading has its own classic text, and its status should be fixed. Carl Gustav Jung's Psychology and Alchemy (1944) proposed that the alchemists, working on matter in ignorance of its chemistry, projected the psyche's own transformation — individuation — into their vessels, so that the opus reads as a symbolic record of an inner process. Jung's reading conquered the 20th century's imagination of alchemy and is the direct ancestor of this section's 'internal athanor' framing. Historians of the laboratory tradition have since qualified it sharply: the early modern texts, read with their processes replicated (§XVII), are about matter first, and their authors would not have recognised a purely psychological opus. The archive's settlement is the one §IV already implied: the internal work is a real, modern practice with a Jungian pedigree; it is not a key that unlocks the historical laboratory, and the two alchemies — bench and psyche — are neighbours in the modern field, not translations of each other.

XIX. Alchemical Medicine — The Future of Spagyrics

Spagyrics is not only a revivalist practice; it persists as a living branch of pharmacy. In Germany, spagyric preparations remain in routine production by small spagyric pharmacies and are prepared under the spagyric procedures codified in the German Homoeopathic Pharmacopoeia, sold through ordinary pharmacies alongside other traditional preparations. Practitioners in the Albertus line point to the same continuity as the context for their own bench work: the methods of §VI are a documented pharmaceutical tradition with a present-day regulated form, not a reconstruction from texts alone.

The open question for the field is evidentiary. The analytical programme Bartlett describes from his Paralab years — process samples retained and re-analysed across decades, so that a preparation's maturation can be shown rather than asserted — is the direction in which the tradition's senior practitioners themselves say its claims must travel if they are to be taken seriously medically. The future of spagyrics, on that internal account, depends less on new preparations than on that kind of record.

The German pharmacopoeial position, stated in this section's main text, can be given its precise form. The German Homoeopathic Pharmacopoeia (HAB) codifies named spagyric procedures — the methods associated with named manufacturers and authors of the tradition, each defined by its fermentation, distillation, calcination and recombination steps in the manner of §VII — so that a preparation labelled by such a procedure is a defined article of pharmacy, reproducible by any laboratory that follows the monograph. Definition is not efficacy (§XXII), and the archive does not confuse them; but a tradition with pharmacopoeial monographs, producing pharmacies and a century of continuous manufacture is a different object of study from a revivalist reconstruction, and the future-of-spagyrics question is in large part the question of whether the defined article can acquire the evidence its definition deserves.

XX. Safety in Full — The Hazard Record of the Modern Laboratory

§XXI states the tradition's ethic of caution; this section states the hazards themselves, unsoftened, because the modern laboratory inherits the old laboratory's materials. The archive's process dossier is blunt about the classes involved, and its panels are adopted here as the standing record.

  • Mercury. Present in the historical mineral work as amalgam, cinnabar and vapour apparatus. Heating mercury or its amalgams releases mercury vapour, absorbed through the lungs, damaging the central nervous system and kidneys. No operation in the historical literature that heats mercury has a safe amateur form.
  • Arsenic compounds. Orpiment, realgar and sandarach — the yellowing and whitening agents of the old metal work — are arsenic sulfides; roasted, they volatilise arsenious oxide. Arsenic is a cumulative poison and carcinogen. Historical description only.
  • Lead and antimony. Lead enters as litharge, ceruse and cupellation metal (fume and respirable dust); antimony, the Albertus school's own regulus metal, is toxic in its compounds, and its preparations are the school's standing example of why analytical checking is not optional (§VIII).
  • Acids, solvents and fire. Aqua fortis and aqua regia belong to the post-medieval laboratory (§VIII's ancestry): corrosive, oxidising, gas-generating. Spirit distillation is a fire discipline. Furnace work adds carbon monoxide — colourless, odourless, and the ambient hazard of every charcoal-fired process in the corpus.

The modern schools' answer, in their own literature, is institutional rather than heroic: graded access (mineral work last), trained supervision, dried and checked solvents, analytical verification of preparations, and refusal to supply potent preparations outside the teaching relationship. A practitioner or vendor who treats the hazard record as initiation theatre — danger as proof of authenticity — has, by the tradition's own stated standard (§XXI), disqualified himself. The archive's position is the dossier's: everything in this section is documented as history and as warning; none of it is a procedure.

XXI. Ethics of the Art — Discernment and Secrecy

The tradition's own literature couples its teaching to a stated ethics of caution. Preparations involving antimony, mercury, and the mineral work of §VIII are taught as dangerous in incompetent hands; solvents for metallic work must be dried and preparations analytically checked, and the senior practitioners of the Albertus line present that discipline as part of the Art itself rather than as an external regulation. The same discourse governs dissemination: potent preparations are not sold indiscriminately, students are advanced grade by grade, and the schools' secrecy is defended by their members on safety grounds — that a published recipe without its bench discipline invites exactly the harm the discipline exists to prevent.

Discernment is the inward counterpart. The practitioner is expected to distinguish testimony from evidence — to say, as Bartlett does of his own best-known preparation, exactly what analysis does and does not show in it — and to refuse claims the bench has not earned. In the terms of §X, the ethics of the Art is Ma'at applied to the laboratory notebook: nothing asserted beyond what the work, honestly recorded, supports.

XXII. Claims and Evidence — What Contemporary Practice Asserts, and What Is Shown

A survey article owes its readers a grading of the field's claims, in the field's own categories. The claims contemporary practitioners make for their preparations are, in the main, three. First, process claims: that a preparation made by §VII differs chemically from a simple tincture of the same plant — in mineral content measurably, since the salts are really returned; in concentration and keeping quality arguably. These claims are testable and, at the level of composition, true almost by definition: the Salt is in the bottle. Second, traditional-use claims: that spagyric preparations have a documented history of use in European pharmacy (§XIX) — a historical statement, and a sound one. Third, efficacy claims: that the recombined preparation heals better than its parts, acts on the patient's constitution or 'vibration', or matures in virtue with age. These are the claims that matter most to practitioners — and they are, at present, practitioners' claims, presented in this archive as such.

The evidence position can be stated without polemic. Analytical chemistry of spagyric preparations exists and is growing; controlled clinical trials do not — a 2026 review of spagyric medicine's evidence base, surveying the literature, found laboratory and observational studies but no controlled clinical trials demonstrating efficacy beyond a conventional tincture. Practitioners answer, in their own terms, that the preparations are individualised, that their endpoints are constitutional rather than symptomatic, and that the trial form fits them badly — answers recorded here as the field's position, not adopted as the archive's. Bartlett's own standard (§XIX) remains the most demanding internal one on record: retain the samples, run the analyses across decades, and say exactly what the analysis shows. Until the field's claims are carried to that standard and beyond it, this article's labelling stands: the method is documented, the tradition is living, the medicines' superiority is claimed — by named people, in named books, as their claims.

XXIII. The 21st Century Renaissance — Re-activating Kemet

We are currently in a period of High-Bandwidth Re-initialization. The digital availability of ancient texts and the development of new scientific tools for analyzing subtle energies have created a unique opening for the Sacred Art.

The legacy of modern alchemy is the realization that Reality is Plastic. We are not passive observers of a dead universe, but active participants in an ongoing act of creation. By re-linking with the "Original Signal" of Kemet and applying it through both laboratory and internal praxis, we contribute to the maintenance of the cosmic order. The "Great Work" is the restoration of the earth to its status as a sacred vessel—the Black Land reborn in the light of the First Time.

The 21st-century renaissance, stripped of this section's opening rhetoric, has three documented components. The texts are open: the corpus of historical alchemy — Newton's million words among them — is transcribed and searchable in a way no previous century could offer (§XVII). The scholarship is experimental: replication has become a recognised historical method, and living practitioners are among its informants. And the practice is networked: schools, guilds, conferences and pharmacopoeial manufacturers (§XVI to §XIX) sustain a small, durable, international craft. What has not arrived is the validation the field's largest claims require (§XXII), or any Egyptian warrant for its modern forms (§IX). The Black Land's contribution to modern alchemy is real but historical — the Graeco-Egyptian workshop literature of Thebes and Panopolis, at the tradition's root (§XVII) — and the archive ends the survey where it began: with a living art, honestly labelled, working in the long shadow of an Egypt it did not descend from in the manner it sometimes claims, and does not need to have done.
