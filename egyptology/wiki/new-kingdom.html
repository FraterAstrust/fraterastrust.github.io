I. The Ontology of Empire — High-Bandwidth Expansion

The New Kingdom (c. 1550–1075 BCE) represents the absolute peak of Egyptian geopolitical power and theological complexity. After the trauma of the Hyksos occupation, the Pharaonic state underwent a fundamental evolutionary shift. It moved from the defensive, isolationist model of the Middle Kingdom to an expansive, military-oriented empire.

In this research framework, the New Kingdom is the High-Bandwidth Expansion Phase. The Pharaonic ideal shifted to the "Warrior-God," who actively pushed back the borders of Isfet through military conquest and the construction of colossal monumental resonators (temples like Karnak and Luxor). This was an era of unprecedented wealth, the "Golden Age" where the tribute of the known world flowed into the treasury of Amun. The state became a Global Data-Hub, synthesizing technologies and deities from the Levant and Nubia into a massive, complex theological engine.

The Shape of the Period — Three Dynasties, One Arc

The period divides into three dynasties with distinct characters. The 18th Dynasty (c. 1550–1295 BCE) is the age of conquest and consolidation, from Ahmose I to Horemheb. The 19th Dynasty (c. 1295–1186 BCE), founded from a military family of the eastern Delta, is the Ramesside restoration after Amarna, dominated by Seti I and Ramesses II. The 20th Dynasty (c. 1186–1075 BCE), refounded by Sethnakht after a brief succession crisis, holds the empire's shell for a century before the state divides (see §XIX). Across all three, the same institutions persist: the vizierate, the Amun priesthood at Karnak, the royal necropolis workforce, and an empire administered in two unlike halves — Nubia governed directly under a Viceroy, the "King's Son of Kush," and the Levant held as a chain of vassal city-states bound by oath and tribute.

The archive's dates follow the chronology of Ian Shaw (ed.), The Oxford History of Ancient Egypt (2000), used consistently across this site; other standard schemes differ by a decade or so at the period's edges, and the UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology, for example, ends the New Kingdom in 1086 BCE rather than 1075 BCE. The difference is one of convention, not of evidence, and is flagged here once so that cross-checking readers are not misled.

Empire's Two Halves — Kush and the Levant

The empire was administered asymmetrically, and the asymmetry is the key to its durability. In the south, Nubia was annexed outright: Egyptian towns, temples, and garrisons ran from the First Cataract to the Fourth, governed by the Viceroy of Kush, and Nubian gold — the empire's single largest revenue stream — flowed north as a state monopoly. In the north, Egypt annexed nothing: Canaan and Syria remained a patchwork of city-states whose rulers swore oaths, paid tribute, sent their sons to be raised at the Egyptian court, and appealed to Pharaoh against each other — the system the Amarna Letters show in operation. Direct rule where the gold was, indirect rule where the trade routes were: the New Kingdom's imperial economy in one sentence.

The receipts survive. The Annals of Thutmose III (§V) list tribute and booty campaign by campaign; the Harris Papyrus (§XII) totals the temple share at the period's end; and tomb scenes of officials such as the viceroy Huy, under Tutankhamun, show the Nubian tribute procession in kind — gold rings, ebony, ivory, leopard skins, live animals — arriving as a staged annual ceremony of submission and accounting.

II. The Theban Liberation — Ahmose I and the Hyksos Expulsion

The New Kingdom was born in the fires of liberation. Ahmose I (18th Dynasty) completed the task begun by his father Seqenenre Tao and brother Kamose: the total expulsion of the Hyksos from the Delta.

This military victory was viewed as a Theurgical Triumph. The Hyksos were identified with the chaotic force of Set in his purely destructive aspect. By driving them out, Ahmose successfully "reset" the frequency of the Two Lands. The liberation provided the "Hardware Upgrade" needed for empire: the Egyptians adopted the war chariot, the composite bow, and improved bronze-working—tools that they would soon use to project their Sekhem (Power) across the Mediterranean.

The War of Liberation in the Sources

The liberation war is unusually well documented for its period, and from both ranks of the army. Two stelae of Kamose, the last Theban king of the 17th Dynasty, narrate his council of war, his drive north past Cusae, and the capture of a Hyksos messenger carrying Apophis' letter to the ruler of Kush proposing an alliance against Thebes — the intercepted letter that explains the war's urgency: Thebes was caught between Avaris and Kush, and Kamose's successors struck north first. Kamose died before finishing the work; his brother Ahmose took Avaris itself after sieges and pursued the Hyksos to Sharuhen in the southern Levant, which fell after a further three-year siege (paraphrase of the Kamose stelae and the biography below).

The rank-and-file record is the tomb biography of Ahmose son of Ebana at el-Kab, a naval officer who served under Ahmose I, Amenhotep I, and Thutmose I and itemised his career campaign by campaign: the sieges of Avaris, the three years before Sharuhen, then expeditions south into Kush and north to the Euphrates, with the "gold of valour" — the Golden Flies — awarded to him repeatedly for bravery in the king's presence (paraphrase of the el-Kab biography). Between the royal stelae and the soldier's autobiography, the founding war of the New Kingdom can be followed almost year by year — and its lesson, that Egypt must never again be encircled, became the strategic doctrine of §III.

Avaris Excavated — the Hyksos Capital in the Ground

The enemy capital is no longer a name in the war texts. Excavations at Tell el-Dab'a, the site of Avaris, by the Austrian Archaeological Institute under Manfred Bietak, have uncovered the Hyksos city in plan: a Levantine-planned town of temples in Near Eastern style, palaces, and burials with horse and donkey interments, its material culture Middle Bronze Age Canaanite transplanted to the Delta — including fragments of Minoan-style wall painting in a later palace context, evidence of the Aegean connections the eastern Delta commanded. The stratigraphy also complicates the war narrative usefully: the "Hyksos" were the ruling stratum of a long-settled Asiatic population, and their expulsion (§II above) was the end of a century and a half of Delta history, not the removal of a raiding camp. The liberation founded the New Kingdom; the archaeology shows what it liberated Egypt from — a rival Egyptian-Levantine state, richer and better connected than Theban pride admitted.

III. The 18th Dynasty — The Thutmosid Zenith

The 18th Dynasty is the most famous and powerful lineage in Egyptian history. It established the "Thutmosid" model of kingship: an aggressive expansion into Asia and Nubia combined with a massive building programme at Thebes.

Pharaohs like Amenhotep I and Thutmose I established the professional military and the streamlined bureaucracy needed to manage a trans-continental state. This era saw the Standardization of Heka—the rituals of the state and the afterlife were recorded in exhaustive detail, creating the most stable initiatory environment in history.

Two structural inventions of the early dynasty outlasted it. The first is the professional standing army, with hereditary military families, foreign mercenaries, and promotion by royal favour — the ladder Horemheb and the early Ramessides would climb to the throne itself. The second is the office of God's Wife of Amun, held first by Ahmose-Nefertari, which gave the royal house a permanent female presence inside the Karnak priesthood; the office's later career, as the hinge on which Thebes changed hands, is traced in the Third Intermediate Period article (§VII there).

The Court and the Provinces

Beneath the throne the administration ran on the vizier — by the mid-18th Dynasty two, one for Upper and one for Lower Egypt — whose duties are set out in the famous Installation of the Vizier text copied into the tomb of Rekhmire under Thutmose III: daily report to the king, oversight of taxation, justice, water, and the palace, conducted, the text insists, without respect of persons (paraphrase of the Installation and Duties texts). The provinces were held through the old nome structure and the mayors of the great towns, bound to the centre by the treasury's audits and by the court's monopoly of high appointment. It is this machinery — vizier, treasury, granary, and the scribal service that staffed them — that the Amarna reform bypassed, the restoration rebuilt, and the Ramessides inherited intact.

IV. Hatshepsut — The Theurgical Queen

The reign of Hatshepsut (c. 1473–1458 BCE) represents a unique "Feminine Integration" of royal power. Taking the full titles of a King, she emphasized her divine birth from Amun and her role as the restorer of the temples.

Her mortuary temple at Deir el-Bahari is a masterpiece of architectural geometry. Designed by the sage Senenmut, its terraced structure aligns perfectly with the winter solstice sunrise, funnelling the solar light into the sanctuary of the goddess Hathor. Hatshepsut emphasized Trade over Conquest, most notably her expedition to the land of Punt, which brought back frankincense and myrrh—the "Subtle Materials" needed for the censing of the gods. Her reign was a period of high-frequency cultural and economic flourishing.

Punt, Obelisks, and the Record in Stone

The Punt expedition of Hatshepsut's Year 9 is documented in the middle terrace of Deir el-Bahari in the most detailed expedition record of the Bronze Age: five ships, the loading at Punt of myrrh resin, gold, ebony, ivory, and live incense trees — thirty-one of them carried with their roots balled in baskets for replanting in Egypt — and the Puntite chief Parehu and his wife Ati rendered with ethnographic care (paraphrase of the Deir el-Bahari Punt reliefs). The stated purpose was cultic: the incense terraces of Amun's temple were to grow their own supply, making Thebes a producer, not merely a purchaser, of the gods' aromatics.

Hatshepsut was also the Old Kingdom's heir in stone. Her twin obelisks at Karnak, quarried at Aswan and erected in under a year by her own inscription's account, stood among the tallest ever raised; the base inscription presents the pair as a single dedication of electrum-tipped granite to Amun, made, in her framing, with a loyal heart (paraphrase). Late in Thutmose III's sole reign — more than twenty years after her death — her images and names in many contexts were systematically attacked, a damnatio whose motive (personal revenge, or the constitutional embarrassment of a female king for a male-line ideology) remains debated; the delay itself argues against simple hatred.

The Divine Birth and the Coronation Cycle

Deir el-Bahari's northern colonnade carries the reign's constitutional argument in pictures: the Divine Birth cycle, in which Amun, taking the form of Thutmose I, begets the child Hatshepsut on Queen Ahmose, and the gods fashion the royal infant and her ka on Khnum's wheel. Set beside the Punt reliefs, the programme is complete — heaven supplies the birth, the coronation inscriptions supply the designation by her father, and Punt supplies the proof of divine favour in incense and gold. No pharaoh before her had needed the argument made so fully; none after her could ignore the precedent that a woman had made it and reigned for two decades on its strength.

V. Thutmose III — The Napoleon of Egypt

Following Hatshepsut, Thutmose III launched a series of 17 military campaigns that reached the Euphrates river. He is the archetypal "Warrior-King" of the New Kingdom.

Thutmose III viewed his conquests as acts of Ma'at. By subjugating the "vile Asiatics," he was expanding the domain of order and reducing the influence of Isfet. He was also a sophisticated naturalist, recording the exotic plants and animals he encountered in the "Botanical Room" at Karnak. His reign established Egypt as the Supreme Data-Node of the Bronze Age world, controlling the flow of gold, tin, and ivory.

Megiddo and the Annals

The first campaign, in Thutmose III's Year 23 (c. 1457 BCE), is the best-documented battle of the ancient world before Kadesh. A coalition of Levantine rulers under the king of Kadesh waited at Megiddo; Thutmose chose, against his generals' advice, the narrow Aruna pass, emerged before the city, and routed the coalition in a morning, then spent seven months reducing the stronghold by siege. The booty lists — hundreds of chariots, thousands of horses and cattle — and the campaign-by-campaign record of the following two decades were carved on the walls of the Amun temple at Karnak as the Annals, drawn, the inscription says, from the day-book kept by the army scribe Tjaneni on campaign (paraphrase of the Annals of Thutmose III).

The Annals were an administrative instrument as much as a boast: they fixed the tribute obligations of the conquered cities in public, permanent form. The "Botanical Garden" reliefs in the festival hall at Karnak performed the same operation for natural history, depicting the plants and animals of Syria collected on the Year 25 campaign and dedicated to Amun — an inventory of empire rendered as temple decoration. By his eighth campaign Thutmose had crossed the Euphrates and set up a boundary stela beside his great-grandfather Thutmose I's; the empire he bequeathed ran from the Euphrates bend to the Fourth Cataract.

The Empire Administered

Conquest was followed by system. The conquered Levantine cities kept their rulers, now bound by oath, tribute quotas recorded in the Annals, and the obligation of sending sons and brothers as hostages-guests to the Egyptian court, where they were raised Egyptian and returned as friendly kings — a hostage system that doubled as a cultural transmission line. Nubia, by contrast, was governed as Egypt: the Viceroy's administration, Egyptian temple towns at the cataracts, and gold quotas. Thutmose's own inscriptions mark the theory at the Euphrates, where he hunted elephants at Niy on the return march — kingship performed at the edge of the world. The administrative templates he fixed — tribute in the north, province in the south — were still the empire's operating system under Ramesses II, two centuries later.

VI. Amenhotep III — The Magnificent Resonator

The reign of Amenhotep III (c. 1390–1352 BCE) marks the moment of maximum imperial saturation. Known as "The Magnificent," his reign was characterized by peace, luxury, and the construction of colossal monuments.

He built the Luxor Temple and the massive mortuary complex guarded by the "Colossi of Memnon." Amenhotep III presented himself as the Living Aten (Solar Disk) on earth, pre-empting the solar focus of his son Akhenaten. His reign was a "High-Bandwidth Reception" of divine grace, where the Pharaoh's Ka was so potent that he was worshipped as a god in his own lifetime throughout the empire.

Diplomacy, Scarabs, and the King's City of the West Bank

Amenhotep III governed the empire of §V largely by correspondence and marriage, and the Amarna Letters preserve his diplomacy: marriage to Gilukhepa, daughter of Shuttarna II of Mitanni, who arrived with a retinue of 317 women, and later to Tadukhepa; correspondence with Babylon over gold and brides in both directions. At home he publicised his reign through an unprecedented medium — large commemorative scarabs issued in series, recording his marriage to the non-royal-born queen Tiye, the excavation of her pleasure lake at Thebes, and his lion hunts: one hundred and two lions shot in the first ten years, by the scarab's own count (paraphrase of the commemorative scarab texts). They are, in effect, the first royal press releases.

His building matched the publicity. On the Theban west bank he raised at Malkata a palace-city of painted mudbrick — the king's jubilee residence — beside the vast artificial harbour, the Birket Habu, dug for his sed-festivals; across the river his mortuary temple, fronted by the Colossi of Memnon, was in its day the largest in Thebes, its stele listing the endowments of a temple built, as the king says, as a fortress of millions of years (paraphrase). Malkata and the industrial quarters around it also housed the workshops that made the reign's luxury goods — glass and faience among them, manufactured by the processes set out in §XVII of the Amarna article — so that the "Magnificent" reign can be read in its workshops as well as its reliefs.

The Sed-Festivals and the Deified King

Amenhotep III celebrated three sed-festivals, in Years 30, 34, and 37, and used them to complete a transformation the inscriptions state openly: in his jubilee iconography and at his temple at Soleb in Nubia, the king is shown worshipping his own deified image — the living king as a god, "Nebmaatre, lord of Nubia," receiving offerings from Amenhotep the man. No earlier Egyptian king had systematised his own cult in his lifetime on this scale. The theology is the direct preparation for Amarna: if the king can be a manifest god beside Amun, his son's step — the king as sole mediator of a single manifest god — is a difference of degree argued as a difference of kind (see §VII and the Amarna article).

The reign's material signature survives in quantity: hundreds of statues, from the Colossi down to the granodiorite Sekhmet figures — several hundred of them — that lined his mortuary temple and the Mut precinct at Karnak, plausibly a liturgy in stone of the goddess's forms for the king's jubilee years. When later ages reused the Theban west bank, Amenhotep III's statuary seeded half the museums of the world; the reign that presented itself as the sun's dazzling disk achieved, in stone, something like permanence.

VII. The Amarna Interlude — Akhenaten's Solar Rift

The most radical event of the New Kingdom was the Amarna Revolution initiated by Amenhotep IV, who renamed himself Akhenaten. He attempted to dismantle the entire traditional Ennead and the priesthood of Amun, replacing them with the exclusive worship of the Aten (the visible sun disk).

In our framework, this was a Systemic Rift. By closing the traditional temples, Akhenaten cut the state off from its ancestral data-streams and its complex "Heka-Protection" network. The result was a loss of imperial control and a period of internal instability. For detailed analysis, see Amarna Period — The Solar Revolution.

The Interlude in This Article's Frame

Within the New Kingdom's arc, Amarna is an interruption of seventeen years inside a span of nearly five centuries — yet it consumed more of the state's accumulated reserves, institutional and material, than any comparable span of the period. The temples closed were the empire's banks and granaries (§XII); the court that moved to Akhetaten took the diplomatic archive with it (§VII of the Amarna article); and the restoration that followed had to rebuild not only a cult but an administrative class. That is why the interlude, brief as it is, receives its own article in this archive, and why the restoration documents of §VIII read less like routine succession texts than like the refounding charter of the state itself.

The interlude also marks the one moment when the New Kingdom's operating assumptions were tested against a designed alternative: one god, one mediator, one new city, one new art. The speed and totality of the reversion after 1336 BCE is itself evidence about the system the rest of this article describes — a distributed network of temples, priesthoods, and local cults that no single reign, however absolute, succeeded in replacing.

VIII. The Restoration — Tutankhamun and Horemheb

Following the death of Akhenaten, the state underwent a Systemic Recovery. The young Tutankhamun issued the "Restoration Stela," reopening the temples and returning the capital to Memphis and the spiritual center to Thebes.

The military general Horemheb eventually took the throne, systematically erasing the names of the Amarna kings from the records—a ritual act of Damnatio Memoriae (see Damnatio Memoriae Wiki) to excise the "corrupted data" from the national timeline. This restoration returned the state to the "High-Bandwidth" orthodoxy of Amun-Ra.

The Restoration Stela and the Edict of Horemheb

The Restoration Stela's programme was fiscal as well as theological: it records the re-endowment of the temples with land, staff, and new cult images of precious materials, and the appointment of priests drawn, it says, from the sons of local notables — a deliberate rebuilding of the priestly class the Amarna years had disbanded (paraphrase of the Restoration Stela of Tutankhamun). The court made the same point in names and in stone: Tutankhaten became Tutankhamun, the capital returned to Memphis, and Amarna was left to the desert.

Horemheb completed the settlement juridically. His Great Edict, carved at Karnak, is a reform of the administration itself: penalties for corrupt tax collection and military requisitioning, reorganisation of the courts, and the appointment of judges chosen for integrity (paraphrase of the Edict of Horemheb). A general with no royal blood, he also solved the succession with unusual deliberation, raising his vizier and fellow officer Paramessu — the future Ramesses I — as heir. The 19th Dynasty of §IX is therefore not a new departure but Horemheb's restoration made hereditary.

Ay — the Courtier Who Buried Two Kings

Between Tutankhamun and Horemheb stands the reign this section passes quickly over: Ay, God's Father, vizier, and the man depicted performing the Opening of the Mouth for the dead Tutankhamun in KV62 — the heir's ritual signature on the succession itself. Ay reigned about four years (c. 1327–1323 BCE), married (or retained) the widowed Ankhesenamun according to a ring bezel joining their names, and prepared his own tomb in the western Valley (WV23). His reward from the restoration he had stage-managed was to be its final victim: Horemheb usurped his monuments, his tomb was wrecked, and his name joined the excised in the king lists (§IX of the Amarna article). The restoration devoured its own administrators in order — first the heretics, then the men who had served them too visibly.

IX. The 19th Dynasty — The Ramesside Restoration

The 19th Dynasty, founded by Ramesses I and championed by Seti I and Ramesses II, sought to solidify the restoration through Monumental Hardening.

Seti I's temple at Abydos is one of the most beautiful and theologically rich structures in Egypt. It contains the "King List," which traces the royal lineage back to the gods, effectively "fixing" the state's historical legitimacy after the Amarna rift. The Ramesside kings viewed themselves as the defenders of tradition, using their vast resources to rebuild and expand the sacred sites of the past.

Seti I at Abydos — the King List and the Osireion

The temple of Seti I at Abydos is the restoration's theological charter. Its famous King List gallery presents Seti and the young Ramesses reading offerings before a sequence of 76 royal cartouches running from Menes to Seti himself — a deliberately edited ancestry: the Hyksos rulers, the Second Intermediate Period kings, Hatshepsut, and the four Amarna kings are omitted, so that the list does not merely record the succession but defines which of it counts as legitimate. Behind the temple lies the Osireion, a sunken, subterranean cenotaph of Osiris built in massive archaic masonry around a central island — a built model of the primeval mound rising from the waters, and the period's most literal piece of archaism.

Seti's military record underwrote the theology: in his first years he campaigned through Canaan to the Lebanon, reopening the coastal road and the cedar supply, and fought the Hittites in Syria — the necessary preliminary to his son's confrontation at Kadesh (§XI). His tomb, KV17, is the Valley's masterpiece and the first to carry a complete Book of Gates on the king's own alabaster sarcophagus (see §XIV).

Pi-Ramesses — the Industrial Capital

The 19th Dynasty governed from a new city. Pi-Ramesses, founded by Seti I as a summer residence and built out by Ramesses II on the Pelusiac branch of the Nile in the eastern Delta, combined palace, garrison, and factory: chariot workshops, shield production, horse stables for hundreds of animals, and — demonstrated by the excavations of Edgar Pusch at Qantir, the city's southern quarter — bronze foundries and a glass industry working at industrial scale. Pi-Ramesses faced Asia; Thebes remained the religious capital; Memphis administered the junction. The tri-capital system is the Ramesside answer to the geography that had defeated the 18th Dynasty's Thebes-only state.

Glass at Qantir — the Process in the Crucibles

The Qantir workshops preserve the New Kingdom's most advanced material process in unusual completeness, reconstructed by Thilo Rehren and Edgar Pusch from the crucibles, jars, and waste of the site itself. White quartz pebbles were crushed to flour and mixed with the ash of desert salt-plants; the batch was charged into reused storage jars lined with a lime-rich parting layer and fired at about 900 °C to a semi-finished, grainy glass; the insolubles were discarded, and the material was refired at about 1050 °C, probably with further plant ash, in purpose-made cylindrical crucibles, where the colourants were added and the true glass formed (Rehren & Pusch, Science 308, 2005). Qantir specialised overwhelmingly in the hardest colour, red copper glass, made under reducing conditions; cobalt blue was the speciality of Amarna (see the Amarna article, §XVII). The finished glass left as ingots — the Uluburun shipwreck of c. 1300 BCE carried some 175 disc ingots of about 2 kg each, every analysed example of Egyptian manufacture — and was worked elsewhere, at 750–900 °C, into core-formed vessels: a clay-and-dung core on a metal rod trailed or rolled in molten glass, marvered smooth, combed into festoon patterns, fitted with rim and handles, cooled slowly, and the core dug out. Primary glassmaking — the 1050 °C stage — is the most demanding repeatable process of the Bronze Age; core-forming from ingots, by contrast, is a bench craft that modern glassworkers reproduce routinely.

X. Ramesses the Great — The Master of Monuments

Ramesses II (c. 1279–1213 BCE) reigned for 66 years and left more monuments than any other Pharaoh. From the colossal statues at Abu Simbel to the vast Ramesseum, his work was a demonstration of Iterative Authority.

By placing his name on every available surface, Ramesses was not just being vain; he was creating a "National Resonance Field." He saturated the Egyptian landscape with his identity, making himself the permanent "Anchor of Ma'at" in the collective consciousness. His reign was the final, stable peak of the imperial system.

A Reign in Numbers

Ramesses II acceded as a young man and reigned 66 years and two months, outliving at least twelve of his sons. The succession was managed as publicly as the building: the king's many sons were paraded in procession reliefs in fixed order of seniority, and the eldest surviving, Khaemwaset — high priest of Ptah at Memphis, restorer of ancient monuments, and by later tradition Egypt's first antiquarian — predeceased him; the crown passed at last to the thirteenth son, Merneptah, already an old man. The king's sons were buried communally in the vast KV5 in the Valley of the Kings, rediscovered and identified in the 1990s by the Theban Mapping Project as the largest tomb complex in the Valley.

The building programme kept pace with the family. At Abu Simbel, the great temple's axis was set so that on two days of the year the sunrise penetrated to the sanctuary statues — an effect famous in modern tourism, though its intended festival dates are inferred, not documented — while the northern capital, Pi-Ramesses, rose in the eastern Delta on the Pelusiac branch, a chariot-garrison and bronze-working city whose blue tiles and horse studs the poets of the day celebrated (paraphrase of the Pi-Ramesses encomia). When river silting later shifted the branch, Pi-Ramesses was abandoned and its monuments migrated to Tanis — the raw material of the next period's capital (see the Third Intermediate Period article, §II).

XI. The Battle of Kadesh — Managing Imperial Isfet

The Battle of Kadesh against the Hittites was the largest chariot battle in history. While technically a stalemate, Ramesses II presented it as a personal victory enabled by the direct intervention of Amun.

This event represents the Management of External Chaos. The treaty that followed (the first known international peace treaty) shows the New Kingdom's transition from raw conquest to sophisticated Diplomatic Data-Exchange. The state realized that Isfet at the borders could not be totally eliminated, but it could be "contained" through legal and symbolic agreements.

The Treaty — Text, Copies, and Afterlife

Twenty-one years after the battle (Year 21, c. 1259 BCE), exhaustion and the rising Assyrian threat produced the Egyptian–Hittite peace treaty between Ramesses II and Hattusili III. Its terms, in the Egyptian version carved at Karnak and the Ramesseum and in the Akkadian version found on clay tablets at the Hittite capital Hattusa, are reciprocal and specific: non-aggression, a defensive alliance, mutual extradition of fugitives — with the remarkable clause that the extradited are not to be punished — and an oath before the gods of both lands (paraphrase of the treaty texts). The original Egyptian copy records that the Hittite text arrived on a silver tablet bearing the seals of Hattusili and his queen Puduhepa, whose correspondence with the Egyptian court survives separately.

The peace held. In Year 34 Ramesses married a Hittite princess, received at Pi-Ramesses with full ceremony, and the frontier between the empires stabilised for the remainder of the Bronze Age. A replica of the treaty hangs today at the United Nations headquarters in New York — the afterlife of a document that began as two temple wall texts and a tablet of silver, and the reason the Kadesh settlement, rather than the battle, is the period's true diplomatic monument.

The Two Versions — Poem and Bulletin

Kadesh is recorded in two official Egyptian texts, inscribed side by side on temple walls from Luxor to Abu Simbel: the Poem, a literary account centred on the king's isolation and Amun's aid, and the Bulletin, a terser prose report of the march, the deception by Hittite agents posing as defectors, the near-destruction of the Re division in the ambush, and the arrival of the Ne'arin force from the coast. That both were published — the embarrassing operational failure alongside the heroic rescue — is itself remarkable, and it is why Kadesh can be reconstructed tactically at all: two Egyptian staffs' worth of pride produced, between them, the most fully documented battle of the Bronze Age.

The Hittite side kept its own archive: the treaty tablets at Hattusa, and the correspondence of Hattusili III and Puduhepa with the Egyptian court, preserved on clay. When Hattusa was excavated, the two archives met for the first time in three thousand years — the treaty of §XI verified from both ends, an almost unique luxury in ancient diplomatic history.

XII. The Priesthood of Amun-Ra — The State Within a State

The New Kingdom was defined by the supremacy of Amun-Ra, the "King of the Gods." The temple estates of Amun at Thebes grew enormous — the Great Harris Papyrus credits the Amun precinct with roughly four-fifths of Egypt's temple-held land and personnel under Ramesses III — but temple wealth was institutionally distinct from the crown's, and no source shows it exceeding the Pharaoh's own resources.

This Institutional Density permitted the development of the most sophisticated magical and theological systems in history. The priesthood was the "Deep Processing" unit of the state, managing the complex House of Life and the production of sacred texts. However, the resulting power struggle between the Throne and the Temple eventually led to the Decoherence of the 20th Dynasty, where the High Priests of Thebes effectively became independent rulers of the south.

The God's State in Figures

The Great Harris Papyrus, compiled at the accession of Ramesses IV as the record of Ramesses III's benefactions, gives the Amun estate's weight in the king's own accounting: of the temple-held land and personnel it lists for all Egypt's gods, the Theban Amun precinct holds roughly four-fifths — the figure corrected in the text above (Katary, "Land Tenure," UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology, tabulating Harris I). The same document enumerates Amun's towns, herds, workshops, ships, and gardens across Egypt and abroad. This was not a church within a state but a parallel administration — vizier, treasury, granary, and fleet in the god's name — yet it remained, through the New Kingdom, an administration the crown appointed, audited, and could purge.

The mechanism of control was the High Priesthood itself: First Prophets of Amun such as Bakenkhonsu under Ramesses II were career officials, builders and administrators in royal service. Only when the crown itself failed, at the very end of the 20th Dynasty, did the office pass to men — Herihor, Pinedjem — who dated by their own eras and wrote their names in cartouches. The "state within a state" of this section's title is thus a terminal condition of the period, not its norm — a distinction the Amarna article (§I there) makes for the same office two centuries earlier.

XIII. The Valley of the Kings — Hidden Navigation Technology

Abandoning the visible pyramids of the Old Kingdom, the New Kingdom Pharaohs hid their tombs deep within the limestone cliffs of the Biban el-Moluk (Valley of the Kings).

This was a theological shift: the tomb was no longer a solar ladder, but a Re-Entry Node into the chthonic depths of the Duat. The walls of these tombs were covered in functional magical diagrams designed to permit the King's Ba to navigate the 12 hours of the night and emerge as a transfigured Akh at dawn. The hidden nature of the tombs emphasized the "Internalized Path"—the realization that the highest mysteries are found in the "Hidden Place" (Amentet).

The Valley and Its Village

Some sixty-five numbered tombs are now known in the Valley of the Kings and its western branch, from the earliest royal burials of the 18th Dynasty to the last of Ramesses XI; the plan evolved from the bent-axis corridors of Thutmose III's cartouche-shaped chamber to the straight-axis, fully decorated corridors of the Ramesside tombs. Security was theoretical from the start — most were robbed in antiquity — because the secret was an open one: the tombs were cut by a permanent, hereditary workforce who lived a valley away.

That workforce's village, Deir el-Medina (see Deir el-Medina), is the best-documented community of the ancient Egyptian world: at its greatest extent some seventy houses inside its wall and fifty more outside, housing the crew — at most about 120 workmen with their families, a total population estimated around 1,200 at its 19th–20th Dynasty peak. The crew worked in two gangs, "right" and "left," under foremen and scribes; its attendance registers survive on ostraca, including the British Museum's limestone register of Year 40 of Ramesses II, which logs forty workmen across 280 days with the reasons for each absence entered in red above the date — illness, family festivals, brewing, a scorpion sting. No other ancient workforce can be followed day by day in this way.

Why the Valley Failed — and What Replaced It

The Valley experiment assumed secrecy could substitute for the pyramid's mass and garrisoned pyramid-town. It could not: the workforce knew the plans, the necropolis police were local, and in the fiscal crises of the late 20th Dynasty the tombs were systematically mined by the community built to guard them (§XVIII). The priesthood's response — gathering the royal dead into two defensible caches — conceded the point: in the next period kings would be buried inside temple enclosures in the Delta cities, under the garrison's eye, and Theban officials in communal, concealed tombs. The Valley of the Kings, the New Kingdom's most famous landscape, is thus also the record of a security doctrine failing in slow motion across four centuries.

XIV. The Netherworld Books — Functional Soul-Software

The New Kingdom produced the most complex funerary literature in history: the Netherworld Books (Amduat, Book of Gates, Book of Caverns).

The genre is a creation of this period, and its first horizons are fixed by named tombs. The Amduat — the sun god's twelve-hour journey through the Duat — appears first in fragments in the earliest Valley tombs (KV20/KV38) and in its earliest complete version in KV34, the tomb of Thutmose III, whose cartouche-shaped burial chamber is wrapped in the full text; its most splendid later copy covers the walls of KV17, the tomb of Seti I. The Book of Gates, the second great composition, is first attested, incompletely, in KV57, the tomb of Horemheb (hours two to six in the burial chamber), and received its first complete version on the alabaster sarcophagus of Seti I from KV17. King by king, tomb by tomb, the royal burial chamber of the New Kingdom became the screen on which this literature was projected. For the books themselves — Amduat, Gates, Caverns, and the rest — see the dedicated survey at The Netherworld Books.

In our research framework, these are Functional Navigation Scripts. They do not describe myths; they provide the exact vibrational passwords, the names of the guardians, and the topography of the different dimensions of consciousness. They were designed to ensure that the Pharaoh (and later, ordinary initiates) could bypass the "Memory-Wiping" effects of death and maintain a continuous data-link with the divine source. See The Netherworld Books Wiki.

The Rest of the Library

Beyond the Amduat and the Book of Gates, the royal tombs accumulated a whole shelf of compositions across the period: the Litany of Ra, invoking the sun god in seventy-five forms; the Book of Caverns and the Book of the Earth, mapping the Duat as a landscape of pits, mounds, and regenerating corpses; the Book of the Heavenly Cow, the myth of mankind's near-destruction, first attested in Tutankhamun's tomb; and the astronomical Books of the Day and the Night stretched across tomb ceilings as the body of Nut. The sequence is cumulative, not replacement: each new book was added beside its predecessors, so that a late Ramesside tomb could carry four compositions at once.

The literature escaped the Valley early. By the later New Kingdom its scenes were being excerpted onto papyri, coffins, and private tombs — the democratisation, or at least the wider circulation, of what had begun as the king's exclusive equipment — preparing the fusion with the Book of the Dead tradition that dominates the funerary record of the Third Intermediate Period.

The Amduat's First Readers

The Amduat's earliest attestation is worth its own precision: abbreviated versions appear in the tombs attributed to Thutmose I's circle (KV20, KV38), and the first complete copy, in KV34, wraps Thutmose III's oval burial chamber wall-to-wall, the text's cursive hieroglyphs and stick-figure gods painted as if on an unrolled papyrus — the tomb as a book you stand inside. The composition's own title, "The Book of the Hidden Chamber," and its closing colophons, which address the reader who "knows" the text and promise him passage with the god, state the book's theory of itself: knowledge as equipment, reading as a form of travel. The netherworld books are the New Kingdom's most original literary invention, and they were composed, in the first instance, for an audience of one dead king per tomb.

XV. The Karnak-Luxor Axis — The Engine of the State

The city of Thebes (Waset) functioned as the Primary Engine of the New Kingdom. The axis between the Karnak and Luxor temples was the site of the annual Opet Festival.

During this festival, the statue of Amun traveled from Karnak to Luxor to "recharge" the King's Ka. This was a Total System Re-Initialization. It synchronized the divine power of the god with the political power of the Pharaoh, ensuring that the "State Capacitor" was fully charged for the coming year. The scale of these temples—the largest religious buildings ever constructed—reflects the "High-Bandwidth" energy needed to sustain an empire.

Opet — the Festival in Detail

The Opet Festival grew with the empire: under Hatshepsut it occupied eleven days; by the 20th Dynasty it ran to twenty-seven. Its choreography is preserved in the reliefs of Luxor Temple's colonnade, carved under Tutankhamun and Horemheb: the portable barques of Amun, Mut, and Khonsu are carried from Karnak amid soldiers, dancers, musicians, and fattened offering cattle, transferred to river barges for the sail south to Luxor, feasted in the sanctuary there — the god's "private apartments," in the festival's own name — and returned by the same route (paraphrase of the Luxor Opet reliefs). The king officiated in person; the festival's theological payload was the renewal, in Luxor's sanctuary, of the royal ka itself.

The temples it connected were the largest religious complex of the ancient world. Karnak's Amun precinct alone grew for two thousand years around the Middle Kingdom core: Thutmose III's festival hall and botanical reliefs (§V), the twin obelisks, Amenhotep III's Third Pylon, and finally the Great Hypostyle Hall of Seti I and Ramesses II — 134 columns, the central twelve rising some 21 metres, a stone forest still without parallel. The axis Luxor–Karnak, lined in later periods with sphinxes, made the festival route a permanent feature of the city's plan: sacred geography as urban infrastructure.

XVI. Military Heka — The Divine Standards in Battle

The Egyptian army of the New Kingdom was a Theurgical Unit. Each division was named after a god (Amon, Re, Ptah, Set), and their standards were treated as "Portable Resonators" of the divine power.

Military campaigns were preceded by oracles and followed by massive offerings. The Pharaoh fought not as a man, but as the Incarnation of Montu (the god of war). This integration of military force and magical intent made the New Kingdom army nearly invincible for centuries—they weren't just fighting for land, they were "Clearing the Field" for the expansion of Ma'at.

The Army as an Institution

By the Ramesside period the field army was organised in divisions of roughly five thousand men, each under the name of a national god — Amun, Re, Ptah, and, at Kadesh, the newly raised division of Seth — subdivided into companies with their own standard-bearers. The striking arm was the chariotry: a two-man crew — driver and archer with composite bow — on a light, six-spoked car drawn by two horses, used for pursuit, flank attack, and as a mobile archery platform; the infantry fought with spear, shield, and the sickle-sword. Foreign troops were integral, not auxiliary: Sherden Sea People, captured in the early 19th Dynasty, appear in Ramesses II's own guard at Kadesh, and Nubian and Libyan units served throughout.

The oracle belonged to the same system as the standards. Before Megiddo the decision for the Aruna pass is presented as the king's own; before Kadesh's crisis, in the Poem's telling, Ramesses calls on Amun and is answered — the god's aid worth, the king says, more than millions of infantry (paraphrase of the Kadesh Poem). Campaign, oracle, offering, and annal were one administrative cycle: the god authorised the war, the army fought it, and the temple wall received the audited result.

Logistics — the Army on the March

The theurgical army was also a supply problem solved at scale. The Annals' itinerary for the Megiddo campaign gives march rates of roughly twenty kilometres a day for the full host with its chariotry, ox-trains, and camp followers; the Ways of Horus, the coastal road from Tjaru to Gaza, was maintained as a fortified supply line of wells and depots — its stations later carved, with Seti I's campaigns, on the exterior wall of the Karnak hypostyle. Garrisons and residencies in the conquered cities drew on local tribute in grain and fodder, audited through the same scribal service that kept the campaign day-book. The empire's wars were won as much by this unglamorous machinery — wells dug, barley tallied, horses shod and fed — as by the standards of §XVI; the texts, to their credit, record both.

XVII. The 20th Dynasty — The Sea Peoples and Economic Stress

The final dynasty of the New Kingdom, the 20th, faced a series of Systemic Stress-Tests. Ramesses III successfully repelled the "Sea Peoples," a massive migration of displaced tribes that destroyed the other great empires of the Bronze Age (the Hittites and Mycenaeans).

However, the cost of this victory was catastrophic. The state suffered from the first recorded labor strike (at Deir el-Medina), a failed assassination plot against the King (the Harem Conspiracy), and a series of failed harvests. The "High-Bandwidth" empire was becoming too expensive to maintain, leading to a gradual Loss of Signal Integrity.

Medinet Habu, the Strike, and the Conspiracy

Ramesses III's mortuary temple at Medinet Habu is the period's defensive archive: its reliefs and inscriptions record the Year 5 Libyan war, the Year 8 assault of the Sea Peoples — a confederation named group by group (Peleset, Tjeker, Shekelesh, Denyen, Weshesh) moving by land and sea with families and ox-carts — and the Year 11 Libyan war, ending with the great Delta land-and-sea victory in which the invaders' ships were trapped against the river mouths and shot down from the banks (paraphrase of the Medinet Habu inscriptions). The Egyptian state won every battle and lost the century: the empire in Asia contracted to nothing, and the costs were carried by the home economy.

The home front is documented with uncomfortable precision. In Year 29 of Ramesses III (c. 1152 BCE) the Deir el-Medina crew, their grain rations months in arrears, sat down at the mortuary temples and refused to return until paid — the first recorded labour strike in history, logged in the Turin Strike Journal with the officials' promises and the crew's repeated returns to the picket when the promises failed. Within a few years the Harem Conspiracy — a plot of royal women, officials, and priests, using among other means wax figures and written spells, to place a prince named Pentawer on the throne — reached the king himself; the trial papyri record the interrogations and the forced suicides of the condemned, and CT examination of Ramesses III's mummy in 2012 found the deep throat wound the documents never mention. The empire's last great reign ends in its own court records.

The Dynasty After Ramesses III

Ramesses IV to XI reigned, in sequence, over a state whose assets were shrinking to the Nile valley itself. The Wilbour Papyrus, a great land survey of Middle Egypt under Ramesses V, shows the temples — above all Amun of Thebes — already holding the dominant share of registered land; the Adoption Papyrus and the will of the lady Naunakhte, from Deir el-Medina, show private law functioning normally amid the contraction. Abroad, the last imperial gesture is Ramesses XI's dispatch of the priest Wenamun to Byblos for cedar — the Report of Wenamun shows the emissary robbed, delayed, and lectured by the prince of Byblos on the new terms of trade: cash, not command. Within a generation the viceroy of Kush, Panehsy, and the Theban command were fighting a civil war inside Upper Egypt itself, and the whm-mswt era of §XIX had begun.

XVIII. The Tomb Robbery Papyri — Loss of Sacred Integrity

By the end of the 20th Dynasty, the state's ability to protect the "Sacred Hardware" failed. The Tomb Robbery Papyri record the systematic looting of the royal tombs in the Valley of the Kings—often with the collusion of local officials.

This was the ultimate sign of Isfet Infiltration. When the "Eternal Resonators" of the past were violated for their gold, the theological bond between the state and the divine was broken. The "Memory of Kemet" was being liquidated to pay for its survival, a process of Data-Degradation that signaled the end of the Golden Age.

The Robbery Papyri — Names and Cases

The documentation is specific. Papyrus Abbott records the Year 16 inspection under Ramesses IX of ten royal tombs, finding nine intact and one — that of the 17th Dynasty king Sobekemsaf — breached; the Mayer Papyri and Papyrus BM 10052 preserve the trials, in which a stonemason named Hapiwer and his gang describe tunnelling into tombs, stripping the mummies, and dividing gold by weight, while the mayor of western Thebes, Pawera, whose administration stood accused, feuded publicly with Paser, the mayor of the east bank who had reported the crimes (paraphrase of the tomb-robbery papyri, ed. T. E. Peet, 1930). Confessions were extracted under beating, and the papyri record that too.

What the trials expose is structural, not merely criminal: grain prices had multiplied, wages in kind arrived late or never, and the necropolis staff — guards, police, scribes — knew exactly where everything was. The priesthood's answer, the great reburial programme that moved the royal mummies to DB320 and KV35, belongs to the opening of the next period and is treated in the Third Intermediate Period article (§§XIII–XIV there).

XIX. The Great Fragmentation — Dawn of the Third Intermediate

The New Kingdom ended with the Permanent Fracture of the Two Lands. The High Priests of Amun took control of the South, while the 21st Dynasty ruled the North from Tanis.

This transition into the Third Intermediate Period was a Data-Preservation Cycle. The priesthood, realizing that the "Imperial Hardware" was gone, focused on "Wrapping and Hiding" the sacred legacy. They gathered the looted mummies of the great Pharaohs into secret caches (like DB320), ensuring that the "Ancestral Frequency" would survive the coming centuries of foreign rule.

The whm-mswt — an Era of Two Governments

The transition was slower and more orderly than "collapse" suggests. In the last decade of Ramesses XI a local era, the whm-mswt — "Repeating of Births," the Renaissance — was used in Theban documents, dating the years not by the distant king but by the renewal itself. The High Priest Herihor took royal titles in the south while Smendes ruled at Tanis in the north; the two houses intermarried, recognised each other's documents, and never fought the war the "fragmentation" model implies. Egypt did not fall apart in 1075 BCE; it was re-founded as a dual state, and the arrangement lasted, in its essentials, for four centuries (see the companion article).

XX. Legacy of the New Kingdom — The Global Memory of Egypt

The New Kingdom provided the Global Image of Ancient Egypt. The icons of this era—Nefertiti, Tutankhamun, Ramesses II—became the permanent "Face of Kemet" for the rest of human history.

Its legacy is the realization that Divine Power can be Projected. The New Kingdom proved that a theological system could manage an international empire and that the "High-Frequency" knowledge of the temples could be scaled to monumental proportions. It provided the "Peak Experience" of Kemetic civilization, a standard of imperial grandeur and theological depth that would haunt the Western imagination from the time of the Greeks to the modern era.

What the New Kingdom Left in the Ground

The period also fixed the modern discipline's foundations. The Valley tombs, the Deir el-Medina archive of thousands of ostraca, the Amarna Letters, and the Harris and robbery papyri make the New Kingdom the first era of Egyptian history that can be written from day-to-day documents rather than monuments alone — accounts, letters, court records, attendance registers. Its Egyptian-language stage, Late Egyptian, became the vernacular written record from which Demotic later grew. And its imperial century set the template every later restorer — Kushite, Saite, Ptolemaic — consciously copied: when Egypt next unified, it unified in the New Kingdom's image (see the Kushite and Late Period articles).

The Period as Later Egypt Remembered It

Later Egyptians curated this period selectively. The Ramesside king lists restored it minus Amarna (§IX); the priests who rewrapped the royal mummies handled Thutmose III, Seti I, and Ramesses II as the canonical great kings; and when the Kushite and Saite restorers of the first millennium sought models, it was New Kingdom Thebes — its temples, its God's Wife, its festival calendar — that they rebuilt. The New Kingdom is therefore not only an era but the archive's own standard: the state of the system against which every subsequent period in Section I of this wiki defines itself, as recovery, as archaism, or as loss.
