I. Words for Boats — the Vocabulary of the River

Egyptian boat vocabulary is working vocabulary before it is theological vocabulary, and this article keeps that order. The archive's Solar Barque article treats the vessel as the sun god's vehicle through sky and underworld. This article treats the same hull forms as machines: built, loaded, rowed, sailed, towed, repaired and, in the end, taken apart and reused. The words below are the words of that working world, and each one is given only where its spelling can be composed sign by sign under the archive's discipline.

The river itself is itrw [Gardiner: M17-X1-D21-G43] [𓇋𓏏𓂋𓅱], the Nile as a watercourse, the road on which everything in this article moves. A boat, in a general and early-attested word, is imw [Gardiner: M17-G17-G43-P1] [𓇋𓅓𓅱𓊛], written with the boat sign P1 as its determinative, the unpronounced classifier that files the word under hulls. A fleet, or a company of boats spoken of together, appears as ḥꜥw [Gardiner: V28-D36-G43-P1] [𓎛𓂝𓅱𓊛], again closed by the boat sign. The cargo ship, the freighter of the river system, is mnš [Gardiner: G17-N35-N37-P1] [𓅓𓈖𓈙𓊛], a word the texts use for the vessels that carried the state's bulk goods rather than for the light craft of fishermen. The seagoing ship bound for the Levantine coast is named by its destination in the compound whose first element is kbn [Gardiner: V31-D58-N35-P1] [𓎡𓃀𓈖𓊛], Byblos: a Byblos-ship is not a hull shape in the first instance but a job description, a vessel built and crewed for that run (Section XVIII). A foreign or sea-going boat of another naming tradition appears in Egyptian texts as br [Gardiner: D58-D21-P1] [𓃀𓂋𓊛], a loanword whose very foreignness is evidence for the traffic it names.

The parts of the vessel carry their own terms, and two of them govern whole sections below. The wind that makes upstream sailing possible is ṯꜣw [Gardiner: V13-G1-G43] [𓍿𓄿𓅱], a word that also means breath and air more generally, so that the language itself joins the sailor's wind to the body's breathing. The steering oar, the great blade worked over the stern quarter by which every Egyptian vessel of size was steered for most of pharaonic history, is ḥmw [Gardiner: V28-G17-G43-P2] [𓎛𓅓𓅱𓊝], written with the steering-oar sign as determinative. An expedition, the organised sending-out of men and ships that lies behind both the mining ventures and the Punt voyages, is a mšꜥ [Gardiner: G17-N37-D36] [𓅓𓈙𓂝] in the word's expeditionary sense, and what such ventures brought back, the produce and tribute unloaded on the quay, is inw [Gardiner: M17-N35-G43] [𓇋𓈖𓅱], the goods delivered. A rope, the material on which the whole construction system of Sections VII to IX depends, can be written wḥꜣ [Gardiner: G43-V28-G1] [𓅱𓎛𓄿]. Where a familiar modern term has no spelling this article can compose with confidence — "keel," "rib," "sail" as a technical noun in a specific period spelling — the article uses the English word alone and says nothing in brackets. That omission is deliberate, and it is the same rule the archive applies in Glass and Metalworking.

The full sign list is given in the hieroglyph reference.

One terminological decision, taken from Steve Vinson's UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology entry "Boats (Use of)" (2013), structures the whole article. Vinson defines his subject by distinguishing river boats from sea-going ships by their primary theatre of work, while acknowledging that the line is a modern analytical convenience rather than an Egyptian category. This article follows that usage: "boat" for the river craft in the first instance, "ship" where the sea is the point, and "vessel" and "craft" where the distinction would mislead. Egyptian builders, as Section X shows, used substantially the same construction system in both theatres, which is precisely why the modern line must be handled as a convenience and not as a fact about the hulls.

II. The Evidence and Its Limits — Hulls, Models, Scenes and Logbooks

No other Egyptian technology can be followed in actual surviving examples across so long a span, and no other is so easy to over-read. The evidence for this article falls into five kinds, and the article labels them as it goes, because each kind can prove different things and each has a characteristic way of lying.

Surviving hulls and timbers come first, because they alone prove construction. Cheryl Ward's census, in her Antiquity study of the Abydos boat-grave cemetery (2006), counts twenty-two boats found in Egyptian contexts dated between about 3050 and 450 BCE, whole, disassembled and recycled in mortuary complexes, or abandoned at the water's edge. Later work has enlarged the timber corpus, above all from the Red Sea ports (Section X), but the shape of the corpus has not changed: a handful of complete or near-complete hulls, a larger body of disassembled and reused timbers, and long centuries represented by no wood at all. Wood survives in Egypt where it was buried dry, sealed in a pit, or sunk in harbour mud. The ordinary working boat, worn out and broken up on a riverbank, is almost never in the record. The survivors are therefore skewed toward royal and funerary vessels, and every generalisation in this article carries that skew with it.

Models come second. Middle Kingdom tombs in particular deposited fleets of wooden model boats, with crews, cabins, masts and steering oars rendered at small scale, and museum collections from Cairo to the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York hold them in quantity. Models prove fittings, crew stations and rig arrangements that hull timbers alone cannot. They do not prove scantlings, fastenings or hull strength, and their proportions are a model-maker's, not a shipwright's. This article uses models for arrangement, never for structure.

Scenes and reliefs come third: tomb paintings of boatbuilding, fishing, fowling and cargo work, temple reliefs of expeditions, most famously the Punt reliefs of Hatshepsut at Deir el-Bahari (Section XVIII), and the shipbuilding scenes in Old Kingdom mastabas such as that of Ti at Saqqara, where shipwrights shape hull planks with adzes while overseers watch. Scenes prove sequence, tools in hands, crew numbers as an artist's convention, and the social organisation of the yard. They compress, they idealise, and they obey the rules of Egyptian representation rather than of naval architecture: a relief that shows two obelisks end to end on one barge (Section XVI) is a document about royal capability before it is a deck plan, and this article reads it in that order.

Texts and logbooks come fourth, and one of them is unlike anything else in Egyptian studies. The papyri from Wadi al-Jarf on the Red Sea coast, excavated by Pierre Tallet and Gregory Marouard from 2011 onward and announced in 2013, include the logbooks known as the Diary of Merer: day by day accounts, from the last years of Khufu's reign, of a named official and his crew hauling limestone by boat from the Tura quarries to the pyramid site at Giza (Sections XVI and XIX). Around that unique survival stand the ordinary textual record: expedition inscriptions, dockyard accounts such as the Ramesside Papyrus British Museum 10056 with its shipyard timber registers, biographical claims by officials who built great barges, and, much later, Herodotus's description of the Egyptian freighter he calls the baris (Section XXII). Texts prove administration, loads, dates and destinations. They rarely describe joinery, and where a modern account puts technical words into an ancient official's mouth, this article does not follow it.

Experiment and reconstruction come last. Ward's reconstructions of the Abydos hull form, naval-architectural analyses of the Thonis-Heracleion freighter known as Ship 17, and full-scale or model replicas of Egyptian rigs have tested what the hulls could carry and how the sails could work. Experiment proves possibility. As the archive's Glass article insists for its own furnaces, possibility and historical practice are different claims, and this article keeps them separate wherever a reconstruction is cited.

III. The River as Road — Current North, Wind North, and the Logic of the Nile

Egypt's transport geography can be stated in two sentences, and everything in this article follows from them. The Nile flows north, from the First Cataract at Aswan to the Delta and the Mediterranean, through some thousand kilometres of navigable river. The prevailing wind blows from the north. A vessel could therefore drift or row north with the current, and sail south against the current on the wind. No other great river system in the ancient world offered so complete a two-way engine, and Egyptian civilisation was organised on the assumption that it would always be there.

The consequences were structural. Heavy goods moved by water wherever water could be made to reach: quarries were worked, where the geology allowed, within hauling distance of a bank or a canal; pyramid and temple sites were served by harbours and basins cut in from the river (Section XIX); the state's grain moved in fleets from collection points to granary cities (Section XVII). The sacred geography of the archive is also a transport geography: temples face the river because the river was the street, the god's processional barque travelled by water between temples because that was how anything large travelled, and a town's importance can be read, in most periods, from its landing.

The river was not, however, a uniform highway, and the procedural detail matters. The annual inundation transformed it seasonally: at flood, roughly in late summer and autumn under the pharaonic regime, water spread across the floodplain, canals filled, basins that stood dry or shallow for much of the year became navigable, and the heaviest stone traffic was timed to ride the high water as close to the building site as possible (Section XVI). At low water, channels shifted, sandbanks emerged in the main stream, and the pilot's local knowledge, the sounding pole, and the practice of towing or lightening a grounded vessel (Section XV) became the difference between a schedule and a stranding. The First Cataract, where granite rapids interrupt the river at Aswan, was a hard boundary: vessels did not shoot it as a matter of routine, cargoes were portaged or transferred, and the quarries of Aswan loaded their stone below the rapids for the long downstream run.

Distances on this road were measured in days, and Section XX sets out what the logbooks and itineraries allow us to say about rates of travel. The governing asymmetry never changed: downstream was the cheap direction for bulk, upstream was possible at scale only because the wind could be trusted, and any cargo that had to move against both wind and current, in a calm or in the wrong season, moved at the speed of oars and tow-ropes and cost accordingly. When the texts boast of a great stone delivered, the boast is logistical before it is architectural.

Beyond the river lay two seas, and the article treats them separately (Section XVIII) precisely because the river's logic did not transfer. On the Mediterranean, the "Great Green" of the Egyptian texts, and on the Red Sea, there was no reliable current to ride home and no single prevailing wind that served both legs of a round trip. Seagoing was therefore organised differently: seasonal, expeditionary, state-funded, and, on the Red Sea, dependent on ports where ships were assembled from timbers carried across the Eastern Desert (Sections X and XIX). The Nile made transport ordinary. The seas kept it an event.

IV. Timber — Local Acacia, Sycamore and Tamarisk against Imported Cedar

Egypt builds great boats in a country with little great timber. That paradox is the first fact of its shipbuilding, and the surviving hulls are, among other things, records of how it was solved: by using short, thick local planks where long ones were unavailable, by designing a construction system that turned short planks into a virtue (Section VII), and by importing long conifer timber at state expense for the vessels whose size or prestige demanded it.

The local timbers come first, because they are the earlier evidence. Wood, as a material in general, is ḫt [Gardiner: M3] [𓆱]. The Abydos hulls (Section VIII) were built substantially of tamarisk, a dense, hard, local tree of the river margins and desert wadis, growing in lengths that imposed short planks on the builder. Sycamore fig, written here as nḥt [Gardiner: N35-V28-X1] [𓈖𓎛𓏏], was the classic Egyptian carpentry wood, available in larger scantlings than tamarisk, relatively light, workable, and used across furniture, coffins and boatbuilding. Acacia, hard and durable and likewise local, supplied smaller structural pieces, tenons, dowels and the kind of tough component for which a short length was no disadvantage. Pearce Paul Creasman's study of Middle Kingdom ship and boat timbers (Göttinger Miszellen 240) reads the surviving wood as a managed "timber economy": species chosen by component, sizes conserved, offcuts and old timbers recycled into new uses, in a pattern that only makes sense where good wood is scarce enough to account for. The dockyard registers of Papyrus British Museum 10056 (Section II) show the same economy from the administrative side, timber issued to a royal shipyard by type and dimension.

Against the local woods stands the imported conifer, above all the cedar of Lebanon, written here in its short form as ꜥš [Gardiner: D36-N37] [𓂝𓈙], the prestige timber that arrived through the Byblos trade from the Early Dynastic period onward. Cedar offered what no Nile-side tree could: long, straight, relatively light planks, resistant to rot, in lengths that let a shipwright lay a sheer strake in few pieces. The Khufu ship (Section IX) is the surviving monument of that trade, built largely of Lebanese cedar planks some of them exceeding ten metres, in a hull whose overall length of 43.4 metres would have been a different and harder problem in tamarisk. The seagoing timbers from Mersa/Wadi Gawasis (Section X) are likewise cedar, massively built, as Cheryl Ward's British Museum Studies paper "Building Pharaoh's Ships" (2012) emphasises: the physical evidence of the Red Sea ports is of cedar ships, not of river boats pressed into sea service with local wood.

The choice between local and imported was therefore never simply chronological, and this article does not present cedar as "replacing" acacia and sycamore. The traditions run in parallel for three thousand years. River freighters, fishing craft and work boats continued to be built of local timber in short, thick planks, because the construction system suited that material and the material was at hand. Royal, funerary and seagoing vessels drew on cedar because the state could pay the Byblos price and because length, weight and durability at sea rewarded it. The companion treatment of the Byblos connection and the wider diplomatic timber trade is given in Trade and Diplomacy; here the point is the shipwright's: species is a design decision, recorded in the hull.

TAMARISK — THE ABYDOS WOOD

Dense, hard, local; short lengths. The demonstrated hull timber of the Abydos boats, c. 3000 BCE. Its shortness is part of why the earliest planked hull is a mosaic of short, thick planks laced together across the hull (Section VIII).

SYCAMORE AND ACACIA — THE RIVER WORKHORSES

Sycamore for planking and general carpentry in larger local scantlings; acacia for hard, tough components, tenons and fittings. The ordinary woods of the ordinary boat, throughout pharaonic history.

CEDAR OF LEBANON — THE IMPORTED LENGTH

Long, straight, light and durable planks by the Byblos route. The Khufu ship and the Gawasis seagoing timbers are cedar. Imported length bought hull size and sea endurance that local timber could not economically provide.

A last timber fact is procedural and easily missed. Egyptian planks are thick. Where later Mediterranean traditions moved toward thinner planking over frames, the Egyptian hull, from Abydos to Ship 17, is built of thick, sturdy strakes that carry much of the hull's strength in themselves (Ward 2012, summarising the corpus). Thickness was the compensation for shortness and for the absence of a frame-first skeleton: the shell was the structure. Section VII takes up what that meant at the bench.

V. The Shipwright's Tools and Yard — Saw, Adze, Chisel, Drill and Mallet

The shipwright's toolkit is not reconstructed from imagination. It survives three times over: as tools in museum collections, as marks on the timbers themselves, and as scenes of the tools in use. Creasman's Middle Kingdom study states the position plainly: tool marks on the timbers permit a confident reconstruction of the toolkit, corroborated by excavated tools, iconography and workshop models. The kit comprises copper-alloy axes, adzes, chisels, drills and pull-saws in several sizes, with wooden mallets to strike chisels and drive tenons, polishing stones for surfaces, and whetstones to keep edges keen.

Each tool leaves a characteristic signature, and the Abydos and Middle Kingdom studies read them in that order. The pull-saw, drawn toward the body rather than pushed like a modern saw, shaped planks and cut them to length; Ward's Abydos report notes metal saws used to shape the planks of Boat 10. The adze, the shipwright's defining tool in every period, dressed plank surfaces, hollowed and faired curves, and, inverted, served as a plane; the tomb scenes show it in constant use, swung between the feet of a seated shipwright against a plank braced upright. The chisel, struck with the mallet, cut the mortises in plank edges and, at Abydos, the lashing channels (Section VIII). The drill, bow-driven, bored the lashing and fastening holes. The axe did the rough conversion of timber. None of this kit is exotic beside the carpenter's and the coffin- maker's, and that is itself a finding: Egyptian shipbuilding grew out of the general woodworking tradition of the early state, and Ward's Abydos paper stresses that its toolkit had already been known for at least five hundred years when the Abydos hulls were laid.

The yard, as the scenes present it, is an open working space at the water's edge or in a state workshop, with hulls built upright on supports, planks stored and seasoned under supervision, and work organised in gangs under overseers whose presence the artists are careful to include. The Old Kingdom shipbuilding scenes, Ti's among them, show the sequence socially as well as technically: several men at one hull, specialists at distinct stations, an overseer with a staff, and, in some scenes, the hull already taking its curved form on its supports while planking continues. What the scenes do not show is equally telling. There is no sawpit architecture of the later European yard, no mould loft, no drawn plan. Form was carried in the shipwright's eye, in battens and templates where they were used, and in the shell-first method itself, which generates the hull's shape from the planks as they are fitted (Section VII).

Materials beyond timber complete the yard inventory, and each has its own supply chain. Rope and cordage, of halfa grass, papyrus, flax, palm fibre and other plant fibres by period and purpose, was consumed in quantities that make rope-making a shipbuilding industry in its own right; the Khufu ship's lashings alone are reckoned in kilometres of grass rope (Section IX). Tenon stock, of hard local wood, was cut in quantity to standard sizes. Resins, oils and bituminous materials enter the record unevenly and are treated at their real, limited strength in Section XI. A royal dockyard was therefore a timber store, a rope walk and a tool store as much as a slipway, and its accounts, where they survive, read accordingly.

VI. Papyrus Craft, Step by Step — the Bundled Hull That Never Went Away

Before the planked boat, and alongside it for the whole of pharaonic history, stands the papyrus craft: a hull made not of wood at all but of bundles of papyrus stems lashed together. Popular accounts treat it as a primitive precursor that wooden boats replaced. The record does not. Papyrus craft were still being built, fished from, hunted from and used to cross the river in the New Kingdom and later, because for marsh, shallows and short crossings they were cheap, light, buoyant, quickly made and easily repaired. Vinson's UEE entry makes the same division of labour: depending on size and function, boats were built of papyrus or of wood.

The Construction Sequence

The sequence below follows the tomb scenes of papyrus-boat making, the surviving depictions collected by Björn Landström in Ships of the Pharaohs (1970), and the logic of the material itself. Where a step rests on depiction rather than on a surviving papyrus hull, of which none endures, the text keeps it as a depicted procedure.

Step 1 — Harvest and sort the stems. Papyrus stems are cut in the marshes, in lengths suited to the intended craft, and sorted: thick, sound stems for the main bundles, thinner material for bindings and for the smaller rolls that shape the ends. The stems are used green or dried according to local practice; the buoyancy is in the stem's own pithy structure, not in any treatment applied to it.

Step 2 — Form the bundles. Stems are gathered into tight, parallel bundles, bound round at intervals with cord. The central bundle or bundles form the floor of the craft; side bundles, laid along and above them, form the rising sides. The bundle is the unit of construction, as the plank is for the wooden hull, and the builder thinks in bundles.

Step 3 — Lash the bundles into the hull form. The bundles are drawn together and lashed to one another with rope passed around and between them, the lashings tightened to pull the assembly into its characteristic crescent: low amidships, rising at bow and stern, the ends often finished in a bound papyrus umbel or a decorative head in larger ceremonial examples. Tightening is the shaping: the hull's curve is the sum of its lashings' tension.

Step 4 — Bind the ends and finish the gunwale line. Bow and stern bundles are bound off securely, loose stem ends trimmed or folded into the binding, and a continuous lashing along the top of the sides stiffens the edge on which the fisherman stands, poles, or sits. Some larger papyrus craft in the scenes carry a light decking, a mat, or a small shelter; the ordinary working skiff carries nothing that cannot be replaced from the marsh in a day.

Step 5 — Launch, use, and accept the service life. The craft floats on its stems' buoyancy. It will, over days and weeks of use, take up water, ride lower, and lose its spring; the owner's remedy is to haul it out, dry it, re-tighten or replace bundles, and in due course build another. That short, renewable service life is not a defect in the technology. It is the technology: a boat whose material grows at the landing place, whose tools are a knife and cord, and whose cost is labour rather than timber.

Propulsion and handling follow from the form. Papyrus craft were paddled, poled in the shallows, and, for the smallest skiffs, simply stood in and punted by a single fisherman or fowler, as the marsh scenes show in endless variation. They were not, in the ordinary case, sailing vessels or cargo carriers, and the occasional depiction of a large boat with papyrus characteristics should be read with the caution of Section II: artists render prestige vessels in a traditional visual language that owes much to the papyrus form, including the upturned bow and stern that wooden prestige hulls themselves adopted. The wooden boat inherited the papyrus boat's silhouette. That inheritance, visible in every flared stem and stern of the planked tradition, is one of the oldest continuities in Egyptian design.

VII. Shell-First — the Governing Principle of Egyptian Hull Construction

Egyptian wooden hulls were built shell-first. The phrase has a precise meaning, and getting it right is the difference between understanding these vessels and merely admiring them. In shell-first construction the hull's skin is created first: planks are shaped, fitted edge to edge, and fastened to one another so that the shell itself takes and holds the vessel's form. Internal timbers, cross-beams at deck level, and such framing as the vessel carries are fitted into a shell that already exists. In frame-first construction, the later dominant Mediterranean and European method, the order is reversed: a skeleton of keel and frames is erected first and the planking is fastened onto it. Egypt, for the whole span covered by the surviving hulls, built the first way.

Three corollaries follow, and each is visible in the excavated timbers. There is no true keel. Egyptian hulls have a central bottom plank, or in the Khufu ship a group of central bottom strakes, sometimes slightly thicker than their neighbours, and a long-running scholarly discussion, to which Ship 17 has added new evidence (Section XXII), concerns whether such a member should be called a keel-plank. It is not a keel in the frame-first sense: it does not project as a backbone to which frames are fastened, and the hull does not hang from it. Frames are secondary and sparse. Ward's summary of the corpus is consistent from Abydos to the Persian-period work boat: a shell of thick, sturdy planks fastened along their edges, beams across the hull at deck level, but few frames. The beams, tying the two sides together at deck level and carrying the deck, do much of the transverse work that frames do in other traditions. Longitudinal strength is supplied artificially where it is needed, above all by the hogging truss of Section XIV, a great rope girder over the hull that resists the tendency of a long, shallow, keel-less vessel to droop at its unsupported ends.

The fastenings that make a shell out of separate planks are of two families, used together. The first is the unpegged mortise-and-tenon joint: a flat slip of wood, the tenon, fitted into mortises cut in the mating edges of two adjacent planks, aligning them and resisting their separation in shear. Egyptian practice, distinctively and consistently, did not lock these tenons with pegs driven through plank and tenon, as later Phoenician and classical practice did. Ward's British Museum paper identifies the avoidance of the pegged lock as a significant marker of the indigenous tradition. An unpegged tenon aligns; it does not, by itself, hold a hull together against working in a seaway. The second family does that work: lashings, rope passed through channels and holes in the planks and drawn tight, sewing the shell together and, crucially, allowing it to be unsewn. A hull held by unpegged tenons and rope is a hull designed, in a sense no frame-first pegged hull is, to be taken apart: for transport across deserts, for repair plank by plank, and for the recycling of its timbers. Sections IX and X show that design intention at work at both ends of the pharaonic story.

Why would a tradition persist in so labour-intensive a system for three thousand years? The evidence suggests an answer in the material (Section IV). Short planks cannot be spiked to a frame and expected to behave; they must be joined to each other, edge to edge, into a self-supporting mosaic, and the joints must accommodate the timber that exists. Shell-first with thick planks is a technology exquisitely fitted to a timber-poor river state, and its conservatism, which Ward's Abydos paper treats as a datum in its own right, is the conservatism of a system that worked. When, in the Late Period, Egyptian hulls at Thonis-Heracleion are found built in recognisably the same tradition (Section XXII), the continuity is the finding, not a failure to innovate.

// SHELL-FIRST IN ONE SEQUENCE //

Shape the planks. Fit them edge to edge, tenons in their mortises aligning strake to strake. Lash or sew the seams tight through their channels. Let the shell, so joined, become the hull's form and strength. Fit deck beams across it. Add such internal timbers as the vessel's work requires. Rig, steer and strengthen longitudinally from outside the shell, by truss and girder, not from a skeleton within it. Every complete protocol in this article, Abydos and Khufu alike, is a variation on that order of operations.

VIII. The Abydos Boats, Step by Step — the Oldest Planked Hulls, c. 3000 BCE

In 1991, beside the funerary enclosures of the earliest kings at Abydos, excavators of the Pennsylvania- Yale-Institute of Fine Arts expedition under David O'Connor identified a cemetery of boat graves: fourteen wooden boats, each buried in its own boat-shaped mudbrick casing, plastered and whitewashed, prows toward the Nile. They date to about 3000 BCE, around the opening of the First Dynasty, earlier than the late Second Dynasty enclosure of Khasekhemwy beside which they lie, and they are, in Ward's formulation, the world's most ancient complex watercraft: not dugouts, not rafts, but planked hulls, built up from shaped timbers into vessels some 18 to 24 metres long (the most fully discussed example is about 23 metres, or 75 feet), only some two feet deep, narrow in the beam at roughly two to three metres, with fine, narrowing prows and sterns, capable, on Ward's assessment, of carrying a crew of as many as thirty rowers. Whether every buried boat had first served on the water is not demonstrable hull by hull; that they are real, full-scale, buildable vessels and not models is the excavation's central finding.

Only a portion of one boat, Boat 10, has been excavated in detail, in the 2000 season, and intellectual honesty requires the article to build its protocol on that portion and to say so. Much of Boat 10's wood had been reduced by termites to frass, the insect residue that, remarkably, retained the hull's shape and preserved the evidence of its construction: plank dimensions, channels, lashing remains and tool marks, readable in the ghost of the timber. The protocol below is Ward's reconstruction from that excavation (Ward, Antiquity 80, 2006, and her earlier reports), stated step by step.

The Abydos Protocol

Step 1 — Convert short local timber into thick planks. Tamarisk (Section IV) was sawn into planks of regular thickness, short by any later standard, their edges dressed for close edge-to-edge fitting. Saws shaped the planks; the regularity of the surviving dimensions is itself evidence of a codified practice, standardised, as Ward argues, by the developing state as early as the centuries around 3300 to 3100 BCE.

Step 2 — Lay the shell without a frame. The hull was constructed from the outside in, in Ward's phrase: there was no internal framing to build upon. Planks were assembled edge to edge into a shallow, flat-bottomed shell, its rise at bow and stern achieved in the fitting of the planks themselves. Out of the water, unsupported by a skeleton, such a shell can twist, and some Abydos hulls did; in the water, the shell was the structure.

Step 3 — Cut the lashing channels. With a chisel, the shipwright cut channels for the lashings, and here Abydos shows its distinctive signature. In most of the world's sewn-boat traditions, planks are lashed along their edges. At Abydos the channels run diagonally through the thickness of the plank, from its inner face to a point beneath the middle of its edge, so that the lashing, when passed, draws the planks together across the hull rather than simply stitching a seam. Ward presents this cross-hull lashing as a unique Egyptian transfer of a wider sewn-boat idea, and it is the earliest detailed fastening system that survives anywhere.

Step 4 — Lash the hull with woven straps. Through the channels passed the lashings, remains of which survived as woven straps or bands of plant fibre. Drawn tight strake by strake, they pulled the short planks into one shell and held them under the working strains of rowing and loading. The hull was, in the literal sense, sewn.

Step 5 — Pack the seams. Bundles of reeds or grass were stuffed between the planks to seal the seams (Section XI), and reeds likewise covered the floor of each boat, a dunnage layer over the bottom planking on which crew and cargo stood.

Step 6 — Finish and colour the hull. Boats were plastered or painted in their graves, several white, Boat 10 with yellow pigment on its planks. Whether that finish reproduces a working livery or belongs to the burial alone cannot be settled from the graves, and the article leaves it open.

What Abydos proves, stated at its real strength, is this: by about 3000 BCE Egypt possessed a mature, standardised planked-boat technology, with sawn planks, chiselled lashing channels, a distinctive cross-hull sewing system, seam packing, and hulls of twenty metres and more, administered at a scale that buried fourteen of them beside a royal funerary monument. Technologies of that maturity are not invented in the generation that buries them. Abydos is the earliest surviving hull evidence, not the birthday of Egyptian boatbuilding, and the Predynastic depictions of large, crewed boats (Section II) are the record of the centuries of development that the desert at Abydos happened, once, to preserve.

IX. The Khufu Ship, Step by Step — Mortise, Tenon and Lashing at Full Scale

Five and a half centuries after Abydos, the same system produced the most famous ancient vessel in the world. In 1954 Kamal el-Mallakh uncovered a sealed pit on the south side of the Great Pyramid at Giza, and in it, laid in thirteen layers in logical, disassembled order, lay a complete ship of the reign of Khufu (c. 2589–2566 BCE): 1,224 pieces of wood, which the restorer Ahmed Youssef Moustafa spent some fourteen years reassembling. The reconstructed vessel is 43.4 metres long and 5.9 metres in beam, about a metre and a half to 1.78 metres deep amidships. It was displayed from 1982 in its own museum at Giza and moved in 2021 to the Grand Egyptian Museum. A second, sister vessel from the adjacent pit has been excavated and studied in recent decades by a Japanese-Egyptian mission. The first ship's funerary and solar meaning is treated in the Solar Barque, Section XXII, and is not repeated here. This section treats the ship as what, constructionally, it also is: the largest surviving demonstration of the Egyptian shipwright's method.

The Khufu Protocol

Step 1 — Lay long cedar planking, shell-first, without a keel. The hull is built largely of Lebanese cedar (Section IV), its bottom formed not on a keel but of several longitudinal planks laid together as a flat base, the strakes rising from them to a flared, elegant sheer, with high, curving stem and stern posts in the inherited papyrus silhouette (Section VI). Planks are thick, individually shaped, some exceeding ten metres, and fitted edge to edge with a precision that the reassembler could read plank by plank. The shell is formed first, as at Abydos.

Step 2 — Align the strakes with unpegged mortise-and-tenon joints. Into mortises cut in the mating edges of adjacent planks fit flat tenons of hard local wood, sidr, Christ's-thorn (Ziziphus spina-christi), in the standard identification. The tenons hold the planks in alignment and register, strake to strake, along the whole hull. They are not pegged through. In the Khufu system, as in the tradition Ward defines (Section VII), alignment and holding are separate jobs done by separate devices.

Step 3 — Lash the shell together with halfa-grass rope. The holding is done by rope. Lashings of halfa grass (Desmostachya bipinnata) pass through V-shaped channels cut in the inner faces of the planks and are drawn tight across the hull, lashing strakes together and tying frames and beams into the shell. Reconstructions of the full lashing system reckon its rope in kilometres, a figure sometimes given as about five thousand metres; the article reports that as a reconstruction estimate, not a measurement of surviving rope. No metal nails fasten the hull. Its strength is the strength of shaped wood in compression, tenons in alignment, and rope in tension, working together.

Step 4 — Fit beams, deck and internal timbers into the finished shell. Cross-beams at deck level tie the sides, carry the decking, and give the shell its transverse stiffness. A deckhouse or canopy structure amidships, the vessel's cabin in the reconstructions, is fitted over the deck. Internal timbers follow the shell; they do not precede it.

Step 5 — Fit propulsion and steering. The ship carries oars, twelve in the usual count of the reconstruction, including two great steering oars at the stern quarters, worked as ḥmw [Gardiner: V28-G17-G43-P2] [𓎛𓅓𓅱𓊝] (Section XIV). Whether the vessel was ever rigged for sail, and whether it ever floated at all before its burial, are genuinely debated: it bears indications that have been read as signs of use in water, and it lacks, in its buried state, a complete sailing rig. The article records the debate and adopts neither romance: the hull is a real hull, built by the full working method, whatever voyages, ceremonial or practical, preceded its dismantling.

Step 6 — Take it apart. The final step of the Khufu protocol is the one the pit itself documents. The ship was disassembled, plank from lashing, beam from shell, and stacked in order in its pit, an operation possible because its fastenings were rope and unpegged tenon rather than nail and peg. Disassembly was not an improvisation of the burial party. It was the last exercise of the design principle of Section VII, and on the Red Sea coast, as the next section shows, it was routine working practice.

Set Abydos and Khufu side by side and the tradition's grammar is fully visible. The materials escalate, tamarisk to cedar, the scale doubles, the tenon joins the lashing as a second fastening family, and the finish becomes royal. The order of operations does not change: shell first, planks edge to edge, rope in tension, beams after, no keel, no nails. Five centuries separate the two anchors, and the second is intelligible as the first's descendant without a single foreign import of method.

X. Continuity and Disassembly — Dahshur, Lisht, Gawasis and Ships Built to Come Apart

Between Khufu and the Late Period, the hull record runs through three Middle Kingdom bodies of evidence, each of a different character, and together they prove that the Abydos-Khufu system was the ordinary technology of the river and the sea, not a funerary speciality.

The Dahshur boats, excavated by Jacques de Morgan in 1894 beside the pyramid of Senwosret III, are four surviving funerary boats of about ten metres, now in museum collections, whose good preservation has made them, in Creasman's assessment, the most revealing of the Middle Kingdom timbers for tools and construction. Their hulls repeat the grammar: a shell of thick planks, a single central bottom strake only slightly thicker than its neighbours where later writers have wanted a keel, edge fastenings, deck beams, sparse framing. The Lisht timbers, disarticulated remains of Twelfth Dynasty work boats from the capital zone, show the same construction in vessels that were never funerary at all, and show it in pieces: a work boat's life ended in dismantling and reuse, and its timbers entered the archaeological record precisely because they had been taken apart.

The Red Sea timbers complete the argument. At Mersa/Wadi Gawasis, the Middle Kingdom port for the Punt voyages (Section XVIII), excavations by Rodolfo Fattovich and Kathryn Bard and their teams from 2001 onward recovered, stored in man-made galleries cut into the fossil coral terrace, the disassembled timbers of seagoing ships: thick cedar planks with paired, unpegged mortise-and-tenon joints, steering-oar blades, anchors in the making, shipworm-riddled wood that had plainly sailed, and timbers recut and recycled into ramps, boxes and other port furniture. Ward, Zazzaro and their colleagues, in the International Journal of Nautical Archaeology report on the 2004–06 seasons (2009), draw the conclusion the timbers compel: Egyptian river-oriented design and construction succeeded at sea, on round trips to Punt of more than two thousand kilometres. At Ayn Soukhna, the Gulf of Suez port used from the Old and Middle Kingdoms, galleries likewise preserved boat timbers stored underground between expeditions.

The system behind the galleries deserves stating as a procedure, because it is one of the most distinctive logistical practices of the ancient world. A seagoing expedition did not keep a standing fleet on an inhospitable coast with no timber, little fresh water and no permanent large population. Instead:

1. Ships were built, or their timbers prepared and numbered, in Nile-side yards where timber, rope, labour and food were available. 2. The vessels were disassembled, and the timbers carried across the Eastern Desert by donkey and manpower along the wadi roads to the Red Sea port. 3. At the port the ships were reassembled, caulked and launched for the voyage south. 4. On return, the ships were taken apart again; sound timbers were stored in the galleries against the next expedition, damaged or worm-eaten timbers were cut down and recycled on site. The Gawasis galleries are warehouses for flat-packed ships, and the assembly marks and recut ends on their timbers are the paperwork of that system in wood. Ward's 2006 observation about Abydos, that Egyptian boats were built to be taken apart, turns out to describe not a funerary quirk but an imperial logistics doctrine, practised for two thousand years.

Recycling closes the section. Ship timbers in Egypt rarely died as ships. Planks became coffins, doors, ramps and boxes; a hull's second and third lives are a standing complication for excavators, who must decide whether a tenoned cedar plank in a tomb or a store-room began as furniture or as a strake, and a standing proof of Section IV's timber economy. Scarcity made the ship a bank of seasoned wood, drawn on when the vessel's sailing life ended.

XI. Caulking, Sealing and Waterproofing — What the Seams Actually Show

A planked shell is not watertight by carpentry alone, and this section is written with deliberate care, because caulking is the point at which popular accounts most often import foreign practice, bitumen by the barrel, hot pitch, tarred oakum, into an Egyptian record that shows something simpler and, in its own terms, sufficient. The rule here is the archive's usual one: the seams say what they say, site by site, and analogy is labelled as analogy.

The primary sealing mechanism of the Egyptian hull was the joint itself. Thick planks, accurately fitted edge to edge and drawn hard together by lashings under tension, leave little gap to fill, and wood swells when wetted: a lashed shell launched dry, its seams visibly open to a modern eye, tightens in the water as its planks take up moisture and expand against one another and against their tenons. Reconstructions and replicas of Egyptian-type hulls rely on that swelling as the first line of watertightness, and the lashing system assists it, because rope that can be re-tensioned lets the crew take up slack as the hull works and settles. A sewn hull is, in this respect, maintainable in a way a nailed hull is not: its seams can be re-drawn by its own crew with its own materials.

The positive caulking evidence, where it survives, is fibrous packing. At Abydos, bundles of reeds or grass were stuffed between the planks to seal the seams (Section VIII): a soft, compressible, locally free material, driven into the seam as the lashings were tightened, functioning as the gasket of the joint. Fibre packing of this kind, renewed as it rotted or worked loose, is the caulking the earliest hulls demonstrably had. For later hulls the direct evidence thins, because packing rots where wood survives, and the article states the honest position: seam packing with plant fibre is attested at the origin of the tradition and is the reasonable default for its continuation, while a universal, detailed caulking recipe for all periods does not survive and is not invented here.

Coatings require the same discipline. Resins and oils were known to Egyptian woodworkers, bitumen was known in Egypt as an imported material and used in other crafts, and hulls were painted and plastered in some contexts (Section VIII). What the surviving hull corpus does not provide is a demonstrated, standard practice of tarring or pitch-paying whole hulls in the manner of later Mediterranean or northern traditions, and this article does not supply one. Where a specific coating claim is made for a specific timber in the excavation literature, it belongs to that timber. The working Egyptian answer to a leaking hull, on the evidence as a whole, was fit, fibre, swelling, re-tensioning, bailing, and, when a strake failed, the repair regime of Section XXI. The bailer, in a shallow, open hull, was not an admission of failure. It was standard equipment, named, as the Solar Barque article shows, even in the spell literature's inventory of a boat's parts.

Shipworm deserves a sentence of its own, because it is the Red Sea evidence's most eloquent detail. Timbers at Gawasis bear the borings of marine shipworm, proof that the stored wood had spent real time in warm sea water, and a standing threat to any wooden hull kept long in that sea. The disassembly system of Section X was, among its other virtues, a worm defence: a ship stored dry in a gallery above the shore does not feed worms. On the Nile, in fresh water, the threat receded, and the river hull's enemies were instead rot at the waterline, abrasion on banks and shallows, and the working loose of its lashings, all of them addressed by hauling out, drying, and the shipwright's ordinary round.

XII. Propulsion I — Paddle, Oar and the Rowing Bench

Muscle is the oldest propulsion, and its history in Egypt is a history of two implements. The paddle, worked freehand by a kneeling or seated paddler who is himself the fulcrum, drives the earliest depicted craft and remains the implement of the small papyrus skiff and the marsh (Section VI). The oar, longer, worked against a fulcrum on the vessel's side or through a loop, by a rower seated or standing at a bench, is the implement of size: it scales, by adding benches, from a ferry to the thirty-oar crews of the Abydos hulls and the Punt ships. Vinson's UEE summary states the sequence in its proper caution: paddling is the oldest form of propulsion, with some evidence for towing beside it, and sailing arrives later (Section XIII). The oar's advantage is organisation. Thirty paddlers are thirty individuals; thirty rowers on benches, pulling to a stroke set by a steersman or a time-beater, are an engine whose power can be ordered, sustained and rested in watches.

Rowing arrangements, as models and scenes present them, place rowers along both sides in the open waist of the vessel, the captain and lookout forward, the steersman aft at the steering oars (Section XIV), and passengers, cargo and cabin amidships where the motion is least. On great state barges and processional vessels the rowing crew could be very large, and the texts' crew figures for royal vessels, which run in some claims into the hundreds, should be read as the archive reads all such figures: as statements of royal scale, anchored where possible by the bench arithmetic of a hull's length. A hull of twenty metres cannot seat a hundred rowers a side, whatever a panegyric says, and the models, which show crews in the dozens for large vessels, are the soberer witness.

Downstream, muscle could rest. A vessel bound north could ship oars and let the current work, rowing only to steer, to cross to a landing, or to make a schedule against a slack reach. Upstream under oars alone, against current and, if the wind failed, with no sail to help, was the expensive case the whole system was designed to avoid (Sections III and XV). The rowing bench was therefore the auxiliary engine of a sailing and drifting system, not its primary mover over distance, except in war, ceremony and the calm: the cases where schedule and display outranked economy.

XIII. Propulsion II — the Sail, and the Earliest Evidence Dated Honestly

The sail is the most consequential single addition to the Egyptian boat, and its earliest evidence is the most frequently overstated in popular writing, so this section separates three claims that are usually run together: a depiction that may show a sail, a depiction that certainly shows a sail, and a sailing system in regular use.

The candidate at the head of every list is a painted storage jar of the Naqada II period, showing a boat with a structure that has been widely reproduced as the earliest sail in the world, dated in the popular literature to about 3200 BCE or earlier. The UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology's entry on Predynastic art (Wengrow, 2009) contains the necessary cold water: that jar, a piece that passed through the antiquities market, is of doubtful authenticity, and its image is, in Wengrow's words in paraphrase, still widely but unreliably cited as the earliest evidence for sail-powered transport in the eastern Mediterranean. This article therefore does not rest the sail's history on it. A claim that needs that jar is a claim the evidence does not make.

What the reliable record does show is a late Predynastic horizon. Boats are depicted in quantity on Naqada II pottery, in rock art such as the fleets at Nag el-Hamdulab (c. 3200–3100 BCE), and in the painted tomb at Hierakonpolis, large, crewed vessels with cabins, standards and many oars; masts and sails enter the depictions toward the end of the Predynastic, and Vinson's considered formulation, which this article adopts, is that sailing was probably introduced towards the end of the late Predynastic period. By the Early Dynastic and Old Kingdom the sailing rig is fully documented in scenes and models, and the Abydos hulls stand at the threshold: built in a world in which the sail existed, though no sail, mast or rigging survives from the boat graves themselves to prove how any individual Abydos vessel was rigged. The honest date for the sail in Egypt is therefore a range and a process, late fourth millennium BCE, secure by the early third, and not a single triumphant year.

The mature Egyptian rig, as Old Kingdom scenes establish it and as it then persists with variations for two thousand years, can be described as a working system. A single mast, in early periods often a bipod mast, two legs stepped either side of the hull's centreline and joined at the head, a form that suits a keel-less shell with no single massive mast-step timber, later generally a single pole mast. On the mast, a single square sail, wider than it is tall in the classic proportions, bent to a yard at its head and, in many periods, a boom at its foot, the sail of linen in the surviving references and reconstructions. Standing rigging, forestay and backstays, steadied the mast; sheets controlled the sail's foot; the whole rig could be lowered, mast and all, for rowing, for towing, for bridges of reeds and shallows, and for the northbound run when the sail was not wanted. The square sail is a downwind and reaching sail: superb for its designed purpose, running south before the north wind, limited for working to windward, which Egyptian practice in any case rarely required on a river that ran the other way. Claims for sophisticated windward performance in pharaonic rigs exceed the evidence and are not made here.

Sailcloth and cordage complete the system and return the article to its materials. A great sail is a major textile product, woven, sewn from cloths, edged and rigged with rope, maintained and repaired as canvas was in every later sailing navy, and its cost is part of why sailing at scale is a state and temple capability before it is a fisherman's. The wind itself, ṯꜣw [Gardiner: V13-G1-G43] [𓍿𓄿𓅱], was the free input; everything that caught it had to be built, and the dockyard accounts count sails and rope among a vessel's accountable stores.

XIV. Steering, Rig and Longitudinal Strength — Steering Oars and the Hogging Truss

An Egyptian vessel of size was steered, for most of its history, not by a hinged rudder on the sternpost, an invention of a much later world, but by one or more great steering oars worked over the stern quarters. The ḥmw [Gardiner: V28-G17-G43-P2] [𓎛𓅓𓅱𓊝] was a long oar with a broad blade, its loom shipped through a lashing or against a post at the quarter, its head fitted in developed periods with a tiller by which the steersman, standing aft with a view over the vessel, could swing the blade and so turn the hull. Early and small craft manage with a single steering oar, commonly on one quarter. Great vessels carry two, one to each quarter, as the Khufu ship was fitted (Section IX), giving redundancy, finer control in a crowd of shipping, and steering power in either current or seaway. The two steering-oar blades among the Gawasis timbers, comparable to early New Kingdom examples (Section X), show the sea-going form of the same implement. A central stern rudder-oar, mounted on the centreline, becomes common only in the latest periods, under wider Mediterranean influence, and Ship 17 (Section XXII) belongs to that late world of transition.

Longitudinal strength, the shell-first hull's known weakness (Section VII), was supplied by rope. The hogging truss is a heavy rope, or set of ropes, carried from the bow over supports or a crutch amidships to the stern, and tensioned, in the classic depiction, by a tourniquet-like twisting with a lever. It acts as the top chord of a girder of which the hull is the bottom chord: it resists hogging, the bending that lifts the middle and droops the unsupported bow and stern, to which a long, shallow vessel with heavy ends is prone, above all at sea and in the great cargo barges. Trusses appear in the iconography from the Old Kingdom seagoing reliefs of Sahure onward, are unmistakable in the Punt ships of Hatshepsut (Section XVIII), and have a riverine cousin in the girdle and truss arrangements of the obelisk barges (Section XVI). A second rope system, the girdle truss worked around the hull itself in some depictions, braced the shell transversely. Both systems share the tradition's signature: strength added in tension, in rope, adjustable and replaceable, over a shell built in compression in wood.

Decks, beams and superstructure complete the working vessel. Through-beams at deck level, their ends visible in profile in the reliefs, tie the shell and carry a deck of planks or of matting over beams, leaving the waist open in working craft and decking the whole length in prestige and seagoing vessels. Forecastle and sterncastle platforms, raised in the New Kingdom seagoing type, give the commander, lookout and steersman their stations. Cabins, from a light reed shelter on a river boat to the deckhouse of a travelling ship and the shrine cabin of a processional barque, occupy the midships deck. None of these additions alters the hull's construction. They are furniture on a shell, and the archaeologist who finds only the shell has lost the furniture, not the ship.

XV. Towing, Poling and Tracking — Moving from the Bank and in Shallow Water

The third propulsion system, after muscle at the oar and wind at the sail, is the tow-rope, and on the Egyptian river it was not a fallback but a routine. Tracking, hauling a vessel from the bank by a line made fast to the mast or the bow, by men or by draught animals on the towpath, moved cargo upstream when the wind failed, moved it in canals where sailing was impossible, and moved the greatest loads of all: the obelisk barge of the Deir el-Bahari relief travels under tow from a flotilla of rowing tugs (Section XVI), the riverine application of the same principle at royal scale. Tomb scenes of agricultural and estate life show the small end of the practice, barges and boats hauled along canals by gangs on the bank, the line led fair, the steersman keeping the hull off the bank with his oar while the tow does the work.

Poling governs the shallows. A long pole, thrust to the bottom and walked along the deck or worked from the bow, moves a light craft in marsh, canal and low-water channel where oars cannot be pulled and sails cannot draw, and the fisherman's and fowler's craft of Section VI lived by it. The sounding pole, its professional descendant, belongs to navigation rather than propulsion: in the pilot's hands it reads the channel, finds the bank in a reach, and warns the steersman before the hull does. Grounding, on a river of shifting sand, was an ordinary incident, and the texts and scenes treat it without drama: lighten the vessel by landing part of the cargo, pole and haul her off, wait for a rise, or, in the worst case, unload her entirely and reload beyond the shoal. The shallow, flat-bottomed, keel-less hull is itself the chief anti-grounding device: it takes the ground upright, draws little, and can be floated off a bank that would hole or heel a deep-keeled vessel.

Warping and kedging, hauling a vessel toward an anchor or a fixed point by its own lines, are the harbour applications of the tow principle, and ferrying by line, the current swung ferry on a rope across a channel, served the innumerable crossings of a river that divided fields, towns and necropolises from one another. The archive's point in gathering these unglamorous methods into one section is that they, and not the sail alone, are what made the system complete. Wind and current served the main stream. Rope from the bank served everywhere else.

XVI. Cargo Practice I — Loading, Stone Transport and the Diary of Merer

Stone is the cargo that tests a transport system, because it is dense, indivisible at the largest sizes, and needed where it does not occur. Egypt's building stone moved by water as a matter of doctrine: fine white limestone from Tura, across the river from the Memphite sites; sandstone from Gebel el-Silsila; granite and quartzite from Aswan and the north, floated hundreds of kilometres to Thebes, Memphis and the Delta. The companion treatment of the building sites themselves is given in Giza; this section treats the carriage, which is this article's subject.

Loading practice can be reconstructed from the convergence of scenes, models and the physics of the hull. Cargo was loaded at a bank, a quay or a sloping revetment, over gangplanks and, for blocks, over prepared timber ways, with the vessel moored head and stern and, for the heaviest lifts, settled or steadied so that a concentrated load could not roll her. Weight went low and amidships, spread over the bottom planking and the deck beams by timber dunnage, never concentrated on a single strake: a shell-first hull carries load as its bottom and beams allow, and the shipmaster's art was distribution. Blocks travelled on sledges or timber cradles that could be hauled aboard and ashore as units. At the destination the process reversed, ideally at flood, when the harbour basin brought the vessel to the foot of the building site's causeway (Section XIX).

Merer's Round Trips — a Working Schedule in a Logbook

For one stone traffic, uniquely, we do not have to reconstruct. The Diary of Merer (Sections II and XIX), the logbooks of an official with the title of inspector, kept in the year after the thirteenth cattle count of Khufu, conventionally his twenty-sixth or twenty-seventh regnal year, records his crew's work across several months, from roughly July to November, hauling white limestone from the quarries of Tura North and Tura South to Akhet-Khufu, the Great Pyramid site at Giza. In paraphrase of Tallet's published translation, the entries follow a repeating pattern over a cycle of days: a day spent with his crew, his phyle, hauling stone at Tura and spending the night there; a cast-off, loaded with stone, for Akhet-Khufu, with a night at an intermediate mooring the texts call She-Khufu; a sail onward to Akhet-Khufu and a night at the destination; then a cast-off upstream, empty, back toward Tura to load again. The round trip occupies about four days in the best-preserved sequences, and the crew, on the usual reading of the accounts, numbers about forty men. Modern summaries of the diary commonly reckon two to three round trips in each ten-day period, loads on the order of thirty blocks of two to three tonnes at a time, and a delivery in the region of two hundred blocks a month from this one crew and its vessel or vessels. Those aggregates are modern arithmetic on a fragmentary log, and the article labels them as such; the log itself, day by day, is primary evidence of a kind Egyptology possesses for no other building project.

What the diary proves about cargo practice is worth listing plainly. The traffic was scheduled, in named stages with named overnight moorings. It ran through canals and a harbour basin at the destination, not simply down an open river (Section XIX). It was crewed by an organised, provisioned, accountable gang whose rations and movements the same papyri record. And it was routine: the diary's tone is that of a foreman's timesheet, not of a wonder accomplished. The Great Pyramid's casing stone arrived the way grain arrived, by boat, on a roster.

The Greatest Loads — Obelisk Barges and the Evidence at Its Strength

At the system's upper limit stand the obelisk barges. The biographical inscription of the official Ineni, in his Theban tomb, records in paraphrase that he built a great boat of 120 cubits in length and 40 cubits in width, about 63 by 21 metres, to transport two obelisks for Thutmose I. The figure is a text's figure for a vessel that does not survive, and the article gives it as such. A generation later, the mortuary temple of Hatshepsut at Deir el-Bahari depicts, in the famous relief, a great barge bearing two granite obelisks lying on its deck, towed by a flotilla conventionally counted at twenty-seven rowing boats in three files, with accompanying vessels, on the run from Aswan to Thebes. Estimates of the depicted obelisks' individual weights vary widely in the modern literature, commonly in the low hundreds of tonnes each. What the relief proves, read as Section II directs, is that the state presented the carriage of two obelisks on one hull, under tow, as an accomplished royal fact, and that its artists knew the towing flotilla, the barge's trussing and the order of the procession in convincing detail. The naval-architectural question, how a timber shell of that beam carried that load, turns on load-spreading, on the barge's great breadth, on travelling at high water, and, in some reconstructions, on the hull being supported or built around its cargo in ways the relief does not specify. The article records the debate and claims neither the sceptics' impossibility nor the popular literature's exact tonnage. The Lateran obelisk, the largest successfully erected in Egypt, at some 32 metres and commonly estimated above 400 tonnes in its original state, guarantees that single stones of that order did travel. The route was the river. There was no other.

Stone, in short, is inr [Gardiner: O39] [𓊌] that floats only by proxy, and the proxy was the broad, flat, lashed freighter, mnš [Gardiner: G17-N35-N37-P1] [𓅓𓈖𓈙𓊛] and its royal enlargements. Every temple wall in Egypt that stands in imported stone is a delivered cargo manifest, read in elevation.

XVII. Cargo Practice II — Grain Fleets, Granaries and Ordinary Freight

Stone built the monuments, but grain built the state, and grain moved by boat. The annual cycle of assessment, collection and redistribution that fed the temples, the court, the workforces and the cities ran on the river: grain assessed in the fields, threshed and winnowed at the village, measured into sacks or loaded in bulk, carried to the riverbank, and shipped in cargo boats to the granaries of the nomes and the capital. The archive's Food and Drink article treats the grain as food; Agriculture treats it as a crop. Here it is freight.

The vessel of the grain trade was the ordinary river freighter in its many sizes, broad, flat-bottomed, open-waisted, its cargo heaped or sacked amidships under matting against sun and spray, its crew small beside the stone barges: a master, a steersman, a handful of hands who were also its porters at the landings. Scenes of the harvest in New Kingdom tombs show the whole chain in sequence, from the measuring of the grain in the field, under the scribes' eyes, to its carriage to the boats and the boats' departure, and the scribal presence is the point. Grain in transit was accounted grain: measured out, measured in, with the boat as the accountable space between two granary doors. The fleets, ḥꜥw [Gardiner: V28-D36-G43-P1] [𓎛𓂝𓅱𓊛] in the collective word, that moved the Theban temple estates' revenues in the New Kingdom, and the state grain fleets of every period, were less a merchant marine than a floating branch of the granary administration.

Beside grain, the ordinary freight of the river was everything the economy contained: cattle, ferried in special crossings and carried standing in open boats in the scenes; timber and firewood; pottery, in cargoes whose wrecks and harbour deposits still map the trade; wine and oil in jars, the jars themselves the unit of account; linen, stone vessels, salt, dried fish, and, in the return direction, the produce of the Delta for the valley and of the valley for the Delta. Passenger traffic rode the same hulls: officials on circuit with their staffs and baggage, priests and festival crowds, work gangs moving to a royal project, soldiers to a frontier, and, at the summit of the system, the king himself, whose travelling palace on the river, described in New Kingdom texts with its great cabin and its escort, is the state moving in state. Ferries, the system's smallest unit, crossed and recrossed at every town, carrying the donkeys, market goods and people for whom the river was a daily obstacle as much as a road.

Two disciplines governed all of it. The first was seasonality: bulk traffic rode the flood and the reliable winds, granaries filled when the river allowed, and a low Nile was a transport crisis as well as an agricultural one, a coupling the famine texts understand perfectly. The second was the toll and the landing: quays were administered places, with officials, measures and dues, and the harbour texts and later customs evidence show the state taxing movement at the points where movement had to pause. A river that everyone must use is a river the state can watch, and Egyptian administration, from the Old Kingdom harbourmasters to the Ptolemaic customs houses, watched it at the landings.

XVIII. Seagoing Ships — Byblos, the Great Green, Punt and the Location Debate

Egyptian seagoing was old, state-run and, by the standards of its river traffic, rare. Two seas, two systems. Northward, across the Mediterranean's eastern basin, ran the Byblos route to the Lebanese coast, the timber road of Section IV, served from the Early Dynastic period by the vessels the texts name for their destination, the Byblos-ships whose defining element is kbn [Gardiner: V31-D58-N35-P1] [𓎡𓃀𓈖𓊛]. South-eastward, through the Gulf of Suez and down the Red Sea, ran the expeditions to Punt, the incense land, served not by a standing route but by periodic royal ventures, their ships assembled at desert ports from timbers carried overland (Sections X and XIX). Ward's 2012 synthesis gives the scale of the Red Sea achievement: round-trip journeys of more than two thousand kilometres, regularly undertaken from the Old Kingdom onward.

The seagoing hull, on the physical evidence of Gawasis (Section X), was the river hull's method built heavier for a harder theatre: thick cedar planking, unpegged tenons and lashings, deck beams, steering oars of developed form, hogging truss above (Section XIV), anchors of stone in the making at the port. The iconography agrees. The earliest detailed seagoing reliefs, those of Sahure's fleet in his Fifth Dynasty mortuary temple, show long, trussed vessels with bipod masts, returning with foreign prisoners and cargo. The Punt reliefs of Hatshepsut at Deir el-Bahari, the fullest seagoing dossier Egypt left, show five great ships at the Puntite coast, their hulls streamlined by Egyptian convention yet convincingly particular: raised fore and aft platforms, screens, a single great square sail on a mast whose yard is as long as the vessel is broad many times over, fifteen rowers a side in the usual reading of the relief, steering oars aft, and the hogging truss carried over crutches the length of the ship. Reconstructions from the relief commonly put such a vessel at roughly twenty to twenty-five metres. Loading scenes show the cargo coming aboard: incense trees in baskets, sacks and jars, ebony, ivory, gold, live animals, the whole inw [Gardiner: M17-N35-G43] [𓇋𓈖𓅱] of Punt, carried down to the water and hoisted or handed into a hull riding at anchor in a roadstead, for Punt, on the relief's own showing, had no harbour works of the Egyptian kind.

Where Was Punt? — the Debate Stated at Its Strength

Punt's location is the classic open question of Egyptian seafaring, and this article states the debate rather than settling it. The reliefs and texts place Punt at the far end of a Red Sea voyage, a land of incense terraces, gold, ebony and exotic animals, reached also, in some periods, by overland and Nile routes through Nubia, which is one reason the evidence can be read in two directions. The mainstream modern position places Punt on the African shore of the southern Red Sea and the Horn, in the broad region of eastern Sudan, Eritrea, Ethiopia and northern Somalia, with the balance of opinion favouring the Eritrean-Ethiopian-Sudanese coast for the classic New Kingdom voyages. A minority tradition has argued for a substantial Arabian component, Punt on both shores of the southern Red Sea. Isotope and, more recently, ancient DNA and isotopic work on mummified baboons, animals imported from Punt, has been brought to bear on the question, with results reported as consistent with an Eritrea and eastern Ethiopia source region for the specimens tested. The article's position is the archive's usual one on a live question: the African Horn and southern Red Sea African coast is the centre of gravity; the specimens tested support it for those specimens; Punt was in any case probably a region and a trading network rather than a single port with a modern equivalent, and the reliefs' harbour-less roadstead is what an expedition found at the end of the voyage, whatever modern coastline it lay on.

Mediterranean seagoing followed a different logic: shorter crossings, a known coast, ports at the far end, and, by the New Kingdom, an eastern Mediterranean world of Egyptian, Levantine, Cypriot and Aegean hulls in one another's harbours, of which the Uluburun wreck off the Turkish coast, a Levantine vessel carrying an international cargo at the end of the fourteenth century BCE, is the excavated emblem. Egypt in that world was a destination and a customer as much as a carrier: foreign hulls, the br [Gardiner: D58-D21-P1] [𓃀𓂋𓊛] of the texts among them, brought goods to Delta ports, and Egyptian bottoms made the Byblos run that the timber supply required. Naval war enters the record at its end: the Sea Peoples battles of Ramesses III at Medinet Habu, with their grappled, crowded fighting ships, show Egyptian naval forces in the Delta in the twelfth century BCE, in reliefs that are the Mediterranean's earliest detailed depiction of a sea battle, and that already show Egyptian and foreign rigs converging. The companion treatment of the diplomatic system within which the timber and incense trades moved is given in Trade and Diplomacy; the hulls that executed it are here.

XIX. Harbours, Basins and Canals — Wadi al-Jarf, Giza and the Red Sea Ports

A boat system is only as good as its landings, and Egypt built them at every scale, from a sloping bank with mooring stones to the oldest artificial harbour yet known in the world.

Wadi al-Jarf — the Oldest Harbour, and a Port That Stored Ships

Wadi al-Jarf, on the Red Sea coast some 119 kilometres south of Suez, excavated by Tallet and Marouard, is a Fourth Dynasty port of the reigns of Sneferu and Khufu, and the oldest known artificial harbour: a stone jetty or mole, about 150 to 160 metres long in the published descriptions, built out into a lagoon and still visible at low tide; a great stone-built complex on the shore, the largest pharaonic building yet found on the Red Sea coast, divided into long rooms; a landmark of heaped stones on the headland; and, set back in the limestone foothills, some twenty-five to thirty galleries cut into the rock, storage chambers in which the expedition's equipment, jars, textiles, wood and, between seasons, boat timbers were kept. More than a hundred stone anchors, over 130 in the fuller counts, many inscribed with boat names, were recovered in the harbour area, the first Old Kingdom anchors found in their original context. The port's business was the crossing to the Sinai, to the turquoise and copper mines of Wadi Maghara and Serabit el-Khadim, and its life was short and intense, bound to the great royal project of its generation. It was in the galleries here, in 2013, that the team found the papyri that include Merer's logbooks (Section XVI), the oldest inscribed papyri known from Egypt, stored with the port's gear when the harbour was closed down at the end of Khufu's reign. A harbour that files its paperwork in its boat stores is a harbour whose whole system, ships, stores and records, was expeditionary and temporary by design.

Giza, Memphis and the River Harbours

At the river end of Merer's run stood the harbour of the pyramid site itself: the "Basin of Khufu" of the logbooks, an artificial basin at the foot of the Giza plateau, joined by canal to the Nile, where the Tura limestone came ashore at the building site (Section XVI). The pattern generalises. Memphite and Theban harbours served the capitals; temple harbours, with their quays, ramps and, in festival, their processional traffic, fronted the great temples, Karnak's quay and canal to the Nile among them; quarry harbours at Tura, Silsila and Aswan were working landings with revetments, moorings and the debris of loading. Excavation recovers them unevenly, because the river has moved, silted and rebuilt its banks for four thousand years, and many a pharaonic quay lies under metres of alluvium or under a modern town. The texts, the basins still traceable at Giza, and the harbour archaeology of the Delta ports, above all Thonis-Heracleion (Section XXII), carry the record between them.

Canals — the Artificial Rivers

Canals extended the river's logic in three directions. Local canals joined sites to the main stream: pyramid basins, temple canals, estate and town canals, dug, dredged and re-dug as they silted, and navigable, in the main, at the higher water levels of the year, which is why the stone traffic's calendar is a flood calendar (Section III). Regional canals in the Delta and the Faiyum organised whole districts' traffic. The Red Sea canal, joining the eastern Delta, through the Wadi Tumilat, to the Gulf of Suez, is the famous case, and the one where claims most need care: attempts and completions are attributed in the sources to Necho II, who, Herodotus reports in paraphrase, began a canal from the Nile toward the Red Sea and desisted; to Darius I, whose stelae along its course proclaim its completion and the sailing of ships through it to Persia; and to the Ptolemies, who rebuilt it with a lock-like device at its sea end. Whether any pharaoh before Necho completed a through canal, Middle Kingdom claims are sometimes advanced from Senwosret III's inscriptions and the Wadi Tumilat evidence, remains debated, and this article presents the Necho-Darius-Ptolemy sequence as the documented line and the earlier completion as an open question. For most of pharaonic history, the desert road and the flat-packed ship (Section X), not a canal, joined the Nile to the Red Sea.

The Red Sea ports themselves, Wadi al-Jarf in the Old Kingdom, Ayn Soukhna at the Gulf's head in the Old and Middle Kingdoms, Mersa/Wadi Gawasis further south for the Punt run, form a chain with one logic: the nearest workable shore to the Nile valley by a wadi road, a water supply or the means to store it, galleries for the gear, and nothing permanent that the next expedition could not reopen. They are among the most eloquent sites in Egyptian archaeology precisely because they are so functional. Nothing at Gawasis was built to be admired. It was built to launch ships, and it did.

XX. Crew Organisation, Provisioning and Travel Times

An Egyptian vessel's crew, as the models, scenes and accounts jointly present it, divides by function with a consistency that amounts to a table of organisation. At its head the captain or master, owner, official or appointee according to the vessel's station, responsible for cargo, schedule and crew. At the stern the steersman, in great vessels two, at the ḥmw [Gardiner: V28-G17-G43-P2] [𓎛𓅓𓅱𓊝], the vessel's skilled officer, whose station the models never omit. Forward, a lookout, who in river work doubles as the leadsman and sounding hand, watching for banks, traffic and the landing. Along the waist, the rowers, where the vessel rows, organised in the gangs the Egyptian labour system used for everything, the phyle, the crew as a named, rotating company of the kind Merer commands: his mšꜥ [Gardiner: G17-N37-D36] [𓅓𓈙𓂝] on the water is a work gang of about forty, with its own internal officers, its rations accounted in the same papyri that log its voyages. Aboard the cargo vessel, porters and hands for loading, a scribe where the cargo is the state's, and, on expeditions, soldiers, interpreters, craftsmen and officials riding as passengers with a mission. Ship's boys, bailers and cooks complete the working picture the scenes supply.

Provisioning was the expedition system's real discipline. A crew eats and drinks whether the wind serves or not, and the accounts are full of it: bread and beer, the Egyptian ration pair, issued by measure; water, the Red Sea ports' scarcest store, carried in jars whose deposits at Wadi al-Jarf and Gawasis still map the system; dried fish and meat, dates, and the fodder of the donkeys who carried the ships across the desert. The Gawasis evidence adds the outward cargo of an expedition's self-sufficiency: food, gear, spare timber, rope, anchors in reserve, everything a crew would need at a Puntite roadstead where nothing could be bought in Egyptian terms except by the trade goods carried for the purpose. An expedition was a village that floated, provisioned for the round trip.

Travel times can be given only as the evidence allows, in ranges with their anchors named. Merer's cycle (Section XVI) is the fixed point: Tura to Giza, a run of some fifteen to twenty kilometres by the water routes of the time, occupied, loaded, the better part of two days with an overnight stop, and the empty return upstream about a day in the log's pattern, a full round trip of about four days with loading. For the long river, the governing figures are the current's speed and the sailing wind's reliability: downstream passages from Thebes to Memphis, some 650 kilometres, were a matter of days to a week or so in favourable water; upstream under sail, the same distance ran to weeks, and every itinerary, pilgrimage account and official journey in the record breathes that asymmetry without needing to state it. Aswan to Thebes, downstream, was the stone fleets' short leg; Aswan to the sea was the long one. At sea, the Punt round trip, over two thousand kilometres each way on Ward's reckoning of the route, with its assembly, trading and disassembly at either end, was the work of a season and more, an inw [Gardiner: M17-N35-G43] [𓇋𓈖𓅱]-bringing venture measured in years of preparation and months of execution. These are orders of magnitude anchored in the logbooks and the geography, and the article offers them in no finer grain, because the sources offer no finer grain.

Life aboard, in the space the sources leave, can be sketched without romance. Crews slept aboard or ashore at the moorings according to the vessel and the season; cooked at bankside fires or on a hearth box; sang the rowing, if the later literary echoes and the work-song tradition of Egypt are any guide, to songs of the same family as every other gang's; and were paid, fed and replaced through the institutions that owned their vessels, temple, crown or estate. The boat was a workplace with a muster roll. That, in the end, is what Merer's diary is: not a traveller's tale, but a workplace record that happens to float.

XXI. Wreck, Loss, Repair and Recycling — the Working Life and Death of a Hull

Egypt left no wreck catalogue, and the sea and river have kept most of their losses. What survives is a scattered record: hulls abandoned at the water's edge in Ward's census, timbers in harbour silt at Thonis-Heracleion where a port's working vessels sank or were scuttled in its basins (Section XXII), shipworm-eaten planks in the Gawasis galleries, and the literary and administrative shadows of loss, the expedition that did not return, the cargo claimed as lost, the crew accounted for. The famous literary shipwreck, the Middle Kingdom Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor, in which a vessel of 120 cubits with a crew of 120 founders on a voyage to the royal mines and its sole survivor washes up on an island, is a story, and this article uses it as one: evidence for what an Egyptian audience found plausible in a great vessel's size, crew and loss, not for any historical sinking.

The causes of loss follow from the system's own physics. On the river: grounding that became stranding when the water fell; collision in crowded festival and harbour traffic; overloading or bad stowage in a hull whose stability depended on its load's distribution (Section XVI); fire, the cooking hearth against a wooden, rope-bound, resinous world; and the slow losses, rot, worm at sea, lashings worked loose, that turned a vessel unseaworthy before any single accident. At sea the list lengthens: lee shores on a coast with few harbours, the Red Sea's reefs, weather on the Mediterranean crossing, and the hogging and working of a lashed shell in a seaway, the failure mode the truss existed to delay (Section XIV).

Repair, by contrast, is richly evidenced, because the construction system was a repair system. A lashed, tenoned shell can be opened at the damaged strake: lashings cut or slackened, tenons eased, the split or rotted plank removed, a new plank shaped to the opening, its mortises cut to the surviving tenons or new ones fitted, the seam re-packed, the lashings re-passed and re-tensioned, the hull re-launched to swell tight. The timbers themselves carry the case histories: replacement planks in different wood, doubled repairs, recut ends, assembly marks from more than one campaign of work, the whole archaeology of maintenance that Creasman and Ward read in the Middle Kingdom and Gawasis corpora. Dockyards existed for exactly this, the royal shipyard of the Papyrus British Museum 10056 register among them, issuing timber by dimension to named vessels, and every harbour town had its shipwrights as it had its rope-makers. A vessel's life was therefore not a single span from launching to wreck but a maintained continuity, plank by plank, in which the question of when the original boat ceased to exist is one the Egyptians, sensibly, never asked.

End of life, when maintenance finally lost, was recycling (Section X). The hull was unbuilt, as it had been built to be, and its sound timbers entered the timber bank: coffins, doors, furniture, ramps, the boxes of Gawasis, fuel at the last extremity. Rope was unpicked or burned out of the channels; tenons and fittings returned to store. Almost nothing of a boat was waste, in a country that imported its best wood by the shipload, and the archaeologist's scattered, reused, multiply-contexted ship timbers are the direct consequence. Egypt's boats are hard to find whole because Egypt was too economical to leave them whole.

XXII. The Late Survivor — Thonis-Heracleion Ship 17 and the Baris of Herodotus

The tradition's last great witness was found where it worked. Thonis-Heracleion, the Delta port at the Canopic mouth of the Nile, sank beneath Aboukir Bay, and its rediscovery by Franck Goddio's teams from 2000 onward recovered, in the harbour's silt, the remains of more than sixty ancient vessels, among them the freighter catalogued as Ship 17, excavated in 2009–2011 and studied by Alexander Belov and colleagues. Ship 17 is a river freighter of the sixth century BCE, some 28 metres long in its preserved hull, about seventy per cent of it surviving, flat-bottomed, built shell-first of thick local planking, its strakes joined by the family's familiar devices at a late, developed stage: long tenons in mortises, lashing in the internal structure, through-beams, and a hull form whose three-dimensional reconstruction gives a displacement of about 151 tonnes, a draught of about 1.6 metres, and a cargo capacity in the region of 112 tonnes. Those figures are the reconstruction's, published from the excavation's measurements, and are given here as such.

Ship 17 matters beyond its own impressive survival because of a text. Herodotus, in Book Two of the Histories, describes the Egyptian cargo boat he calls the baris, built, in paraphrase of his account, from acacia planks laid like courses of brickwork, joined with long, closely set tenons, with beams laid across above, without ribs, its seams caulked from inside with papyrus, steered by a single rudder-oar passed through the hull's bottom, and rigged with a papyrus sail and a mast of acacia. For centuries that passage stood as a traveller's description without a hull to test it against. Ship 17, built in the port where Herodotus's informants worked, matches the description in its construction system point by point closely enough for its excavators to present it as the type Herodotus describes, the first excavated baris. The article records that identification as the excavation team's, strongly argued from the timbers, and the most satisfying closing of a text-and-timber loop in all nautical archaeology. Two details deserve their caution: Herodotus's internal papyrus caulking is his report, and the correspondence of a sixth-century hull with a fifth-century text is a correspondence of system, not proof that the historian measured this ship. With those cautions entered, the loop holds.

The debate Ship 17 re-opened belongs in the record. Its central bottom member prompted a fresh round of the keel question (Section VII): whether such a strake, in this and earlier hulls, should be called a keel-plank, a thickened central strake with structural duties, without making the hull frame-first or keel-built in the later sense. The International Journal of Nautical Archaeology discussion of the question, with Ship 17 as its case, states the positions on both sides from the timbers. This article's usage throughout, keel-plank where a central member is meant, no true keel where the system is meant, follows that discussion's careful middle. What is not debated is the continuity the ship embodies: in the century of Persia's conquest, twenty-five hundred years after Abydos, an Egyptian freighter was still a thick-planked, tenoned, lashed shell, carrying over a hundred tonnes of the Delta's trade. Traditions do not come more durable, or better excavated.

XXIII. Repeat It — the Master Protocols in Brief

// PROTOCOL A — BUILD A PLANKED RIVER HULL, SHELL-FIRST //

1. Select and convert the timber: local acacia, sycamore or tamarisk for a river hull, cedar where length and state funds allow; saw to thick planks of regular dimension (Sections IV, V).

2. Shape each plank to its place in the shell, edges dressed for close fitting, the hull's form carried in the fitting itself (Sections V, VII).

3. Cut mortises in the plank edges; fit unpegged tenons of hard wood to align strake to strake; do not peg them (Section VII).

4. Cut the lashing channels; pass the rope lashings and draw the shell tight, strake by strake, packing the seams with fibre as the joints close (Sections VII, XI).

5. Fit deck beams across the finished shell; add deck, internal timbers, cabin and platforms as the vessel's work requires (Sections VII, XIV).

6. Fit steering oars, mast and rig; add the hogging truss where length or sea work demands longitudinal strength (Sections XIII, XIV).

7. Launch; let the hull swell tight; re-tension lashings as it settles; bail, prove under load low and amidships, and commission (Sections XI, XVI).

// PROTOCOL B — WORK A CARGO PASSAGE ON THE NILE //

1. Load at the bank or basin, weight low, spread and amidships, blocks on their cradles, grain measured under the scribe's eye (Sections XVI, XVII).

2. Northbound: ride the current, rowing to steer and to make the landing (Sections III, XII).

3. Southbound: sail on the north wind; if it fails, track from the bank or row, and count the cost accordingly (Sections XIII, XV).

4. Moor nightly at named stages, as Merer's log moors; account for cargo, rations and crew daily (Sections XVI, XX).

5. Time the heaviest stone for the flood, and the basin and canal legs for the water that fills them (Sections III, XIX).

And the whole article in one sentence: Egypt, short of timber and long on river, invented a way to sew short, thick planks into great shells without keel or nail, proved it at Abydos by 3000 BCE and at Giza in a 43-metre cedar ship, carried its ships in pieces across deserts to launch them on a second sea, and ran on those hulls, for three thousand years, the stone, grain, timber and incense traffic of the most durable river state of the ancient world, until a freighter in a Delta harbour could still be recognised, plank for plank, in a Greek historian's description. For what those same hulls meant when the passenger was a god, see the Solar Barque. For how they were built and worked, this has been the record.
