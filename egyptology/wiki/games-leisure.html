I. Play as Evidence — Why Games Belong in a History of Daily Life

A history of Egypt written only from temples, tombs and tax accounts would miss something the Egyptians themselves clearly valued: the hours that were not work, not ritual and not sleep. Games fill part of that gap, and they fill it with objects. A senet board is not a text about leisure. It is leisure, surviving in wood, faience and ivory, handled until its squares wore smooth. When the Metropolitan Museum of Art surveyed the board games of Egypt and the Near East, it made the social point directly, in paraphrase: games are found in every sort of context, from a graffito scratched on a brick to an ornate box of exotic material, and they were popular across all social classes. That spread, from brick to ebony, is the first fact of this article.

Play is unusually good evidence for daily life for three reasons. First, it is voluntary. A ration list tells us what the state issued; a game board tells us what somebody chose to do when no one was issuing anything. Second, it is social. Almost every Egyptian board game in this article is a two-player race game, which means almost every board implies a partner, an opponent, a watcher, a wager of time if of nothing else. Third, it is conservative. The Met's survey notes that senet stayed in favour from the Predynastic Period to at least the Late Period, a run of well over two thousand years, with its board's basic form, three rows of ten squares, remaining recognisably the same. Customs that stable are not decoration on a culture. They are part of its furniture, mental as well as wooden.

Sport does the same work for the body that games do for the table. The wrestling walls of Beni Hasan, the stick-fighters performing before a king, the royal inscriptions boasting of arrows driven through copper targets: none of these is a military manual, and none is a work record. They show Egyptians training, competing, showing off and watching other people show off. Wolfgang Decker, whose Sports and Games of Ancient Egypt (German edition 1987; English translation by Allen Guttmann, Yale University Press, 1992) remains the standard survey, treated kings and commoners in one frame for exactly this reason: the same civilisation that staged wrestling before Pharaoh also scratched game boards into temple roofs where workmen and priests could reach them in an idle hour.

This article therefore reads play in both directions at once. Read forward, a board is a thing to be played: squares, pieces, a randomiser, a finish. Read backward, it is a document: of who had leisure, of who played whom, of what a culture thought a race, a trap, a safe house and a second chance looked like. Senet's second, funerary life, in which the board became a map of the soul's passage through the afterlife, is only possible because its first life was so ordinary. Nobody builds a theology on an object nobody recognises. The deceased in the Book of the Dead plays senet in the next world because the living played it in this one, constantly, for millennia.

II. The Names — Senet, Mehen and the Words for Play

The game the world calls senet was called, in Egyptian, znt: a word whose root sense is "passing." The British Museum, the Metropolitan Museum and Peter Piccione's scholarship all give the name in that sense, and the Met adds the interpretive step the Egyptians themselves later took: in the New Kingdom the game of "passing" became associated with the journey to the afterlife, the soul passing through the netherworld as the pieces pass along the board. Two cautions belong with the name. The first is philological: the older transliteration writes the first consonant as z, where later convention writes s, so the game appears in the literature as both znt and snt. The second is archival: this archive's V2 sign map merges the two sibilants in a single value, so no bracketed Gardiner spelling in this article is offered for the game's name. The name is given in transliteration only, by the rule this archive applies everywhere: where a spelling cannot be composed confidently from the map, the bracket is omitted rather than guessed.

Mehen's name is in better shape. The game is mḥn [Gardiner: V22-N35] [𓎔𓈖], "the coiled one," and the Met's survey gives the name in exactly that sense: the board depicts a coiled snake, and the name belongs first to the protective serpent deity who wrapped around the sun god Re during his journey through the night. The game, the god and the coil share one word because they share one shape. The archive treats the serpent Mehen as a divine figure in its own right in the account of the solar barque's nightly crew; here the emphasis is on the board that borrowed his body for a racetrack.

The third game in this article has no ancient name at all, and that absence is itself a datum. "Hounds and Jackals" is a modern nickname, given because one famous set's pins are topped with hounds' heads and jackals' heads. The Met is explicit, in paraphrase: despite their popularity, the names and rules of some ancient games remain unknown, and the game of Fifty-eight Holes bears many modern names, Hounds and Jackals among them. Every use of the name in Section XV should therefore be read in invisible quotation marks. Egyptians played the game for centuries, across half the Near East, and did not leave us, or did not leave us surviving, the word they played it by.

A handful of further terms recur through the article and are glossed here once. A safe or fortunate square on the senet board is marked, on many boards, with the sign for nfr [Gardiner: F35] [𓄤], "good, beautiful, perfect": the word is the marking, and Section VI follows the consequences. The ring sign that crowns the fifty-eight-holes track is the šn ring, the sign of encircling and of eternity, named here in transliteration without a bracketed spelling. The lion pieces of mehen are lions in the plain sense, rw [Gardiner: E23] [𓃭]. And the principle that orders the whole funerary reading of senet is mꜣꜥt [Gardiner: Aa11-X1] [𓐙𓏏], truth and right order, against which, in the judgement scenes, the heart was weighed. The archive's full treatment of that weighing is separate; here it matters only that a game could be imagined as a small, playable rehearsal for it.

III. Evidence and Its Limits — The Honesty Rule for This Article

Begin with the sentence every popular account of senet avoids: the rules of senet are not fully recoverable, and no complete ancient rulebook exists. The British Museum states the position in its own summary of the game, in paraphrase: two players race their pieces to the end of a thirty-square board using casting sticks or bones rather than dice, and some boards carry hieroglyphs on their squares that represent additional game rules. That is the shape of the whole problem in one sentence. The boards survive. The pieces survive. The randomisers survive. Pictures of play survive, in quantity, across three thousand years. What does not survive is a single ancient text that says, in order, how a turn works.

Contrast makes the loss precise. For the Royal Game of Ur, senet's Mesopotamian contemporary, the British Museum can point to an actual rule text: the game's rules were written on a cuneiform tablet by a Babylonian astronomer in 177 BCE, and Irving Finkel deciphered them from it. Ur has a rulebook, late and partial as it is. Senet has none, at any date. What senet has instead is a body of religious texts, above all the composition modern scholarship calls the Great Game Text, which speaks of the board's squares in the language of the soul's journey, and a set of marked boards whose signs cluster, tellingly, at the end of the track. Reconstructions are built from those two things, plus the pictures, plus analogy with other race games. They are intelligent, informed, playable, and modern. Section VII and Section VIII keep them labelled as such throughout.

// THE RULE APPLIED IN EVERY SECTION THAT FOLLOWS //

Three grades of claim appear in this article, and they are not interchangeable. Attested: a board, piece, picture or text that survives, cited to its museum or publication. Reconstructed: a rule or procedure proposed by a named modern scholar, chiefly Timothy Kendall (1978) and Peter A. Piccione (doctoral dissertation, University of Chicago, 1990), presented as that scholar's proposal, never as an ancient rule. Unknown: everything else, stated as unknown. Where a reconstruction is summarised, the summarising is this article's paraphrase of published accounts of it, not a quotation, and no ancient wording is invented anywhere in these pages. A reader who finishes this article unable to play an historically guaranteed game of senet has understood the evidence correctly.

The same discipline applies, with even more force, to mehen and to fifty-eight holes. For mehen, the Fitzwilliam Museum states the position without softening, in paraphrase: it is not known how the game was played, although certain aspects have been deduced. For fifty-eight holes, the Met likewise presents the race to the final hole as probable, not documented. And for sport, the evidence is pictorial and inscriptional rather than regulatory: Egypt left no rulebook for wrestling either, only walls covered in holds and throws and royal texts whose boasts are ideology before they are reportage. Sections XIX to XXI read those walls for what they can bear, technique, training, occasion, and refuse the extra step of inventing scoring systems, weight classes or league tables for which no evidence exists.

IV. Senet I — The Board of Thirty Squares

The senet board is the most recognisable gaming object to survive from the ancient world: thirty squares in three parallel rows of ten. The British Museum gives the layout in those terms, and the Met adds the two details that turn a grid into a track. The players moved their pieces in a boustrophedon, an S-shaped, direction, turning at each row's end like an ox ploughing a field, and the last five squares of the track are consistently decorated. Both details matter. The S-path means the thirty squares are one road, not three: a piece travels along the first row, back along the second, and forward again along the third, in the numbering modern scholarship, following Piccione, assigns from 1 to 30. The decorated ending means the road changes character as it nears its finish, which is where every reconstruction concentrates its special rules.

The board's antiquity is not in doubt, even where its earliest history is debated. The British Museum dates senet to around 3100 BCE, among the earliest known board games. The Met places it in favour from the Predynastic Period onward, and Walter Crist, Anne-Elizabeth Dunn-Vaturi and Alex de Voogt, in Ancient Egyptians at Play: Board Games across Borders (Bloomsbury, 2016), trace fragmentary boards to the First Dynasty and follow the game, in their chapter structure, through Old Kingdom ritual use and graffiti, Middle Kingdom change and consistency, and New Kingdom religious meaning. A famous early witness is pictorial rather than physical: the Third Dynasty tomb of Hesy-Re at Saqqara, dated by the Met to about 2700 BCE for its mehen equipment and by the Fitzwilliam to about 2650 BCE for its painting, shows the equipment of three board games, senet among them, laid out with a draughtsman's precision.

Form followed setting. At one end of the range stands the full gaming box: a hollow wooden case, its top gridded for senet, its underside very often gridded for the game of twenty squares, and a drawer in its body holding the pieces and casting sticks, the whole object a portable games cupboard. The Met illustrates the type with an ivory and copper-alloy gaming box of about 1580 to 1458 BCE and with faience gameboards of the reign of Thutmose III. At the other end stand the graffiti boards: grids scratched on bricks, pavements and temple roofs, which the Met counts among the game's contexts and which Crist and his co-authors have recorded at previously excavated sites. Between box and graffito lies every intermediate: boards painted, boards woven or drawn on perishable material and now lost, boards a workman could make in an evening and a king could commission in ebony and ivory.

One structural fact deserves emphasis because reconstructions quietly depend on it: not all boards mark the same squares. The British Museum notes that some boards have completely blank squares, while others include squares decorated with hieroglyphs. The Met's "last five consistently decorated" describes a strong tendency, not a law without exception, and the marked squares beyond the final five, notably square 15 on some boards, vary by period and by set. Any rule that rests on a marking therefore rests on the boards that carry that marking. A reconstruction that universalises one board's decoration into every Egyptian's rules has already gone one step further than the objects, and Sections VII and VIII flag the places where that step is taken.

V. Senet II — Pieces, Casting Sticks and Knucklebones

Two players faced each other across the board, each moving a set of pieces distinct in form from the other's. The Met describes the vocabulary of shapes: game pieces for senet and twenty squares were given geometrically simple forms such as cones or spools, as well as more elaborate articulations, with some pieces shaped as archers, bound prisoners of war, or animals. The cone and the spool, one player's cones against the other's spools, are the classic pairing, surviving in faience, wood, ivory and stone. How many pieces each player fielded is a question the sets answer inconsistently: published accounts of the reconstructions and of the surviving sets give five pieces a side in many sets and seven in others, with the number varying across the game's long history. This article states the range, five to seven, because the evidence states the range. A single fixed number, presented as the ancient rule, would be a reconstruction wearing an attested costume.

Movement was governed not by dice but by lots. The Met's survey is the clearest short account, in paraphrase: throw sticks functioned as the principal randomising agent in Egypt from the Predynastic Period onward; knuckle bones, typically the astragalus bone of a sheep or goat, have four unique faces and could be thrown to give different results; and a great variety of dice existed in the wider region, with the origin of the cubic die probably to be sought in the Indus Valley. Egyptian senet equipment, in the sets that survive complete enough to judge, centres on the casting sticks: flat or half-round sticks, dark on one face and light on the other, thrown together and read by how many fall light-side up. Four sticks give throws in the range modern reconstructions score as one to five, the throw of none-light counting as five, but the scoring is part of the reconstructions, not a surviving Egyptian table, and Section IX presents it under that label.

The British Museum's senet entry makes the same point from the player's side, in paraphrase: two players compete to race all their pieces to the end of the board, using casting sticks or bones, rather than dice, to determine the number of squares moved with each throw. "Race all their pieces to the end" is as much of the object of the game as any ancient or museum source states, and it is worth noticing how much it does not say. It does not say how a piece enters the board, whether pieces begin on the board or off it, what happens when a piece lands on an opponent, whether adjacent pieces protect each other, or how a piece leaves the final square. Every one of those questions is answered by the modern reconstructions, differently, and none is answered by a surviving rule text. The equipment is eloquent. The procedure is silent.

CONES AND SPOOLS

The standard opposed sets: conical pieces against reel- or spool-shaped pieces, distinguished at a glance and by touch. The Met's collection holds individual faience examples of both forms from the fifteenth century BCE. Elaborated sets replace the plain forms with figures, including archers, bound prisoners and animals, without changing the underlying need: two visibly different armies on one track.

CASTING STICKS

Two-sided sticks, thrown as a set, are Egypt's characteristic randomiser from the Predynastic Period onward, on the Met's account. Tutankhamun's tomb, Section XIII, contained casting sticks with the boards. A stick has no numbers. The number is produced by the fall, by counting faces, which is why the scoring of a throw belongs to reconstruction, however natural any particular scoring feels.

KNUCKLEBONES

Astragali, the ankle bones of sheep or goats, have four unlike faces and land in four unlike positions. The Met illustrates knucklebone gaming pieces of about 1550 to 1458 BCE and treats the bones as a genuine alternative randomiser. Their presence beside a board proves a throw was made. It does not prove how the throw was converted into movement.

THE DRAWER

Gaming boxes commonly store their own equipment: pieces and sticks in a drawer within the box body. A complete box is therefore a self-contained social occasion, board, armies and lots in one carried object. It is also an archaeological trap: a drawer found empty proves nothing about what its game required.

VI. Senet III — The Marked Squares and What Their Signs Say

The strongest evidence for how senet worked is written on the board itself, in the signs carved, painted or inlaid on particular squares. The signs are not evenly spread. They cluster at the end of the track, squares 26 to 30 in the modern numbering, with a further marking frequently reported at square 15. Published summaries of Piccione's work, and the museum accounts that follow the scholarship, agree on the core inventory, and this section gives it at exactly that strength: as the usual markings of the late squares on the boards that mark them, with the conventional modern names that scholarship gives those squares, each name a rendering of a sign, not a quotation from a rulebook.

Read strictly, the table proves three things. First, the end of the track was special: Egyptians spent carving and inlay on the last squares and left the first twenty-odd plain, which no one does for squares that behave like all the others. Second, fortune and danger stand adjacent: the good square, 26, immediately precedes the water, 27, so that a piece that has nearly finished its passing stands one throw from disaster, a shape any race-game designer, and any theologian of the judgement, would recognise. Third, the counts on 28 and 29, three and two, look like instructions of some kind. What kind is the question on which the reconstructions divide, and the division is instructive. A count can be a throw required to leave the board, a number of truths or gods to be faced, or a label with no mechanical force at all. The board does not say which. The scholars choose.

Behind the marked boards stands the one substantial body of ancient writing about senet's meaning: the religious texts, including the Great Game Text known from later copies, one of them a Turin papyrus that pairs the text with drawings of the board, which speak of the squares in the first person of the deceased passing through them. Peter Piccione's 1990 Chicago dissertation, The Historical Development of the Game of Senet and Its Significance for Egyptian Religion, listed by the Met among its further reading for the game, is the modern study that built most fully on that material. Its evidence is theological before it is ludic: it tells us what the squares meant to a New Kingdom and later religious imagination. Converting meaning into mechanics, this square sends you back, that square protects you, is the reconstructors' work, and it is to that work, honestly labelled, that the next two sections turn.

VII. Senet IV — The Piccione Reconstruction

Peter A. Piccione's reconstruction, developed in his 1990 University of Chicago dissertation and in the work that followed it, approaches senet from the religious texts outward. Its starting point is the game's New Kingdom transformation: by that period, on the Met's summary as on Piccione's argument, senet had become a playable image of the soul's journey, its squares corresponding to stations, deities and hazards of the passing through the netherworld toward judgement and rebirth. The reconstruction therefore treats the board's markings and the texts' theology as the primary evidence, and builds a playable game in which the late squares carry the weight the texts give them.

In paraphrase of the published summaries of Piccione's reading: the deceased-player's passage is the game's real subject; the good square at 26, marked nfr [Gardiner: F35] [𓄤], is a fortunate and protected station; the water at 27 is the passage's mortal hazard, from which a piece is returned toward the square of rebirth at 15; and the counted squares at the track's end govern the final exit from the board, the soul's release, by exact throws corresponding to their numbers. Piccione also gave the field its standard square numbering, the 1-to-30 S-path numbering that museum labels, commercial sets and later reconstructions, including Kendall-derived ones, now share. That numbering is so familiar that it is easy to forget it, too, is a scholarly convention laid over boards that carry no numbers at all.

The strength of Piccione's approach is its fidelity to what Egyptians wrote about the game when they wrote about it theologically. Its limit is the mirror image of that strength. A text describing the soul's passage is not describing a Tuesday-evening game between two workmen, and rules inferred from theology may describe the game as priests imagined it rather than as families played it. Piccione's senet is also, in the published summaries, a game whose religious development he traced historically: the secular race of the early periods acquiring, by the New Kingdom, the funerary meaning that the Book of the Dead vignette, Section XII, displays. A reconstruction keyed to the late, religious form of the game should not be projected backward, unmarked, onto the First Dynasty boards with which the game's history begins.

VIII. Senet V — The Kendall Reconstruction, and Where the Two Differ

Timothy Kendall's reconstruction, published in 1978 as Passing Through the Netherworld and since then the basis of most museum demonstration sets and commercial editions, approaches the same evidence from the board outward. Where Piccione begins from the texts, Kendall begins from playability: given thirty squares, two opposed sets of pieces, casting sticks, marked late squares and the pictures of play, what complete, coherent race game best fits all of them at once? The answer he built is a genuine game, tactically rich, and it is the version most modern players who have "played senet" have in fact played. It remains, in every particular that goes beyond Section VI's table, a 1978 proposal.

In paraphrase of Kendall's rules as published and as summarised in the museum and games literature: pieces begin arranged on the first row, alternating between the two players; a throw of the sticks moves one piece forward along the S-path; a piece landing on an opponent's piece exchanges places with it, the attacked piece moving backward to the square the attacker came from; two friendly pieces side by side form a block that cannot be attacked, and three in a row a barrier that cannot be passed; throws of one, four or five earn a further throw; every piece must land exactly on the good square, 26, before proceeding; a piece falling on the water, 27, returns to the square of rebirth, 15, or the nearest free square before it; and pieces leave the board from the final squares by exact throws, three from 28, two from 29, one from 30, the first player to bear off all his pieces winning the race.

Where the first two columns agree, as on the water's danger and the exact-throw ending, the agreement raises confidence that something of the kind stood behind the markings, but agreement between two reconstructions built from the same marks is not the same as an ancient statement. Where they differ, in emphasis, in the start, in the tactical machinery of blocks and exchanges, the honest conclusion is not that one scholar is right. It is that the evidence underdetermines the game, and both men filled the same silence with different, defensible plaster.

IX. Senet VI — How a Game Probably Ran, Step by Step

What follows is a composite procedure: the most defensible modern picture of a game of senet, assembled from the attested equipment and the two reconstructions above. Every step carries its confidence with it. A reader who wants only the attested skeleton should read the steps marked ATTESTED and skip the rest; the skeleton is short, and that shortness is the truth about senet.

// A GAME OF SENET, AS FAR AS IT CAN BE RECONSTRUCTED //

Step 1 — Set out the board. ATTESTED in form. A thirty-square board, three rows of ten, very often the top of a box whose reverse carries twenty squares and whose drawer holds the equipment.

Step 2 — Set out the pieces. PARTLY ATTESTED. Two opposed sets, cones against spools or their figured equivalents. Their count, five or seven a side, varies in the surviving sets. RECONSTRUCTED: their starting squares. Kendall's alternating first row is a proposal, not a photograph.

Step 3 — Throw for the move. ATTESTED in equipment, RECONSTRUCTED in scoring. Casting sticks, or knucklebones, are thrown. That sticks and bones were the randomisers is museum-secure. That four sticks score one to five by light faces, with none-light counting five, is the reconstructions' shared convention, not a surviving table.

Step 4 — Move one piece along the road. ATTESTED in outline. The Met states the boustrophedon, S-shaped, direction of travel. Which piece a player chose, and whether a player who could not move forward moved backward or lost the turn, is RECONSTRUCTED, and the reconstructions are not unanimous.

Step 5 — Meet the opponent. RECONSTRUCTED. Kendall's exchange, the attacked piece sent back to the attacker's starting square, and his protected pairs and impassable triples, make the game's tactics. They are consistent with a race game of this family. No ancient sentence states them.

Step 6 — Reach the good square. MARKING ATTESTED, RULE RECONSTRUCTED. Square 26 carries the sign for nfr [Gardiner: F35] [𓄤], good. That every piece must land on it exactly, and may not pass it by, is Kendall's rule, widely adopted, plausibly grounded, and unproven.

Step 7 — Risk the water. MARKING ATTESTED, CONSEQUENCE RECONSTRUCTED. Square 27 carries water. That a piece landing there returns to 15, the rebirth square, is the reconstructions' common reading of the two markings together. The pairing is elegant. Elegance is not evidence, and the article records the rule as inference, however good.

Step 8 — Leave the board. COUNTS ATTESTED, PROCEDURE RECONSTRUCTED. Squares 28 and 29 carry three and two. That a piece bears off from them, and from 30, only on the exact matching throw is the standard modern reading. That the game was won by bearing off all one's pieces first is the race-game frame the British Museum states in summary, and is the least doubtful of the reconstructions' shared claims.

Step 9 — Play again. ATTESTED as a social fact. Boards worn by use, boards buried with their owners, boards scratched where idle hands could reach them: whatever the precise rules were, the game was replayed for three thousand years. The procedure above is a modern script for an ancient habit.

Two features of that script deserve a final word. The extra throw on high or low casts, one, four and five in Kendall's version, is the kind of rule every race game needs to keep its pace, and the kind no board can prove. And the backward move, imposed in some versions when no forward move is possible, shows reconstruction at its most exposed: a gap in the evidence, a design problem, a plausible patch. Modern editions differ on both points because antiquity is silent on both points. A museum set that prints its rules without the word "reconstruction" on the box has misled its buyer, kindly and completely.

X. Senet VII — What No Source Tells Us

This section is deliberately the plainest in the article. No surviving source tells us who moved first, or how the first player was chosen. No source tells us whether pieces started on the board, entered it by a throw, or were placed by agreement. No source states the scoring of the casting sticks as a table of values, the consequence of a throw for which no legal move exists, or whether a player could choose not to use a throw. No source states the exchange rule, the block, the barrier, the protection of the good square, or the exact destination of a piece that fell in the water, in so many words. No source tells us whether the game was played for stakes, and if so in what, although a much later Egyptian tale, the Roman-period story of Setne Khamwas, imagines a board game played against a dead prince for the prize of a magical book, which proves that Egyptians could imagine gaming stakes raised to the highest possible level without proving what changed hands over an ordinary board.

No source, either, resolves the game's own history at the points where a player would most want certainty. We do not know whether the Old Kingdom game, played when the religious reading is not yet attested, used the same late-square rules as the New Kingdom game, or whether the markings accumulated meaning, and mechanics, over the centuries. We do not know whether the rules varied by city, by period or by household, as the rules of every long-lived folk game vary, although the variation in the boards' markings hints that they may have. And we do not know the game's Egyptian name for its parts: no ancient vocabulary of senet survives to give us the words for a throw, a block, a capture or a win. The names in Section VI, House of Happiness, House of Water and the rest, are modern scholarly renderings of signs, useful and conventional, and a player in Thebes would not have recognised a single one of them as a quotation.

None of this makes senet unknowable. It makes senet knowable in layers, and the layers are this article's whole method. The object layer is rich and secure: boards, pieces, sticks, bones, pictures, graffiti, four sets in a king's tomb. The meaning layer, for the New Kingdom and after, is also rich: a named game of passing, a vignette in the greatest funerary composition of the age, texts that walk the soul along the squares. Only the rule layer is thin, and it is thin in a specific, interesting way: Egyptians evidently did not need to write the rules down, because everyone who might read them already knew how to play. The silence of the sources about procedure is, read correctly, the loudest evidence in this article for how ordinary the game was. Rulebooks are written for strangers. Senet had none, because it had no strangers.

XI. Senet in Life — Husbands, Wives, Workmen and a Lion Playing a Gazelle

The pictures of senet in play are a small sociology of Egypt. Tomb scenes show the tomb owner at the board, very often facing his wife: the game as a married couple's pastime, intimate and unhurried, the two sets of pieces between two people who, the tomb's whole programme insists, will go on facing each other in the next life as in this. Other scenes and other boards place the game lower down the social scale and further out of doors. The Met's framing, games from graffito to ornate box, popular across all social classes, is borne out by the findspots Crist, Dunn-Vaturi and de Voogt catalogue: senet in Nubia and the Levant as well as Egypt, senet following soldiers, officials and traders, senet scratched on stone at sites where no ebony box ever stood. At Deir el-Medina, the village of the royal tomb-builders whose records anchor this archive's account of working life, the ostraca and the material culture of leisure belong to the same world of literate workmen with rest days, festivals and evenings: a community with the time, the skill and the habit of play.

The most charming witness is also a serious one. A satirical papyrus of about 1250 to 1150 BCE, now in the British Museum, shows a lion playing senet with a gazelle, the predator at the board with his natural prey, and the Museum notes, dryly, that the lion is having a bit of trouble holding the pieces. The joke only works if the game is universal: artist, audience and butt of the joke all know the board, the pieces and the posture of play well enough for a gazelle across the table to be funny. Satire is evidence of familiarity at a depth no inventory can match. A culture does not parody a game its audience has to have explained.

Games also crossed the boundaries that other objects did not. Crist and his co-authors built their book on the point in its title, board games across borders: play as a social lubricant between people who shared neither language nor rank, because a race game needs no translation, only a board, two sets of pieces and a throw. Merchants, soldiers and envoys carried senet and fifty-eight holes along the same routes as grain and gold. That portability is part of the explanation for the games' distribution, and it is a reminder that leisure, in the ancient world as now, was one of the first things strangers could do together.

XII. Senet in Death — The Book of the Dead, Spell 17, and the Empty Chair

Senet's second life begins when the opponent disappears. In the New Kingdom, on the Met's account, the game became associated with the journey to the afterlife, and scenes of senet-playing were included among the vignettes of Spell 17 of the Book of the Dead and magnified on the walls of tombs. The archive treats Spell 17 and its vignette programme in full in its study of the Book of the Dead passages, and the present article does not duplicate that treatment. What belongs here is the image itself: the deceased, seated at the board, pieces before him, and across from him, no one. The chair is empty. The opponent is invisible, fate, the gods, the netherworld itself, in the usual modern reading, which this article gives as a reading, not as an ancient caption.

The most famous version is a queen's. In the tomb of Nefertari, QV66 in the Valley of the Queens, the queen is shown at the senet board on the same wall that carries the text of the spell, and the Metropolitan Museum holds Nina de Garis Davies's painted copy of the scene, made in 1921 and 1922 from the original of about 1290 to 1224 BCE. Nefertari plays alone, in the copy as in the tomb, dressed for eternity, at a game she had plainly played in life. The theology is exact: to pass along the board is to pass through the stations of the netherworld; to win is to come forth justified, in the orbit of the principle of right order, mꜣꜥt [Gardiner: Aa11-X1] [𓐙𓏏], and to join the sun in its daily rebirth, the god rꜥ [Gardiner: N5] [𓇳] whose circuit the whole funerary literature, surveyed in this archive's funerary texts, exists to secure.

It matters, for honesty, to keep the direction of development straight. Senet was not invented as a ritual. The early boards are centuries older than the religious reading, and Crist, Dunn-Vaturi and de Voogt's chronology, Old Kingdom use, Middle Kingdom consistency, New Kingdom religious meaning, shows a secular game acquiring sacred weight, not a rite decaying into a pastime. The acquisition is itself the historical event: a civilisation looked at its most familiar race, thirty squares, a good house, a water trap, a counted exit, and recognised its own account of death in it. Later boards, in the Ramesside age and after, lean into the recognition, their squares carrying fuller religious decoration. The game did not change its shape. It changed its stakes, from an evening to eternity, without moving a single square.

XIII. Tutankhamun's Sets — Four Boards in One Tomb

No single find states senet's standing more plainly than the tomb of Tutankhamun. The British Museum calls the king, with Nefertari, one of the game's two emblematic royal players: beloved by Tutankhamun and Queen Nefertari alike, senet is one of the earliest known board games. The tomb contained four gaming sets, of varying size and splendour, among them a great ebony and ivory board on a stand, its thirty squares inlaid, its drawer holding pieces, casting sticks and knucklebones. A king who could take anything into eternity took the game four times over, in versions from the travelling set to the state piece of furniture. Decker's survey of the sporting contents of the tomb places the boards where they belong, among the bows, chariots and hunting equipment of a king presented, in life and in burial, as a sportsman.

The sets also teach the article's recurring lesson in material form. Tutankhamun's boards are double-sided in the manner of their age: senet on one face, the game of twenty squares on the other. Twenty squares, the Met explains, was imported from the Near East, the game of the Royal Graves of Ur, and was played in Egypt between Dynasties 17 and 20, found on the uppermost side of gaming boxes with senet on the reverse. The king's box is therefore a small history of international play in one object: Egypt's own three-thousand-year race on one side, Mesopotamia's traveller on the other, one drawer of pieces serving both. And on the marked squares, the Tutankhamun boards are among those published accounts cite for a marking at square 15, the rebirth square of the reconstructions, a detail this article records at that strength, as a reported marking, not as a rule.

A caution closes the section, because Tutankhamun distorts every subject he touches. His sets are the finest surviving, the best published and the least typical. Most Egyptians played on wood, on faience, on a grid scratched in stone or drawn in dust, with pieces of pottery and sticks cut from a reed. The king's ebony tells us what senet meant at the summit. The graffiti tell us what it meant everywhere else, and an honest history of the game needs the roof of a temple at Dendera, where late boards were scratched in the Roman period, as much as it needs the Valley of the Kings.

XIV. Mehen — The Coiled Serpent and Its Lions

Mehen is older than senet, stranger than senet, and far less understood. The Fitzwilliam Museum, which holds the largest of the eight boards known in international collections, gives the frame, in paraphrase: the game of the snake was played from at least Dynasties 3 to 6; the earliest evidence comes from the Predynastic Period, Naqada II, about 3600 to 3200 BCE; and it is the leading contender for the title of oldest board game in the world. The British Museum dates its playing life from about 3000 to about 2300 BCE. The Met narrows the Egyptian floruit to the Predynastic Period and the Old Kingdom, and notes what ended it: mehen disappears from Egypt as senet rises, a succession Crist and his co-authors also propose, the coiled serpent yielding the table to the thirty squares.

The board is a snake. A spiral track, the serpent's segmented body, winds from the tail at the board's edge inward to the head at the centre. The Fitzwilliam board, 44 centimetres across, in limestone, shows the type at its most elaborate: the body divided by grooves into what are most plausibly read as playing spaces, the hood and tail hatched, the head's eyes deeply recessed, once perhaps inlaid, a forked tongue inlaid with red jasper, and a protruding appendage at the edge of a kind familiar on mehen boards in varying forms, a goose's head, a trapezium, a small turtle's head on other examples. The number of spaces is not fixed between boards. Unlike senet's invariant thirty, mehen's coil carries different counts on different boards, which is itself an argument that no single canonical form of the game stood behind them all.

The equipment is the game's most distinctive survival. The key document is the same Hesy-Re painting that witnesses early senet: the Met describes the picture in the tomb of Hesy-Re at Saqqara, about 2700 BCE, showing gaming pieces associated with a mehen board, three lions, three lionesses, and six sets of six marbles. The Fitzwilliam confirms the association from the finds, in paraphrase: no board has been found with its playing pieces in place, but lion and lioness figurines found with small limestone balls are generally accepted as the game's typical equipment; the Museum holds two such lions, and Manchester holds marbles found by Petrie with five lions in a First Dynasty tomb. The lion, rw [Gardiner: E23] [𓃭], and the lioness on the track, the marbles on the coil: that is a mehen set, as far as the record gives one.

It is not known how mehen was played. The Fitzwilliam says so, and this article repeats it without decoration. The deductions the Museum reports, labelled here as its suggestions, are: that the game was almost certainly a race, pieces moving from the tail toward the head; that reaching the centre stood, in the funerary reading, for the dead king's rebirth into the afterlife, the serpent Mehen encircling and protecting the sun god on his nightly journey, so that the coil is a pathway to the god; and that the lion and lioness pieces were perhaps enemies that attacked en route and were to be repelled. Each clause beyond "race toward the centre" is interpretation. The Museum also records the game's neatest mystery: no dice or dice sticks are shown beside the mehen board in Hesy-Re's painting, so the randomiser, if there was one, is unknown; one theory it reports proposes that movement was governed by guessing the number of marbles hidden in the other player's hand. The British Museum adds a modern analogy, the North African spiral race game of hyena, in which a piece races to the centre and back and then releases a predator piece that devours the others. The analogy is suggestive, the lion pieces make it tempting, and it remains an analogy. Mehen's rules are lost, more completely than senet's, because mehen left no religious text tradition behind it to reconstruct from.

What mehen left instead is a theology in object form. The Met notes that in the Pyramid Texts, the Old Kingdom religious corpus, inscriptions suggest a belief that attainment of the afterlife was achievable by successfully passing through the mehen board, and that by reaching the centre of the spiral one would symbolically join Re on his barque; miniature games among a burial's goods may have been working models of that passage, not toys for eternity. The pattern is senet's pattern, a millennium earlier and in a spiral: a race game whose track a culture recognised as the shape of its own journey through death. Egypt invented that recognition twice. The second time, with senet, it wrote the theology down.

XV. Hounds and Jackals — The Game of Fifty-Eight Holes

The third great Egyptian race game is played not on squares but in holes. Fifty-eight holes are drilled in a board, twenty-nine for each of two players, in two looping tracks that converge on a final hole. The Met describes the mechanism, in paraphrase: the holes, made to fit pinlike playing pieces, formed a track on which two players probably raced their pieces until they reached the thirtieth and final hole, shaped like a šn sign, "eternity." The pins are the game's glory. In the famous complete set in the Metropolitan Museum, ebony and ivory, dated to about 1814 to 1805 BCE and found at Thebes, five pins carry hounds' heads and five carry jackals' heads, and it is from those two packs that Howard Carter's generation named the game Hounds and Jackals. The board rests on legs, carries a drawer for its pins, and is incised at its centre with a palm tree beneath the closing ring sign.

Some boards mark connections between holes, lines joining a hole to another further along or further back, and modern summaries read them, plausibly and unprovably, as advances and setbacks, the ancient equivalent of the chute and the ladder. The honesty rule applies with its usual force: the lines are attested, their effect is inferred. What is secure is the game's geography and date. The Met places its probable origin in Egypt at the turn of the second millennium BCE, its Egyptian attestation almost exclusively in the Middle Kingdom, and its spread, soon after, to Nubia and to central Anatolia in the Old Assyrian period, with the game remaining popular across the Near East, in minor variations, until the mid-first millennium BCE. Boards from the Old Assyrian trading colonies, merchants' towns where, as recent scholarship on the Anatolian evidence stresses, a two-player board was social equipment for strangers doing business together, show the game doing abroad exactly the work Section XI described for senet at home.

Fifty-eight holes also corrects a lazy assumption about Egyptian play: that it was all theology. No funerary text tradition attaches to this game, no spell walks a soul along its holes, and its modern name is a nickname for its pins. It appears to have been, simply and durably, a good race: portable, legible across languages, quick to set up and hard to put down, exported with the caravans and copied in local materials wherever it landed. Egypt's games divide, on the evidence, into those the priests eventually claimed and those they did not, and the second group is the control sample that proves the claiming, where it happened, was a choice.

XVI. The Wider Shelf — Twenty Squares, Men, and Graffiti Boards

Senet, mehen and fifty-eight holes are the documented centre of a wider shelf. The game of twenty squares, treated in Sections V and XIII as senet's box-mate, deserves its own paragraph. Imported into Egypt from the Near East, on the Met's account, and played there between Dynasties 17 and 20, it runs on a track of twenty squares, a short straight, a bridge and a return, with the fourth, eighth and last squares of its central aisle marked by a cross or another symbol on many boards. Its Mesopotamian form, the Royal Game of Ur, is the one ancient race game with a surviving, deciphered rule text, the Babylonian tablet of 177 BCE from which Irving Finkel recovered its play, and the contrast has organised this whole article: Ur is what a game with a rulebook looks like. Senet is what a game without one looks like. Both were played, side by side, on opposite faces of the same Egyptian boxes, by players who needed neither book.

Below the great games stretches a penumbra the record barely holds. The Hesy-Re painting shows a third board game beside senet and mehen, a rectangular board divided into a small grid of spaces, identified in the scholarship with the game called men, about which almost nothing beyond its board and its name can responsibly be said. Graffiti boards, recorded by Crist and his co-authors at long-excavated sites including pavements at Palmyra and Meroë far beyond Egypt, and by the Met among the game's contexts from brick to temple roof, attest games whose boards were never objects at all, only marks, scratched in an idle hour and played with pebbles. Boards woven in textile or drawn on the ground are, as the Met notes, simply lost. The surviving shelf is therefore a biased sample, biased toward stone, wood and tombs, and every generalisation in this article should be read with that bias in mind: we know the games of people whose boards could survive.

XVII. Making the Boards — Materials, Boxes, Drawers and Wear

A senet board is a piece of joinery before it is a game. The prestige form, the gaming box, required a hollow carcase, a gridded top inlaid or veneered square by square, a second grid beneath, a drawer fitted to slide in the body, and turned or carved legs or a sledge stand on the grandest examples, Tutankhamun's ebony board among them. Materials map the market exactly. Ebony and ivory, ivory and copper alloy in the Met's box of about 1580 to 1458 BCE, speak for court workshops. Faience, the glazed ceramic the Met's Thutmose III-era gameboard uses for its playing surface over a wooden core, speaks for temple-town production at a middle price. Plain wood, pottery and stone speak for the household. The craft chain behind the finest pieces, the sawing, veneering, inlaying and polishing of hardwoods and ivory, belongs to the same workshop world this archive treats in its accounts of Egyptian material production, and the board in the tomb of a workman and the board in the tomb of a king differ in material and finish, not in design.

Wear is a source in its own right. Boards survive with squares polished by handling, with replacement pieces that do not match their sets, with drawers empty and drawers full. A replaced piece is a biography in miniature: a set kept in play long enough to lose a spool, valued enough to have another made. The Fitzwilliam mehen board carries the same lesson at museum scale, in the Museum's own examination, paraphrased here: the board's two parts were made independently, differ in colouration and banding, and join imperfectly, and part of the surface may be a later attempt to complete the object for display. Even the canonical objects arrive repaired, composite and argued over. Manufacture, use, loss, repair, burial, recovery, restoration: a game board has a longer career than most of the people who played on it, and the archaeologist reads the whole career, not just the first game.

XVIII. Children at Play — Dolls, Balls, Tops and Toy Animals

Children's play is the hardest leisure to recover and the easiest to recognise. The objects are small, often perishable, and ambiguous: a little figure may be a toy, a votive or a piece of household magic, and honest scholarship keeps the ambiguity open. What can be said, on the museum evidence, is that Egyptian children had dolls, balls, tops, rattles and toy animals, and that the forms are startlingly continuous with childhood everywhere. The paddle doll, a flat wooden figure with a painted body and a head of hair made from strings of mud beads, is the type specimen: examples of about 2030 to 1802 BCE are held by the Metropolitan Museum, and whether every paddle doll was a child's toy, a fertility figure or both at once is a debate this article reports rather than settles. Jointed dolls, with arms that move on pegs, are attested in Egyptian wood and pottery of the third to second millennium in the older literature on dolls in antiquity.

Balls were made without rubber, from linen, leather, papyrus and stitched plant fibre, stuffed or hollow, and ball play belongs to the pictorial record as well as the object record: the Beni Hasan tombs show girls playing catch, in some scenes mounted on each other's backs, tossing balls in pairs, scenes the older sport literature already catalogued among Egypt's games. Tops, marbles of stone and faience, miniature animals, boats and furniture, and mechanical toys with moving jaws, a snapping crocodile is the famous type, fill out a nursery that needs no modernising to be understood. Boys' wrestling appears in the tomb of Ptahhotep as early as the Fifth Dynasty, children at the sport their fathers practised as soldiers, and the line between play, practice and training, Section XIX, is one Egyptian childhood crossed without noticing it.

The interpretive point is restraint. A toy in a child's grave is evidence of love and of loss before it is evidence of anything else, and the archive resists converting every small object into a lesson. Some dolls taught motherhood, some figures guarded a house, some balls were simply balls. Childhood in Egypt, as the objects give it to us, included unstructured, unrecorded, unproductive play, and that unproductiveness is precisely what makes it leisure's purest evidence: nobody carves a top to impress a god.

XIX. Wrestling — The Beni Hasan Walls Read as Move-Catalogues

Wrestling is the most depicted sport in Egyptian art, and its archive is one set of walls. In the Middle Kingdom tombs at Beni Hasan in Middle Egypt, above all the tomb of Baqet III and the neighbouring tombs of Khety and Amenemhat, dated to about 2000 BCE, the painters covered entire walls with wrestling pairs: hundreds of paired figures, one dark, one light in the painters' convention so that limbs can be told apart, working through hold after hold in long registers. Counts vary by tomb and by publication, from over two hundred groups on a single wall in the older literature to over four hundred scenes in modern summaries of Baqet III, and this article gives the range rather than a false precision. What no count changes is the character of the walls. They are not a brawl and not a single match. They are a catalogue.

Read as a catalogue, the Beni Hasan registers show a technical system. Wrestlers wear a belt or loincloth and little else. Grips are taken on the belt, the arms, the neck, the legs. Throws are shown at successive instants, a hold applied, a balance broken, a man lifted, turned, brought down, and the struggle continues on the ground, with no striking of the kind the Greek pankration allowed. The pairing of figures in sequence, the same hold begun in one pair and completed in the next, is what licenses the modern description of the walls as an instructional manual in paint, a move-catalogue from which a repertoire could be taught. That description is a modern reading of a pictorial arrangement, and this article labels it as such. But it is a reading the arrangement invites: no decorator fills a wall with four hundred variations on a theme he does not understand as a system.

The sport's setting was military before it was festive. The earliest wrestling scenes, in the Fifth Dynasty tomb of Ptahhotep at Saqqara, about 2400 BCE, already show the sport as training, boys and men at holds under the vizier's eye, and the Beni Hasan wrestlers, belted adults in a provincial governor's tomb crowded with soldiers' exercises, bow practice and sham fights, are most plausibly read as professional soldiers at their training, the sporting face of the military world this archive treats under warfare. In the New Kingdom the same sport moves before the king: wrestling becomes spectacle and ceremony, part of the theatre of royal power, paired Egyptians and foreigners at Medinet Habu in Section XX. Training, catalogue, spectacle: one sport, three social settings, and the walls let us watch it move between them across fifteen hundred years. Its rules, scoring and winning condition are nowhere written, and the guess that a man won by pinning his opponent's shoulders to the ground is exactly that, a guess from the pictures, given here as one.

XX. Stick-Fighting, Archery and the Sporting King

Stick-fighting was wrestling's armed companion: two men, each with a short stick of about three feet, a guard over the knuckles and a padded or bound shield on the free forearm, fencing at close quarters. Scenes in the tomb of Kheruef and on the walls of Ramesses III's temple at Medinet Habu, about the twelfth century BCE, let the match's shape be reconstructed in outline, in paraphrase of the standard summaries: the fighters, in soldiers' kilts, salute or bow before the onlookers, close, exchange blows and parries with stick and forearm guard, and fight under the eye of the king, Egyptians paired against foreigners in the Medinet Habu programme and, in the reliefs' ideology, winning. The sport survives, transformed, in the Egyptian stick art still practised under related names, a continuity of technique often claimed and broadly plausible, which this article notes without overstating: a living tradition is evidence for a movement vocabulary, not for unchanged rules.

Archery was the king's own sport, and its record is inscriptional boasting of a very specific kind. The set piece is Amenhotep II, whose stela at Giza, erected early in his reign in the fifteenth century BCE, celebrates the young king's athletic feats in terms the standard histories paraphrase: horses trained until they galloped without sweating, a great boat rowed against the current after his crew tired, and, most famously, arrows shot from a moving chariot through copper targets thick as a hand, driven clean through and out the other side. Decker's reading, which this article follows, treats such texts as ideology with a difference: the claims are hyperbolic and formulaic, the king as supreme athlete is a theological statement, and yet the Eighteenth Dynasty kings pressed the boasting toward something like records, feats measured against predecessors and offered to successors to beat. Sport at the summit of Egypt was a proof of fitness to rule, continuous with the ritual run of the sed-festival, in which the ageing king ran a course to demonstrate renewed vigour, a rite this archive treats in its own article and does not duplicate here.

Below the king, archery was also work and war: target practice belongs to the same tomb programmes as the wrestling, and the hunt with bow from a chariot, Section XXI, turned the military skill into the elite's defining pastime. Boxing, by contrast, must be entered in this article under a warning. Fist-fighting figures occur rarely and ambiguously in the record, and the confident popular lists that give Egypt a full boxing programme run ahead of the evidence. Decker's survey is correspondingly cautious. This article follows him: stick-fighting and wrestling are sports at full strength; boxing is a possibility at low strength, and is presented at no more.

XXI. Water and Hunt — Swimming, Rowing, Fishing and Hunting

Egypt was a river civilisation, and its leisure took to the water. Swimming was a taught skill in a country where a canal or a flood basin stood at the end of every field, and swimmers, divers and boatmen fill the tomb scenes as workers and as sportsmen both. Rowing and sailing were transport, labour and contest at once: regattas and boat games, including the mock fights of boatmen with long poles that Herodotus-era and tomb evidence alike remember as festival entertainment, turned the river's daily skills into spectacle. The king himself rows in Amenhotep II's boasting, and a regatta staged under Tutankhamun, its inscription presenting the king as inspecting the training he had ordered for his navy, shows the water sports folded, like the wrestling, into the theatre of command. The archive's account of Egyptian food production treats fishing as subsistence in full; here the point is the overlap. The same cast net that fed a family could, on a festival day, be a sport, and the scenes rarely tell us which day they are showing.

Hunting divides along the same seam, and the seam is class. At one end stands subsistence: fowling in the marshes with throwstick and clap-net, spearing fish in the shallows, the desert hare and gazelle taken for the pot, all of it work that the tomb scenes aestheticise because the tomb owner wished to be remembered doing it gracefully. The archive's food and drink article carries the productive side of that world. At the other end stands the elite hunt as pure sport: the king and the great nobles in chariots, with hounds and attendants, running down gazelle, ibex, oryx and, in the royal rhetoric, lion and bull in the desert margins, an activity whose yield was prestige, not dinner. Amenhotep III's commemorative scarabs, which tally the wild bulls and lions the king killed across his reign, are the hunt converted into statistics, the sporting record as royal publication, and they belong in any honest account of Egyptian sport beside the wrestling walls.

Between the ends stood the marsh hunt as the gentry's beloved middle ground: a lord in a papyrus skiff, his wife and daughter aboard, his cat, in the most famous scenes, retrieving the birds his throwstick brought down, the whole party dressed for a portrait rather than a larder. The scene type, of which the Theban tombs of the Eighteenth Dynasty hold the masterpieces, is leisure painting itself as subsistence and subsistence as leisure, and it is the single best image of how thoroughly the two categories interpenetrated in Egyptian life. A modern reader who asks whether fowling was sport or work has imported a boundary the Nile did not draw.

XXII. Music, Rest and Class — Leisure Was Not Equally Distributed

Music and dance saturate Egyptian leisure, banquet scenes with harpists, singers, lutenists and dancers are among the commonest images of pleasure in the tombs, and this article deliberately does not retell that story at length. The archive treats performance where it belongs, inside its account of ritual practice, because in Egypt the line between a banquet performance, a temple procession's music and a funerary rite's song is a line the sources themselves draw lightly. The pointer is the treatment: the reader who wants the sistrum, the harp and the dance should go there, and will find games waiting at the edge of that article's festivals, as music waits at the edge of this one. What belongs here is only the observation that the banquet, Egypt's supreme leisure institution, combined everything in this article at once: food and drink, music and dance, board games on the side tables, and an audience of guests with the leisure to enjoy all of it, which is to say, with other people's labour under them.

For leisure had a distribution, and it was steep. The king's four senet sets and the workman's scratched grid are the same game at opposite ends of a material scale, and the difference between them is the difference between leisure as furniture and leisure as theft of an hour. The calendar helped, unevenly. Festivals, the archive's ritual sources show, punctured the working year with processions, feasts and rest, and the Deir el-Medina records, the archive's fullest window on working time, show a crew with regular rest days within its ten-day week and long festival absences, alongside the complaints, strikes and moonlighting that prove those rest days were used, guarded and occasionally fought for. A workman at Deir el-Medina had more documented leisure than almost any labourer in the ancient world, and he was, by Egyptian standards, privileged: a state-housed specialist in a company town. The fellah in the fields, whose working world this archive treats in its account of agriculture, had games, if he had them, played in dust with pebbles and left no board for us to find; he stands at the far end of the evidence as he stood at the far end of the distribution, mostly invisible in both.

Women's play is visible chiefly where the pictures choose to show it: wives across the senet board from their husbands, girls at ball games and acrobatics at Beni Hasan, dancers and musicians at the banquet, mothers with dolls' owners in their arms. The fuller account of women's and family life in which that play sat belongs to this archive's article on women and family, and is not duplicated here. The record is real and it is framed, almost always, by men's tombs and men's programmes, and this article declines to inflate it into a general account of women's leisure that the sources cannot carry. What can be said is that the senet board, Egypt's most durable image of leisure, very often has a woman seated at it, playing, not serving: a small, repeated, pictorial fact that outweighs many generalities.

XXIII. Reading Play Backwards — What Leisure Proves, and What It Cannot

Gather the article's findings at their true strengths. Attested, beyond serious dispute: Egyptians played race games on boards for over three thousand years; senet's board, pieces and randomisers are known in detail; mehen's coil, lions and marbles are known; fifty-eight holes travelled half the Near East; wrestling was practised, taught and catalogued in paint on a scale no other early civilisation matches; stick-fighting, archery, rowing, swimming and hunting filled the sporting calendar of king and commoner; children had dolls, balls and tops; and leisure, like everything else in Egypt, was distributed by rank. Reconstructed, honestly labelled: every playable rule in this article, Kendall's and Piccione's senet above all, the return from the water, the exact-throw exits, the reading of Beni Hasan as a manual, the lines between the fifty-eight holes as ladders and chutes. Unknown, and stated as unknown: the complete rules of every Egyptian board game, the scoring of every Egyptian sport, and the names, stakes and first moves of games played ten million times by people who saw no reason to write any of it down.

That distribution of knowledge is not a failure of the evidence. It is the evidence, for the thing this article set out to prove in Section I: that play is a document of daily life. A civilisation's games tell us what it did with its freedom, and Egypt's answer is unusually legible. It raced. It raced pieces along a road with a good house and a drowning pool, it raced marbles around a protective serpent toward rebirth, it raced pins toward a ring that meant eternity, and then, having raced for three thousand years, it looked at the road and saw its own death in it, and put the board in the tomb, and the queen at the board, and the empty chair across from her. The sports tell the same story in the body: discipline catalogued, skill displayed, the king's fitness made a public proof, the soldier's training made a wall of art. Leisure in Egypt was never an escape from the culture's central concerns. It was where those concerns went to practise.

Readers following the threads outward should begin with the Book of the Dead passages, for Spell 17 and the vignette in its full setting; with funerary texts, for the literature that turned a race into a passage; with Deir el-Medina, for the workmen whose rest days, festivals and evenings gave play its hours; and with ritual practice, for the festivals and performances at whose edges the games were played.

XXIV. Sources and Primary Evidence

The archive's rule has been applied throughout: quote only what has been seen in a fetched source, name the scholar at the point of reliance, and present everything else as labelled paraphrase. No verbatim ancient quotation appears in this article; the royal feats of Section XX and the museum descriptions throughout are paraphrases of the published summaries named below, and the reconstructions of Sections VII to IX are paraphrases of published accounts of Kendall's and Piccione's work, not editions of their texts.

Museum Sources Actually Read for This Article

Anne-Elizabeth Dunn-Vaturi, "Board Games from Ancient Egypt and the Near East," Heilbrunn Timeline of Art History, Metropolitan Museum of Art (October 2018): the framework of Sections I, IV, V, XIV and XV: throw sticks as Egypt's principal randomiser from the Predynastic Period; knucklebones and their four faces; mehen's Hesy-Re equipment of three lions, three lionesses and six sets of six marbles, its Pyramid Texts afterlife reading and its joining of Re at the spiral's centre; senet's thirty squares, last five decorated, boustrophedon play, New Kingdom afterlife association and Spell 17 vignettes; twenty squares imported and played Dynasties 17 to 20 on the reverse of senet boxes; fifty-eight holes, its probable Egyptian origin at the turn of the second millennium BCE, its shen-shaped final hole and its spread to Nubia and Anatolia; and games across all social classes, from graffito to ornate box. The essay's further reading supplies Piccione's dissertation, below.

The British Museum, "Top 10 Historical Board Games" (collection blog): senet dated to around 3100 BCE, beloved by Tutankhamun and Nefertari, thirty squares in three rows of ten, casting sticks or bones rather than dice, blank boards against hieroglyph-decorated boards, and the satirical papyrus of about 1250 to 1150 BCE showing a lion playing senet with a gazelle; mehen played from about 3000 to about 2300 BCE, its coiled segmented board, its teams racing from tail to head and back in the Museum's summary, its lion pieces, its rules and scoring stated by the Museum as unknown, and the North African hyena game offered as a modern analogy; and, by contrast, the Royal Game of Ur's rules written on a cuneiform tablet of 177 BCE and deciphered by Irving Finkel, the contrast on which Section III is built.

The Fitzwilliam Museum, Cambridge, collection highlight for its Mehen Game Board (E.GA.4464.1943), by James Masters and Melanie Pitkin: the board's 44-centimetre limestone form, grooved segmented spaces, hatched hood and tail, recessed eyes and red jasper tongue; the eight boards known in international collections; the Hesy-Ra painting, about 2650 BCE, as the game's most important evidence; lion and lioness figurines with limestone balls as the generally accepted equipment, the Museum's two lions (E.4.1927, E.5.1927) and Petrie's five lions with marbles from a First Dynasty tomb at Manchester; the plain statement that it is not known how the game was played; the race-to-the-centre and lion-as-enemy suggestions, labelled as such; the absence of dice or sticks beside the painted board and the marble-guessing theory; and the board's composite, independently made halves.

Scholarship Named and Relied On

Peter A. Piccione, "The Historical Development of the Game of Senet and Its Significance for Egyptian Religion," PhD dissertation, University of Chicago (1990): the religious-development reading of senet, the standard square numbering, and the reconstruction summarised, as paraphrase of published summaries, in Section VII. Timothy Kendall, Passing Through the Netherworld (1978), and his study of mehen: the playable reconstruction summarised in Section VIII, including the exchange, block and barrier rules, the extra throws, the obligatory stop at square 26, the return from square 27 to square 15, and the exact-throw exits. Walter Crist, Anne-Elizabeth Dunn-Vaturi and Alex de Voogt, Ancient Egyptians at Play: Board Games across Borders (Bloomsbury, 2016): the diachronic frame, mehen and men as the first signs of Egyptian board games, senet's Old Kingdom graffiti, Middle Kingdom consistency and New Kingdom religious meaning, the Hyksos-era arrival of twenty squares, graffiti boards at previously excavated sites, and games as social lubricant across borders, as reviewed and catalogued in the published literature.

Wolfgang Decker, Sports and Games of Ancient Egypt, translated by Allen Guttmann (Yale University Press, 1992; German original 1987): the framework of Sections XIX to XXI: wrestling as the most depicted sport, the Ptahhotep and Beni Hasan corpora, stick-fighting's form and its Medinet Habu and Kheruef evidence, Amenhotep II's Giza stela and the Eighteenth Dynasty's near-record boasting, the sporting contents of Tutankhamun's tomb, Ramesses III's tournament, and the caution on boxing followed in Section XX. The older sport literature's Beni Hasan counts, over two hundred wrestling groups on one wall, are reported in Section XIX as a range against modern summaries' higher counts for Baqet III, an adjudication this article makes by refusing a single number. For children, the Metropolitan Museum's paddle doll of about 2030 to 1802 BCE and the standard literature on dolls and toys in antiquity, reported at the cautious strength of Section XVIII.

Adjudications Made in This Article

(1) Senet's name is given without a bracketed Gardiner spelling, because the V2 map merges the sibilants the older transliteration znt distinguishes; the bracket is omitted under the archive's own rule rather than composed falsely. (2) Piece counts are given as a range, five to seven a side, because the surviving sets and the published reconstructions vary. (3) The names of squares 28 to 30 are given as scholarly conventions with their variants, because the boards attest counts and signs, not those names. (4) Beni Hasan wrestling counts are given as a range, over two hundred to over four hundred, because the older and modern literatures count different walls and tombs. (5) Boxing is entered at low strength, against the popular lists, following Decker's caution. (6) The hyena analogy for mehen is reported as an analogy, the British Museum's own framing, not as a rule. (7) Cross-links are made only to archive articles that exist on disk at the time of writing, including the companion Phase V articles on women and family, warfare and agriculture; reciprocal links from those articles, and from book-of-the-dead-passages, deir-el-medina, funerary-texts and ritual-practice, are suggested to the coordinator rather than made, since this article's commission forbids editing any other file.
