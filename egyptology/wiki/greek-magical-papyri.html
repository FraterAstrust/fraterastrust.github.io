I. The Ontology of the PGM — A Cross-Cultural Hub

The Greek Magical Papyri (Latin: Papyri Graecae Magicae, or PGM) represent one of the most profound syntheses of esoteric knowledge in human history. Dating from the 2nd century BCE to the 5th century CE, these scrolls record the magical operations of Roman Egypt—a period when Ancient Egyptian Heka merged with Greek philosophy, Jewish mysticism, and early Christian motifs.

In this research framework, the PGM is viewed as a Universalized Esoteric Operating System. It demonstrates how high-frequency Kemetic data was re-coded to permit interaction with the broader Mediterranean awareness field. The PGM are not "corruptions" of earlier magic, but systematic adaptations designed for a multicultural initiatory environment where the ancient Medu Netjer (Hieroglyphs) had to be supplemented with the sounds of the common tongue (Hu).

The table makes the corpus's shape plain: not a literature but a workbench — a few great rolls of hundreds of recipes, kept by professionals, from which the procedures of the following sections are drawn.

The Theban provenance deserves its detail, because it is the corpus's archaeology. The great rolls reached Europe through the collections formed in the 1820s by Giovanni Anastasi, the Swedish-Norwegian consul-general in Alexandria, whose agents bought antiquities up and down the Nile; family tradition in the scholarship holds that the magical handbooks came from a single Theban find — a tomb or cache that served some late-antique practitioner or collector as a library. The rolls were dispersed at auction to Leiden, London, Paris, Berlin, and Stockholm, which is why the corpus's greatest handbooks now sit in five countries' museums while remaining, codicologically, one library. Preisendanz's edition (§XVII) was, in this light, an act of reassembly: the PGM numbers reunite on the page what the art market scattered in fact.

II. The Roman Period Context — The Migration of Temple Knowledge

The surviving papyri cluster in Roman Egypt, above all in the great rolls recovered from Thebes and now cited from the collections formed by Giovanni Anastasi and others in the 1820s. Many of the largest handbooks — including the Great Magical Papyrus of Paris (PGM IV) — are 4th-century copies of much older material, working books rather than temple inscriptions, written to be carried, consulted, and added to.

That portability is the historical point. As temple institutions contracted under Roman administration and temple scripts fell out of general use, priestly expertise in Heka, divination, and healing passed into handbook form in Greek, the administrative language of the province. The PGM are therefore best read as a Migration of Temple Knowledge into private, professional practice — not as a new invention, but as the same repertoire re-housed for a changed world (see The Roman Period).

III. Voces Magicae — The Language of Pure Vibration

Central to the PGM is the use of Voces Magicae ("Magical Words" or "Barbarous Names"). These are strings of syllables that often lack semantic meaning in any ordinary language but are believed to vibrate at the exact frequency of specific divine or daemonic forces.

AUTHORITATIVE UTTERANCE

The use of Voces Magicae is a direct continuation of the Kemetic concept of Hu. By vocalizing these names with the correct intonation and intent (Heka), the magician bypasses the intellect and speaks directly to the underlying source-code of reality. See Hu — Authoritative Utterance.

VIBRATIONAL OVERRIDE

These names (e.g., ABRASAX, IAŌ — the spelling IAOTH also occurs in the corpus as a variant) function as Master Keys. They are designed to "Override" the standard linguistic filters of the brain, allowing the practitioner's consciousness to achieve a state of resonance with the unmanifest potential of the Nun.

Charaktêres — The Written Half of the Voces

Beside the spoken strings stand their written twins: the charaktêres, signs like rings, hooks, stars, and barred crosses that no alphabet contains, inscribed at the heads and feet of spells, on lamellae, and around figurines. The handbooks treat them as the script of the powers themselves — characters to be copied exactly, never interpreted, exactly as the barbarous names are to be pronounced exactly, never translated (§IV). A finished spell is therefore a bilingual object in a stranger sense than Greek and Demotic: Greek for the instructions, and the powers' own signs for the operative text. Modern editions print them in the line like letters, which is what the practitioners, in effect, believed them to be.

IV. Barbarous Names — Overriding the Intellectual Filter

Ancient writers themselves called the untranslatable strings of the papyri Barbarous Names (onomata barbara) — "foreign" names that no Greek dictionary could parse. Iamblichus, defending Egyptian practice in the De Mysteriis (VII.4–5, paraphrase), argued that such names must not be translated at all: rendered into another tongue, he held, they lose the power that belongs to them in their original sound.

Within the spells the names work by accumulation and authority rather than by sense. Long palindromes, wing-shaped arrangements of letters, and strings such as the famous ablanathanalba are written to be spoken exactly as received. In the archive's reading they form an Intellectual Filter Override: language used not to describe a power but to present a credential to it — a continuation, in Greek dress, of the Egyptian insistence on the exact, secret name (see The Power of Names).

V. The Mithras Liturgy — The Soul's Ascent

One of the most significant tractates within the corpus is PGM IV.475-829, known as the Mithras Liturgy. The name is Albrecht Dieterich's (1903), not the text's own, and the tractate's relationship to the actual Mysteries of Mithras is disputed: an earlier version of this section called it "purely Hellenistic-Egyptian" as if settled, but scholarship remains divided between reading it as a Hellenistic-Egyptian ascent rite with Mithraic colouring and seeking genuine Mithraic connections. What is not disputed is its content: a method for the Ascent of the Soul, detailed in §V below.

The practitioner utilizes rhythmic breathing and vowel chants to rise through the atmosphere and the planetary spheres (the Archons), eventually achieving a vision of the solar god (Ra/Helios). This text provides a bridge between traditional Egyptian "Star-Ascent" (found in the Pyramid Texts) and the systematic theurgy of Neoplatonism. It is a Functional Navigation Script for the Ba to escape the gravity-well of the material plane.

The Liturgy as Procedure — Breath, Sound, Ascent, Return

Meyer's division (§V above) separates the ascent liturgy proper (lines 475–750) from the instructions that follow it (751–834), and the procedure can be followed in that order. The practitioner begins with a prayer for revelation and a phylactery for protection, then performs the rite's signature physical act: he draws breath in from the sun's rays, three times, as deeply as he can — the solar power taken into the body as air. The ascent is then sounded rather than spoken: a prolonged hiss, a popping of the cheek, and long strings of the seven vowels and of divine names, each sound assigned to an encounter. Fire-breathing gods, gatekeepers, and the seven pole-lords are met and addressed in sequence; the practitioner identifies himself with cosmic powers as he rises; the great god — Helios-Mithras in the text's syncretism — is beheld, questioned on the matter at hand, and the soul then descends again into the body, the vision complete. The instructions that follow adapt the ascent for repeated and for delegated use, including the initiation of another person into the rite.

Two procedural details deserve emphasis because they correct the tractate's mystical reputation. First, the rite is repeatable and professional: it comes with protective equipment, fallback identifications if the gods resist, and instructions for performing it for a client — it is a service in a handbook, not a unique mystic's diary. Second, its goal is stated in the text's own terms as a temporary immortalisation: the practitioner ascends "as if" to die and be reborn, and returns able to prophesy. Theurgy (§VIII) is what later philosophy made of such rites; in PGM IV it is still a procedure, with breath counts.

The names' arrangement on the page is part of the technology. Spells write their voces in formations: the wing formation, in which a name is written in full and then letter by letter shorter, like a closing wing; the grape-cluster and heart-shaped arrangements of the vowel permutations; and the great palindromes — ablanathanalba reads the same backward, a word that cannot be unwound, deployed wherever a binding must not come loose. These are not decorations added by scribes with time on their hands: the formations are prescribed in the rubrics, drawn in the exemplars, and counted among the spell's working parts, as much as its herbs or its hour. The page is a machine, and its typography is engineering.

VI. Syncretism as Strategy — Integrating Egyptian, Greek, and Hebrew

A single PGM spell may invoke an Egyptian god under a Greek name, address the Jewish God as Iao, and close with a string of names drawn from all three repertoires. This is not confusion. The handbooks treat divine names as Addresses in Different Registers: to name a power in every language in which it had ever been successfully called was to maximise the chance that the call would be recognised and answered.

Scholars therefore read the corpus's syncretism as Strategy, not Dilution. The practitioner worked in a province where Egyptian temples, Greek civic cult, Jewish communities, and early Christian groups shared the same streets; a handbook that could move between their vocabularies served more clients and more situations. Egyptian material supplies the ritual frame and much of the cosmology, Greek supplies the language and hymn-forms, and Hebrew names — above all Iao, Sabaoth, and Adonai — supply some of its most frequently invoked authorities.

VII. The PGM Pantheon — Anubis, Thoth, and Helios-Ra

The gods of the papyri are the gods of Egypt seen through Greek eyes. Anubis appears as psychopomp and opener of ways in necromantic and divinatory spells; Thoth, as Hermes and Hermes Trismegistus, presides over the written spell itself — several handbooks present their contents as temple books of Hermes, copied from sanctuaries. Bes, Typhon-Set, and the decans of the Egyptian sky all recur as working powers.

Above them stands the solar god. Helios-Ra — Helios identified with Ra, and often further assimilated to Iao and the highest God — is among the corpus's most frequently invoked powers, addressed in hymns that move without seam between Egyptian solar theology and Greek hymnody, from the early hymn material of the Paris codex (PGM IV) to the great ascent of the Mithras Liturgy (PGM IV.475–829). Two stronger claims printed in an earlier version of this section are withdrawn as unverifiable, following the archive's accuracy pass: that Helios-Ra is the most invoked figure in the corpus (no invocation census of the corpus exists, and the Jewish names Iao, Sabaoth, and Adonai rival the solar god in frequency — see §VI), and that "the daily hymns of PGM III" show him approached as routine (no such daily-hymn cycle in PGM III could be verified in Betz's edition). What the evidence supports is the claim as now stated: a solar supreme god, hymned across the corpus, at the head of a working pantheon whose membership is Egyptian, Greek, and Jewish at once.

VIII. From Heka to Theurgy — The Philosophical Pivot

A small part of the PGM points beyond traditional Egyptian Heka (Magical Power) toward Theurgy (God-Working) — but the corpus as a whole does not make that pivot.

The corpus is overwhelmingly practical: love spells, curses, healing, divination, and protection, aimed at material outcomes of the kind Heka always served. Theurgy aimed at the Transfiguration of the Soul, and the Mithras Liturgy is the only text in Betz's collection that requests the practitioner's immortality and transfiguration. That single exceptional text does show the practical techniques of the House of Life being re-tasked for the goals of the Hermetic path — the achievement of Nous (Divine Mind) — and in that limited sense it helped the Kemetic data-stream survive the end of the Pharaonic state by becoming an Interiorized Technology. It cannot, on its own, stand for the trajectory of the whole corpus.

IX. The Practitioners — The Scribes of the Subterranean Stream

The PGM were not the work of "amateur sorcerers." They were likely the working libraries of professional Magician-Priests in Roman Egypt.

The priestly origin of the handbooks is a scholarly hypothesis, and this section now labels it as one. Betz's introduction argues that the great papyri likely derive from a late-antique Theban collector's or scholar's library (the Anastasi collection, §XVII) — a milieu of literate, temple-connected experts. That displaced temple specialists moved into private practice as the temples were restricted, preserving initiatory knowledge in portable handbook form, is the standard elaboration of that hypothesis, and it fits the handbooks' contents well; but no practitioner's biography documents the transition, and the narrative detail in an earlier version of this section — consultants to the elite, a deliberate preservation programme — goes beyond the evidence. The archive's figure of the practitioners as Maintenance Technicians of the Hidden Stream is retained as the archive's interpretation of the hypothesis, not as its proof.

X. Ritual Anatomy — Invocations, Offerings, and Defenses

Most PGM operations share a recognisable Ritual Anatomy. A preparation (paraskeuē) prescribes purity, fasting, and the making of the required objects; an invocation (epiklēsis) calls the power by its names, epithets, and deeds; a demand or request states the business; and a release (apolysis) dismisses the power at the close. Hymns in verse, prose prayers, and strings of voces magicae can occupy any of these positions.

Around that spine the spells hang their working parts: offerings of incense, cakes, or libations matched to the god; written charms (phylaktēria) to be worn or buried; figurines, rings, and inscribed lamellae; and defensive formulae protecting the practitioner if the power arrives angry. The handbooks are explicit that the structure is professional equipment — timing by the moon and the planetary hours, the direction faced, and the materials used are specified because, in the practitioners' own terms, the form was part of the efficacy.

A Complete Operation Dissected — Acquiring the Paredros (PGM I)

The anatomy of this section is best shown whole, in the corpus's most famous service spell: the acquisition of a paredros, a supernatural assistant, in PGM I (the spell is presented in Betz's edition under the name of Pnouthis, a sacred scribe, writing to a fellow practitioner). The preparation is ascetic and timed: purity, abstinence, and an invocation performed at sunrise over days, with the offering and the written prayer the handbook prescribes. The invocation calls the lord of the air by his names and histories until he appears — in the text's promise, visibly, and in a form the practitioner can address. The business is then stated as a commission: the assistant is bound by oath to serve.

The handbook's own catalogue of the assistant's powers (in paraphrase of Betz's translation) is the finest surviving job description in ancient magic: he sends dreams; he brings women and men, without the use of material; he kills and destroys; he stirs up winds from the earth; he carries gold, silver, and bronze; he will carry the practitioner through the air and hurl him into the sea and bring him out; he freezes rivers so they can be run across; he lights lamps and extinguishes them; he shakes walls and sets them blazing — "he will serve you suitably for whatever you have in mind." The spell closes with the practicalities of the relationship: how the assistant is fed, housed in a shrine, dismissed, and recalled. Preparation, invocation, commission, maintenance: the four-beat anatomy of §X, preserved entire in a single recipe three pages long.

The Sun-god's own phylactery tradition shows the type at its grandest: the great Paris codex opens with protective hymns and prayers meant to arm the practitioner before any greater operation — the corpus defending its user first, in its first pages, before it teaches him anything else. The ordering is professional ethics in codicological form.

Lamp Divination in Procedure — PGM VII.540–578

The lamp divination of PGM VII shows the divinatory family at bench scale. A clean lamp — the rubrics insist on new or purified equipment throughout this corpus — is filled with pure oil, given a clean linen wick, and set in the prescribed position, in several versions on a new brick or before the practitioner in a darkened room. The invocation calls the god into the flame by his names and by the names of the flame itself; the practitioner (or, in the vessel-inquiry pattern of §XVI, a boy medium) gazes into the light until the figure is seen in it, and the questions are put. The handbook family is large — lamp, vessel, and direct-vision variants recur across PGM I, II, VII, and the Demotic corpus — because the service was the profession's bread: a lamp, a boy, an hour of darkness, and any question a client could pay to ask.

XI. Phylacteries and Defenses — Creating the Amuletic Shield

A large share of the corpus is not attack but Defense. Phylacteries — the word is the papyri's own, phylaktērion — are prescribed against fever, demons, hostile magic, and the dangers of travel and childbirth. PGM VII, for example, preserves a run of short protective and healing charms meant to be copied onto metal leaf, papyrus, or linen and worn on the body, in direct continuity with the Egyptian amulet tradition (see Amulets and Talismans).

The defensive logic is consistent: the charm names the threat, names a greater power set against it, and binds the two together in an object that stays with the client. Silver and gold lamellae, knotted cords, and inscribed rings all serve as the Amuletic Shield — a permanent, portable form of a spoken spell, still working after the magician has gone home.

XII. Compulsion Magic — The Darker Bandwidth of the PGM

The most ethically difficult material in the corpus is the class of erotic spells scholars call agōgē — "drawing" or Compulsion Spells. As historical artefacts they are unambiguous about their intent: their formulae aim to draw a named person to the client, speaking in the language of burning, sleeplessness, and compulsion, and they are preserved here as evidence for the social history of magic, not as usable procedures. Their coercive character is stated plainly because the texts themselves state it plainly.

The best-known examples include the figurine spell of PGM IV.296–434, in which a bound figure is pierced and inscribed, and the drawing-spells of PGM XXXVI. These texts were commissioned work in a competitive urban market, and modern scholarship (notably Christopher Faraone's Ancient Greek Love Magic) reads them as documents of desire, gender, and power in Roman Egypt. In this archive they mark the Darker Bandwidth of the corpus: the same techniques of name, image, and invocation that elsewhere heal and protect, turned to overriding another person's will — the precise abuse the corpus's own closing ethics warn against.

The Louvre Figurine in Procedure — PGM IV.296–434

The spell §XII cites can be followed step by step in Betz's translation, and the archive gives the steps because they are the evidence for its ethical reading. The practitioner models a figure of the target — in the text's prescription, of wax or clay — kneeling and bound, with the hands tied behind the back. He inscribes the figure with the target's name and the voces magicae, front and back. He then pierces it with thirteen needles of bronze or copper, at the head, mouth, ears, eyes, chest, hands, genitals, and feet, reciting for each piercing the formula that the named faculty be bound. The figure is attached to a lead tablet inscribed with the full spell — the same lead, cold and heavy, as the curse tablets of §XV — and the whole assemblage is deposited at sunset in the grave of a person who died untimely or by violence, with the final invocation spoken over the deposit.

The archaeological counterpart survives: a clay figurine of exactly this type, pierced and bound, was found in Egypt in a vessel with its inscribed lead tablet, and is now in the Louvre — the spell and its product excavated together. The archive presents the procedure in full because abridging it would be a second falsification: the historian's duty to this material is accuracy about what the texts intend — coercion, stated as method — neither softened into metaphor nor offered as instruction. It stands here as evidence, in the section this article built for it, of the corpus's darker bandwidth.

XIII. Dream Invocations — Obtaining the Divine Diagnostic

Dreams were a standard channel of divine answer in Egypt, and the papyri preserve a professional technology for requesting them. Dream Oracles (oneirētata) prescribe the lamp, the wick, the inscription, and the prayer with which the practitioner sleeps, so that a named god — Bes, Serapis, Anubis, or the practitioner's own paredros — will appear and answer a stated question. PGM VII and VIII preserve series of such lamp-and-dream procedures.

The premise is the temple practice of Dream Incubation moved into the practitioner's bedroom: purification before sleep, the question written or spoken, and the answer received as image or speech and interpreted on waking. For diagnosis the dream functioned as a Divine Diagnostic — the god showed the cause of an illness, the location of a lost object, or the outcome of a venture, and the handbook's business was to make that showing happen on demand.

Dream Procedures in Detail — The Linen Wick, and the Two Bes Oracles

The corpus's dream technology is concrete enough to itemise. In the request at PGM VII.359–369 (Betz, p. 127), the practitioner writes the magical formula on a strip of linen, makes the linen itself into a lamp wick, lights it, and recites the prayer seven times — an address to the god "who shakes, who thunders, who has swallowed the serpent, who surrounds the moon and hour by hour shakes the disk of the sun," asking him, as lord of the gods, to reveal the matter in question. The dream is then slept for, and the answer taken from it on waking. Writing, burning, reciting, sleeping: the word is literally consumed by flame at the threshold of the dream it requests.

Bes, the pharaonic dream-god of §XV's temple practice, has two full oracles in the corpus: the "Request for a dream oracle from Besas" at PGM VII.222–249 and the "Request for the dream oracle of Besas" at PGM VIII.64–110 — the Abydos oracle of the literary sources (§XV of Ritual Practice) carried into handbook form, ink and lamp in place of the temple chamber. And the request at PGM VII.664–685, "for a dream revelation," heads the series of short dream spells in PGM VII that this section's temple-to-bedroom argument rests on. The god who once required a journey to Abydos or Saqqara now answers, in the handbooks' promise, in the practitioner's own room — the whole history of late Egyptian religion in the distance between those two addresses.

The Homer Oracle — Dice, Verses, and the Poet as God (PGM VII.1–148)

PGM VII opens with the corpus's most literary instrument: the Homer oracle (Homēromanteion). Its procedure is cleromancy of beautiful economy. The practitioner first recites the prescribed prayer and invocation to Homer — the poet addressed as a god, in hymns the handbook supplies — on a day and at an hour the rubric approves. He then throws the dice (three astragaloi or dice, in the standard description) and notes the three numbers cast. The handbook's list assigns to each possible throw a line of Homer — verses drawn from the Iliad and Odyssey and chosen, across the list, to answer as oracles: of journeys, marriages, ventures, dangers. The verse indexed by the throw is the answer, to be interpreted like a dream (§XIII) by its fit to the question.

The oracle matters beyond its charm. It shows the corpus's syncretism (§VI) at the level of canon: Homer, the Greek scripture, is operated by an Egyptian handbook technique — lot, list, and keyed text — that descends from the temple processional oracle's written tickets (§XIV of Ritual Practice). The god has changed; the filing system has not.

XIV. Seven Vowels — Planetary Frequency Modulation

A unique feature of the PGM is the intensive use of Vowel Chants—strings of the seven Greek vowels (α ε η ι ο υ ω) repeated in specific patterns.

In the PGM system, each vowel corresponds to one of the seven classical planets and thus to one of the seven "Heavens." Chanting these vowels was a method of Frequency Modulation. By vocalizing the sequence correctly, the practitioner "Tuned" their own energy body to the planetary grid, allowing them to draw down specific celestial influences or bypass the filters of the Archons. This is the Phonetic Foundation of the later Western ritual tradition.

The Vowel Permutations in Practice — How a Chant Is Built

A vowel chant is constructed, not improvised, and its constructions can be described. The simplest form runs the seven vowels in planetary order as a single word — aeeiouō in the Greek spelling — and repeats it by rubricated counts. The developed forms permute: each vowel is paired with each other in turn, building the long systematic strings that fill columns of the great handbooks; or the sequence is written in the wing and cluster formations of §VI, so that the chant's shape on the page diagrams its ascent through the spheres. In the Mithras Liturgy (§V) the permutations are scored to the drama — this string for the fire-gods, that hiss and vowel-chain for the gatekeepers — so that the practitioner's voice climbs a ladder whose rungs are sounds. §XIV's "frequency modulation" is the archive's metaphor for a practice the texts themselves present as itinerary: the vowels are the road, in the order of the planets who own them.

XV. Ritual Hardware — Wax, Lead, and Exotic Herbs

The spells are precise about Ritual Hardware. Figurines are modelled in wax, clay, or dough; curses are scratched on lead, the cold, heavy metal of the underworld, and deposited in graves and wells; charms are written in inks compounded for the purpose — the handbooks give recipes mixing soot, plant juices, wine, and in some prescriptions blood or other charged fluids, each matched to the power addressed.

Ingredients are specified with a pharmacist's care: named herbs, resins, and minerals, gathered at stated times and sometimes identified by the "secret names" of the herbal tradition, in which an ordinary plant travels under a divine alias. The material logic is sympathetic and administrative at once — the right substance, inscribed with the right names and delivered to the right place (a grave, a threshold, a river), filed the spell where its addressee would find it.

The Sword of Dardanos in Procedure — PGM IV.1716–1870

The rite "which has no equal because of its power" (the text's own billing, in paraphrase) is a masterclass in the corpus's hardware. Its centrepiece is an engraved magnetite — "a magnetic stone which is breathing," the handbook says, meaning a lodestone that draws iron and so seems alive. On it the craftsman engraves Aphrodite sitting astride Psyche, holding the younger goddess by her bound-up hair; above Aphrodite's head, a string of voces; below the pair, Eros standing on the vault of heaven with a blazing torch, and further names around the stone's edge. The stone is then worked with in the rite's invocations — an image of desire's dominion, cut into the one stone that visibly compels other matter.

The spell's second instrument is the "sword" itself: a gold leaf inscribed with the names of the seven archangels — in the text's list, Thouriêl, Michaêl, Gabriêl, Ouriêl, Misaêl, Irraêl, Istraêl — and the formula "Unique is Thouriêl… May it be a propitious day for this name and for me who know it and am wearing it! I summon the immortal and infallible strength of God! Grant me the submission of every soul that I name." The leaf's delivery is pure PGM: it is given to a partridge to swallow, the bird is killed, the leaf retrieved, the herb paiderôs ("boy-love") inserted in the strip, and the whole worn around the neck. A gold amulet from a Tunisian crypt, inscribed with the same sword and the same angelic names (published in Greek, Roman, and Byzantine Studies 61, 2021), shows the recipe manufactured in the real world — recipe and artefact, as with the Louvre figurine, surviving as a pair.

Curse Tablets in Procedure — Lead, Nail, and Deposit

The defixio, the corpus's most archaeologically abundant product, follows a five-step manufacture. The text is composed — target named by name and mother's name, offence stated, gods of the underworld invoked, the binding phrased as an entrusting: the target is "handed over" to Hermes Chthonios, Hecate, and the untimely dead. It is scratched with a stylus or nail onto a thin lead sheet, often with charaktêres and vowel-strings among the names. The sheet is folded or rolled and pierced with a nail — the binding made literal in metal. It is then deposited where the addressees live: in a grave (ideally of the untimely dead, who must carry the message), a well, a sanctuary of Demeter or Hecate, or the stadium seats of a rival's fortunes. Thousands survive because lead does not rot: the deposit was designed, like the archive's own papyri, to keep working after the magician went home.

// HAZARD — LEAD AND THE CORPUS'S TOXIC MATERIA //

The materials of this section are documented as history, and their hazards are stated unsoftened. Lead — curse tablets, lamellae, and the lead compounds the handbooks prescribe — is a cumulative neurotoxin; its period handling (bare hands, mouth-level work, burial in soil and wells) is precisely the exposure modern safety rules exist to prevent. The corpus also prescribes ingredients that are poisonous in themselves and substances drawn from corpses and execution sites; several spells require materials from the violently dead. Nothing in this article's procedural detail is a recommendation to reproduce these operations: the handbooks are evidence for the history of practice, and their materia medica belongs to the same hazard class as the arsenic and mercury pigments of the pigment dossier — reproducible on paper, not to be replicated at the bench.

The Compounded Inks — Writing as Materia Magica

§XV's hardware includes the inks, and they repay a paragraph. The handbooks do not send the practitioner to the desk ink-cake of §VII in House of Life; they compound inks for the rite: carbon inks enriched with myrrh or plant juices for hymns and charms; red inks for the names that must burn; and, in the corpus's darker registers, inks whose "charge" is the point — fluids the texts name by their divine owners, as the Demotic tradition speaks of Typhonian materials for Typhon's work. The principle is §X's anatomy applied to the pen: the medium is part of the message, and a name written in the god's own ink arrives, in the practitioners' theory, already introduced. (The hazard note of §XV applies here with full force: the historical ink-pot held lead compounds and worse, and is documented, not reproduced.)

XVI. The Demotic Magical Papyri (PDM) — The Vernacular Base

Behind the Greek handbooks stands an Egyptian-language corpus: the Demotic Magical Papyri, cited as PDM. The largest, the London-Leiden papyrus (PDM xiv), is a single 3rd-century handbook written mainly in Demotic with Greek passages and glosses in Old Coptic — a practitioner moving between scripts on the same page, and the clearest surviving proof that the Greek and Egyptian traditions were one workshop, not two.

Janet Johnson's introduction to the Demotic spells in Betz's translation makes the methodological point this archive follows: the Greek texts cannot be fully understood until their Demotic counterparts are read beside them. Spells for vessel divination, god-summoning, healing, and erotic compulsion exist in both languages, often in closely parallel forms. The PDM are the Vernacular Base — the temple tradition in the people's script, of which the PGM are the Greek-facing edition.

Vessel Divination in Procedure — The London-Leiden Method (PDM xiv)

The London-Leiden handbook's signature operation is the vessel inquiry, and its staging shows the Demotic workshop at its most theatrical. A vessel of oil — or, in the lamp variants, a clean lamp with a pure wick — is set before a boy medium, a child chosen for purity, who gazes into the shining surface while the practitioner, concealed or behind him, recites the long invocation that calls the god down into the vessel. The boy reports what he sees in the oil's light: the god's arrival, his form, his attendants. The practitioner then puts the client's questions through the boy, receives the god's answers as the boy's report, and dismisses the power with the prescribed release. Variants in the same handbook substitute a lamp's flame, a darkened room, or the practitioner's own vision, but the division of labour is the family's signature: the adult owns the text, the child owns the seeing, and the god owns the answer.

The procedure's ancestry is temple divination (§XIV of Ritual Practice) re-staged for a room and a fee; its posterity runs through every later scrying practice of the Mediterranean world. In the archive's presentation it is the PDM's clearest proof of §XVI's thesis: read the Greek handbooks without this Demotic twin and you have the corpus's vocabulary without its grammar.

XVII. Deciphering the PGM — The Work of Preisendanz and Betz

The corpus exists as a corpus because of two editorial labours. Karl Preisendanz collected the scattered magical papyri and published them as Papyri Graecae Magicae in 1928 and 1931, establishing the PGM numbering still used by every scholar; a revised second edition, prepared by Albert Henrichs, appeared in 1973–74. Preisendanz printed the Greek texts with German translation — but, working to a Greek brief, he omitted the Demotic portions of bilingual papyri.

The English-speaking world reads the corpus in Hans Dieter Betz's edited volume, The Greek Magical Papyri in Translation, Including the Demotic Spells (1986; 2nd ed. 1992). Betz's team restored the Demotic material in full, added papyri published since Preisendanz (extending the sequence to PGM CXXX), and supplied the introductions and glossaries that made the handbooks usable beyond papyrology. All PGM numbers cited in this archive follow Betz's numeration.

A third editorial labour is now under way. The GEMF project (C. A. Faraone and S. Torallas Tovar, The Greco-Egyptian Magical Formularies) is re-editing the handbooks formulary by formulary — treating each great roll as a working library with a compilation history, rather than a bag of separate spells. Its early results already sharpen this article's picture: the "Sword" text of §XV, for example, is tracked across recipe and excavated amulet as one tradition. The corpus Preisendanz numbered and Betz translated is becoming, a century on, a corpus whose books — their makers, owners, and copying lives — can be studied as books.

XVIII. Hermetic Continuity — Theoretical vs. Operative Paths

The papyri and the Corpus Hermeticum come out of the same Egyptian-Greek world and sometimes out of the same ground: Codex VI of the Nag Hammadi library binds Hermetic tractates alongside Gnostic ones, and the PGM themselves contain prayers and hymns of a recognisably Hermetic cast, including material parallel to the Hermetic Asclepius tradition. Thoth-Hermes presides over both literatures as the patron of the written, secret book.

The difference is one of Path, not Country. The philosophical Hermetica theorise the ascent of the soul and largely leave practice at prayer and contemplation; the papyri are the Operative Path — the same cosmos of sympathies, decans, and divine names, worked with lamps, inks, and figurines for named clients and dated problems. Read together, each corrects the other: the Hermetica show what the practitioners believed, and the papyri show what the believers actually did (see Hermeticism).

XIX. Modern Operative Use — The Chaos Magic and Hermetic Revival

The corpus's modern afterlife is a documented matter of publication history. Charles Wycliffe Goodwin first published the Stele of Jeu (PGM V.96–172) in 1852; Aleister Crowley adapted that exorcism text as the "Bornless Ritual," printing it as the preliminary invocation of his 1904 Goetia edition and reworking it as Liber Samekh, a rite for the Knowledge and Conversation of the Holy Guardian Angel. The adaptation changed the rite's purpose — from expelling a demon from another to invoking a higher self — while keeping its barbarous names substantially intact.

From the Golden Dawn and Crowley the papyri passed into the wider Hermetic Revival, and from the 1970s into Chaos Magic, whose writers treated the PGM as a toolkit of techniques detachable from any single belief system. Modern scholarly editions and practitioner-scholar handbooks have since brought the two readings back into contact. This archive records that reception as history: the papyri are primary evidence for ancient practice, and their modern uses are a separate, later chapter of the same books' lives.

The modern scholarly recovery completes the reception history §XIX began. Alongside the editions of §XVII, the later twentieth century produced the first practitioner-scholar handbooks to treat the papyri whole — and, in Stephen Skinner's Techniques of Graeco-Egyptian Magic (2014), a systematic attempt to reconstruct the corpus's operating system from the texts themselves: its cosmology, its timing, its materia, its grammar of names. Whatever a reader makes of the attempt, its existence marks the corpus's third life — temple handbook, antiquarian edition, and now a studied technology again — which is the longest afterlife any Egyptian paperwork has ever had.

XX. Ethics of the Command — Responsibility of the Voice

The PGM consistently teaches that the use of the "Commanding Word" carries a profound Ethical Risk.

To use the names of the gods for trivial or purely selfish ends was viewed as an act of Isfet that would eventually recoil upon the practitioner. The "True Magician" is one who uses the PGM as a ladder to achieve Ma'at, aligning their personal will with the divine will. The ultimate lesson of the Greek Magical Papyri is that Knowledge is Power, but Integrity is Authority. The voice only "commands" when the heart is in a state of absolute transparency.
