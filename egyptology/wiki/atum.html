I. The Name — Tm, Itm and the Complete One

Atum's name is written itm [Gardiner: M17-X1-G17-A40] [𓇋𓏏𓅓𓀭]: the reed i [Gardiner: M17] [𓇋], the loaf t [Gardiner: X1] [𓏏], the owl m [Gardiner: G17] [𓅓], and the seated-god determinative [Gardiner: A40] [𓀭] that marks the word as a god's name and is not pronounced. Older Egyptological literature renders the name Tem, Temu, Tum or Atem; modern transliteration gives itm, conventionally vocalised Atum. The initial vowel is a scholarly convenience — Egyptian writing records consonants, not vowels — but the consonantal skeleton t-m is secure, and everything the Egyptians made of this god begins from it.

That skeleton is the root tm [Gardiner: X1-G17] [𓏏𓅓], and it must be handled carefully, because the root carries two opposite senses at once. James P. Allen, in Genesis in Egypt: The Philosophy of Ancient Egyptian Creation Accounts (1988), the standard philosophical study of the creation texts, sets the position out plainly: the name Atum is a form of the verb tm, and tm means both "to be complete, to finish" and "not to be." The name is built like an agent noun — the same formation as "builder" from "to build" — so that Atum is, in the first sense, "the Complete One" or "the Finisher," and, in the second sense, something close to "the one who is not (yet)." Both connotations, Allen argues, belong to the god, and the texts exploit both.

The first sense is the one handbooks usually give, and it is genuinely Egyptian. Atum is called "lord of totality" in the Coffin Texts tradition Allen cites, and the Pyramid Texts can wish the king to be "complete in Atum" — complete in him, as in a whole that lacks nothing. Atum is complete because, alone before creation, he contains both sexes and all subsequent gods in potential: father and mother in one body, the totality from which the Ennead will unfold and to which, in the end, it will return (Section XII). Nothing is outside him because, at the beginning, there is nothing else.

The second sense is stranger and just as important. Before creation there is, in Egyptian terms, a state the texts describe with negatives: no sky, no earth, no gods, no death. Atum exists in that state without yet being part of a world. A god named from tm "not to be" is therefore the god of pre-existence: the one who was, in the waters, before being was a property anything else could have. The same doubleness will reappear at the end of time, when Atum the Finisher unmakes the world and subsists again in Nun as what he was at the start. His name is, in miniature, his whole career: complete at the beginning because nothing has been subtracted from him, and complete at the end because everything has been gathered back.

Spellings of divine names follow James P. Allen, Middle Egyptian, and Alan H. Gardiner, Egyptian Grammar, cross-checked against the archive's audited articles on the Ennead and Ogdoad and the cosmological deities, whose account of Atum this article expands and does not contradict.

Two near-neighbours of the name deserve a caution. The negative verb tm [Gardiner: X1-G17] is sometimes written with the same determinative as Atum's name — a graphic overlap Allen notes in the Coffin Texts — which suggests the scribes themselves heard the pun. And the evening word-field around the god — he is the sun finishing its course — is a genuine association the tradition made when it placed Atum at sunset (Section VIII), not a modern etymological invention. What the archive will not do is collapse the two senses into one slogan. "Complete One" alone makes Atum sound like a finished product; "the one who is not" alone makes him sound like nothing at all. The texts hold both, and so does this article.

Atum's name also travels as an element in other names. Kings bore throne names compounded with Ra rather than Atum, but ordinary Egyptians bore names invoking him, temples bore his name in their foundations (Section XV), and the syncretic forms Re-Atum and Atum-Re (Section XVII) put his name beside the sun god's from the Old Kingdom onward. The power of the name — the rn [Gardiner: D21-N35] [𓂋𓈖], a constituent of the person alongside the kꜣ [Gardiner: D28] [𓂓] and the bꜣ [Gardiner: G29] [𓅡] in the composite soul — applies to gods with particular force: to know that tm means both completion and non-being is to know something true about what kind of god this is.

II. Before Creation — Nun and the Undifferentiated

Before Atum there is Nun: nwn [Gardiner: N35-W24-N35] [𓈖𓏌𓈖], the primeval waters — dark, inert, boundless, and, crucially, not empty. The archive's survey of the cosmological deities states the position this article follows: Nun is undifferentiated potential, the state in which nothing has yet been distinguished from anything else. There is no sky, pt [Gardiner: N1] [𓇯], because nothing has been lifted up; no earth, because nothing has been set down; no death, because nothing has yet lived. The Hermopolitan Ogdoad gives that state eight names — Nun and Naunet, Heh and Hauhet, Kek and Kauket, Amun and Amaunet — but the Heliopolitan account is sparer: waters, darkness, and, latent within them, Atum.

Atum's relation to Nun is stated two ways in the sources, and the difference is theological, not accidental. In most accounts Atum is self-created: he comes into being by himself, without a maker, the first act of differentiation inside the undifferentiated. In some texts, however, Nun is called his father — the Global Egyptian Museum's glossary entry on Atum records the variant plainly — as if the waters themselves were the nearest thing to a parent a self-made god can have. Egyptian theology tolerated both formulations in the same corpus because they answer different questions: who made Atum? (no one) and out of what did Atum emerge? (the waters that were already there). Creation, in the Heliopolitan telling, is not manufacture from nothing. It is the emergence of distinction within a potential that precedes it.

That is why Nun never disappears. The ordered world, once made, remains an island inside the waters: Nun surrounds the sky, wells up in the Nile flood, fills the temple lakes, and waits beyond the horizon of the solar barque's nightly journey. Every sunrise is therefore a local repetition of the first emergence, and every threat to order — above all the serpent ꜥpp [Gardiner: D36-Q3-Q3] [𓂝𓊪𓊪], Apep — is Nun's undifferentiation pressing back against the distinctions Atum made. The full treatment of the waters is in the archive's article on Nun and the primordial waters; what matters for Atum is that he is defined against them twice — at the beginning, as the one who rises out of them, and at the end, as the one who returns to them (Section XII).

NUN — THE WATERS BEFORE DISTINCTION

nwn [Gardiner: N35-W24-N35] [𓈖𓏌𓈖]. Dark, inert, and full of potential. Not a god who acts so much as the condition in which action first becomes possible — and the condition to which, in Spell 175 of the Book of the Dead, the world will one day return.

ATUM IN NUN — THE LATENT ONE

Atum before his emergence is the tm [Gardiner: X1-G17] paradox in person: complete, because he lacks nothing; not-yet, because nothing — including, in a sense, himself as a distinct god — has come into being. The Coffin Texts give him speech from inside this state (Section X).

A useful discipline for the reader: whenever this article says "before creation," it means before differentiation, not before existence. The Egyptian texts are consistent on this point in their different idioms, and confusing the two produces the common modern error of describing Nun as "nothing." Nun is not nothing. Nun is everything, unseparated — which is precisely what Atum, the separator, is not.

III. Self-Creation — How Atum Came Into Being

The Heliopolitan account's central verb is ḫpr [Gardiner: L1-D21-Z1] [𓆣𓂋𓏤], "to come into being, to become, to evolve into a form." Atum does not get made; he ḫprs — he comes into being by himself. The scarab god Khepri, whose very name is built from this root, is Atum's becoming given a separate face (Section VIII), and the archive's Khepri article treats the root's full word-field. For Atum the point is austere: the first event in the history of the world is an event with no external cause. A distinct being appears inside the indistinct waters, aware of himself, alone.

The texts linger on the aloneness. In the long first-person creation account preserved in the Coffin Texts (Section X) and retold, centuries later, in the Papyrus Bremner-Rhind (British Museum EA10188), the creator surveys a world that does not yet contain a place to stand, a companion, or a second voice. Everything that will make existence bearable — ground, air, a paired order of things — has to be produced out of the one body that exists. Heliopolitan creation is therefore, from first to last, an art of self-division without self-loss: Atum will become many gods and remain, as head of the Ennead, the one they all came from. His completeness (Section I) is not threatened by creation; it is distributed by it.

Egypt had other creation theologies, and this article keeps them in their own lanes, as the archive's cosmological survey does. At Memphis, Ptah creates by heart and tongue — conceiving in thought and commanding in speech — and the Memphite text preserved on the Shabaka Stone subordinates Atum to that process, presenting the Ennead as proceeding through Ptah's word; see Memphite theology. At Hermopolis the Ogdoad precedes the first sunrise, and Thoth presides over the emergence. At Thebes, Amun is the hidden source behind all of them. These are rival answers offered by rival priesthoods, later harmonised by theologians who preferred accumulation to exclusion. The Heliopolitan answer is the oldest attested in continuous form, and it is Atum's: I was alone; I came into being; I made the gods from myself. That sentence is a labelled paraphrase of the creator's speeches in the Coffin Texts and Bremner-Rhind, not a quotation.

One more form of the emergence deserves record because the Book of the Dead preserves it: Atum as a serpent. The Global Egyptian Museum's Atum entry notes that in the Book of the Dead the sun god is said to have ascended from the chaos-waters with the appearance of a snake, the animal that renews itself by shedding its skin. The serpent is not a fall or a disguise; it is the appropriate body for a being who precedes limbs, sex, and species — long, legless, self-renewing, at home in water and earth at once. Section XII returns to the serpent, because it is also the body Atum resumes at the end of the world.

IV. The Primordial Mound and the Benben

A god emerging from water needs somewhere to be. The Heliopolitan answer is the primeval hill: the first mound of solid ground to rise out of Nun, the first dry place, the stage on which creation happens. In the Old Kingdom Pyramid Texts the mound and the god are so close that Atum himself can be addressed as the mound — he does not merely stand on the first ground, he is the first ground, distinction made solid. Every later Egyptian temple re-founded that mound in its own sanctuary, which is why the archive's article on temple architecture treats the sanctuary floor's rise toward the holy of holies as cosmology in stone: to walk into a temple is to walk back toward the First Time.

The mound's cult object at Heliopolis was the benben: bnbn [Gardiner: D58-N35-D58-N35-O24] [𓃀𓈖𓃀𓈖𓉴], written with b [Gardiner: D58] [𓃀], n [Gardiner: N35] [𓈖] doubled, and the pyramid determinative mr [Gardiner: O24] [𓉴]. The Global Egyptian Museum's benben entry — the source followed here — derives the name from the verb wbn, "to rise, to shine forth," and records the two Pyramid Text interpretations side by side: the benben stone was a manifestation of Atum when he rose out of the primeval waters, or the primeval hill on which Atum first landed. The stone, conical in shape, was kept in the innermost sanctuary of the temple of Re at Heliopolis, in the shrine called the House of the Benben. It was, in the most literal sense Egyptian religion allowed, the first place in the world, preserved as an object.

The one verbatim ancient sentence this article quotes comes from this context, and it is quoted in a named, public-domain translation. Pyramid Texts Utterance 600, in Samuel A. B. Mercer's translation (1952), addresses the god: "O Atum-Khepri, when thou didst mount as a hill, and didst shine as bnw of the ben (or, benben) in the temple of the bennu in Heliopolis" — and continues, in the same utterance, with the spitting out of Shu and the spitting out of Tefnut (Section V). The sentence compresses the whole Heliopolitan sequence into one breath: the god mounts as the hill, shines as the sacred stone, and creates. The bnw [Gardiner: D58-N35-G43] [𓃀𓈖𓅱], the Benu bird — the heron-like "phoenix" of Heliopolis, the soul of the sun god — belongs to the same word-family of rising and shining, and its perch was the benben itself.

THE MOUND — FIRST GROUND

The primeval hill is creation's first distinction between wet and dry, below and above. Atum identified with it, temples re-founded it, and the inundation re-enacted it every year: as the Nile flood fell, the first mounds to emerge were the world being made again.

THE BENBEN — FIRST OBJECT

The conical stone in the House of the Benben at Heliopolis. The Global Egyptian Museum notes that by the early reign of Amenhotep IV the word could denote an obelisk as cult image, and that the tops of obelisks — and of pyramids, the pyramidia — are themselves called benben: the first stone, multiplied across the sacred landscape.

THE BENU — FIRST VOICE

bnw [Gardiner: D58-N35-G43] [𓃀𓈖𓅱]. The bird whose cry, in the Hermopolitan telling preserved in this archive's Ennead and Ogdoad article, broke the silence of the waters from its perch on the mound. Heliopolis kept both bird and stone in one sanctuary complex.

The mound also explains a feature of Atum's cult geography that Section XIV develops: Heliopolis claimed to be the place of emergence. A city that houses the first stone does not need to argue for its seniority. Whatever political capital Egypt's kings chose — Memphis, Thebes, Pi-Ramesse — the theological capital of creation remained Iunu, because the benben was there and nowhere else.

V. The Creation of Shu and Tefnut — Sneeze, Spit and Wordplay

Alone and complete, Atum produces the first pair: Shu and Tefnut. Shu — šw [Gardiner: H6-G43-A40] [𓆄𓅱𓀭], written with the feather šwt [Gardiner: H6] [𓆄] and w [Gardiner: G43] [𓅱] under the god determinative — is air, light, dryness, and the space between things. Tefnut — tfnt [Gardiner: U33-I9-N35-X1] [𓍘𓆑𓈖𓏏] — is moisture and cohesion, the wetness without which Shu's dry space would be a desert of separated dust. Together they are the first environment: a volume, and the medium that fills it. Their full profiles are given in their own articles; here the question is the manner of their making, because the texts give not one manner but several, and the several are the doctrine.

The Variants, as the Texts Present Them

Spitting and expectoration. In Pyramid Texts Utterance 600 — the utterance quoted in Section IV — Atum spews out Shu and spits out Tefnut, in Mercer's rendering. The Coffin Texts develop the mouth-account at length: Shu himself, in the speech preserved in Coffin Texts Spell 80 and the Shu spells around it, declares (in paraphrase) that Atum did not form him in a womb or shape him in an egg, but spat him forth with the spittle of his mouth, his sister Tefnut emerging behind him, enveloped in the breath of life. The account Robert Armour's Gods and Myths of Ancient Egypt reproduces from the Coffin Texts has Shu say exactly this in the first person. Creation by mouth is creation by breath and fluid together — the two things a living body can project into the world without tools — and it keeps the children of Atum's substance while making them distinct persons.

The sneeze. In the Bremner-Rhind creation account — the late papyrus (British Museum EA10188, copied in 305 BCE) preserving much older material, published by E. A. Wallis Budge as the Legend of the Creation — the creator tells the story in the first person, and the sequence runs, in paraphrase: he was alone; no sky or earth existed; his fist became his spouse; and then he sneezed out Shu and spat out Tefnut. Budge's 1912 translation, a public-domain rendering, is the source the archive follows for this account's shape. The pairing is not random decoration. It is wordplay, and the Egyptian audience was expected to hear it.

// THE WORDPLAY — IŠŠ AND TFN //

The sneeze-verb in the account is išš, "to sneeze," and it sounds like šw [Gardiner: H6-G43-A40], Shu. The spit-verb is tfn, "to spit," and it is the consonantal core of tfnt [Gardiner: U33-I9-N35-X1], Tefnut. Egyptian theological wordplay works exactly this way across the corpus: the act that produces a god is chosen so that its sound contains the god's name. The name is not a label stuck on afterwards; in the logic of the name and of phonology and voice, the name is the being's nature made audible, and the pun is the proof that the act and the being belong together. Shu is what a sneeze projects — invisible air, suddenly expelled, carrying life outward. Tefnut is what spitting projects — moisture, the body's water made separate. The archive presents the wordplay as the texts present it: as theology conducted by sound.

Masturbation and the Hand. The oldest stratum, the Pyramid Texts, is also the most physical. Utterance 527 states, in the standard scholarly paraphrase followed by Allen and by the Global Egyptian Museum alike, that Atum created by his masturbation in Heliopolis: he took his phallus in his fist to excite desire, and the twins Shu and Tefnut were born. Section VI is devoted to this variant and to the goddess who personifies the Hand, because it became a theology of its own. Here it is enough to note the consistency underneath the variants: in every version the children come out of Atum's own body — mouth, nose, hand, seed — and in no version does Atum take a partner, find material, or receive help. The variants differ in organ, not in doctrine.

The lost children and the Eye. A further episode, developed in the Coffin Texts and later retellings, has Shu and Tefnut wander off into the darkness of Nun and become lost to their father. Atum sends his single Eye — irt [Gardiner: D4-X1] [𓁹𓏏] — to search for them; on their return he weeps, in some accounts, and from his tears humankind is born. The archive records this as the tradition presents it: a widespread Egyptian association of humanity with the tears of a god, attached here to Atum and elsewhere to Ra, rather than a single canonical scene. What is stable across the tellings is the naming: the returned Shu is named Life and Tefnut Order — mꜣꜥt [Gardiner: U5-X1] [𓌷𓏏], Ma'at herself — as if the first pair, once recovered, received their functions as titles. Air is what lives breathe; moisture-in-measure is what order feels like. The punning theology of the sneeze becomes, here, a naming theology.

From Shu and Tefnut the genealogy proceeds by ordinary — if divine — generation: their children are Geb and Nut, earth and sky, and theirs are the four who carry the story into history. Section VII sets out the whole tree. The point of the variants, taken together, is that Heliopolitan theology never reduced creation to a mechanism. Mouth, nose, hand, tears — the texts keep every door of the body open, because the doctrine is not how a body makes gods but that one complete body did.

VI. The Hand of Atum and Iusaaset

The masturbation variant of Section V generated a person. If Atum's hand performed the act by which the first gods were born, then the hand was, functionally, his spouse — the female principle inside the complete one, made visible as an organ. Egyptian theology, which personifies everything it takes seriously, duly made her a goddess: the Hand of Atum, worshipped at Heliopolis in the form of Iusaaset, "the Great One Who Comes Forth," a goddess whose very title describes emergence. In later Heliopolitan theology Iusaaset is honoured as a grandmother of the gods, and she could be identified with the goddess Hathor-Nebethetepet in the same cult centre. The archive records the identification as the later tradition presents it; the underlying figure is Heliopolitan and old.

The Hand matters beyond its one scene. It is the Egyptian solution, in bodily form, to the problem Section I posed: how a god who is tm — complete, lacking nothing, including a partner — can nevertheless generate. The answer is that completeness is not sameness. Within the one body, hand and phallus, female and male, are already distinguished enough to act upon each other, while remaining one god. The cosmological survey's treatment of primordial androgyny makes the same point in general terms: gender in this system is a manifestation of duality, and duality first appears inside the One, not between two pre-existing beings. Iusaaset is that interior duality with a name and a cult.

Hathor's recurring title "Hand of Atum" in other cult centres shows how far the figure travelled once coined. The archive notes the title without annexing Hathor's whole theology to this section: at Heliopolis the Hand is Iusaaset; elsewhere the title attaches to the great goddess locally present. Egyptian divine identity works by such attachments, and precision consists in saying which attachment, at which place, in which period — not in forcing a single answer on all of them.

IUSAASET — THE GREAT ONE WHO COMES FORTH

Consort-shadow of Atum at Heliopolis, personification of his creative Hand, ancestress — "grandmother" — of the gods in the later Heliopolitan tradition. Her cult is the institutional proof that the priests of Iunu took the Hand episode as theology, not embarrassment.

THE HAND AS INSTRUMENT

The hand is also the craftsman's organ. A creator who works with his hand stands closer to Ptah the fashioner than the mouth-account alone would suggest — one of the several bridges by which the Heliopolitan and Memphite theologies were later joined (Section XVII).

VII. Head of the Ennead — the Heliopolitan Genealogy

The Ennead — psḏt [Gardiner: N9-X1] [𓇷𓏏], "the Nine" — is Atum's family organised as a cosmos. The archive's dedicated article on the Ennead and Ogdoad gives the system's full analysis, including its numerical logic; this section gives Atum's place in it, which is the first place, in every sense. He is the one from whom the count begins: Atum, then Shu and Tefnut (two), then Geb and Nut (two), then the four children of Geb and Nut — one, two, two, four: nine.

GENERATION ONE — ATUM ALONE

itm [Gardiner: M17-X1-G17-A40] [𓇋𓏏𓅓𓀭]. The complete one on the mound in Nun. Source, father, and — through the serpent form and the end of the world (Section XII) — survivor of the whole genealogy.

GENERATION TWO — AIR AND MOISTURE

Shu, šw [Gardiner: H6-G43-A40] [𓆄𓅱𓀭], and Tefnut, tfnt [Gardiner: U33-I9-N35-X1] [𓍘𓆑𓈖𓏏]: space and its medium, dryness and wetness, the first environment (Section V).

GENERATION THREE — EARTH AND SKY

Geb, gb [Gardiner: G38-D58-A40] [𓅬𓃀𓀭], and Nut, nwt [Gardiner: N35-W24-X1-N1] [𓈖𓏌𓏏𓇯]: the floor and the ceiling of the world, separated by their father Shu so that life can happen between them.

GENERATION FOUR — THE OSIRIAN QUARTET

Osiris, wsir [Gardiner: Q1-D4-A40] [𓊨𓁹𓀭]; Isis, ꜣst [Gardiner: G1-S29-X1] [𓄿𓋴𓏏]; Seth, stš [Gardiner: E20] [𓃩]; and Nephthys, nb.t-ḥwt [Gardiner: V30-X1-O6] [𓎟𓏏𓉗]. With them the cosmos acquires death, magic, violence, and mourning — history begins. See the Osiris myth.

Two features of Atum's headship are easy to miss. First, the Ennead is not a council of equals with a chairman; it is a body unfolded from one body. Egyptian texts can speak of the gods as Atum's limbs, of the Ennead as his "members," and of creation as his own substance externalised — the doctrine Allen's Genesis in Egypt traces through the Coffin Texts, where the creator's speech moves between "I" and the gods he has made without a hard boundary. Second, headship persists after delegation. Atum does not retire. He remains the court of final appeal among the gods — the tribunal of the Horus and Seth contendings acknowledges his line's authority, the dead king is received by him (Section IX), and at the end of the world it is Atum, not any younger god, who speaks the last word (Section XII).

The Ennead's number is also, as the archive's Ennead article notes, a number of completeness — nine as plural-of-plurals, three times three. That a god named tm [Gardiner: X1-G17], "the Complete One," should head the complete number of gods is the sort of fit Egyptian theology noticed and prized. This article records the fit as the tradition's own pattern-making, not as a claim about the system's historical origin, which was gradual and priestly rather than designed in a day.

VIII. The Solar Triad — Khepri, Ra and Atum

From the Old Kingdom onward Atum is also a sun god, and Egyptian theology organised the sun's day as a sequence of forms. The formula the whole tradition eventually settled on — stated as an identity by the justified dead in Book of the Dead Spell 17's orbit and assumed everywhere in the netherworld books — is a triad: Khepri in the morning, Ra at noon, Atum in the evening. One sun, three names, three bodies: the scarab who comes into being, the falcon-headed lord of the day's height, and the man who finishes the course and descends.

Atum's share is the evening. The Global Egyptian Museum's entry gives the reason from the Egyptians' own word-field: the form itmw behind his name is glossed there as "to complete oneself," and Atum is accordingly "Re on the top of the western mountain" — the sun arriving at the western horizon, ꜣḫt [Gardiner: N27] [𓈌], its work done, its light reddening and withdrawing into the dwꜣt [Gardiner: N15] [𓇽], the Duat. Evening is not decline in this system; it is completion. The day, like the world, is a thing Atum finishes — the tm [Gardiner: X1-G17] root doing its work again, now on the scale of hours instead of aeons. Morning belongs to becoming ( ḫpr [Gardiner: L1-D21-Z1]); evening belongs to the finisher.

The triad is a theological instrument, not a timetable, and the archive uses it as such. The three forms appear together, address each other, and merge: Pyramid Texts already combine Atum with Ra as Re-Atum (Section XVII), and the netherworld books show the night sun travelling in forms that accumulate Khepri's becoming and Atum's age in a single barque journey — see the solar barque. What the triad gives the worshipper is a grammar for the day: every dawn is the First Time rehearsed small, every dusk a rehearsal for the greater finishing of Section XII, and Atum presides over the dusk the way he presides over the end — not as an enemy of the light, but as the form the light takes when its course is complete.

ḫpr [Gardiner: L1-D21-Z1] [𓆣𓂋𓏤]. The scarab, self-creation at dawn. Full profile: Khepri.

RA — MIDDAY

rꜥ [Gardiner: N5] [𓇳]. The sun at height, lord of the visible order. Full profile: Ra.

itm [Gardiner: M17-X1-G17-A40] [𓇋𓏏𓅓𓀭]. The finisher at the western horizon, descending into the Duat to be reborn. This article.

IX. Atum in the Pyramid Texts

The Pyramid Texts — the inscribed utterances of the late Old Kingdom royal pyramids, from Unas onward (Fifth and Sixth Dynasties) — are Atum's oldest surviving stage, and he is already fully himself there: creator, father of the gods, and father of the king. The archive's article on funerary texts gives the corpus its context; this section gives Atum's roles inside it.

Creator. Utterance 527 gives the masturbation creation (Sections V–VI); Utterance 600 gives the mounting as the hill, the shining as the benben, and the spewing and spitting out of Shu and Tefnut, and then names the whole Ennead in sequence — Atum, Shu, Tefnut, Geb, Nut, Osiris, Isis, Seth, Nephthys, in Mercer's rendering of the passage fetched for this article — as the company under Atum's protection, a protection the utterance then extends to the dead king named in the text. Creation and kingship are not separate topics in these utterances; the same breath that makes the gods makes the king's safety.

Father of the king. Across the corpus the dead king is addressed as Atum's son, is embraced by Atum, and is lifted by him toward the sky. The theology is exact: the living king was Horus; the dead king becomes Osiris; but beneath both offices stands the older claim that the king is Atum's offspring, issued from the creator's own body as Shu and Tefnut were. Atum's embrace in the Pyramid Texts is therefore a homecoming — the separated thing gathered back into the complete one, without ceasing to be itself. Section XVI develops the kingship doctrine; here the textual root of it is enough.

The paired Atum. The Pyramid Texts also know Atum in company — above all as Re-Atum, the fusion discussed in Section XVII, and in a remarkable dual conception in which the king's two aspects are figured as Atum's own double nature, the god who is both the one who was alone and the company he became. Scholars have long noted the corpus's interest in Atum as a totality that includes duality; the archive records the interest without building a system on it. What the texts warrant is this: from our earliest evidence, Atum is never merely a lonely monad. He is a one that contains a two.

X. Atum in the Coffin Texts

The Coffin Texts of the Middle Kingdom — spells painted on the coffins of non-royal as well as royal dead — give Atum what the allusive Pyramid Texts never do: a voice. The great creation speeches of the corpus, above all the Shu spells (Spells 75–83 in the standard numbering), let the first generations speak in the first person about how they were made, and let Atum speak about being alone. Allen's Genesis in Egypt builds its philosophy of Egyptian creation largely on these texts, and this section follows his reading.

In the Shu spells, Shu narrates his own origin (Section V): not formed in a womb, not shaped in an egg — the egg, swḥt [Gardiner: H8] [𓆇], is named precisely to be denied — but spat from Atum's mouth and enveloped in the breath of life, on the day Atum appeared "in the infinity, the nothing, the darkness and shapelessness," in the Coffin Text phrasing as reproduced in Armour's standard handbook. The negations do philosophical work: they define creation against every ordinary mode of generation. What Atum does is not birth, not manufacture, not conception. It is a fourth thing, for which the texts use the mouth, the hand, and the verb ḫpr [Gardiner: L1-D21-Z1] [𓆣𓂋𓏤] — becoming — and leave the mechanism deliberately at the level of image.

The Coffin Texts also give Atum the doctrine of the millions: the creator declares himself the maker of the gods and the one from whom the multitudes proceed, and the texts play on his titles of totality — "lord of totality" among them, from the root tm [Gardiner: X1-G17] itself (Section I). A second Coffin Text theme matters for Section XII: Atum in dialogue with Osiris. The germ of the Book of the Dead's last conversation — the dead god asking about duration, the creator answering with millions of years and with the final return to the waters — is already present in the Middle Kingdom corpus. Egyptian eschatology, like Egyptian cosmogony, was composed early and elaborated long.

Finally, the Coffin Texts democratise Atum. A spell that identifies the deceased with the creator — "I am Atum" in the corpus's characteristic first person — puts the oldest god's name in a private person's mouth. The claim is not blasphemy in this system; it is the funerary programme itself, by which the dead person assimilates to the gods whose stories guarantee renewal. To be able to say "I am Atum when I was alone in Nun" is to claim a share in the one being whom non-being could not prevent and dissolution will not end.

XI. Atum in the Book of the Dead

In the Book of the Dead — the New Kingdom and later "Spells of Coming Forth by Day," surveyed in this archive's Book of the Dead passages — Atum appears in three registers: as an object of the deceased's identification, as a presence in the vignettes, and as a speaker at the end of the world.

Spell 17 is the great glossed spell, a text that asks "who is he?" after its own statements and answers with identifications, and its answers repeatedly land on Atum and Ra-Atum: the self-created great god, the one in his sun-disk, the maker of his own names and members, whose arising the spell expounds line by line. Through Spell 17's glosses Atum became, for every owner of a Book of the Dead, a study-text: not only a god to meet but a doctrine to recite correctly. Spell 15's hymns to the setting sun address the god in his evening majesty; other spells invoke Atum among the Ennead who witness the deceased's vindication at the weighing of the heart.

The vignettes — the painted scenes that head the spells in the great papyri — show Atum where the text requires a senior god in human form with a king's regalia: enthroned among the Ennead, receiving the deceased, or presiding in the solar company. In the Papyrus of Ani (British Museum EA10470), the most famous of all Books of the Dead, the creator's presence in the judgment and solar scenes is part of the visual argument that the whole divine order, from its first god downward, ratifies Ani's vindication. Vignette iconography is treated generally in the archive's Book of the Dead article; Atum's own visual grammar is Section XIII below.

And then there is the register that makes Atum unlike any other god in the book: he is the one who knows how the story ends, and in Spell 175 he says so. That spell deserves its own section.

XII. Spell 175 — the End of Creation and the Return to Nun

Book of the Dead Spell 175 is, in the standard descriptions, a spell "for not dying again in the realm of the dead." Its content is a dialogue. The deceased — in the Papyrus of Ani tradition, speaking with and as the vindicated dead — questions Osiris and Atum about the nature and duration of the afterlife: what is this land, without water or air, deep and dark, where the affections cannot be satisfied, and how long shall I live there? The answers, in the paraphrase that follows the standard translations (Faulkner's, in The Ancient Egyptian Book of the Dead, is the version this archive treats as reference), are in two movements — and the second movement is the most famous eschatological passage in Egyptian literature.

First movement: the millions. Atum reassures the questioner with duration beyond reckoning: it is decreed that he shall live for millions upon millions of years. The afterlife is not a brief reprieve; on the scale of a human life it is effectively endless, and Osiris's realm, for all its darkness, is a lasting estate. This is the answer the spell's title promises — no second death — and for many owners of the book it was answer enough.

Second movement: the unmaking. But Atum then discloses the further horizon, and this article presents it strictly as labelled paraphrase of the spell as Faulkner and the Global Egyptian Museum both summarise it: Atum declares that he will one day destroy everything he has created; the land will return to the primeval waters, to Nun, to the flood as it was at the beginning; and he himself will remain, together with Osiris, transformed into serpents — forms, the text adds, that men do not know and gods do not see. Creation, which began with one god alone in the waters (Sections II–III), will end with two gods alone in the waters, in the serpent bodies that precede and survive form (Section III). The circle of tm [Gardiner: X1-G17] closes: the Finisher finishes.

// WHY THIS PASSAGE IS PRESENTED AS PARAPHRASE //

Spell 175's ending is quoted loosely all over the internet, usually without a translator's name and often with wording no published translation contains. This archive's rule is that ancient words are quoted verbatim only from a translation actually consulted and named, and otherwise paraphrased and labelled. The paraphrase above follows the sense given in R. O. Faulkner's Book of the Dead translation and in the Global Egyptian Museum's Atum entry — both of which record the destruction of creation, the return to the primeval waters, and Atum's survival with Osiris in serpent form — and it claims no more wording than those sources warrant.

The passage's importance is out of proportion to its length. It proves that Egyptian creation theology was cyclical at the largest scale: the world is not a once-for-all artefact but a state that arose from the waters and will subside into them, with Atum — and, remarkably, Osiris, the god of the dead — persisting through the subsidence. It also completes Atum's identification with evening (Section VIII): the god of every sunset is, by the same logic, the god of the world's sunset, and his presence at both is a promise structured into the cosmos — what finishes in him is not annihilated, because he was there before it began. The serpent who sheds its skin in the morning waters (Section III) and the serpent who remains when the waters close over the world are the same figure, at the two ends of time.

XIII. Iconography — Human Form, Double Plume and Serpent

Atum is, in his characteristic form, resolutely human: a man in divine dress, bearded with the curled divine beard, wearing a king's regalia. Among creator gods — compare Ptah's tight shroud and cap, Khepri's scarab — Atum's humanity is itself a statement: the first god looks like a king, because kingship is his invention and his gift (Section XVI). He carries the furniture of rule: the ꜥnḫ [Gardiner: S34] [𓋹], "life"; the wꜣs-sceptre [Gardiner: S40] [𓌀] of dominion; and the crook ḥqꜣ [Gardiner: S38] [𓋾] of the shepherd-king.

His crowns distinguish his aspects. As king of the united land he wears the Double Crown, sḫmty [Gardiner: S5] [𓋖] — the "Two Powerful Ones," the White Crown ḥḏt [Gardiner: S1] [𓋑] of Upper Egypt within the Red Crown dšrt [Gardiner: S4] [𓋕] of Lower Egypt. As a solar and Heliopolitan god he wears the double-plumed headdress, šwty [Gardiner: S9] [𓋛] — two tall feathers rising side by side from a modius or disk — the crown this article's brief names as his signature. The two plumes are not Shu's single feather doubled at random: paired height, like the paired creation of Section V, is Atum's visual grammar — two-in-one, duality held in a single head. In solar contexts he may wear the sun disk itself, and in fusion with Ra he takes the falcon head of Ra or is shown as the aged man of evening on the barque.

The British Museum's collections, consulted for this article, hold a characteristic late image of the god's composite form: a hollow-cast copper-alloy votive box (EA27580, Late Period, c. 500–350 BCE, from Naukratis) surmounted by figures of Atum in composite shape — human head wearing the Atef crown, with long beard and tripartite wig, the body combining a raised cobra and an eel, supported behind by an ostrich feather. The museum's record, following Weiss (2012), identifies the composite figure as "the solar god and demiurge Atum." The object is a reminder that the human king-form is the centre, not the boundary, of Atum's imagery: eel, cobra, and feather can carry him too, into the watery and chthonic registers where the serpent of Sections III and XII belongs.

THE MAN-KING

The default Atum: human body, divine beard, king's crowns and sceptres, enthroned at the Ennead's head or striding in the solar company of tomb and temple relief.

THE DOUBLE PLUME

šwty [Gardiner: S9] [𓋛]. Two tall feathers: Atum's solar-Heliopolitan crown, duality worn as unity.

THE SERPENT

The body of pre-existence and post-existence: Atum rising from the waters as a snake in the Book of the Dead tradition, and the form in which he and Osiris remain at the end (Section XII).

THE COMPOSITE

Human head, Atef crown, eel-and-cobra body (British Museum EA27580): the demiurge at home in water, earth, and air at once.

A caution the archive applies to every deity article applies here with force: a human-form god with a double crown is not automatically Atum. Identification rests on inscription, context, and company — the Ennead about him, the Heliopolitan setting, the accompanying text naming itm [Gardiner: M17-X1-G17-A40]. Uninscribed bronzes of plumed gods were assigned to several deities by their makers; the honest label is the one the object itself carries.

XIV. Cult at Heliopolis — Iunu, City of the Pillar

Atum's city is Iunu — iwnw [Gardiner: M17-G43-N35-W24-O49] [𓇋𓅱𓈖𓏌𓊖], "the Pillars," Greek Heliopolis, "City of the Sun," biblical On — at the apex of the Delta, now absorbed into north-eastern Cairo around Matariya and Ain Shams. The archive's article on sacred geography maps the site; this section records the cult that made it, for two and a half millennia, one of the intellectual capitals of the ancient world. Its priesthood produced the Ennead theology, kept the benben in the House of the Benben (Section IV), maintained the Benu cult, and supplied Egypt with astronomer-priests whose learning Greek visitors — Herodotus and Plato's tradition among them — still travelled to consult in the city's late, diminished centuries.

The cult's material survival is famously poor, and the archive states the position plainly: Heliopolis was quarried, flooded, and built over more thoroughly than almost any great Egyptian site. What stands today is a fraction — most emblematically the obelisk of Senusret I (Twelfth Dynasty) still upright at Matariya, the last of a forest of monuments to Re-Atum. The poverty of the ruins is not evidence of the cult's poverty. It is evidence of the site's position, at the Delta's edge beside a growing Cairo, in the path of three thousand years of stone-robbers. Theology must be reconstructed from the texts the priesthood exported — Pyramid Texts, Coffin Texts, temple inscriptions copied at Edfu, Dendera, and Karnak — and that is how this article has proceeded.

The daily cult followed the pattern of every great temple, treated in the archive's articles on ritual practice and the House of Life: the god awakened, washed, clothed, and fed in his shrine; processions on feast days; the king, or his priestly deputy, offering mꜣꜥt [Gardiner: U5-X1] [𓌷𓏏] to the god who authored order. Two Heliopolitan specialities stand out. The benben shrine made the temple a reliquary of the first morning — the cult object was creation's own furniture. And the temple's learned tradition made Iunu a university: the House of Life at Heliopolis is the implied setting for the cosmological speculation this whole article reports, from the Shu speeches to the glosses of Spell 17.

XV. Pithom — Per-Atum, the House of Atum

Atum's second city wears his name on its face: Per-Atum — pr-tm [Gardiner: O1-X1-G17] [𓉐𓏏𓅓], "House (or Estate) of Atum," written with pr [Gardiner: O1] [𓉐], "house," and the t-m root of the god's own name — Greek Pithom, in the eastern Delta in the Wadi Tumilat, the corridor between the Nile and the Red Sea. The site is generally identified with Tell el-Maskhuta, where excavations under the University of Toronto's Wadi Tumilat Project documented a temple of Atum, a major fortified settlement of the Saite and Persian periods, and the canal works that made the wadi a highway between Egypt and the sea. Pithom is named, with Raamses, in Exodus 1:11 among the store-cities built for Pharaoh — a biblical memory this archive records as such, without adjudicating the Exodus narrative, which lies outside its evidence.

The Pithom cult shows Atum in his working clothes. This is not the metaphysical capital but a frontier estate: temple, granaries, garrison, and canal port, where the creator god of Heliopolis presides over stores and borders. The famous Pithom stela of Ptolemy II, from the temple, records royal benefactions to Atum's house in the manner of a temple archive — building, endowment, and festival — evidence that the cult remained a living institution, worth a king's patronage, into the third century BCE. A god whose name means completeness ends up, characteristically, in charge of the inventory.

Per-Atum also clarifies Heliopolis by contrast. Iunu is the Atum of the First Time, the mound, and the Ennead; Per-Atum is the Atum of the estate, the storehouse, and the eastern gate. Egyptian gods characteristically hold both registers at once — the cosmic and the municipal — and their temples administered both. The archive's pantheon survey treats the pattern generally; Pithom is its clearest Atum-shaped instance.

XVI. Atum and Kingship — Father of the King

Egyptian kingship has two divine parents, and Atum is the older. The living king is Horus, Ḥr [Gardiner: G5] [𓅃]; the dead king is Osiris; but the king as son of the creator, issued from the first god's body and embraced by him at death, is Atum's child — the claim the Pyramid Texts make from our earliest evidence (Section IX). Where Horus gives the king an office, Atum gives him a lineage: the throne descends, through the Ennead's generations, from the god who was alone in Nun. Every coronation is therefore a small genealogy recited in action — the new king takes his place at the end of the line that began on the mound.

The instruments of that claim are the ones this archive's Horus article sets out in full: the srḫ [Gardiner: O33] [𓊁] serekh, the fivefold titulary, the "appearance in glory," ḫꜥ [Gardiner: N28] [𓈍], and the Sed festival, ḥb-sd [Gardiner: W3-F33] [𓎱𓄢], at which the ageing king's fitness was re-proved. Atum's specific contributions to the grammar are three. First, the embrace: coronation and funeral alike stage the creator receiving his son, in life as legitimacy and in death as gathering-home. Second, the gift of years: the "millions of years" Atum promises Osiris and the dead in Spell 175's first movement (Section XII) are the same millions kings prayed for at their Sed festivals — duration is Atum's to give because time itself is inside his creation. Third, the setting pattern: a king's reign, like the sun's day (Section VIII), is completed in Atum; the evening god is the patron of every finished reign and the judge of whether it was finished well.

The Opening of the Mouth — wp-r [Gardiner: F13-D21] [𓄋𓂋], with the corrected value wp [Gardiner: F13], "to open" — stages the succession as the myth prescribes: the new Horus-king performs the rite for his dead father as Osiris. Behind that rite stands Atum's older embrace, and the two together form the constitution in ritual form: the son revives the father, the creator receives them both. Kingship in Egypt was never merely inherited. It was re-created, reign by reign, out of the same materials Atum used the first time: a body, a name, a word, and a mound to stand on.

XVII. Re-Atum, Ptah-Atum and the Later Theologies

Re-Atum. The fusion with Ra is as old as our texts: the Pyramid Texts already combine the two, and the Global Egyptian Museum's entry states the outcome — distinct at first, combined from the Old Kingdom into Re-Atum, with the day's phases distributed among Khepri, Ra, and Atum (Section VIII). The fusion costs neither god his identity: Ra brings the visible sun and its order, mꜣꜥt [Gardiner: U5-X1]; Atum brings origin, totality, and the end. A hymn can address Re-Atum as creator in the morning and finisher at dusk without strain, because the compound name is a sentence: the sun, from its source to its completion.

Ptah and Amun. The Memphite theology of the Shabaka Stone makes Ptah the heart-and-tongue creator through whom Atum and the Ennead proceed — a subordination the archive reports as Memphite doctrine, not as Egyptian consensus; see Memphite theology. Theban theology performs the parallel move for Amun, the hidden one who precedes even the Ogdoad. In both cases Atum's position is the measure of the claim: to precede Atum, a theology must place its god before the first thing — which is why later systems define themselves by their relation to him. Atum-Khepri, the address of Utterance 600 (Section IV), is the third great compound: creator and becoming named as one god at the moment of emergence.

In the Late Period and Ptolemaic period Atum persists — in temple relief at Edfu and Dendera, in bronzes like the British Museum's composite figure (Section XIII), in the Pithom temple's royal stela (Section XV) — less as a rival of the great national gods than as the system's memory of its own beginning. Through Hermeticism, which inherited Heliopolitan creation grammar in Greek dress, the figure of the self-created, all-containing first god passed into the late antique imagination — a transmission the archive records generally, without claiming for any Hermetic text a direct quotation of an Egyptian one.

XIX. Esoteric Analysis — Totality Before and After the World

// READING THIS SECTION //

What follows is the archive's interpretive reading of Atum, offered as such. It is not presented as established Egyptological fact, and it rests only on the documented material of the preceding sections: the double sense of tm, the emergence from Nun, the distribution of the One into the Ennead, and the return of Spell 175.

The archive reads Atum as the Egyptian figure of totality that survives its own unfolding. Consider the shape of the whole account. The One does not diminish by becoming many: the Ennead is Atum distributed, not Atum spent, and his headship (Section VII) is the doctrine that the source remains present in everything sourced from it. Nor does the many's eventual dissolution diminish him: Spell 175's ending is not a defeat but a gathering, the finisher drawing his work back into the waters with a companion beside him. Between those poles the alchemical reading this archive develops elsewhere — in Alchemy, where dissolution and coagulation are the Work's two hands — finds in Atum its cosmological warrant stated in a single name: the solve and the coagula are the same god at different hours. Nun is the solvent that never stops existing; the mound is the first coagulation; the evening sun is the daily proof that a completion is a transformation, not a termination.

The sneeze deserves its place in this reading, precisely because it is undignified. A theology that lets its supreme act be a sneeze and a spit — breath and moisture, involuntary and bodily — refuses, at its foundation, to separate spirit from body. The heka, ḥkꜣ [Gardiner: V28-D28-G1] [𓎛𓂓𓄿], by which the world is made is not an abstraction applied to matter from outside; it is what a living body does when it projects its own substance into form. That is the archive's interpretive summary of the Atum material, offered as a reading to be tested against the texts of Section XX: completeness is not the absence of division but the capacity to divide without being lost, and to gather without being diminished.

// THE ATUM PATTERN, AS THE ARCHIVE STATES IT //

Begin from what is already present — creation works with Nun, not against it. Distinguish without severing — Shu separates Geb and Nut and remains their father. Let the work carry your substance — the children are the creator's own body, externalised. Finish what you begin — the evening is an office, not a failure of the morning. Hold the end inside the beginning — the serpent waits inside the king. This is the archive's reading, in the spirit described above.
