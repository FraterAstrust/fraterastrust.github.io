I. The Ontology of the Map — Functional Geography

The Netherworld Books represent the most sophisticated "magical maps" ever produced by the Egyptian priesthood. Found primarily on the walls of New Kingdom royal tombs, these compositions provide a minute-by-minute guide to the journey of the sun god Re through the twelve hours of the night (the Duat [Gardiner: N15]).

In this research framework, these books are viewed as Functional Soul-Software. They do not merely describe a myth; they establish the precise Navigational Frequencies, passwords, and identifications required for the individual consciousness to survive the "Nocturnal Ascent" and achieve transfiguration into an Akh. The Netherworld Books are Iterative Schematics of reality, designed to prevent the "Decoherence" of the soul after the death of the physical vessel.

II. The Duat — The Non-Linear Awareness Field

The Duat is the realm the sun crosses between setting and rising, and the books describe it in geography, not abstraction: a river to be sailed, banks, fields, caverns, mounds, gates and towns, each with named inhabitants. Its regions are watery and dry by turns — the well-watered Wernes and the Waters of Osiris in the early hours of the Amduat, then the parched, sand-choked land of Sokar, where the barque must be towed and the way narrows to zigzag paths in darkness.

It is not, however, one consistent map. Each composition organises the same night differently — the Amduat by twelve hour-regions along the river, the Book of Gates by gated divisions, the Book of Caverns by caverns, the Book of the Earth by the body of the earth god Aker — and the texts were used side by side in the same tombs without any attempt to reconcile them. The Duat also has more than one location at once in Egyptian thought: below the earth, and, in the Books of the Sky, along the body of Nut, through whom the sun travels to its morning birth. The “topography” is therefore a set of functional itineraries, each true for the journey its book performs.

The books also disagree, productively, about where the Duat is. The Amduat sets it below, along a subterranean river. The Books of the Sky (Section XIII) set the same journey inside the body of Nut, overhead. The Book of the Earth sets it within the ground itself, in the body of Aker. Egyptian theology did not choose between these maps; royal tombs of the Ramesside age install several of them in a single monument, on different walls and ceilings of the same descending corridor system.

For the modern reader the lesson is methodological. These are not competing cosmologies awaiting a referee; they are complementary instruments, each built for a different ritual and theological task, and the tomb that holds them all is richer, not more confused, for the plurality.

III. The Amduat — "What is in the Netherworld"

The Amduat (imy-dwꜣt [Gardiner: M17-G17-M17-N15], "That which is in the Underworld") is the foundational text of the corpus. It details the passage of the Solar Barque through twelve distinct regions of the night.

Each hour is a Dimensional Bandwidth with its own geography, inhabitants, and challenges. The text provides the "Names of the Gods" who dwell in each hour—knowledge that is essential for the practitioner to avoid being consumed by the entropic forces of the deep night. The Amduat is a system of Relational Theurgy: the soul must know its position in the cycle to maintain its vector toward rebirth.

IV. The 12 Hours of the Night — Sequential Processing

The Amduat gives the night a fixed architecture of twelve hours, and the pattern it set governs the whole corpus. Each hour has its own name, its region of the Duat, its gate, and its population — gods, the blessed dead, and beings to be repelled — catalogued in three registers around the solar barque at the centre. The first hour is a threshold, the sun entering the western horizon; the second and third cross the watery Wernes and Waters of Osiris; the fourth and fifth enter the realm of Sokar, where Osiris's tomb lies hidden; the sixth brings the midnight union (section V) and the seventh the binding of Apep (section VI). The later hours distribute clothing, provisions and fields to the justified dead, and the twelfth brings the sunrise (section VII).

The earliest complete copy is the one painted in cursive hieroglyphs around the burial chamber of KV34, the tomb of Thutmose III, laid out like an unrolled papyrus on the walls; earlier fragments survive in KV20 and KV38. Thutmose III's version became the reference form of the work — short and long versions circulated — and the twelve-hour scheme it fixed was taken over, with different contents, by the Book of Gates and the Book of the Night.

THE TWELVE HOURS — CONTENTS AFTER HORNUNG (1999)

Hour 1: Entry at the western horizon, the transition between day and night.

Hour 2: The watery region of Wernes; fields allotted to the blessed dead.

Hour 3: The Waters of Osiris; the gods of Osiris's following in their boats.

Hour 4: Imhet — Sokar's sandy realm; zigzag paths; the barque towed on a serpent-boat.

Hour 5: Sokar's mound over the tomb of Osiris; a lake of fire below.

Hour 6: The midnight union — the ba of Ra with the body of Osiris (Section V).

Hour 7: Apep bound on his sandbank by Isis's magic and Serqet's fetters (Section VI).

Hour 8: Doors of the tomb opened; Osiris's enemies burned by the fire-serpent.

Hour 9: The crew rows free of Sokar's island, back into open water.

Hour 10: Regeneration continues in the waters; the drowned are tended.

Hour 11: The god's eyes are fully regenerated; final punishments prepared.

Hour 12: Through the serpent "Life of the Gods" to the eastern horizon (Section VII).

V. The Sixth Hour — The Union of Re and Osiris

The deepest point of the nocturnal journey occurs in the Sixth Hour. This is the moment of the Midnight Union.

HOUR 6: THE UNION

Here, the ba (soul) of Re unites with the body (corpse) of Osiris. In alchemical terms, this is the Coniunctio—the wedding of the solar (spirit) and chthonic (body) principles. This union triggers a theurgical "Flash" that generates the necessary spark for the sun's rebirth. It is the moment when "Potential" and "Actuality" become one, proving that the solar order cannot exist without the fertile depth of the underworld. See The Osiris Template.

VI. The Seventh Hour — The Strategic Defeat of Entropy

Immediately following the union, the system faces its greatest challenge in the Seventh Hour. The serpent Apep waits on his sandbank, on which the barque risks running aground — the threat is grounding and stoppage, the river's flow arrested at the journey's most fragile point.

This is the Entropic Crisis. Apep is subdued by the magic spells of Isis and fettered by the scorpion goddess Serqet, with Set's strength assisting: bound, speared, and cut, he is held while the barque passes. This hour represents the moment in any transformative work where the "Fuel" (the water) seems to vanish, and the operator must rely on Pure Magical Will to maintain the trajectory. The Amduat provides the specific "Words of Power" needed to bind the serpent and restore the flow of time.

VII. The Twelfth Hour — Total System Re-initialization

The journey ends as it must: in birth. In the twelfth hour of the Amduat, the solar barque is towed toward the eastern horizon through the body of a great serpent — “Life of the Gods” — entering at its tail and emerging from its mouth, the passage itself performing the renewal. The god who went down as the aged evening sun, and who was joined in the sixth hour to the corpse of Osiris, comes out changed in form: Khepri, the scarab, the morning sun in the act of becoming.

The barque then rises into the day sky, received by Shu and the gods of the eastern horizon, while the Duat closes behind it and its inhabitants sink back into darkness until the next night. Rebirth in the Amduat is not a metaphor laid over the journey; it is its mechanism and its stated purpose. The dead king, united with Re in the barque, undergoes the same renewal: what the sun does nightly, the justified dead are promised as their own future.

VIII. The Book of Gates — Guardians and Jurisdictions

The Book of Gates (sbḫwt [Gardiner: S29-D58-Aa1-G43-X1-Z2]) appeared in the late 18th Dynasty (Horemheb) and emphasized the Boundaries between the hours.

Each hour is separated by a Gate guarded by a specific serpent and a group of "Terrible Ones." This text reframes the journey as a series of Legal and Vibrational Jurisdictions. To pass each gate, the soul must prove its fitness and its knowledge of the "Secret Name" of the guardian. The Book of Gates also includes the Hall of Judgment directly within the path, making ethical alignment a physical prerequisite for further descent.

IX. The Secret Names — Vibrational Key Protocols

The Book of Gates builds its journey out of divisions separated by gates, each gate watched by a serpent guardian and attendants, and passage is a matter of knowledge. Throughout the netherworld corpus the same rule operates: to know a being's name is to have standing before it — to address it, command it, or pass it — and the books exist in large part to supply those names: the names of gates, guardians, ferrymen, and of the hours and regions themselves. The Amduat's stated usefulness, in its own introduction, is precisely this knowledge, for the dead and for the one who knows it on earth.

The Book of Gates is first attested in KV57, the tomb of Horemheb, where its second to sixth hours occupy the burial chamber, and its first complete version was carved on the alabaster sarcophagus of Seti I. The gate structure makes explicit what the whole corpus assumes: the Duat is administered. Nothing in it is passed anonymously, and the traveller who cannot name what confronts him does not pass.

X. The Hall of Judgment in the Book of Gates

The Book of Gates sets its judgment scene at the end of the fifth hour, in the middle of the journey rather than at its destination. Osiris presides enthroned over his court; before him stands the balance, and the dead are weighed and assessed as the solar barque passes through the hall. In KV57, Horemheb's tomb, the Book of Gates material (its second to sixth hours) occupies the burial chamber itself, so the judgment stands in the room of the king's own burial. A more specific claim sometimes repeated — that the scene was placed directly above the sarcophagus — could not be verified against Hornung's published account in this archive's accuracy pass, and is not asserted here.

The verdict divides the company. The justified are provided for — fed, clothed and assigned their place among the blessed, who in the wider corpus go on to Aaru, the Field of Reeds — while the condemned are handed over to punishment and destruction: in the Gates' imagery, bound enemies and the fiery places of annihilation that the later hours display. Judgment here is not a private ordeal appended to the journey, as it stands in the Book of the Dead's Spell 125; it is a station of the sun's own road, administered nightly, in public, as part of the order of the Duat.

XI. The Book of Caverns — The Psychological Depths

The Book of Caverns (ḳrrwt [Gardiner: N29-D21-D21-G43-X1-Z2]) is the most "Chthonic" of the Netherworld books. It focuses on the Psychological Shadow.

The Book of Caverns is organised not in twelve hours but in six sections, named for the caverns (ḳrrwt) through which the sun passes. Its earliest version is in the Osireion at Abydos, the cenotaph of Seti I, and its first complete version covers the tomb of Ramesses VI (KV9). It describes the souls of the damned trapped in "caverns of fire" and the "corpses" of the gods awaiting the solar light. This text is an Audit of the Unconscious. It requires the practitioner to confront the parts of the self that have been "de-linked" from Ma'at. The solar light does not just pass through; it "visits" these caverns, temporarily animating the occupants and proving that even the deepest darkness is subject to the solar frequency.

XII. The Book of the Earth — Reclaiming the Corpse

The Book of the Earth — also called the Creation of the Sun Disc, or the Book of Aker — is the latest and least linear of the great compositions. Its subject is the sun's passage through the realm of Aker, the earth god shown as a double sphinx, within whom the solar disc is formed and from whom it is born. Around that central image the book arranges, in parts modern editors label A to E, the tomb of Osiris guarded by serpents, the punishment of the damned, the twelve goddesses of the hours, and the emergence of the disc raised into the morning sky.

Elements of the composition appear in the Osireion at Abydos, the cenotaph of Seti I, and its principal surviving copy covers the walls of the burial chamber of KV9, the tomb of Ramesses VI, with further copies under Merneptah, Twosret, Ramesses III and Ramesses VII. Where the Amduat travels a river, the Book of the Earth stays with the earth itself: the corpse in the ground is not an obstacle to the sun's renewal but its site — the disc is created inside the earth, among the dead, and rises out of it.

XIII. The Books of the Sky — The Nut Schematic

A second family of compositions moves the journey out of the earth and onto the sky. The Books of the Sky — the Book of Nut, the Book of the Day and the Book of the Night — share one image: the body of the sky goddess Nut, arched over the world, who swallows the sun at evening and gives birth to it at dawn. The Book of Nut, titled in its tradition the Fundamentals of the Course of the Stars, sets the decanal stars along her body and explains their risings, settings and seventy days of invisibility; its fullest copy is in the cenotaph of Seti I at Abydos (the Osireion), with a Roman-period commentary, preserved on papyrus, still expounding it more than a thousand years later.

The Book of the Day follows the sun's daytime voyage along Nut's body; the Book of the Night follows the nocturnal voyage through her, divided into its twelve hours. From Ramesses IV onward the two were set together on royal tomb ceilings, and in the burial chamber of KV9 (Ramesses VI) they appear complete as a pair: two figures of Nut back to back, Day on one side and Night on the other, the whole circuit of the sun drawn on a single ceiling. The Duat of these books is not under the earth but inside the goddess — the same journey, mapped on a different body.

XIV. Enigmatic Writing — High-Level Encryption

In the royal tombs (particularly Seti I and Ramesses VI), the Netherworld Books are often written in Enigmatic or Cryptographic Scripts.

This was an Access Control Protocol. The most potent navigational data was encrypted so that only a prepared initiate (Sesh) could decode it. The act of "reading" these scripts was itself a form of Frequency Calibration—the effort required to decode the message "tuned" the mind of the reader to the specific vibration of the hour described. XV. Architectural Implementation — Biblical-Scale Data Displays

The Netherworld Books have no existence apart from their medium: they were composed for the walls of royal tombs, and the tomb is part of their meaning. In the Valley of the Kings the texts are laid out in journey order along the descending corridors — the visitor, and the dead king, travel the night by travelling the tomb — until the burial chamber, the deepest room, holds the journey's centre or its completion: the Amduat unrolled around KV34, the Book of Gates programme in the burial chamber of KV57, the Books of the Day and Night on the ceiling of KV9.

The corpus grew tomb by tomb. Seti I's KV17 set the encyclopaedic standard, gathering several books in one monument; Ramesside tombs combined Amduat, Gates, Caverns, Earth and the Books of the Sky in a single descending programme. Because the medium was a sealed royal tomb, the books survived where papyrus copies have perished — and because it was a tomb, they were written for an audience of gods and one dead king, a fact that governed both their content and their circulation (section XVI).

XVI. Restricted Access — The Knowledge of the Few

In the New Kingdom these books were royal texts. The Amduat remained almost exclusively a composition for kings — found in royal tombs and on royal sarcophagi — until the Twenty-first Dynasty, and the other books of the corpus follow the same pattern. Their setting enforced the restriction: inscribed in tombs that were sealed at burial, in a necropolis closed to the unentitled, they were not available to be read, copied or consulted by anyone outside the small circle of priests, draftsmen and officials who produced them.

The books say as much about themselves. The compositions carry secrecy formulae: the Amduat's introduction presents its contents as the hidden knowledge of the Duat, effective for the one who knows it — on earth as in the necropolis — and the corpus repeatedly frames its names and images as concealed things, not to be shown to the multitude. Only after the New Kingdom does the material begin to move outward, onto the coffins and mythological papyri of high priestly families in the Twenty-first Dynasty — the first step in the long diffusion that eventually put extracts of the royal books into private hands.

XVII. Heka as Navigational Engine — Practical Application

For the practitioner, the Netherworld Books serve as Initiatory Templates. By mentally "tracing" the solar path and internalizing the guardians and passwords, the initiate prepares their own Ba for the eventual transition.

This is the practice of Internal Navigation: using the ancient maps to identify the entropic forces (Apep) and the regenerative centers (Osiris) within one's own awareness field. Success in this internal journey is the prerequisite for the external transfiguration into light. The books are not "mythology"; they are Operational Manuals for the Soul.

XX. Ethics of Knowledge — The Responsibility of the Map

To possess the maps of the Netherworld is to take on a profound Ethical Responsibility. The Egyptians believed that knowing the names of the gods and the passwords of the gates granted the soul immense power.

The corpus's own ethic is stated in its judgment scenes (Sections X and XXI): knowledge is weighed, not merely possessed. The traveller who knows every name in the Duat and fails the balance is destroyed with the ignorant; the books never promise that information substitutes for justification.

Misuse of this power—attempting to navigate the Duat for selfish or chaotic ends—was viewed as an act of Isfet. The "Justified" soul is one whose Knowledge is balanced by Character. The map is useless if the heart is too heavy to carry it. The ultimate lesson of the Netherworld Books is that the path to rebirth is built upon the foundation of Ma'at; the map only functions for the one who has already become the territory.

XVIII. The Litany of Ra — The Companion Composition

No account of the netherworld books is complete without the composition that so often accompanies them. The Litany of Ra invokes the sun god in seventy-five forms, and is first known from the burial chamber of Thutmose III and the tomb of his vizier Useramun — the same milieu as the first complete Amduat.

Where the Amduat is a journey, the Litany is a naming. Form after form of the god is called and figured, and the reciting king identifies himself with each in turn. Set beside the hour-books in the same tombs, it supplies what they presuppose: complete knowledge of who the traveller-god is, in every shape the night requires.

Hornung (The Ancient Egyptian Books of the Afterlife, 1999) treats the Litany as the Amduat's companion piece, and the pairing is the New Kingdom royal tomb's basic library in miniature: a map of the road, and a directory of the god who travels it.

XIX. The Enigmatic Netherworld Books — Solar-Osirian Unity

A distinct group of compositions pushes the corpus's secrecy to its limit. The Enigmatic Netherworld Books, edited by John Coleman Darnell (The Enigmatic Netherworld Books of the Solar-Osirian Unity, 2004), are written in cryptographic hieroglyphs and appear on the gilded shrines of Tutankhamun and in the tombs of Ramesses VI and Ramesses IX.

Their subject is the mystery at the corpus's centre, stated in Section V: the union of Ra and Osiris, here explored through deliberately recondite writing — sportive sign values, acrophony, and the consonantal principle (see Enigmatic Writing). The encryption is not decoration. A text about the hidden union is itself hidden, legible only to the reader who already holds the keys.

Darnell's edition established these compositions as a corpus in their own right, and they mark the point where the netherworld books and the cryptographic tradition fully merge: content, script, and secrecy doctrine in a single artefact.

XXI. Punishment and Annihilation — The Fiery Places

The netherworld books are unsparing about the other outcome. Alongside the fields and provisions of the justified stand the places of annihilation: pits of fire tended by knife- and flame-bearing goddesses, cauldrons in the Book of Caverns in which the enemies of order are cooked, and the bound, beheaded figures of the condemned in the later hours of the Amduat and the Book of Gates.

The punishment is ontological, not merely painful. What is burned is the whole person — corpse, ba, shadow, and name — so that the condemned do not suffer forever but cease: the second death the Book of the Dead fears, administered nightly as part of the sun's government of the Duat.

In this research framework, the fiery places complete the corpus's moral physics. The Duat is not a neutral landscape with a judgment appended; judgment, provision, and annihilation are its standing institutions, operating every night whether or not any particular traveller attends to them.

XXII. Transmission — From Royal Walls to Priestly Coffins

For two centuries the netherworld books were a royal monopoly of the tomb wall (Section XVI). The monopoly broke in the Twenty-first Dynasty, when the Theban priestly families who then governed the south began to place extracts and adaptations of the royal books on their own coffins and on mythological papyri.

The most famous of these adaptations is the papyrus of the chantress Tayuheret and the related mythological papyri, in which scenes from the Amduat and the Books of the Sky are redrawn for private owners. Extracts travelled further still: onto sarcophagi of later periods, into temple contexts, and, in abbreviated vignettes, into the Book of the Dead tradition itself.

The transmission matters for how the books are read now. What survives is a double record: the sealed royal originals, complete and in place, and a wider, fragmentary afterlife of excerpts — the first draft, in effect, of every later attempt, including this archive's, to make the maps public.

XXIV. The Solar Barque and Its Crew

At the centre of every hour stands the solar barque, and it is never empty or uncrewed. In the Amduat's central register the ram-headed night form of the sun god stands in a shrine on deck, protected by the coiled Mehen serpent, while the crew that works the boat is named figure by figure: Wepwawet at the prow, who opens the ways; Sia, perception, at the god's side; Heka, magic itself, crewing the vessel whose journey is his element; and the towing gods and goddesses who haul the barque when the river fails, above all in Sokar's dry realm.

The crew list is a theology in personnel form. The god does not travel by omnipotence: he travels by competences — way-opening, perception, magic, and hauling power — each embodied, named, and positioned. To know the crew, as the books teach their names hour by hour, is to know what capacities a successful passage requires.

The dead king sails in this company, not as cargo but as crew and as the god's companion; the tomb versions place his figure and his names inside the journey. The barque is therefore also the corpus's model of the justified community: a vessel in which gods, powers, and the transfigured dead each hold an assigned station, and in which no one travels alone.

In this research framework, the barque is the netherworld books' answer to the solitary-soul picture of later religions. Salvation here is crewed, scheduled, and collective — a night shift worked by a named company, in which the traveller's task is to take his station and hold it until the eastern horizon.

XXV. Reading the Registers — Image and Text Together

The netherworld books are not illustrated texts; they are image-text machines in which neither element is complete without the other. Each hour of the Amduat is laid out in three horizontal registers: the solar barque in the middle, and the beings of that hour above and below, every figure captioned with its name and a word of explanation, and the whole hour framed by an introduction and a closing text.

The layout is the meaning's skeleton. Above and below are banks of the river: what the god passes, blesses, judges, or destroys is placed in spatial relation to his course, so that the tomb wall reads as a chart to be travelled with the eye. In KV34 the effect is deliberate and total — the burial chamber's walls are treated as the leaves of a giant papyrus, the book unrolled around the king's sarcophagus.

Modern editions (Hornung's above all) reproduce this layout hour by hour, and reading them in facsimile, rather than in excerpted translation, is the single best discipline this corpus offers a student: the books were composed for walls, and they still explain themselves best at wall scale.

XXVI. The Book of Caverns — Division by Division

Section XI stated the book's frame; this section walks its six divisions in order. The Book of Caverns bears no ancient title — its modern name comes from the ḳrrwt [Gardiner: N29-D21-D21-G43-X1-Z2], the caverns or vaults in which its beings are enclosed — and its organisation is unlike the hour-books. Six divisions, grouped in two halves of three, are framed on the wall by two large figures of the ram-headed sun god; the divisions do not correspond to hours, and the "caverns" through which Ra passes are traditionally counted as twelve, not six. Daniel A. Werning's critical edition (2011) registers thirteen text witnesses, from the Osireion and the Ramesside royal tombs to the complete Late Period copy in the tomb of the lector priest Pediamenopet (TT 33) and extracts on papyri and sarcophagi.

First division. Ra descends and negotiates entry: the upper register shows him persuading the serpent doorkeepers who guard the doors of the necropolis, while the corpse of Osiris lies displayed in his shrine, guarded by serpents, with the guardian gods of the Ennead in attendance. Ra and Osiris are at this point pointedly separate — visitor and visited — and below the shrine the beheaded enemies of the god already announce the book's second theme.

Second division. Ten scenes continue the negotiation with the serpent keepers and carry the narrative to the death of Osiris. In the first two divisions the book's format is distinctive: text and picture are kept apart, the long speeches of Ra — in which he invokes, one by one, the beings depicted — being inscribed as a continuous monologue after the representations they govern. The sun god of this book is above all a speaker: his light arrives in the caverns in the form of address.

Third division. With entry achieved, thirteen scenes show Ra inside the realm, and the book's central process begins: the disc of Ra is set above the figure of Osiris, the first pictorial statement of the union that the Amduat reserves for its sixth hour (Section V). From here to the end, solar and Osirian are shown progressively merged. The lower registers of the third and fourth divisions give the counterweight — the punishment of the damned, bound, head-down, and consigned to the cauldrons of the places of annihilation (Section XXI).

Fourth division. Nine scenes gather the gods of the Osirian circle around the passing sun — Isis, Nephthys, Anubis, and Horus among them — the family of the dead god attending the visitor who is becoming him. Throughout these middle divisions the beings of the caverns are drawn enclosed in ovals: coffin-like vaults in which gods, corpses, and the damned alike lie waiting for the passage of the light, each oval a small tomb that the sun's address opens for a moment and closes again.

Fifth division. The turn toward rebirth. The second half of the book is introduced by litanies — thirteen precede this division alone — and its scenes bring in Nut, the sky who bears the sun, alongside the reassembled body of Osiris: the corpse that lay in pieces under guard in the first division is here whole, and the presence of Nut imports the Books of the Sky's machinery of birth into the deepest of the underworld books. Sixth division. Scarabs and sun discs rise out of mounds in the ground, and the mummified, ram-headed figure in whom Ra and Osiris are one makes the union visible in a single body. The solar barque, absent from the whole book until now, appears only in the final tableau, where Ra is reborn in his successive forms — child, scarab, and ram-headed god — at the eastern edge of the night.

The editions track the book's difficulty. Champollion described the KV9 copy in his letters from Thebes without yet having a name for it; the Osireion version, uncovered by Margaret Murray and Flinders Petrie in 1902–03, was first taken for part of the Book of Gates on the opposite wall. Piankoff published the first full translations (French, 1942–45; English, 1954), Hornung a German version in 1972, and Werning's two-volume edition of 2011 — built on all thirteen witnesses — is now the reference text, the first to treat the book as a composition in its own right rather than an appendix to its neighbours.

In this research framework, the Caverns is the most interior of the books: where the Amduat charts a river and the Gates a jurisdiction, the Caverns charts a series of sealed containers, each opened by a voice. Its doctrine is stated in its format — nothing in the deep places is reached by travel; everything is reached by address, and the light that revives the dead is a light that speaks their names.

XXVII. The Book of the Earth — Part by Part

Section XII introduced the Book of the Earth; this section follows the arrangement modern editors give it. A caution first: unlike the hour-books, the Earth has no surviving ancient sequence. Its scenes are known chiefly from the walls of the burial chamber of Ramesses VI (KV9), where they face right and are read right to left — the reverse of the usual orientation — and the division into parts lettered A to E is Alexandre Piankoff's editorial construction from that wall, refined by Hornung. Each panel is organised in three registers. What follows is the composition as the KV9 wall presents it, part by part.

Part D is in all likelihood the beginning, and holds the bulk of the composition. Its presiding scene is the tomb of Osiris: the god's corpse within a shrine guarded by serpents, Anubis and a second god stretching protective arms over the body, with punishment scenes — gods of the place of annihilation at their cauldrons — flanking the renewal on both sides. Near it stands the book's signature image: the mummy of the sun god upright upon a great disc, the disc cradled by two pairs of arms rising out of the depths of Nun, and around the whole a wreath of twelve stars and twelve small discs marking the course of the hours. Part D's middle register gives the generation of Horus — the falcon god rising from a figure called the Western One, seven mounds each containing a god, and Horus emerging from the body of Osiris, which the corpses of Isis and Nephthys protect. Its final scene sets the solar barque upon Aker, the double sphinx who is the earth itself become a vessel, with Khepri and Thoth praying within it. The bottom register closes the part in the Place of Annihilation: a great sarcophagus holding the corpse Re addresses as the "corpse of Shetit" — the Mysterious One — and, at the very end, the serpent Apophis seized by ram-headed gods.

Part C, in three registers, is probably continuous with D. It opens with the ram-headed sun god greeted by two ba-birds and by gods who stretch out their hands toward the disc in protection; from that gesture of shielding the falcon head of "Horus of the netherworld" is projected — the heir generated, once again, out of an act of guardianship over the sun's body.

Part B is the book's meditation on breath and enclosure. Four ovals hold mummies who are able to breathe because the rays of the sun god reach them; four burial mounds stand overturned under serpent guard. Its central figure is a standing mummy named, in the captions' paraphrase, the corpse of the god — who is himself the sun disc; before him a serpent rises from a pair of arms holding up a god and goddess in praise, and behind him a second pair, the "arms of darkness," is borne up by the crocodile Penwenti. Below, barques carry the mummies of Osiris and of the falcon-headed Horus: the dead gods in transit inside the earth that is gestating their sunrise.

Part A opens at the Mound of Darkness, where the sun god lies enclosed by mummies with the solar barque above the mound, and Aker's double-sphinx body frames the barque between the entrance and the exit of the realm of the dead. There follows the resurrection of the sun's corpse — the scene that recurs in royal sarcophagus chambers — in which a falcon head emerges from a sun disc and its light falls upon the "mysterious corpse" lying below. Twelve goddesses of the hours, each marked with the signs of star and shadow and a beaming disc above her, attend the process; a central god, generally taken for Osiris, stands surrounded by the corpses of Shu, Tefnut, Khepri, and Nun — the first generation of creation present as dead witnesses at the re-creation. In the middle register the barque is towed by fourteen ram-headed gods, and the part's upper register ends with a pair of arms rising from the depths while a goddess named Annihilator reaches to embrace a sun disc, the whole supported by two praying goddesses, West and East, in reversed orientation. Part E, the smallest fragment, shows six gods praying to a sun disc at burial mounds; it is generally held not to be the composition's opening.

The scholarship of the book mirrors its fragmentation. Champollion first published the KV9 scenes in his Monuments de l'Égypte; Piankoff's La Création du Disque Solaire (1953) gave it its alternative name, the Creation of the Sun Disc, and its first systematic reading; B. H. Stricker proposed in 1963 that the whole be read as a divine embryology — the sun gestated in the earth as in a womb — an interpretation later scholarship treats as a lens, not a key. Hornung's account, on which this section chiefly rests, presents the Earth as the deliberate counterpart of the Caverns: the one book follows the sun through the caverns of the dead, the other stays with the body in the ground and watches the disc being made there.

In this research framework, that is the Book of the Earth's contribution to the corpus's physics. The Amduat's sun is reborn by travelling; the Earth's sun is reborn by being formed — assembled inside Aker from corpse, disc, arms, and light, in the same ground where the king's own body lay. The tomb that carries the book is not illustrating the doctrine. It is claiming to be its location.
