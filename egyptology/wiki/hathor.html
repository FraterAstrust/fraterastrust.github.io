I. The Name and Nature of Hathor — The House of Horus

Hathor (Ancient Egyptian: ḥwt-ḥrw, "The House of Horus") is one of the most multi-faceted and consistently venerated goddesses in Kemet. Her name identifies her as the celestial space — the womb of the sky — that contains and protects the sun god Horus. She is the divine feminine principle in its nurturing, creative, and erotic aspects.

Depicted as a cow, a woman with cow's horns and a sun disk, or a stylized female face with cow's ears, Hathor represents the underlying harmonic resonance of the universe. She is the goddess of love, beauty, music, and the western horizon where the day ends and the soul begins its nocturnal journey.

The name is a sentence. ḥwt [Gardiner: V28-G43-X1] [𓎛𓅱𓏏] is a house, a mansion, a temple enclosure; ḥr [Gardiner: G5] [𓅃] is Horus, the falcon god of the sky and of kingship. Hathor is the House of Horus — the sky that contains the falcon as he crosses it, and, in the theology of kingship, the womb and the throne-room of every living king, who is Horus in office. She is therefore older in function than any single story told about her: before she acts in a myth, she is the space in which the sun god exists.

Her titles map the range. She is Lady of the Sky and Mistress of the Stars; the Golden One, from nbw [Gardiner: S12], gold, the metal Egyptian theology took to be the flesh of the gods; Lady of Turquoise in the mines (Section VIII); Lady of the West in the cemeteries (Section XI); Mistress of Music at the head of every temple orchestra (Section IX); and, in the Book of the Heavenly Cow, the first form of the Eye of Ra sent against mankind (Sections IV–V). Geraldine Pinch's standard survey of her cult lists her aspects as protector of women in childbirth, patroness of lovers, mistress of music, goddess of the distant places from which incense, turquoise, and gold are obtained, tree goddess of the dead, and celestial cow — and even that catalogue, drawn from the British Museum's collections, reads like the table of contents of several goddesses.

The archive's working rule for Hathor, applied throughout this article, is the one the evidence itself imposes: she is not a bundle of unrelated portfolios but a single figure whose centre is contained, generative power — the sky holding the sun, the cow holding the calf, the mother holding the king, the flooded field of beer holding the raging Eye until it cools. Every section below is that one idea at a different address.

II. The Image of Hathor — Cow, Face, Ears, and Horns

Hathor has three canonical images, and Egyptian art uses all three, sometimes in the same temple, for three thousand years. The first is the cow: a complete bovine, gentle-faced, wearing between her horns a sun disk and often a mnit [Gardiner: G17-N35-M17-X1] menat counterpoise at her neck, standing in a thicket of papyrus or emerging from the western hillside. In this form she suckles the king, licks his hand in affection (Section XV), receives the dead, and carries the sun. It is the oldest of her forms and the one that never goes out of use.

The second is the woman: a beautiful female figure in a long dress and broad collar, wearing on a modius or directly on her wig the pair of lyre-shaped cow's horns cradling a sun disk — the headdress that Isis would later borrow entire (Isis, Section XXI). She carries a papyrus sceptre, a sistrum, or a menat, and she is the form in which kings embrace her and in which she plays, in temple relief, the wife, mother, and daughter of the gods of each local triad.

The third is the strangest and the most recognisable: the Hathor face — a woman's face shown full-front, framed by a heavy wig, with the ears of a cow. Frontal images are rare in Egyptian art, which works in profile by deep convention; the Hathor face breaks the convention because it is not a portrait but a presence, made to meet the worshipper's eyes. It crowns the handle of the naos sistrum, stares from the capitals of her temple columns at Dendera and Deir el-Bahari, and appears on mirrors, cosmetic spoons, faience votive plaques, and the little "Hathor masks" dedicated by women at her shrines. To be looked at by the goddess, levelly and without turning away, was itself one of her gifts.

A scholarly disentangling belongs in this section. The frontal face with cow's ears also belongs, in the earliest periods, to Bat, a distinct cow goddess of the seventh Upper Egyptian nome, whose emblem adds horns curving inward at the tips. The faces at the top corners of the Narmer Palette are most often read as Hathor, with Bat also proposed; by the Middle Kingdom Bat has been largely absorbed into Hathor, her name surviving chiefly in place and title. The archive keeps the two apart where the sources do, and notes the absorption where they do not.

Her remaining forms are functional transformations. As a lioness she is the Eye at war (Section IV). As a sycamore tree she feeds the dead (Section XIII). As a uraeus cobra she rides the brow of Ra as his fiery defender. As the celestial cow of Section VII her body is the sky itself, starred along the belly, the sun sailing beneath her. Egyptian iconography is a filing system, and Hathor's file is thick because her functions were: every form is a docket for one of the sections that follow.

III. Hathor Through Three Millennia — From Bat to the Mammisi

Cow goddesses are among the first divine images Egypt produced. Predynastic palettes and pottery carry bovine faces and horned heads centuries before the pyramids, in a Nile valley where cattle were wealth, food, and calendar at once. Hathor herself is named from the earliest dynasties, and by the Old Kingdom she is fully installed in the machinery of kingship: on the famous triads from Menkaure's valley temple at Giza (Fourth Dynasty), the king stands flanked by Hathor — holding his hand in protection — and by a nome goddess, the divine county made female. The message of the group is constitutional: the king rules between the sky goddess and the land.

The Middle Kingdom takes her abroad and underground at once. Abroad, the royal mining expeditions to Sinai build and endow her temple at Serabit el-Khadim under Amenemhat III and his successors (Section VIII). At home, the reunified kingdom's Theban kings honour her in the cliff bay of Deir el-Bahari, where a Hathor shrine already serves the necropolis she governs as Lady of the West. The Coffin Texts know her; the love of provincial elites for her image, on mirrors and sistra, is one of the period's signatures.

The New Kingdom is her flood tide. Hatshepsut builds her the great chapel of Deir el-Bahari, with its Hathor-headed columns and its reliefs of the cow licking the royal hand and suckling the royal child (Section XV). Thutmose III's shrine at the same site housed the magnificent freestanding statue of Hathor as a cow, papyrus around her head, sheltering and suckling Amenhotep II — now in the Egyptian Museum, Cairo. The workmen of Deir el-Medina make her, with Meretseger of the Peak, a patroness of their village; the Theban tombs fill with her image emerging from the western mountain. She is, for five centuries, close to the default form of the divine feminine in Upper Egypt.

The first millennium rearranges rather than diminishes her. Her birth houses, the mammisi, rise beside the great temples to celebrate the birth of the local child god — at Dendera her own son Ihy, the sistrum-player. The Ptolemies rebuild Dendera itself on the grandest scale (Section XIV). At the same time Isis is absorbing her headdress, her nursing image, and eventually much of her popular cult; the two goddesses do not so much compete as merge at the edges, Hathor keeping the music, the mines, the necropolis, and Dendera, Isis taking the universal mission. The last Hathor temples were still being decorated when Rome ruled Egypt — a working life, as a named and templed goddess, of well over three thousand years.

IV. The Eye of Ra — Power and Pacification

Hathor functions as the primary Eye of Ra — the active, protective, and sometimes destructive force of the sun god. In this role, she is the defender of Ma'at. Hathor and Sekhmet are distinct goddesses, though both can embody the Eye of Ra; in the Book of the Heavenly Cow, the goddess Ra sends against rebellious mankind is Hathor, and it is in the course of the slaughter that the text has Sekhmet "come into being."

The Eye itself is irt [Gardiner: D4-X1] [𓁹𓏏] — the word is spelled with the eye sign D4 and the feminine ending, never with D8, which writes a different word. The Eye is the sun's own power detached and personified as his daughter: it goes out from him, acts at a distance, defends him as the uraeus on his brow, and must, when it rages, be brought home again. Hathor, Sekhmet, Tefnut, Bastet, and a dozen local lioness and cobra goddesses can each be the Eye; the office is one, the holders are many, and the archive's articles on each goddess keep her own dossier separate, as the sources do.

THE WRATHFUL ASPECT

When the Eye becomes enraged, she represents uncontrolled entropic force. This aspect must be pacified through ritual, music, and the "offering of the sistrum" to return the goddess to her benevolent, joyful state.

THE LADY OF TURQUOISE

As nb.t mfkꜣt, "Lady of Turquoise," Hathor presided over the turquoise mines of Serabit el-Khadim in Sinai, where her temple stood and expeditions invoked her by that very title. The green stone was believed to be a physical crystallization of her life-giving power, used in amulets to attract fertility and renewal.

V. The Book of the Heavenly Cow — Destruction and Withdrawal

The Book of the Heavenly Cow is the one great myth in which Hathor is the protagonist, and it survives not on papyrus in a library but on the walls and furniture of kings. Its earliest witness is the incomplete copy inscribed inside the outermost gilded shrine of Tutankhamun; its fullest versions fill a side chamber of the tomb of Seti I and appear again in the tombs of Ramesses II and Ramesses III, with a shorter passage under Ramesses VI. All the surviving copies are New Kingdom; Nadine Guilhou's study of the myth in the UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology argues for a redaction between the beginning of the Eighteenth Dynasty and the Amarna period, and the archive reports the dating question in that form rather than assigning the composition to a remoter age on no surviving evidence.

The first movement is the Destruction of Mankind, told in full — with the verified details of Ra's stratagem, the red ochre from Elephantine, the 7,000 jars, and the field flooded three palms deep — in Section XIX below, where the text is quoted in Erik Hornung's translation. The points of discipline bear repeating at the head of the story: the stratagem is Ra's alone; Thoth plays no part in it; and the goddess sent out is Hathor, in whose slaughter the text has Sekhmet "come into being." Any retelling — including, formerly, other pages of this archive — that gives the beer to Thoth or the mission from the outset to Sekhmet has conflated this book with the Distant Goddess cycle of Section VI, where Thoth's persuasion genuinely belongs.

The second movement is quieter and stranger. Ra, weary of ruling a mankind that plots against him, withdraws. Nut — and here the archive insists on the distinction the tomb paintings insist on — takes the form of the great celestial cow and lifts the sun god on her back, her legs trembling at the height until Ra has Shu stand beneath her belly and the eight Heh gods support her legs. The picture in Seti I's tomb, the star-spangled cow upheld by gods with the solar boats sailing beneath her body, gives the book its modern name. The cow of the image is Nut; the cow goddess of the cult, Hathor, is the Eye of the book's first movement. Later theology merges the two bovine skies freely, and Hathor's own celestial-cow form (Section VII) grows in that merger's shade — but in the text itself the roles are cast separately, and this article casts them so.

The withdrawal reorganises the world. Ra settles the order of the new, distanced cosmos: the Field of Offerings is created for the blessed dead, Geb inherits the earthly rule of the gods' generation, and Thoth is appointed the sun's deputy in the night sky, to keep watch and record while Ra is absent — the one genuine Thoth role in the book, administrative rather than alchemical. The text then turns, in its final part, to spells and to the power of magic itself, as if to hand the reader the tools of the world the story has just made: a cosmos whose god is now distant, whose order must be maintained by rite, and whose dangers arrive, as the Eye arrived, from inside the divine household itself.

VI. The Myth of the Distant Goddess

In this foundational myth, the Eye of Ra — the goddess as raging lioness — quarrels with her father and flees into the Nubian desert. Ra sends Shu and Thoth to bring her home. No trick with drink is used: Thoth, in baboon form, wins her over slowly, entertaining her with animal fables, music, and flattery until her anger cools and she agrees to return. (The red-dyed beer belongs to a different story — the Book of the Heavenly Cow, in which Ra floods the fields with beer dyed red like blood to stop the Eye's slaughter of mankind in Egypt itself.)

The fullest surviving telling of the cycle is not a temple inscription but a storybook: the Demotic Myth of the Sun's Eye, preserved principally on Papyrus Leiden I 384 with a companion fragment, Papyrus Lille 31, in manuscripts of the second century CE. There the goddess is in Nubia in the form of a cat — the text's Nubian or Ethiopian cat — and the envoy sent after her is Thoth in his small, clever form, who holds her, dialogue by dialogue, through fables of animals, arguments about justice and homesickness, and descriptions of the festival waiting for her in Egypt, until her anger spends itself and she consents to the journey home. The temple tradition that sends Shu with Thoth is a second layer beside the Leiden letter, and this archive keeps the two apart, as its Tefnut article sets out at length: in that article's dossier Tefnut is the cycle's central holder of the Eye, with Hathor the other great name the returning goddess bears. The identity rotates; the plot — flight south, persuasion by word and music, return timed to the rising flood and the New Year — does not.

A second retrieval tradition belongs in the margin. At Leontopolis and in the Nubian temples the goddess who brings the Eye home is paired with a hunter-consort: Shu in his form of Onuris-Anhur, "he who brings back the distant one," whose very name is the plot of this myth compressed into a title, and whose consort Mehit, the northern lioness, is one of the Eye's own local bodies. The cycle was not a fixed scripture but a travelling company — goddess, envoy, hunter, desert, river, festival — that each temple cast from its own pantheon. What no version does is end the exile by force or by drink. The beer is in the other book (Section V), and the discipline of keeping the two apart is this archive's standing correction to a century of conflated retellings.

Pacified, she reverts to the joyful Hathor and returns to Egypt in a great procession, her homecoming celebrated with music and drinking festivals along the way. Esoterically, this myth represents the transmutation of base desire and aggression into refined creative energy — an alchemical shift from Nigredo to Rubedo.

VII. Sky Goddess and Royal Mother — The Golden One

Beneath all her offices Hathor is a sky. Her name makes her the container of Horus the falcon, who is the sun in transit; in her celestial-cow form her body arches over the earth starred along the belly, her four legs the pillars at the world's corners, and the sun is born from her each morning and carried in her each night. The image must be distinguished, as Section V insists, from Nut's: Nut is the sky of the cosmogonies, the body that separates from Geb at creation. Hathor's sky is a working sky — the sky as mother, nurse, and musician of the sun rather than as his grandmother — and temple theology, especially at Dendera, was happy to let the two skies overlap in a single bovine silhouette when the hymn required it.

The royal form of the same doctrine is suckling. The king is Hathor's calf. Hatshepsut drinks from the cow's udder at Deir el-Bahari; Amenhotep II shelters beneath the cow's head and suckles in Thutmose III's shrine statue; Menkaure already holds her hand at Giza. To be nursed by the goddess is to have kingship itself enter the body as milk — legitimacy not as a decree but as a first food. No other Egyptian goddess nurses kings so often or so publicly; Isis nurses Horus, her own son, but Hathor nurses pharaohs, other women's sons, as a function of the crown.

"The Golden One" is her most intimate epithet, and Dendera's hymns worry it like a refrain: gold, nbw [Gardiner: S12], is the gods' flesh, incorruptible and shining, and Hathor is the golden flesh of the sky at its kindest hours — dawn and the hour before sunset, the light in which the Egyptian world actually did its living. The love poetry of Section XVII calls the beloved simply "the Golden One" and expects the reader to know whose name is being borrowed. When this archive speaks, in its interpretive voice, of Hathor as contained generative power, the gold is the colour of the idea: value that does not tarnish, held in a form that gives itself away daily as light, milk, music, and desire, and is not diminished.

VIII. Lady of Turquoise — Sinai, Byblos, Punt, and Timna

Hathor's most concrete title is a mining concession. As nbt mfkꜣt, Lady of Turquoise — mfkꜣt [Gardiner: G17-I9-V31-G1-X1] [𓅓𓆑𓎡𓄿𓏏] is turquoise, the blue-green stone of Sinai — she presided over the royal expeditions that crossed the desert to the mines of Wadi Maghara and Serabit el-Khadim. The card in Section IV states the title; this section gives the industry behind it. (A note the audit requires: the older headline "Lady of Malachite" is wrong. Malachite occurs in the same Sinai field and was mined there, but the epithet names turquoise, and the temple on the plateau is turquoise's temple.)

The expeditions were state enterprises with a liturgy. From the Old Kingdom — Snefru's reign opens the surviving record at Wadi Maghara — and with full documentation in the Middle Kingdom, royal parties travelled in the cool season to the sandstone plateau of Serabit, cut galleries into the turquoise-bearing rock, and recorded themselves on the forest of stelae that still lines the approaches to the goddess's temple. That temple grew from a rock-cave shrine into a long, additive complex of courts and pylons, with a second shrine to Sopdu, Lord of the East. Every stela is a payroll, a prayer, and a proof of survival in one object: the leader's name, the expedition's size, the goddess thanked by the title that told the desert whose ground this was.

Serabit also preserves one of the great accidents of writing. The Semitic-speaking workers in the Egyptian parties worshipped the Lady of Turquoise in their own tongue as Baalat, "the Lady," and scratched on stone, in the mines and on votives, the short inscriptions in the script scholars call Proto-Sinaitic — among the earliest alphabetic writing known. The little red sandstone sphinx now in the British Museum (EA 41748) carries the whole meeting on its two sides: hieroglyphs naming Hathor on one, the new alphabetic signs for Baalat on the other. Alan Gardiner's 1916 reading of the repeated alphabetic sequence as l-b-ʿ-l-t, "to the Lady," was the decipherment's key. The goddess of turquoise is thus, by her miners' devotion, a godmother of the alphabet.

Sinai was not the edge of her map. At Timna in the Arabah, Egyptian copper expeditions of the thirteenth–twelfth centuries BCE worked under a small rock temple of Hathor built against the cliff — the same goddess, the same logic, a second metal. At Byblos on the Levantine coast, the city's goddess, Baalat Gebal, was identified with Hathor from Egypt's earliest trading centuries, and Egyptian royal gifts crowded her temple. For Punt, the incense land of the southern Red Sea, Hathor was invoked as the mistress of the source country itself — the Hatshepsut expedition reliefs at Deir el-Bahari sail under her patronage. Turquoise, copper, cedar, incense: wherever Egyptian logistics reached for precious matter in foreign ground, the same name stands at the head of the manifest. She is the goddess of distant places because distance, to an Egyptian expedition, was precisely where her protection was needed and where her title did its work.

XI. Mistress of the West — Hathor of the Necropolis

The West (imnt [Gardiner: R14]) is the direction of the setting sun and of the necropolis — at Thebes, the desert cemeteries of the west bank. Among Hathor's most constant funerary titles are ḥnw.t imnt.t, "Mistress of the West," and "chieftainess of the desert necropolis" (ḥry.t-tp smy.t), both catalogued throughout the New Kingdom Theban tombs, where she also wears the emblem of the West as her crown. In funerary vignettes she appears in bovine form emerging from the western hillside to receive the deceased, and in tomb offering scenes she stands behind Osiris in support: the goddess of the horizon is the first face the dead meet on the far side of it.

The funerary Hathor has an address. On the Theban west bank she is the mountain itself: the goddess in cow form steps out of the cliff face of the necropolis in the vignettes, head and forelegs emerging from the hillside among the tombs, to welcome the justified dead into her own body of stone. The peak above Deir el-Medina was Meretseger's, "She Who Loves Silence," but the face of the western cliff as a whole, the first thing the funeral procession met as it crossed the river, belonged to Hathor of the West. Every Theban who was ferried over for burial travelled, in the geography of his own religion, into her lap.

XII. The Seven Hathors — Fate at the Cradle

In her plural form, Hathor becomes the Seven Hathors — a sevenfold company who attend at births and decree the fate of the newborn. Their best-known appearance is the Tale of the Doomed Prince (Papyrus Harris 500, British Museum), where they come to the royal child at his birth and decree, in paraphrase, that his death will be by the crocodile, the serpent, or the dog — the three fates the whole tale then tests. The same company decrees the fate of the wife of Bata in the Tale of the Two Brothers. In the funerary literature the seven are regularly identified with the seven celestial cows of Book of the Dead Spell 148, who provision the justified dead in the afterlife — the fate-speakers at life's entrance become the providers at its far side.

The seven are individuals, not a chorus. In the vignette of Book of the Dead spell 148 in the tomb of Nefertari, the seven cows stand in a row with the Bull of the West behind them and the four rudders of the sky's quarters before them, each cow captioned with her own name — among them Lady of the Universe, Sky-Storm, the one from the Land of Silence, the one from Khemmis, Red-Hair, and Bright Red, in the usual renderings; other lists, in the mythological papyri, name them as mistresses of the house of jubilation, of the west, of the east, and of the sacred land. The variation between lists is itself the evidence: the seven are a standing company that local theology could re-cast, like a bench of judges whose clerkship rotates.

Their literary appearances repay a closer look, because they show fate in the Egyptian manner — spoken, specific, and not quite final. In the Doomed Prince the Hathors' decree names three possible deaths and the tale (whose ending is lost) tests each in turn, as if a fate were a set of doors rather than a wall. In the Tale of the Two Brothers they come to the woman fashioned by the gods for Bata and decree that she will die by the sword — a fate the story then duly, and terribly, fulfils. And in the birth scenes of the temples the seven attend as the divine midwives' bench, decreeing the newborn god-king's reign at the moment of his first breath. Birth and death, cradle and coffin: the company that speaks a life's end at its beginning is the same company that provisions its owner after its end, and Hathor's Egyptians evidently found that symmetry not morbid but just.

IX. Ritual Music and Dance — The Sistrum and the Menat

Music was not merely entertainment in Hathoric ritual; it was a vibrational technology.

THE SISTRUM (SESHESHET)

A metallic rattle used to "drive away the clouds" of the mind. Its sound was believed to mirror the cosmic hiss of creation and was used to pacify the gods and maintain the structural integrity of the divine order.

THE MENAT NECKLACE

A heavy, beaded necklace with a counterweight. When shaken, it produced a rhythmic sound that transmitted the goddess's maternal protection and ensured the stability of the physical body.

Two instruments carry her name into every temple in Egypt. The sistrum — sššt [Gardiner: Y8], a word that imitates its own sound — comes in two forms: the naos sistrum, a handle topped by a small shrine framing the Hathor face, and the arched or loop sistrum, a hoop strung with loose crossbars and sliding disks of metal. Shaken in rhythm, it does not play melody; it makes a continuous bright shimmer, the audible equivalent of sunlight on water, and its stated purposes in the texts are to please the goddess and to drive off hostile powers — above all, to pacify the Eye (Section IV), whose rage is a kind of noise that only a better noise can answer.

The menat — mnit [Gardiner: G17-N35-M17-X1] — is a necklace engineered as an instrument: many strands of beads with a heavy counterpoise that hangs down the back. Held by the counterpoise and shaken, the bead strands rustle and clink; worn, it is regalia; presented to a god or a king in the rite called "offering the menat," it is Hathor herself in portable form — temple reliefs show the goddess receiving her own necklace as the summary of her cult. Sistrum and menat are played together, typically by women: queens, princesses, and the temple chantresses (šmꜥyt), whose title, "she who sings," names one of the few senior religious offices Egyptian women held in their own right, in Hathor's temples as a matter of course.

Around the instruments stands the dance. Hathor's festivals employ acrobatic dancers, girls playing clappers and hand-drums, processions swaying under garlands — the whole sensorium of her worship run at full gain, because her theology holds that joy is not the reward of the rite but its mechanism. The pacified Eye, the returning Distant Goddess, the goddess of the New Year roof at Dendera, are all met with the same technology: rhythm, shimmer, song, and drink, applied until wrath ferments into celebration. Section XVI gives the festivals in which that technology was deployed at civic scale.

X. Love, Beauty, and Birth — Mirrors, Gold, and the Birth House

Hathor governs the Egyptian arts of the body. The mirror — a polished disk of copper or bronze on a handle often carved as her face, a girl, or a papyrus stalk — is her instrument as much as the sistrum: the disk is the sun, the face that meets it is hers, and the act of adornment is a small daily cult of the Golden One. Cosmetic spoons, kohl pots, and unguent vessels carry her image into the dressing room; faience "Hathor amulets" and votive plaques in the form of her face were dedicated by women at her shrines, at Deir el-Bahari and elsewhere, in numbers that make her women's cult one of the best-attested pieces of private religion in the whole record.

Love and desire are hers without embarrassment and without the moral fog later ages wrap them in. The love songs (Section XVII) invoke the Golden One as the patroness who makes the beloved's arrival a festival; her festivals licence the drunkenness and the dancing in which courtship happened; her epithets praise her as beautiful of face and sweet of love. Egyptian theology did not separate this Hathor from the Eye of Ra or the Lady of the West, and neither does this article: the same goddess who is offered the sistrum is offered the mirror, and the same face is on both handles.

Birth completes the triad. With the birth gods Bes and Taweret she attends the labour bed, and her own mythology of birth is architectural: the mammisi, the "birth house" attached to the late temples — at Dendera, Philae, Edfu, and a dozen more — staged the birth of the temple's child god in relief and in festival, Hathor presiding as mother of the child and nurse of the cosmos. At Dendera the child is Ihy, the sistrum-player, music made a god: the goddess of joy giving birth to joy itself, annually, in a stone house built for the purpose, with the king in attendance as the child's earthly counterpart. The sanatorium by Dendera's sacred lake, where the sick slept for healing dreams under her priests' care, belongs to the same complex of services — desire, birth, healing, sleep — that her temples offered the body across a whole life.

XIII. The Tree Goddess — Bread and Water Under the Sycamore

In the vignettes of the Book of the Dead the justified dead kneel at the edge of the water in a garden, and out of a sycamore fig tree leans the upper body of a goddess, pouring water from a jar into the dead man's cupped hands and offering him a tray of bread and fruit. The goddess in the tree is most often Hathor, in her title Lady of the Southern Sycamore — the sycamore of the southern, Memphite, necropolis — though Nut and Isis also take the tree form in individual manuscripts. The image is the afterlife's welfare state in a single scene: the dead do not hunt or harvest for their eternity; a goddess feeds them, by hand, forever.

The form has roots in the soil. Sacred sycamores stood at the desert edge where the cemeteries began, their shade the last shade of the living world and the first of the next; the tree goddess is the genius of that tree made personal. Her milk-white sap and her fruit made the sycamore a nurse among trees, and the coffin itself — the wooden box that would hold the body through eternity — was ideally of sycamore fig wood, so that the dead lay, quite literally, inside the goddess. When the vignette shows her pouring, the archive reads it against that whole system: tree, shade, sap, fruit, coffin, water, bread — a supply chain of mercy with Hathor at every link.

XIV. Dendera — The House That Remains

Hathor's primary cult center was the Temple of Dendera (Iunet). The temple was designed as a microcosmic resonator for celestial harmonies. Its ceiling contains the famous Dendera Zodiac, a monumental record of Kemetic and Hellenistic astronomical knowledge.

The "Hathor-headed" columns of the hypostyle hall represent the goddess as the pillar of the sky, supporting the vault of heaven and ensuring that the frequencies of the stars are correctly channeled into the earthly realm.

Dates first, because Dendera is where this article's first version sinned by silence. The site is ancient — a chapel of Mentuhotep II and remains reaching back toward the Old Kingdom stand within the enclosure, and the oldest surviving building of the present complex is the birth house of Nectanebo II, the last native pharaoh (360–343 BCE). But the great temple a visitor walks today is a late Ptolemaic and Roman building: its construction began, by its own foundation texts, on 16 July 54 BCE under Ptolemy XII, and its decoration ran on under the Roman emperors into the second century CE. Dendera's theology is pharaonic in substance and often very old in its texts; its stones are, in Egyptian terms, almost modern. Both halves of that sentence are the fact.

The plan is a complete temple world inside a mudbrick enclosure some 40,000 square metres in extent: the main temple with its great hypostyle hall of twenty-four Hathor-headed columns and its astronomical ceiling, where the sky goddess arches among decans and zodiac signs; two birth houses; a sacred lake; a sanatorium for incubation healing (Section X); a small temple of the birth of Isis; and, in the thickness of the main temple's own walls and beneath its floors, a network of crypts — narrow, hidden chambers in several levels where the temple's sacred equipment, statues, and archives were stored, their walls carved with inventories in relief. The famous reliefs of objects in the crypts are catalogues of cult implements, whatever later speculation has made of them.

On the roof stand the two complexes that tie this article together: the open kiosk of the Union with the Disk, where at the New Year the goddess's statue was carried up the processional stair — its walls carved with the procession itself — and exposed to the rays of her father the sun, to be recharged with light for the year (Section XVI); and the Osiris chapels, where the Khoiak mysteries of the Osiris article were staged, their walls carrying the festival inscriptions and the relic lists of the god. It was from the ceiling of one of those chapels that the circular Dendera Zodiac was cut in 1820 and carried to France; the original is in the Louvre, a copy in its place. The relief is a late Ptolemaic sky-map of about 50 BCE — its planetary configuration and the eclipses of 52/51 BCE it registers fix the date — in which the Babylonian zodiac, newly naturalised in Egypt, is drawn in wholly Egyptian style, scarab for Cancer and scales for Libra, ringed by the thirty-six decans. It is a document of two astronomies meeting, dated to within a generation, and it needs no greater antiquity to be remarkable.

XV. Other Thrones of Hathor — Deir el-Bahari, Thebes, and Memphis

Dendera was her capital, not her country. At Deir el-Bahari, in the terraced mortuary temple of Hatshepsut (c. 1479–1458 BCE), the Hathor chapel occupies the southern end of the middle terrace: a vestibule and hypostyle hall fronted by Hathor-headed columns, its walls carrying the theology of Section VII in narrative form. The cow, in her shrine, licks the hand of the enthroned queen as a cow licks her calf; the queen kneels to drink from the goddess's udder; processions bring Hathor of Dendera by boat to her Theban home. The chapel is the earliest major Hathor structure on the Theban west bank, and it made the bay of Deir el-Bahari — already sacred to her as the valley of the Lady of the West — into the necropolis's front door.

Across the Theban hills her cult was a village affair as much as a state one. The royal workmen of Deir el-Medina, who cut the kings' tombs in her western mountain, dedicated stelae and small shrines to Hathor among their household and community gods, and her image presides in the painted tombs of their own cemetery — in TT1, the tomb of the workman Sennedjem, the dead couple work the fields of the afterlife under the eye of the gods of the west, Hathor's country. At Memphis she kept her sycamore shrine as Lady of the Southern Sycamore (Section XIII), hard by the great temple of Ptah. At Cusae in Middle Egypt she was the city goddess outright; at Gebelein, Aphroditopolis, and a dozen smaller towns her temple headed the local pantheon. Her geography is the inverse of Amun's: no single imperial centre with provincial branches, but a mistress in every valley, locally married, locally housed, and universally recognised.

One royal monument states her rank in the south with unusual plainness. The smaller of the two rock temples of Abu Simbel in Nubia, cut under Ramesses II, is dedicated to Hathor and to the Great Royal Wife Nefertari, who is shown on its facade at the king's own scale and identified with the goddess inside — the only Egyptian temple in which a queen stands as co-dedicatee with a god in this way. At the empire's southern edge, where her Distant Goddess had fled and her miners had prayed, Hathor receives a king's wife into her own person. The centres of Section XV's title are, in the end, of three kinds: the capital she owned, the villages that adopted her, and the frontiers where no expedition or garrison would be without her.

XVI. Festivals of Hathor — Drunkenness, the Reunion, and the New Year

The Festival of Drunkenness is her signature feast, and it is the ritual descendant of the Book of the Heavenly Cow: as the goddess was pacified by beer in the myth, so her people pacified her annually by drinking it, in her temple courts, to music, until they slept — the community taking the goddess's own medicine, in company and under rite, as the Sekhmet article sets out in full. The feast is attested at Dendera and, archaeologically, at Thebes: in the Mut temple precinct at Karnak, excavations directed by Betsy Bryan of Johns Hopkins University uncovered a "porch of drunkenness" built under Hatshepsut, its inscriptions tying the hall to the festival's drinking rite. The Heavenly Cow itself supplies the charter, in the closing ordinance of the myth: intoxicating draughts are to be prepared for the goddess at her yearly festivals, in memory of the night of the jars.

The Beautiful Feast of the Reunion was her state procession. Each year in the third month of summer, Hathor's image sailed from Dendera some 170 kilometres upriver to Edfu, to meet Horus of Edfu in his temple — the divine marriage visit, watched and feasted by the towns along the bank, the couple spending the festival days together before Hathor returned home. Egyptian temples ran a whole diplomacy of such visits, but the Dendera–Edfu reunion, documented in the festival calendars of both temples, was the grandest surviving example, and its theology is Section I's in processional form: the House of Horus travelling to Horus, the container to the contained, once a year, by boat, in public.

At the New Year Dendera kept the rite of the Union with the Disk (Section XIV): before dawn the priests carried the goddess's statue up the carved stair to the roof kiosk, drew back its curtains as the sun rose, and let the first light of the year strike the image — the daughter rejoined to her father, the statue refilled with light. Around these three fixed points turned a year of lesser feasts: her birthday celebrations, the feasts of her sailing to the necropolis at the Beautiful Festival of the Valley, when Theban families feasted and drank in the tombs of their dead in her western country and the boundary between her joy and her funerary office dissolved, quite deliberately, in wine.

XVII. Hathor in Song and Story — Love Poetry and Personal Piety

The New Kingdom love songs — preserved on papyri and ostraca from the world of Deir el-Medina, among them Papyrus Harris 500 and the Cairo love songs — are Hathor's scriptures of the heart. The lovers call her simply the Golden One and swear by her: a girl resolves to make an offering of song to the Golden One so that the goddess will give her the beloved; a boy boasts that the Golden One has decreed the girl for him. The poems are artful, courtly in their way, and entirely unembarrassed, and they show the goddess of the temple festivals at work in the first person singular — the scale at which most Egyptians, most of the time, actually needed a god.

Her presence in the wisdom and narrative literature is quieter but constant. The Seven Hathors walk into the tales (Section XII) as fate's delegation. The frame of the Tale of Sinuhe, the Middle Kingdom's most copied story, opens at the death of a king whose household stands under her Theban forms. In the personal piety of the Ramesside age — the age that invented, in the Deir el-Medina stelae, a confessional literature of sin, blindness, and divine mercy directed chiefly to Amun, Ptah, and Meretseger — Hathor receives the votives of women and the prayers of the necropolis workforce, the goddess of the place where they both lived and expected to be buried. A religion's centre of gravity shows in what its people carve when no priest is commissioning it; Hathor, by that measure, was load-bearing.

XVIII. Hathor Abroad and Afterward — Aphrodite, Isis, and Survival

The Greeks recognised her at sight: to them she was Aphrodite. Herodotus and the whole interpretatio graeca after him map Hathor onto the love goddess, the town of Aphroditopolis bears the equation in its very name, and the identification is one of the few in the Greek mapping that an Egyptian would have found merely incomplete rather than wrong. In the other direction, the Semitic world had already met her as Baalat (Section VIII), and the miners' double-named goddess is the archive's earliest documented case of her travelling under translation.

Her deepest afterlife, though, is inside another goddess. Isis takes her horns and disk, her nursing pose, her sistrum, her menat, her music, and her title of mistress of the sky, piece by piece, across the first millennium BCE — not by conquest but by the ordinary Egyptian process of fusion that the Osiris article describes as a treaty between cults. What Isis never took was Dendera, the mines, and the necropolis: to the end of the temples, Hathor keeps her capital, her industry, and her dead. Readers of the two articles in sequence will see the division of the estate plainly, and the archive intends them to: it is the correct picture, and the popular one, in which a generic "cow goddess" floats free of both dossiers, is the invention.

When the temples closed — Philae, her younger sister's house, last of all in the 530s CE — Hathor's images stayed in the landscape her cult had mapped: the columns of Dendera under their defaced and beautiful faces, the cow in Cairo, the sphinx of the alphabet in London. Her modern recovery has been scholarly rather than initiatory, and this article has kept it so: what survives of Hathor that can be used is a body of knowledge — texts, buildings, instruments, a festival calendar — and the next section's contemporary reading is labelled, as this archive's rule requires, as a reading.

XIX. Alchemical Transmutation of Wrath into Joy

The reading offered in this section is this archive's alchemical interpretation of the myth, labelled as such: the ancient text is a royal funerary composition, not an alchemical treatise. The myth is the Destruction of Mankind, the first part of the Book of the Heavenly Cow. Humanity plots against the aging Ra; his council advises him to send out his Eye, and the Eye descends in the form of Hathor (§IV). She slaughters mankind in the desert, and when Ra calls a halt — in paraphrase: he will keep power over them as king, and she is to hold off diminishing them — the text marks the turn in a single clause: "And so Sakhmet came into being" — the Book of the Heavenly Cow, trans. Erik Hornung in William Kelly Simpson (ed.), The Literature of Ancient Egypt (2003).

The remedy is Ra's own stratagem, ordered by Ra — no other god performs it. Ra sends swift messengers to Elephantine for red ochre in quantity; the ochre is ground into a barley beer-mash until it looks like human blood, and 7,000 jars of the beer are made and poured out where the goddess plans to kill. Coming at dawn to wade in blood, she finds the field flooded red, drinks, becomes drunk, and does not recognise mankind; she returns in peace, again Hathor, and Ra ordains that intoxicating draughts be prepared for her at her yearly festivals — the mythic charter of the Festival of Drunkenness. (Thoth plays no part in this episode; his pacification of the raging goddess by fable and flattery belongs to the separate myth of the Distant Goddess, §VI.)

Read alchemically — and only as a reading — the sequence is a compact transmutation. The uncontrolled fire of the Eye is not opposed by force but dissolved in liquid: wrath is given a red body it mistakes for its proper object, is taken up into the vessel of the flooded field, and is fixed again at a lower, joyful temperature as drunken peace. Colour does the operative work (ochre-red for blood-red), the number is industrial (7,000 jars — transformation at scale, not by a single dose), and the product of the operation is not the destruction of the fiery principle but its change of state: the same goddess, Sekhmet in the slaughter and Hathor in the return.

XX. Working with Hathor — A Contemporary Reading

The material of this closing section is this archive's contemporary interpretation, offered as such. It is not a recovered ancient rite, and nothing in it should be cited as Egyptian practice. The older articles of this archive close their gods with a working, and Hathor's working, honestly framed, is the simplest of them — because her cult's central technologies are the ones no age has had to reconstruct. Music made together, rhythm kept by hand, a feast eaten in company, adornment undertaken seriously, grief and joy given their scheduled days: the festivals of Section XVI are a technology for regulating a community's emotional weather, and they regulate a single practitioner's by the same means.

The contemporary reading this archive offers is therefore a calendar before it is anything else. Keep a feast of return — the Distant Goddess brought home with fable, music, and patience rather than force — for whatever in one's own life has gone raging into the desert. Keep a New Year rite of exposure: take the work of the year up, as the Dendera priests took the statue, into the first light, and let it be seen whole. Keep the menat and the mirror in their honest senses: make the sound that pacifies, and do the adornment that honours the face in the glass as the goddess's frontal face honours the worshipper — levelly, and without turning away. And keep, for the dead, the sycamore's practice: bread and water actually given, in their names, under a tree.

That is as far as the evidence lets a modern working go, and it is far enough. The goddess whose miners spelled the first alphabet in her honour, whose priests ran a sanatorium and a festival of licensed drunkenness out of the same precinct, and whose face still meets the visitor's eyes from the columns of Dendera, does not need a reconstructed priesthood to be present to a reader. She needs what her own cult always asked of its people: show up, make the music, pour the beer, remember the dead, and come back next year.
