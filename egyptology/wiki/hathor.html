I. The Ontology of Hathor — The Celestial Container

Hathor (Ancient Egyptian: ḥwt-ḥrw, "The House of Horus") is one of the most multi-faceted and consistently venerated goddesses in Kemet. Her name identifies her as the celestial space — the womb of the sky — that contains and protects the sun god Horus. She is the divine feminine principle in its nurturing, creative, and erotic aspects.

Depicted as a cow, a woman with cow's horns and a sun disk, or a stylized female face with cow's ears, Hathor represents the underlying harmonic resonance of the universe. She is the goddess of love, beauty, music, and the western horizon where the day ends and the soul begins its nocturnal journey.

II. The Eye of Ra — Power and Pacification

Hathor functions as the primary Eye of Ra — the active, protective, and sometimes destructive force of the sun god. In this role, she is the defender of Ma'at. Hathor and Sekhmet are distinct goddesses, though both can embody the Eye of Ra; in the Book of the Heavenly Cow, the goddess Ra sends against rebellious mankind is Hathor, and it is in the course of the slaughter that the text has Sekhmet "come into being."

THE WRATHFUL ASPECT

When the Eye becomes enraged, she represents uncontrolled entropic force. This aspect must be pacified through ritual, music, and the "offering of the sistrum" to return the goddess to her benevolent, joyful state.

THE LADY OF TURQUOISE

As nb.t mfkꜣt, "Lady of Turquoise," Hathor presided over the turquoise mines of Serabit el-Khadim in Sinai, where her temple stood and expeditions invoked her by that very title. The green stone was believed to be a physical crystallization of her life-giving power, used in amulets to attract fertility and renewal.

III. The Myth of the Distant Goddess

In this foundational myth, the Eye of Ra — the goddess as raging lioness — quarrels with her father and flees into the Nubian desert. Ra sends Shu and Thoth to bring her home. No trick with drink is used: Thoth, in baboon form, wins her over slowly, entertaining her with animal fables, music, and flattery until her anger cools and she agrees to return. (The red-dyed beer belongs to a different story — the Book of the Heavenly Cow, in which Ra floods the fields with beer dyed red like blood to stop the Eye's slaughter of mankind in Egypt itself.)

Pacified, she reverts to the joyful Hathor and returns to Egypt in a great procession, her homecoming celebrated with music and drinking festivals along the way. Esoterically, this myth represents the transmutation of base desire and aggression into refined creative energy — an alchemical shift from Nigredo to Rubedo.

IV. Mistress of the West and the Seven Hathors

The West (imnt [Gardiner: R14]) is the direction of the setting sun and of the necropolis — at Thebes, the desert cemeteries of the west bank. Among Hathor's most constant funerary titles are ḥnw.t imnt.t, "Mistress of the West," and "chieftainess of the desert necropolis" (ḥry.t-tp smy.t), both catalogued throughout the New Kingdom Theban tombs, where she also wears the emblem of the West as her crown. In funerary vignettes she appears in bovine form emerging from the western hillside to receive the deceased, and in tomb offering scenes she stands behind Osiris in support: the goddess of the horizon is the first face the dead meet on the far side of it.

In her plural form, Hathor becomes the Seven Hathors — a sevenfold company who attend at births and decree the fate of the newborn. Their best-known appearance is the Tale of the Doomed Prince (Papyrus Harris 500, British Museum), where they come to the royal child at his birth and decree, in paraphrase, that his death will be by the crocodile, the serpent, or the dog — the three fates the whole tale then tests. The same company decrees the fate of the wife of Bata in the Tale of the Two Brothers. In the funerary literature the seven are regularly identified with the seven celestial cows of Book of the Dead Spell 148, who provision the justified dead in the afterlife — the fate-speakers at life's entrance become the providers at its far side.

V. Ritual Music — The Sistrum and the Menat

Music was not merely entertainment in Hathoric ritual; it was a vibrational technology.

THE SISTRUM (SESHESHET)

A metallic rattle used to "drive away the clouds" of the mind. Its sound was believed to mirror the cosmic hiss of creation and was used to pacify the gods and maintain the structural integrity of the divine order.

THE MENAT NECKLACE

A heavy, beaded necklace with a counterweight. When shaken, it produced a rhythmic sound that transmitted the goddess's maternal protection and ensured the stability of the physical body.

VI. Dendera — Architecture of the Stars

Hathor's primary cult center was the Temple of Dendera (Iunet). The temple was designed as a microcosmic resonator for celestial harmonies. Its ceiling contains the famous Dendera Zodiac, a monumental record of Kemetic and Hellenistic astronomical knowledge.

The "Hathor-headed" columns of the hypostyle hall represent the goddess as the pillar of the sky, supporting the vault of heaven and ensuring that the frequencies of the stars are correctly channeled into the earthly realm.

VII. Alchemical Transmutation of Wrath into Joy

The reading offered in this section is this archive's alchemical interpretation of the myth, labelled as such: the ancient text is a royal funerary composition, not an alchemical treatise. The myth is the Destruction of Mankind, the first part of the Book of the Heavenly Cow. Humanity plots against the aging Ra; his council advises him to send out his Eye, and the Eye descends in the form of Hathor (§II). She slaughters mankind in the desert, and when Ra calls a halt — in paraphrase: he will keep power over them as king, and she is to hold off diminishing them — the text marks the turn in a single clause: "And so Sakhmet came into being" — the Book of the Heavenly Cow, trans. Erik Hornung in William Kelly Simpson (ed.), The Literature of Ancient Egypt (2003).

The remedy is Ra's own stratagem, ordered by Ra — no other god performs it. Ra sends swift messengers to Elephantine for red ochre in quantity; the ochre is ground into a barley beer-mash until it looks like human blood, and 7,000 jars of the beer are made and poured out where the goddess plans to kill. Coming at dawn to wade in blood, she finds the field flooded red, drinks, becomes drunk, and does not recognise mankind; she returns in peace, again Hathor, and Ra ordains that intoxicating draughts be prepared for her at her yearly festivals — the mythic charter of the Festival of Drunkenness. (Thoth plays no part in this episode; his pacification of the raging goddess by fable and flattery belongs to the separate myth of the Distant Goddess, §III.)

Read alchemically — and only as a reading — the sequence is a compact transmutation. The uncontrolled fire of the Eye is not opposed by force but dissolved in liquid: wrath is given a red body it mistakes for its proper object, is taken up into the vessel of the flooded field, and is fixed again at a lower, joyful temperature as drunken peace. Colour does the operative work (ochre-red for blood-red), the number is industrial (7,000 jars — transformation at scale, not by a single dose), and the product of the operation is not the destruction of the fiery principle but its change of state: the same goddess, Sekhmet in the slaughter and Hathor in the return.
