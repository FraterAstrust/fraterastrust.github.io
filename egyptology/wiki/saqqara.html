SAQQARA

Egypt's Longest-Used Necropolis · The Cemetery of Memphis

// THREE THOUSAND YEARS OF BURIAL :: FROM FIRST-DYNASTY MASTABAS TO A COPTIC MONASTERY //

No other Egyptian cemetery was used, reused, robbed, re-cut and used again for so long. Saqqara is not one monument. It is the accumulated record of a capital city burying its kings, its officials, its priests, its sacred animals and, at the end, its monks, on the same desert plateau, generation after generation.

I. Name and Place — What Saqqara Is

Saqqara — also spelled Sakkara or Saccara — is the principal necropolis of Memphis, for most of pharaonic history Egypt's capital or one of its capitals, and it lies on the west bank of the Nile, roughly 30 kilometres south of modern Cairo, beside the modern village from which it takes its present name.

The modern name is Arabic. Its derivation is debated — a connection with the funerary god Sokar, lord of the Memphite necropolis, is often proposed and is plausible in geography, since Sokar's domain was precisely this desert — but it is a proposal, not a demonstrated etymology, and this article does not present it as settled. What is not debated is the scale. The archaeological zone conventionally called Saqqara runs for roughly 7 kilometres north to south and about 1.5 kilometres at its widest, along the edge of the low desert plateau above the cultivation.

That strip is only the core. North of it lies Abusir, and south of it Dahshur, and together with Giza to the north they form the continuous pyramid fields of Memphis, inscribed by UNESCO in 1979 as Memphis and its Necropolis — the Pyramid Fields from Giza to Dahshur. To speak of Saqqara alone is already to cut a slice out of a single, larger cemetery landscape. The cut is conventional and useful, because Saqqara's slice is the densest and the longest-used, but the reader should remember that an ancient Memphite choosing a tomb plot did not observe the modern boundary.

The claim in this article's title — Egypt's longest-used necropolis — is a claim about duration and density together. Burial at Saqqara begins in the Early Dynastic Period, at the very opening of the pharaonic record, and it does not stop at the fall of the Old Kingdom, or at the end of the New Kingdom, or at the Persian conquest, or at the coming of Rome. It continues, in changed forms, through the animal catacombs of the Late Period and the Ptolemaic age, and it ends, if it ends anywhere, with a Christian monastery built among the tombs and occupied into the early medieval period. That is a span of roughly three thousand years of primary use, with later visitation, quarrying, pilgrimage and excavation layered on top.

Every other great Egyptian cemetery has a period. Saqqara has all of them, stacked. A Late Period shaft may cut an Old Kingdom mastaba; a Coptic cell may be built from a New Kingdom block; a dog catacomb may be tunnelled through a Third Dynasty tomb. The site's difficulty and its value are the same thing: nothing here is single-period.

This article therefore treats Saqqara as a site, not as a backdrop. Earlier articles in this archive visit single facilities inside it — the Step Pyramid in Imhotep, the embalming workshop in Mummification, the Anubieion in Anubis, the Bes chambers in Bes, the Serapis cult in Alexandria. What follows is the whole those visits belong to: the plateau, its zones, its institutions, and the modern history of digging it.

A note on zones, because the literature assumes them. North Saqqara holds the Early Dynastic mastabas, the Step Pyramid enclosure, the pyramids of Userkaf and Teti, the Serapeum and most of the animal catacombs. Central Saqqara, around and south of the Unas causeway, holds the New Kingdom tomb cluster and the great Late Period shafts. South Saqqara holds the pyramids of Pepi I and his successors and, further south still, the Mastabat al-Fir'aun and the later, smaller pyramids. The Bubasteion cliff forms the eastern edge in the north. Excavation concessions, museum divisions and publication series still follow these divisions, which is why the bibliography of Saqqara reads as several literatures, not one.

II. Geography and the Relation to Memphis

Saqqara exists because Memphis exists, and Memphis exists because of a river junction. The city stood near the apex of the Delta, where the Nile valley opens into its fan — the balance point, in the Egyptians' own geographic imagination, between Upper and Lower Egypt. The archive's account of that imagination is in Sacred Geography; here the point is practical. A capital at the junction commands movement upriver and down, taxes it, feeds itself from both zones, and needs, close at hand and on dry ground, a place to put its dead.

The west bank supplied it. Egyptian burial was overwhelmingly western — the dead went where the sun went — and the low desert west of Memphis offered exactly what a necropolis requires: ground above the inundation, soft limestone that could be cut for shafts and galleries, harder stone and plateau gravel for building, and a sharp visual presence. From the cultivation, the plateau edge reads as a wall of pale rock and sand. The pyramids placed on it were not hidden monuments. They were the city's skyline, seen daily across the fields by everyone who lived in it.

The living city and the dead city faced each other across a narrow band of fields and canals. The distance matters for procedure. A funeral at Memphis was a crossing — the body prepared in the city or in workshops at the desert edge, carried west, and installed in a tomb that its owner had usually built, decorated and furnished during his own lifetime. The embalming side of that crossing, its workshops, personnel and timetable, is treated in full in Mummification and is not repeated here; Saqqara's own excavated embalming workshop is discussed below as a building, in Section XIX, where it belongs with the Late Period complex that contained it.

Memphis's god was Ptah, and Ptah's presence runs through the necropolis as more than decoration. The Apis bull, buried in the Serapeum with a state funeral, was in life the living image of Ptah. The deified Imhotep was reckoned Ptah's son. Craftsmen, sculptors and builders — Ptah's people — fill the mastaba reliefs. When later periods returned to Saqqara after intervals in which the court had buried elsewhere, they were not discovering a new site. They were returning to the cemetery of a city that had remained, through every political shift, a religious and administrative centre of the first rank.

Three assets never expired. First, sanctity by accumulation: to be buried near Djoser, near Unas, near the Serapeum, was to be buried in ground already proved holy for a thousand years. Second, infrastructure: causeways, harbours at the desert edge, workshop quarters and priesthoods persisted or could be revived. Third, visibility: a tomb on the Saqqara escarpment was seen from Memphis. New Kingdom officials whose king was buried at Thebes still built their tombs here, at home, where their families, estates and gods were. The necropolis was never only royal, and that is why it outlived every dynasty that used it.

The plateau's geology set the terms for everything built on it. The limestone is workable but variable — good beds alternate with marly, fractured layers — and that variability explains features a visitor might otherwise read as choices. New Kingdom tomb-builders in the Bubasteion cliff lined poor rock with better stone. Late Period engineers sank enormous shafts to reach stable levels and built burial chambers as freestanding stone boxes inside them. Catacomb cutters followed beds that would hold a roof, and sometimes misjudged: collapsed galleries in the animal catacombs are geological events as much as archaeological ones.

Water, paradoxically, is the plateau's other governing fact. The desert surface is dry; the danger comes from below and from rare rain. Rising groundwater, fed by modern irrigation in the cultivation, now threatens low-lying galleries and painted chambers that survived three thousand drier years — a conservation problem taken up in Section XXIV. In antiquity the same edge between field and desert that made Saqqara dry enough for preservation also made it close enough for robbery. No Saqqara tomb was ever remote. Every generation of thieves lived within walking distance of every generation of burials.

III. Before the Pyramids — The Early Dynastic Tombs

The first great tombs at North Saqqara are large mudbrick mastabas of the 1st and 2nd Dynasties — bench-shaped superstructures over storerooms and a burial chamber, their exteriors modelled with the recessed panelling of the palace facade. Walter Bryan Emery excavated the principal series in the 1930s to 1950s and published them as the Great Tombs of the First Dynasty. The richest, such as Tomb 3035 (Hemaka, chancellor under King Den) and Tomb 3504, produced the object assemblages — stone vessels, copper tools, gaming pieces, sealings, labels — on which much of the modern picture of the early court still rests.

Who was buried in them was once the field's sharpest Saqqara argument. Emery held that the North Saqqara mastabas were the actual royal tombs of the 1st Dynasty and that the great tomb complexes at Abydos, in the south, were cenotaphs. The mainstream position today is the reverse, and this archive follows it, as its Early Dynastic article states: the kings were buried at Abydos, and the North Saqqara mastabas belonged to the highest officials of the Memphite court. The correction matters beyond attribution. It makes Saqqara, from its first century, what it remained — a cemetery in which proximity to power, not only possession of it, was buried in monumental form.

The officials' reading also fits the objects. Seal impressions name kings, but they name them as the source of goods and authority, the way a ministry seal would. The tombs' owners — viziers, treasurers, stewards — were the men who ran the new state from Memphis, and their cemetery stood where they could reach it. Abydos kept the kings and the ancestral, southern sanctity. Saqqara kept the administration. Both claims on the dead were permanent, and Egyptian history is in part the negotiation between them.

Two Early Dynastic finds deserve naming because later Saqqara keeps quoting them. The tomb of Hesy-Re (Hesire), a high official under Djoser at the dynastic boundary, excavated by J. E. Quibell and published in 1913, held eleven painted wooden panels — among the masterpieces of early Egyptian art — showing the owner with his scribal equipment and titles. And the storeroom mastabas preserve, in their pottery and sealing deposits, the paper trail of the early state in physical form: estates, domains and royal deliveries, itemised. The archive's point, here as elsewhere, is procedural. Before Saqqara had a pyramid, it had filing.

Burial practice in these tombs was already the practice the later necropolis inherited: a body contracted or extended in a wooden coffin or lined pit, goods arranged in magazines around it, offerings maintained by endowment and by family, and robbery — the storerooms were entered in antiquity, repeatedly. The mudbrick superstructures have mostly melted back into the plateau, which is why the Early Dynastic cemetery is the least visible of Saqqara's layers to a modern visitor and, in the publication record, one of the most consequential.

IV. Gisr el-Mudir and the Dry Moat — The Plateau before Djoser

West of the Step Pyramid stands the great rectangular enclosure known as Gisr el-Mudir: a stone enclosure wall, roughly 650 by 350 metres, built of rough limestone, enclosing an apparently empty space. It is generally dated to the late 2nd Dynasty and associated with the horizon of King Khasekhemwy, whose paired enclosures and tomb at Abydos and Hierakonpolis supply the closest parallels. If that dating holds, Gisr el-Mudir is among the earliest large stone constructions in Egypt — older than the Step Pyramid complex it neighbours — and it is a standing warning against reading the Djoser complex as an invention from nothing. Stone, scale and the enclosure idea were all in rehearsal on this plateau before Imhotep began.

Its emptiness is the honest part of the record. No pyramid, temple or tomb has been demonstrated inside it. Geophysical survey and limited excavation have clarified stretches of wall and construction, not a programme. This article therefore describes Gisr el-Mudir as an enclosure of late Early Dynastic date whose function is unresolved — funerary enclosure by analogy, nothing stronger — and flags the stronger claims sometimes made for it as unproven.

Around the Step Pyramid complex runs the second great pre- and para-Djoser feature, the so-called Dry Moat: a broad, deep trench cut in the rock, traced by the Polish-Egyptian mission working west of the pyramid, in segments totalling well over a kilometre around the complex. Excavated stretches show it was no simple quarry, though quarrying and boundary-marking remain among the explanations offered. The mission's director, Kamil Kuraszkiewicz, has argued from the excavated evidence for a symbolic reading — the trench as a modelled passage of the dead king through the netherworld. That reading is presented here as its author's interpretation of real excavated data, not as consensus: the trench is certain, its full course and date are still being established, and its meaning is contested.

Together, Gisr el-Mudir and the Dry Moat reframe the plateau's biography. The Step Pyramid did not found a sacred landscape. It monumentalised one that the 2nd Dynasty had already enclosed, bounded and cut into the rock. Later builders inherited not a blank desert but a worked one, and the habit of inheriting — of building inside, against and through earlier sacred construction — is the single most constant behaviour in Saqqara's three thousand years.

V. Djoser's Step Pyramid Complex as Architecture

The Step Pyramid complex of King Djoser — Horus name Netjerikhet — built in the 3rd Dynasty, conventionally around 2670–2650 BCE, is the oldest large stone building complex in Egypt to survive as architecture rather than as foundations. The full account of its designer and his afterlife is this archive's Imhotep article, which this section does not duplicate. What belongs here is the complex as a piece of Saqqara: its fabric, its construction sequence, and what building it did to the plateau.

The pyramid itself rose in six steps to about 62.5 metres, on a base of roughly 121 by 109 metres, and it was not designed in that form. Jean-Philippe Lauer's decades of excavation and restoration established the sequence still used in the literature: a square stone mastaba, enlarged, extended eastward over a series of deep family shafts, then raised first to four steps and finally to six. The changes of plan are legible in the masonry. The complex is an experiment that kept its drafts.

Beneath it runs a subterranean system of galleries and chambers totalling nearly 6 kilometres, centred on a shaft about 7 metres square and some 28 metres deep, at whose bottom stood the king's granite burial vault. Chambers lined with blue-green faience tiles imitate, in permanent material, the reed-mat hangings of a palace interior. Tens of thousands of stone vessels, many inscribed for earlier kings, were stored in the galleries — a treasury of the first two dynasties gathered, for reasons still discussed, into the founding monument of the third.

Around the pyramid, the enclosure: a paneled limestone wall about 10.5 metres high and more than 1.6 kilometres in circuit, 544 by 277 metres, with fourteen false gateways and one true entrance. Inside it, in about 15 hectares, stands a court city in stone — the entrance colonnade with its engaged, ribbed columns; the Great South Court with the South Tomb; the Heb-Sed court with its chapels, built solid, as stage architecture for the king's eternal jubilee (see Sed Festival); the Houses of the North and the South; the northern mortuary temple; and the serdab, the sealed chamber whose life-size statue of the king looked out through two drilled holes toward the northern stars. Almost none of these buildings was meant to be entered and used in the ordinary way. They are a palace, a festival ground and a kingdom, translated into stone for a dead king's permanent use.

The translation is literal, and it is the complex's most instructive feature. Columns are carved as bundled reeds or papyrus stems. Ceilings are carved as palm logs. Door leaves stand carved open, in stone, forever. The core masonry is of small, locally quarried limestone blocks laid in inward-leaning layers; the fine white casing came from Tura, across the river. Imhotep's workforce was not yet handling the multi-ton blocks of the Giza age. The revolution here is not block size. It is the decision that everything perishable in the king's world would be re-made, at full scale, in a material that does not rot — and the organisation, on one plateau, of the quarries, workshops and ramps to do it.

The complex also fixed Saqqara's centre of gravity. For the rest of the Old Kingdom, royal and private building on the plateau orientates itself by Djoser's enclosure — north of it, south of it, in sight of it. When the site was revived in later ages, the Step Pyramid was already ancient beyond ordinary memory, and it was visited, inscribed on, quarried around and revered accordingly. Its modern history includes a long consolidation programme, principally addressing the pyramid's unstable core and the subterranean galleries, after which the pyramid reopened to visitors in March 2020.

VI. Djoser's Complex as Institution

A mortuary complex is not finished when its masonry is. It is finished when its cult is staffed, endowed and supplied — and the institution, unlike the masonry, had to be re-founded by every generation that kept it running. Djoser's complex created, at Saqqara, the template: mortuary priests holding land in return for service, offering endowments administered through the royal house, workshops and stores at the desert edge, and a calendar of festivals at which the dead king's establishment met the living city.

The Heb-Sed court is the institutional claim in architectural form. The sed festival renewed the king's power in life, at intervals, before the gods and the court. Built in stone, it asserts that the renewal will continue without interval and without a living court to witness it. The chapels of the gods of Upper and Lower Egypt line the court because the festival's witnesses were the country's gods in their local forms. What the complex institutionalises, then, is not a funeral but a permanent court — the first, and for centuries the model, of the establishments that every later pyramid on this plateau would replicate at smaller scale.

The human evidence for the institution stands, characteristically, in two inscriptions. The base of a statue of Djoser found in the complex names Imhotep with his titles — the contemporary proof of his existence and office discussed in his article. And a graffito in the neighbouring unfinished pyramid of Sekhemkhet records a visit by Imhotep, suggesting the architect outlived his king and served the next reign's works. An institution is people continuing. The graffito is the earliest Saqqara document of exactly that.

How long Djoser's own cult was actively maintained is harder to state, and this article does not invent a terminal date for it. Mortuary cults decayed as endowments were diverted and priestly families died out; by the later Old Kingdom the active centres on the plateau were the newer royal complexes to the south. But decay is not erasure. The complex remained a named, visited holy place — New Kingdom visitors left inscriptions in the necropolis praising its antiquity, and the Late Period cults of the plateau treated Djoser's ground as consecrated by priority. The institution's longest-lived product was not its offerings. It was the idea, renewed by every later builder here, that Saqqara was where Memphis kept what must not be forgotten.

VII. Old Kingdom Mastabas — The Officials' City of the Dead

Around the royal pyramids of the 5th and 6th Dynasties, the officials of the Memphite court built the mastaba fields that make Saqqara, for the Old Kingdom, the richest single source on Egyptian life ever recovered. A mastaba of the developed type is two buildings: a decorated chapel above ground, where offerings were presented before a false door through which the dead owner's kꜣ [Gardiner: D28] [𓂓] was understood to pass, and a burial apartment below ground, reached by a shaft, sealed at the funeral and not intended to be entered again. The chapel was public, didactic and competitive. The burial was none of those things, which is why chapels survive inscribed and burials survive robbed.

The chapel reliefs are the archive from which whole subjects are reconstructed. The mastaba of Ti, overseer of royal works under several 5th Dynasty kings, excavated for Auguste Mariette in 1865, shows estate agriculture, boat-building, metalworking, market scenes and bird-trapping in register after register. The joint mastaba of the manicurists Khnumhotep and Niankhkhnum, found in 1964 beneath the Unas causeway, is famous for the intimacy of its paired imagery, on which this article takes no position beyond the record: two men of the same household and office, buried in one tomb, shown throughout in the poses Egyptian art reserves for couples and close kin. The mastaba of the vizier Mereruka, son-in-law of King Teti, is among the largest private tombs of the age, with more than thirty rooms. The mastaba of Ptahhotep preserves, in its owner's other afterlife, the name attached to one of Egypt's classic wisdom texts — for which see Wisdom Literature.

Read as a population rather than as highlights, the mastabas document an institution: hereditary office, estate endowment, the offering formula spoken for the dead by any literate passer-by, and the expectation — carved, painted and legally endowed — that the tomb would be maintained by descendants and priests in perpetuity. The expectation failed almost everywhere, and the failure is itself documented on the plateau, in reused blocks, usurped chapels and secondary burials cut through chapel floors. Saqqara's Old Kingdom layer is not a frozen city of the dead. It is a city that was already being redeveloped while it was being built.

THE FALSE DOOR

The chapel's focus: a stone door that does not open, set in the west wall, before which offerings were placed. It is the interface between the living who bring and the dead who receive — the single most repeated architectural element in the necropolis, from Old Kingdom mastabas to Late Period chapels that quote them deliberately.

THE SERDAB

A sealed statue chamber, communicating with the chapel, in the classic type, only by narrow slots through which incense and the sound of prayer could reach the statue. Djoser's serdab, with its eye-holes, is the royal prototype; private serdabs made the same claim at household scale — presence maintained behind stone, permanently, without access.

Royal building in this period frames the fields. Userkaf, first king of the 5th Dynasty, placed his small pyramid hard against the north-east corner of Djoser's enclosure — a deliberate act of adjacency to the founder. Unas, last king of the dynasty, built at the enclosure's south-west corner, and his pyramid is the subject of the next section. Teti, first king of the 6th Dynasty, built north of the Step Pyramid, and the queens' and officials' tombs around his pyramid form their own dense quarter. Adjacency was argument: each king, and each official around each king, stated a lineage by choosing an address.

VIII. Unas and the Pyramid Texts

The pyramid of Unas is externally unremarkable — a small, now much-ruined pyramid at the south-west corner of Djoser's enclosure, reached by a long causeway decorated with scenes that include famine relief and the transport of granite columns from Aswan. Internally it changed the history of writing. Its burial chamber and antechamber walls carry, carved and filled with blue pigment, the first inscribed corpus of the Pyramid Texts — the oldest large body of religious literature to survive from anywhere.

The numbers, as this archive's funerary-texts article gives them: the corpus in Sethe's and Faulkner's numbering runs to 759 utterances across all the pyramids that bear it; Unas, the first king to bear it, holds 228 of them in that numbering, divided differently — into 283 — in James P. Allen's newer edition. The discrepancy is editorial, not substantive, and it is flagged here because popular accounts quote both figures as if they were rival counts of different things. They are two segmentations of the same walls.

Gaston Maspero's entry into the pyramids of Saqqara in 1880–81 is the discovery moment: at Pepi I's pyramid in the south he first recognised inscribed chambers, and the Unas corpus was recognised in the same campaign season of work. Kurt Sethe's edition (1908–22) made the texts a corpus; R. O. Faulkner's translation (1969) and Allen's edition of the Unas material made them readable in English. The texts' content — the king's ascent to the circumpolar stars, his identification with Osiris, wsir [Gardiner: Q1-D4-A40] [𓊨𓁹𓀭], the spells against serpents and hazards, the offering liturgy — is treated in Funerary Texts and is summarised here only to place it: it happened at Saqqara, in this pyramid, first.

What the Unas decision did to the necropolis deserves its own sentence. After Unas, a royal tomb was a text as well as a building. The 6th Dynasty pyramids at South Saqqara carry the corpus onward; the Middle Kingdom inherits it onto coffins; the New Kingdom transforms it into the Book of the Dead tradition. The whole later funerary literature of Egypt, which this archive treats in its own articles, descends from a choice made in one small pyramid beside Djoser's wall: to carve the spells where the king lay, permanently, in stone, whether or not any living reader would ever see them again.

The Unas causeway contributed a second, quieter legacy. The New Kingdom cemetery of Sections XI–XIII lies immediately south of it, and the Late Period great shafts cluster around the pyramid — both generations building in the lee of a monument whose sanctity they inherited. Unas's pyramid was also, in the 19th Dynasty, an object of early restoration: Prince Khaemwaset, son of Ramesses II, left inscriptions across Egypt recording his renewal of ancient monuments, Unas's among them — the necropolis's first documented conservator, working three thousand years ago, on a building already eleven centuries old.

IX. South Saqqara — Teti, Pepi and the End of the Old Kingdom

South Saqqara is the Old Kingdom's second act. Around the pyramid of Pepi I grew a royal complex of queens' pyramids — among them those of Ankhesenpepi II and Nebwenet — with their own inscribed chambers, extending the Pyramid Texts, for the first time, to royal women. Around Pepi II, who succeeded as a child and whose reign the Turin tradition makes the longest of the pharaonic age, the court cemetery continued to accumulate even as the state's cohesion, on the standard account, thinned around it. The French mission at Saqqara-South, working the Pepi I complex for decades, has recovered queens' pyramids, temples and texts season by season — a reminder that even the most excavated zone of the plateau is a work in progress, not a finished plan.

South Saqqara also holds the period's strangest royal monument, the Mastabat al-Fir'aun — the Pharaoh's Bench — the tomb of Shepseskaf, last king of the 4th Dynasty, who declined the pyramid form altogether and built, at the southern end of the necropolis, an enormous mastaba with the vaulted profile of a sarcophagus or a Buto shrine. Why he did so is not recorded, and the explanations offered — economy, theology, reaction against the solar pyramid age — are all inference. The monument is the record: at Saqqara, even refusal of the pyramid was built at pyramid scale.

The Old Kingdom's end at Saqqara is not a destruction layer. It is a fading of commissions, a thinning of endowments, and then, in the First Intermediate Period, a necropolis maintained by a city whose kings were no longer buried there. That pattern — the plateau persisting at lower intensity through a political eclipse, its priesthoods and local burials continuing — will repeat, and recognising it here, in its first occurrence, is the key to the Middle Kingdom section that follows.

X. The Middle Kingdom Presence — Thin, Real, and Easily Overstated

When Egypt reunified under the Middle Kingdom, the court moved south and the kings built at Lisht, Dahshur and Thebes. Saqqara lost the royal commissions that had defined it. Popular summaries sometimes convert that loss into abandonment. The record does not support abandonment, and this article does not claim it.

What the record supports is continuity at reduced scale. Memphis remained a great city — the Middle Kingdom capital at Itjtawy stood near Lisht, within the Memphite region — and Memphites continued to be buried in their ancestral necropolis. Middle Kingdom burials at Saqqara are attested in reused Old Kingdom shafts, in modest new tombs, and in the stray but consistent finds of the period across the plateau. Officials with Memphite duties still chose Saqqara addresses. The cults of the plateau, above all Ptah's and the Apis cult in its early form, continued in the city below.

The one royal monument of the age at Saqqara belongs to its end: the small pyramid of Khendjer, a 13th Dynasty king, in South Saqqara, excavated by Gustave Jéquier — complete with pyramidion, subterranean apartments and a granite sarcophagus chamber, a full royal tomb in miniature from a dynasty whose kings reigned briefly and built accordingly. Khendjer's pyramid is the exception that states the rule. The Middle Kingdom at Saqqara is a presence of private burial and civic cult, not of royal building, and its thinness in the record is partly real and partly an artefact: later New Kingdom and Late Period building cut through, reused and buried exactly the strata in which a modest Middle Kingdom cemetery would lie.

// ON ABSENCE OF EVIDENCE AT SAQQARA //

Every period claim for this plateau must be discounted by its excavation history. Zones were dug for objects in the nineteenth century, for architecture in the twentieth, and stratigraphically only in recent decades. A period that built modestly, in mudbrick and reused shafts, in a zone later quarried for a catacomb, can be historically substantial and archaeologically faint. The Middle Kingdom is Saqqara's standing example. This article reports it as attested, secondary, and certainly under-recorded — and no stronger.

XI. The New Kingdom Necropolis — Memphis Buries Its Servants Again

In the New Kingdom the kings were buried at Thebes, in the Valley of the Kings — and their Memphite officials, generals, treasurers, viziers and priests were, in large and increasing numbers, buried at Saqqara. The logic is Section II's: Memphis was again a working capital, the northern seat of government and the harbour for the empire's Mediterranean traffic, and a man built his eternal house where his household, his estate and his temple appointments were. The result is a second private necropolis, of the later 18th and 19th Dynasties above all, as rich in its way as the Old Kingdom fields — and, until the 1970s, almost entirely lost.

Lost is the precise word. Karl Richard Lepsius's Prussian expedition recorded New Kingdom tombs at Saqqara in the 1840s, copied reliefs, and removed blocks and statues to Berlin. The tombs were then covered again by sand and their locations forgotten — statues in European museums, among them the celebrated dyad of Maya and Merit in Leiden, acquired as early as 1823, stood for a century as orphans from tombs no one could find. The systematic recovery of the New Kingdom necropolis is therefore a modern excavation story, told in Sections XII and XIII, and one of the clearest cases in Egyptology of a cemetery being discovered twice.

The New Kingdom tomb at Saqqara has a recognisable plan, distinct from the Old Kingdom mastaba it superficially resembles. A pylon-fronted forecourt, often of mudbrick with limestone revetment, opens into a colonnaded courtyard; behind it lie statue rooms and a cult chapel, frequently crowned by a small pyramidion- topped pyramid of mudbrick — the private pyramid, unthinkable for a commoner in the Old Kingdom, now a standard claim of high officials. Below, a shaft descends, in the great tombs some 18 metres, to burial chambers that could themselves be decorated. The whole is a temple-tomb: less a house for the dead than a small mortuary temple for one man's cult, staffed, like Djoser's, by endowment.

The population is a cross-section of the empire's northern government at its height: the general who became king (Section XII); the treasurer who served Tutankhamun and his successors (Section XIII); the chief steward Amenemone; the vizier Aperel, buried in the Bubasteion cliff with his family, in a tomb later filled with cat mummies (Section XVII); Maya, wet-nurse of Tutankhamun, in the same cliff; scribes, gold-workers, fan-bearers and priests in their dozens. Their reliefs — of funeral processions, of the Opening of the Mouth performed on statues in a garden court, of the deceased judged before Osiris — are primary evidence for New Kingdom funerary practice precisely because these are working tombs of working officials, not royal theological programmes.

The necropolis had two main fields. South of the Unas causeway lies the cluster recovered by the Anglo-Dutch and successor missions — Horemheb, Maya, Tia, Amenemone and their neighbours, in orderly rows that suggest an administered cemetery, plots allocated rather than seized. In the Bubasteion cliff to the north-east, high officials cut rock tombs into the escarpment, in two levels, in the second half of the 18th Dynasty and the 19th. Between and around them, lesser shafts and the burials of the less exalted fill the ground. It is the second field's later reuse — as a cat cemetery — that preserved and concealed it in equal measure.

XII. Horemheb at Saqqara — The General's Tomb before the King's

Horemheb built his Saqqara tomb when he was not yet king: he was the army's commander and the childless Tutankhamun's deputy, and the tomb is a general-official's monument of the largest class — forecourt, courtyards, statue room and chapel, with reliefs of military campaigns, foreign prisoners presented to the king, and the funeral rites of his first wife, Amenia, who was buried here. When Horemheb succeeded as the last king of the 18th Dynasty, he built a royal tomb in the Valley of the Kings and was buried there. The Saqqara tomb remained in use for his family — his second wife, the queen Mutnedjmet, is generally held to have been buried in it — and became a focus of his posthumous cult. Two tombs, two statuses, one man: Saqqara kept the official, Thebes the king, and the division is the New Kingdom necropolis in miniature.

The tomb's modern history is a parable of the site. Lepsius recorded parts of it in the 1840s; relief blocks were dispersed to museums in Europe and America; the tomb itself was lost under sand. In 1975 the joint expedition of the Egypt Exploration Society and the Rijksmuseum van Oudheden, Leiden — directed by Geoffrey T. Martin and Hans D. Schneider — began work south of the Unas causeway looking, in the first instance, for the lost tomb of Maya. The first great tomb they uncovered, in that first season, was Horemheb's. Excavation and restoration continued through the following seasons; the tomb's reliefs, reassembled in part from blocks returned on loan or identified in museum stores, are published in the mission's excavation memoirs, and Martin's The Hidden Tombs of Memphis (1991) remains the accessible account of the whole rediscovery.

The publication point is not a courtesy detail. Horemheb's Saqqara tomb had been published once already, in fragments, by the nineteenth century — and the fragments, divorced from plan and context, misled as much as they informed. The modern memoirs re-established the monument as a building: phases, additions after the owner's accession, the burials of two wives, later intrusions. That sequence — record, dispersal, loss, rediscovery, republication — is the standard biography of a major Saqqara tomb, and Sections XIII and XXII generalise it.

XIII. Maya and Merit — Loss, Rediscovery and Publication

Maya, Overseer of the Treasury under Tutankhamun, Ay and Horemheb, was the fiscal manager of the restoration after Amarna — charged, an inscription of Horemheb's Year 8 records, with taxation for the whole country and with renewing offerings to the gods. His tomb, beside Horemheb's, is a slightly smaller and abbreviated version of his king's earlier monument: forecourt, colonnaded courtyard, statue room with the dyad of Maya and his wife Merit that had stood in Leiden since 1823, chapels, and a shaft descending some 18 metres to limestone-paved, relief-decorated burial chambers — decoration at that depth and of that quality being among the tomb's distinctions.

Lepsius partly excavated the tomb in 1843, recorded reliefs, and removed some to Berlin. Sand took the site back. The Anglo-Dutch mission searched for it from 1975; on 6 February 1986 Geoffrey Martin and Jacobus van Dijk entered its subterranean apartments and recognised, by the inscribed reliefs, that the eleven-year search had ended. The tomb was excavated and conserved through the seasons to 1991 and published in the mission's memoir series; the Leiden–Turin mission that now works the concession — the Rijksmuseum van Oudheden with the Museo Egizio, Turin, since 2015, reviewed in Weiss and Greco's account of nearly fifty years of Leiden work at Saqqara (Heritage, 2022) — continues documentation and new excavation in the same field.

Maya's tomb was unfinished, and the unfinished state is evidence. The outer courtyard received columns on its west side only; its floor stayed mud where Horemheb's was paved; its pylon was never revetted in limestone. Read with the inscriptions, the fabric tells a career overtaken by events — a treasurer whose king died, whose successor died, under whose third king the money or the years ran out. Saqqara's tombs are usually read for their decoration. The Maya memoir tradition is this article's model because it reads the building whole: architecture, inscription, burial, dispersal and recovery, in one account.

The south-of-the-causeway missions set the plateau's modern publication standard: a preliminary report for the season, a full excavation memoir for the monument — architecture, reliefs and inscriptions, objects, human and animal remains, pottery — and a synthetic account for general readers. The sequence takes years, sometimes decades, per tomb. It is the reason this field of the necropolis can be written about at the strength this article attempts, and the measure of what is missing for the many Saqqara monuments, ancient and modern, that never received it (Section XXIV).

One distinction of names, because the cliff and the causeway are both in play in this article: the treasurer is Maya (male, wife Merit), buried south of the Unas causeway. The wet-nurse of Tutankhamun is Maia (female), buried in the Bubasteion cliff, in a tomb found by Alain Zivie's French mission in 1996 and opened to visitors in December 2015. The two are contemporaries, near-namesakes, and unrelated in any way the record shows. Saqqara literature confuses them often enough that the archive states the distinction wherever both appear.

XIV. The Serapeum — The Apis Burial as Procedure

The Serapeum is the burial place of the Apis bulls of Memphis, and it is treated here as what it was: a working institution with a procedure, a staff, a budget and a building programme, running for some fourteen centuries. The god Serapis, whose name the Greek form of the place carries, and the Alexandrian Serapeum with which he is chiefly associated, are treated in Alexandria, including that article's account of the Osiris-Apis root of the cult. This section does not duplicate it. The Saqqara Serapeum is the older, Memphite, bovine institution from which the Alexandrian theology in part descends: not a temple of Serapis in the Ptolemaic sense, but the cemetery of the living god of Memphis.

The Apis was a single bull, identified by prescribed markings, installed in the temple of Ptah at Memphis with a court and a harem of cows, honoured in life as the herald and living image of Ptah, and — at death — mourned nationally, embalmed, and buried in the Serapeum with a state funeral. On death he was identified with Osiris, becoming Osiris-Apis. A successor was sought, identified by the markings, and installed; the institution's calendar was thus driven by bovine lifespans, and its records — birth, installation and death dates, reign by reign — became, in the nineteenth century, a backbone of Egyptian chronology.

The Procedure, Step by Step

1. Death and proclamation. The bull's death was a public event. Herodotus and later classical authors describe national mourning; the Egyptian record is administrative — the death date entered, the embalming commissioned, the burial vault ordered. The commemorative stelae set up in the galleries, hundreds of which Mariette recovered, record the bull's dates and the reigning king with a bookkeeper's regularity.

2. Embalming. The Apis was embalmed as a king was — washed, eviscerated, dried and wrapped — at a scale no human embalming approached. The procedure's craft is Mummification's subject and is not re-derived here. What belongs to Saqqara is the logistics: a bull's body, of several hundred kilograms, moved from Memphis to a preparation place at the desert edge, processed over the ritual period, and transported, wrapped and coffined, to the vault. The embalming house of the Apis stood in the temple quarter at Memphis, and the funeral procession from city to plateau was among the great public ceremonies of the Memphite year.

3. The funeral and interment. The wrapped bull, in its coffin, was carried up to the Serapeum, along the dromos — the sphinx-lined sacred way Strabo describes and Mariette followed — and lowered and drawn into the gallery system to the vault prepared for it. Offerings, equipment and the canopic provision of the bull accompanied it; the vault was then sealed, and in the developed system the sarcophagus chamber was blocked and the event stela set into the gallery wall.

4. Record and succession. The stelae, the Serapeum's archive in stone, close the procedure: dates of birth, installation and death, the length of the bull's life, the king in whose reign each event fell. It is because the institution kept its own records, in its own walls, that its history can be reconstructed reign by reign from the New Kingdom to the Ptolemies — at least sixty Apis burials are attested across that span.

The earliest burials, from the reign of Amenhotep III in the 18th Dynasty, were individual tombs. Under the 19th Dynasty — the reorganisation is credited above all to Prince Khaemwaset, son of Ramesses II, the same prince who restored Unas's pyramid — the burials were gathered into a communal gallery system, the Lesser Vaults. From the 26th Dynasty, under Psamtik I, the Greater Vaults were cut: the system the visitor now enters. The institution grew by accretion, gallery by gallery, bull by bull, and its plan is its history drawn in rock.

XV. The Serapeum — Vaults, Sarcophagi and the Engineering Problem

The Greater Vaults are a single rock-cut corridor system, extended under the Ptolemies to roughly 350 metres of gallery with a parallel service tunnel, off which open vaulted side chambers. In twenty-four of them stand the great sarcophagi: boxes of granite, basalt and other hard stones, in the developed Late Period and Ptolemaic system weighing, with their lids, up to about 62 tonnes — most boxes around 40 tonnes without the lid, lids of some 25 tonnes. Three bear dedications naming Amasis II, Cambyses II and the rebel king Khabash. One was found abandoned in the gallery, never installed in a chamber — the institution's own unfinished work, left where the hauling stopped.

The installation method is partly recoverable and is stated here at that strength. The sarcophagi were manoeuvred on rollers, levers and winches along the gallery; to set a box down into its final position in a chamber, the chamber was first filled with sand, the box drawn in over the sand at gallery level, and the sand then gradually removed so that the mass settled, controllably, into place. The technique is elegant because it converts the hardest problem — lowering forty tonnes in a confined rock chamber, without a crane that could bear it — into a problem of removing sand, which any workforce can do slowly and reversibly. Claims of unattainable precision or of methods beyond the documented Egyptian repertoire are not supported by the galleries themselves, which show competent, difficult, fully human engineering, including its failures: the abandoned box is part of the evidence.

Mariette entered the vaults in November 1851 (Section XXII). He found the system comprehensively robbed: of the dozens of burials, only two undisturbed Apis burials survived, in the older vaults. The sarcophagi remained because they could not be removed; the bulls' bodies, jewellery and equipment were taken in antiquity, and the superstructures above the galleries were in large part dismantled for stone. What the Serapeum preserves, therefore, is not treasure but system — gallery, chamber, box, stela — and for the history of an institution, system is the better evidence.

THE LESSER VAULTS

The 19th Dynasty gallery system and its Third Intermediate Period continuation: smaller chambers, wooden coffins alongside stone, and the two intact burials Mariette recovered. The Lesser Vaults are where the Serapeum's chronology is densest, stela by stela.

THE MOTHERS OF THE APIS

The cows that bore Apis bulls were themselves sacred and were buried in their own catacomb — the Iseum of the classical sources — north of the Serapeum proper. The institution honoured the god's mother as it honoured the god; the mothers' vaults complete the Serapeum as a family cemetery of a single, endlessly reincarnated individual.

The Serapeum closed at the beginning of the Roman period, after 30 BCE — no successor bull was installed and buried in the old system under Roman rule, though the Apis cult itself continued at Memphis in other forms. The galleries were then left to the sand, the robbers and, in time, to Strabo's description, which preserved the dromos above ground long enough for Mariette to read the landscape by it eighteen centuries later.

XVI. The Anubieion and the Catacombs of Anubis

At the eastern edge of North Saqqara, where the plateau meets the cultivation, stood the Anubieion: the temple enclosure of Anubis, jnpw [Gardiner: M17-N35-Q3-G43-E16] [𓇋𓈖𓊪𓅱𓃣], lord of the sacred land, the embalming god — imy-wt, He Who Is in the Place of Embalming, in the epithets this archive's Anubis article sets out. The enclosure, excavated in part by Quibell in the early twentieth century and investigated since, contained temples, priestly housing, animal burial in its own grounds, and the surface installations of the dog catacombs behind it: the temple was the catacombs' front office, where the votive business of the cult was transacted.

The Catacombs of Anubis themselves are a gallery system cut into the plateau, reusing in part the shafts of earlier tombs, with a main axial corridor of about 173 metres and forty-nine side galleries spanning some 140 metres at the widest. They were known to the nineteenth century — Jacques de Morgan's 1897 map of the Memphite necropolis marks two dog catacombs here — but they were first systematically surveyed and sampled by the Cardiff mission of Paul T. Nicholson, with Salima Ikram and Steve Mills, whose report appeared in Antiquity in 2015.

The numbers require the honesty this article has promised, because they are the most quoted and the most easily misunderstood on the plateau. Nicholson, Ikram and Mills estimated, from the volume of the galleries and the density of animal remains in sampled deposits, that the catacombs once held on the order of 7.7 million animals — about seven million if the central corridor is excluded from the calculation. That is a modelled estimate of the original deposits at their fullest, built on stated assumptions about how full the galleries were. It is not a count of surviving mummies. What survives is overwhelmingly fragmentary — bone, wrappings and disturbed deposit, the galleries having been robbed, cleared and collapsed in places — and the identifiable animals are mostly domestic dogs, very many of them puppies of a few weeks or months, with jackals, foxes, cats, mongooses and a few falcons among them.

The interpretation follows from the ages. These were not, in the main, pets or temple animals honoured in old age. They were votive animals: bred, in large numbers and at short lives, so that a pilgrim could purchase a mummified dog, dedicate it to Anubis, and have it interred in the god's catacomb as a permanent prayer. The industry behind that sentence — breeding, killing, rapid embalming by the wt [Gardiner: G43-X1] [𓅱𓏏], sale, procession, burial, and the priestly administration that the Archive of Hor documents for the neighbouring ibis cult — is treated at the craft level in Mummification, Section XVII of which this section is the site-level companion to. The catacombs flourished from the Late Period, above all the 26th Dynasty onward, into the Roman period — the age when Saqqara's animal cults were, by volume, the largest religious enterprise on the plateau.

The Anubieion quarter also held, in its temples and chapels, the plateau's healing and incubation cults in their Memphite form — the deified Imhotep's sanctuary, the Greek Asklepieion of the sources, stood in this northern sacred quarter, and the dream-incubation practice attached to it is treated in that article and in Dream Incubation. North Saqqara in the Late and Ptolemaic periods was not a quiet cemetery with temples attached. It was a pilgrimage town of the dead and the animals of the gods, with the living — priests, embalmers, breeders, vendors, pilgrims — outnumbering, on any festival day, any population the plateau had carried since the Old Kingdom.

XVII. The Bubasteion — Bastet, Cats, and Reused New Kingdom Tombs

The Bubasteion is the sanctuary of Bastet — bst [Gardiner: D58-S29-X1] [𓃀𓋴𓏏] — set into the cliff at Saqqara's eastern edge, south-east of the Teti pyramid and south of the Anubieion, within an enclosure some 275 by 325 metres. Bastet was honoured here, from the New Kingdom, as Lady of Ankhtawy, a Memphite title; the developed temple complex is Ptolemaic and Roman in its surviving form. Its Arabic name, Abwab el-Qotat — the Gates of the Cats — records what the place became.

The Bubasteion's archaeology is a double exposure, and Alain-Pierre Zivie, who recognised the site's character in 1976 and founded the French Archaeological Mission of the Bubasteion (MAFB) in 1986 after first excavations in 1980, built its study on keeping the two exposures separate. The first exposure is New Kingdom: in the second half of the 18th Dynasty and in the 19th, high officials cut rock tombs in two levels into the cliff — the vizier Aperel and his family; the sculptor Thutmose; and, found in 1996, Maia, wet-nurse of Tutankhamun, whose relief of the boy king on her lap is among the most reproduced images of the reign. The second exposure is the cat cemetery: from the later first millennium BCE, the New Kingdom tombs, plundered and empty, were remodelled — propped with Ptolemaic walls and pillars where the poor rock threatened collapse — and filled with mummified cats dedicated to Bastet.

The excavated cat evidence is, by the Anubieion's standard, modest in count and rich in information: more than a hundred cat mummies in place and thousands of cat bones, across the mission's seasons, with radiography showing most cats died young, by strangulation or by a blow to the skull — the votive industry again, documented at the level of the individual animal. Cats were mummified in two manners, bound close or wrapped limb by limb with modelled ears and eyes; some lay in wooden or stone sarcophagi; some were buried with kittens, jewellery and amulets. In the tomb of the vizier Aperel, an early MAFB season recovered some two hundred cat mummies from a New Kingdom vizier's burial place — the double exposure in a single room.

Maia's tomb held the quarter's most famous animal: the skeleton, excavated in 2001 in the tomb's lower level among cat mummies and human burials, of a male lion — unbandaged but with signs of mummification, read by the excavators as a manifestation of Maahes, son of Bastet. In November 2019 the Egyptian mission working at the Bubasteion announced five further lion mummies, of young animals, among a cache that also produced the cat statues and scarab mummies of the 2018–19 seasons (Section XXIII). The lion finds matter because they are rare: Egypt's animal cults ran to millions of small votives and a handful of great beasts, and the Bubasteion, alone, documents both scales in one sanctuary.

The Bes connection belongs here, stated as this archive states it everywhere: the Bes chambers are at Saqqara, in this northern sacred quarter — incubation chambers with their hymns, attached to the healing landscape of the Anubieion and the Bubasteion — and they are a different phenomenon from the Bes oracle at Abydos, attested by Ammianus for 359 CE and by the Greek magical papyri. Conflating the two sites is the commonest error in the popular literature on Bes, and Saqqara's claim is the chambers: pilgrims slept here, in the necropolis, among the animal gods, and dreamed.

XVIII. The Other Animal Galleries — Ibis, Baboon, Falcon and the Mothers of the Apis

North Saqqara's animal necropolis was a system of parallel institutions, one per god, and the Serapeum, Anubieion and Bubasteion were only its largest members. The ibis, bird of Thoth, was buried in gallery complexes in the northern plateau — the North and South Ibis Galleries of the excavation reports — in pottery jars stacked by the thousand in side niches. Estimates for the Saqqara ibis deposits run into the millions across the system's life — a figure of about 1.75 million is commonly cited for Saqqara in the scientific literature on the sacred ibis — and, like the dog figure of Section XVI, it is a modelled estimate of original deposits, not a surviving count. The ibis cult's administration is unusually well documented: the Archive of Hor, the papers of a priest of Thoth and of the ibis cult in the 2nd century BCE, records feedings, burials, disputes and dreams, and is this archive's recurring witness that the catacombs were run by literate, anxious, record-keeping men.

The baboon, Thoth's second animal, had its own gallery near the ibis system, its mummies set in niches and coffers; the falcon, bird of Horus, had a smaller gallery of its own, its birds buried in jars as the ibis were. Cows — the Mothers of the Apis — had their catacomb north of the Serapeum (Section XV). Each gallery system had its temple above, its priesthood, its breeding or collecting supply chain, its festival calendar and its accounts. Taken together they constitute a second city at Saqqara, contemporary with the first: the human necropolis of mastabas and shaft tombs, and interwoven with it, at far greater numerical scale, the necropolis of the gods' animals.

Two disciplines govern any honest account of that second city. The first is arithmetic discipline, already applied above: every large number in this literature is an extrapolation from sampled density to gallery volume, and the responsible form of each claim names its authors, its method and its status as estimate. The second is supply discipline. Millions of votive animals imply an industry — puppy and kitten breeding at scale, wild ibis netted in the Delta marshes (the DNA evidence, from Wasef and colleagues' 2019 study of sacred ibis mummies, points to wild-caught birds held briefly rather than to long-term ibis farms), workshops, jar-makers, linen, natron and resin supply, and a sales operation at the temple gate. The animal necropolis is among the largest religious economies of the ancient world that can be reconstructed from its rubbish, and Saqqara is where that reconstruction is furthest advanced.

It is also where the practice's end is clearest. The galleries fill, reign by reign, into the Roman period; the latest deposits thin; the temples above them were dismantled in late antiquity and their blocks reused, some in the monastery of Section XXI. What closed the animal necropolis was not a single edict but the long Christianisation of Egypt's public cults across the fourth and fifth centuries — the same transformation that emptied the Serapeum's Alexandrian counterpart in 391, told in Alexandria. At Saqqara the transition left no destruction layer. It left full galleries, abandoned temples, and, presently, monks building among them.

XIX. Late Period Shaft Tombs — Depth as Defence

The Late Period gave Saqqara its most extreme human architecture: the great shaft tombs of the 26th (Saite) Dynasty and the Persian period that followed. Their premise is robbery, learned from three thousand years of it — much of it, by then, on this plateau. If a burial cannot be hidden, it can be made expensive to reach: sink the access shaft tens of metres through rock, build the burial chamber as a freestanding vaulted stone structure at its bottom, set the sarcophagus inside it, and then fill the shaft with sand. A robber must re-excavate the tomb to rob it. Some did. The depth is the lock.

The type is best documented south of the Unas pyramid, where Alexandre Barsanti, working for Gaston Maspero, uncovered a cluster of Saite sarcophagus-tombs in campaigns of 1899–1903 — among them the tombs of the officials Psamtik, Tjanehebu (Djenhebu), Padiniset and Hekaemsaf — and where Zaki Saad added the tomb of Amuntefnakht in 1940–41. The three principal tombs share a single main shaft some 9 by 8 metres in section and about 30 metres deep, dividing at the bottom into separate vaulted burial apartments built of limestone blocks, with niches in their walls. Their occupants were among the highest officials of the last Saite reigns — Amuntefnakht under Apries, Psamtik under Amasis — and their tombs date to the end of the 26th Dynasty and the opening of the 27th, the Persian dynasty: the shaft system was built by Egyptians of the Saite restoration and used straight through the conquest.

The Saqqara Saite Tombs Project, launched in 2016 at the University of Tübingen under Ramadan B. Hussein (1971–2022), re-excavated and undertook the epigraphic documentation of these tombs to modern standard — the necessary second discovery, a century after Barsanti, of monuments whose first publication no longer sufficed. The project's work, and the joint Egyptian–German mission's, produced the plateau's most instructive recent complex in the same ground: the Saite–Persian embalming workshop and communal burial shaft (K24), announced in July 2018.

The workshop, a surface and subterranean installation south of the Unas pyramid, is the building behind Mummification's chemistry. Its embalming cachette — a shaft some 13 metres deep ending in a chamber — held a large corpus of pottery: bowls, beakers and measuring cups inscribed with the names of oils and substances, the labelled vessels whose residue analyses (Rageot and colleagues, Nature, 2023) identified the mixtures the embalmers actually used, including ꜥntjw — antiu — as a manufactured conifer-oil-and-fat preparation, not myrrh, a correction this archive applies throughout. Beside the workshop, shaft K24, about 3 by 3.5 metres and 30 metres deep, opened into burial chambers holding, on the mission's announcement, some fifty-four mummies and skeletons across six tomb units — among them a mummy wearing a gilded silver mask, a priest of Mut, in a wooden coffin painted with the goddess Nut. Workshop, cachette and communal shaft together are the Late Period funerary industry in one excavation: manufacture, materials science and mass burial, administered from one yard.

Elsewhere the type repeats at every scale the period could afford: the rock-cut cliff tomb of the vizier Bakenrenef under Psamtik I, east of the Step Pyramid, its poor rock lined with imported better stone and its vaulted, pillared hall's ceiling now in part in the Metropolitan Museum; the shaft tombs around the Teti pyramid; and, at the period's popular end, the plain pit and shaft burials by the thousand that fill every open metre of the plateau — including the stacked wooden-coffin shafts of the 2020 announcements (Section XXIII), which are the great-shaft principle democratised: many modest burials, one deep shaft, sand as the seal.

XX. The Persian Phase — Conquest Written in Tombs and Stelae

Egypt fell to Cambyses II in 525 BCE, and Saqqara is one of the places where the conquest can be read without rhetoric. Cambyses has a sarcophagus dedication in the Serapeum's Greater Vaults (Section XV): the conqueror buried an Apis in the full Egyptian form, at the full Egyptian expense, in the reign's opening years. The Serapeum stelae of his reign record the burial with the institution's usual bookkeeping. Whatever Cambyses did or did not do elsewhere in Egypt — the hostile tradition in Herodotus, of the king who stabbed the Apis, is contradicted by the Serapeum's own record and is not followed by this archive — at Saqqara the documentary evidence shows a Persian king performing the Memphite rite correctly, because the institution that performed it had every reason to keep its procedure and its new patron.

The shaft tombs of Section XIX tell the same story from the officials' side. Padiniset's tomb, latest of the Unas cluster, belongs to the opening of the 27th Dynasty; its owner's father is named as a Psamtik — the Saite official class continuing, by name and by office, under Persian rule. The embalming workshop complex is dated by its excavators to the Saite–Persian period as a whole, 664–404 BCE in the announcement's framing: the industry did not pause for the conquest. Memphis was a Persian administrative capital, its satrap resident, its temples taxed and watched — and its necropolis, as ever, open for business.

The second Persian occupation, after the native dynasties of the fourth century, ended with Alexander in 332 BCE, and the Ptolemaic age at Saqqara is, in the main, the age of the animal necropolis at full stretch (Sections XVI–XVIII) and of the Serapeum's last, longest galleries. The Persian phase's specific legacy is documentary: stelae in demotic and in Aramaic — the empire's administrative language, spoken by a garrison and merchant community at Memphis — and a necropolis record in which Egyptians, and presently Greeks, Carians, Phoenicians and Jews of the Memphite garrison towns, bury side by side on the old plateau. Saqqara had been Memphis's cemetery for two thousand years when the Persians came. It absorbed them, as it had absorbed every previous holder of the city, by the simple means of burying them.

XXI. The Coptic Phase — The Monastery of Apa Jeremias

The last community to live at Saqqara, rather than to bury or to dig there, was Christian. The Monastery of Apa Jeremias (Dayr Apa Jeremiah) stood at the plateau's western edge, its northern boundary at the Unas causeway, built among and out of the ancient tombs. The Coptic Encyclopedia's entry, built on J. E. Quibell's excavation volumes of 1907–1913, gives the frame this article follows: the foundation is conventionally set around 470 CE, in the founder's legendary connection with the future emperor Anastasius; the surviving remains are not demonstrably older than the middle of the sixth century; the monastery was in full operation until the middle of the eighth; and it was abandoned probably around the middle of the ninth, though Quibell's own wider frame — coins from Anastasius to the early Abbasids, a Muslim visitor's graffito of 960 CE on a pillar in the ruins — allows the site's afterlife a longer tail. The discrepancy between the encyclopedia's mid-ninth-century abandonment and older summaries that end the monastery in the mid-tenth is noted here rather than resolved: the dated evidence thins, and both endings are readings of it.

Quibell excavated the monastery principally in 1908–10 and published it in Excavations at Saqqara (1908–9, 1909–10): The Monastery of Apa Jeremias (1912), with the Coptic inscriptions edited by Herbert Thompson. What he found was one of the few archaeologically recoverable examples of a coenobitic monastery — monks living not as scattered hermits but densely, in large communal buildings: a main church, rebuilt after at least one destruction, with further churches, refectory, cells in their hundreds by the excavator's numbering, painted chapels, and an extensive cemetery of the monks themselves. The inscriptions, paintings and sculpture — much of it now in the Coptic Museum in Cairo — document a literate community praying, in Coptic, in rooms built from pharaonic blocks, some of them within sight of the Step Pyramid.

The monastery's relation to the necropolis was practical and theological at once. Practically, the plateau offered building stone, ready-cut chambers, solitude at walking distance from the villages, and land no farmer wanted. Theologically, the desert of the dead was the desert of the fathers: to live among the tombs was to live in the geography of resurrection, and the monks' neighbours, in their own understanding, included the justified dead of three thousand years and the demons who haunted their empty houses. The archive's Coptic article treats the language and the wider world; here the point is the site's. Saqqara's last ancient chapter was not a burial at all. It was a community that moved into the cemetery and lived there for four centuries, at prayer among the mastabas — the longest single occupation the plateau ever housed.

After the abandonment, the plateau reverted to what it had been between its ages: a quarry, a landmark, a source of stone, lime and treasure-stories for the villages below, visited by medieval travellers and, from the seventeenth century, by European ones. The monastery itself was buried by sand so thoroughly that Quibell's men dug it as archaeology, not as a ruin. The necropolis had entered its longest sleep since the Early Dynastic age — and, this time, the next people to come to it in numbers came with notebooks.

XXII. Looting and Excavation — From Mariette Onward

Looting is not an episode in Saqqara's history. It is a continuous condition, coeval with burial itself: Old Kingdom chapels usurped in the New Kingdom, New Kingdom tombs emptied for the catacombs, catacombs cleared for the antiquities market, and, in the nineteenth century, a traffic in mummies, coffins and reliefs that fed every museum in Europe. The honest excavation history begins by admitting that excavation, for its first century, was one of the forms the traffic took — licensed, better recorded, and continuous with the market it also supplied.

Auguste Mariette is the hinge. Sent to Egypt in 1850 to acquire Coptic manuscripts, he recognised, in a sphinx head standing above the dunes of North Saqqara, the avenue Strabo describes before the Memphite Serapeum. He dug along the sphinxes' line, uncovered the dromos, and in November 1851 entered the Serapeum vaults. The discovery — the stelae, the two intact Apis burials, the thousands of objects of the cult — founded his career and, through the Antiquities Service he went on to direct from 1858, the institutional framework within which all later Saqqara work stands. It was also, by modern standards, a clearance conducted at speed, with gunpowder used on obstructions and with context lost that no later season can recover. Both sentences are true, and the second does not cancel the first: without Mariette's Service, there would have been considerably less Saqqara left for anyone to excavate carefully.

The roll of the plateau's excavators is, in sequence, a history of the discipline's methods. Mariette's men opened the mastaba of Ti (1865) and the Serapeum. Gaston Maspero, his successor, entered the inscribed pyramids in 1880–81 and gave the world the Pyramid Texts. Victor Loret cleared royal tombs around the Teti pyramid in 1897–99. Barsanti found the Saite shafts (Section XIX). James Quibell, chief inspector at Saqqara from 1905, excavated the monastery, the tomb of Hesy-Re, and archaic mastabas in long, well-published seasons. Cecil Firth and then Jean-Philippe Lauer took the Step Pyramid complex — Lauer for the better part of a working lifetime, restoring by anastylosis the colonnade, chapels and wall stretches the visitor now sees, and establishing the construction sequence of Section V. Gustave Jéquier worked the south. Walter Emery dug the Early Dynastic mastabas and then, in the 1960s, North Saqqara's Sacred Animal Necropolis — in explicit search, among other aims, of the tomb of Imhotep, which he did not find and which remains unfound (see Imhotep, final sections).

The modern era — from the 1970s — is the era of the long concessions and the full memoir: the Anglo-Dutch, then Leiden, then Leiden–Turin mission south of the Unas causeway (Sections XII–XIII); Zivie's French mission at the Bubasteion from 1976/1986 (Section XVII); the French mission at Saqqara-South on the Pepi I complex; the Polish-Egyptian mission west of the Step Pyramid, on the Dry Moat and the Old Kingdom and later necropolises there, from the 1980s; the Tübingen Saite Tombs Project from 2016; Cardiff at the dog catacombs; Japanese, Czech, Spanish, Italian and other missions on their concessions; and, throughout and increasingly in the lead, Egyptian missions of the Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities and the Supreme Council of Antiquities, whose announcements of 2018–2023 are Section XXIII's subject. Cairo University's work, including the long excavation of the tomb of the ambassador Wahtye's near-contemporary officials and, earlier, of tombs south of the causeway, belongs in the same sentence. Saqqara today is worked by more concurrent missions than any comparable site in Egypt, which is a measure of what remains in it.

// WHAT EXCAVATION COST, AND WHAT IT SAVED //

The ledger must be kept in both columns. Cost: the nineteenth-century dispersal of reliefs and statues from tombs then lost (Sections XI–XIII); Mariette's speed in the Serapeum; object-led digging that treated strata as spoil; animal deposits cleared, in the market years, by the ton. Saved: everything in this article. The Pyramid Texts, the Step Pyramid's sequence, the New Kingdom necropolis, the catacombs' survey — all are excavation products, and the unpublished residue of the saving is as large as the published (Section XXIV). The discipline's Saqqara standard, set by its best missions, is that a monument is not excavated until its publication is funded and staffed. The plateau's older clearances are the standing demonstration of why.

XXIII. Recent Discoveries — Reported at Their Real Strength, and Dated

Saqqara's recent announcements arrive through ministry press events, and their framing — largest, first, unique — is a genre convention of the announcement, not a finding. What follows reports the principal discoveries of the last decade at the strength of their underlying record: dated, attributed to their missions, and stripped of superlatives the evidence was not asked to carry. All of them are real. None of them needs the adjectives.

July 2018 — the embalming workshop. The Egyptian–German (Tübingen) mission announced the Saite–Persian embalming workshop and communal shaft K24 south of the Unas pyramid: cachette pottery inscribed with substance names, a gilded silver mask, and dozens of burials at 30 metres (Section XIX). The follow-through is the discovery's distinction: the labelled vessels' residues were published biomolecularly in Nature in 2023, converting a press announcement into a permanent correction of the embalming literature.

November–December 2018 — Wahtye and the Bubasteion caches. An Egyptian mission announced, in November, seven tombs at the Bubasteion edge, among them tombs reused for cat burials with dozens of cat mummies, some one hundred wooden and gilded cat statues and one bronze Bastet, and a pair of scarab-beetle mummies in limestone sarcophagi — a rare attestation of insect mummification. In December the same mission announced the 5th Dynasty tomb of Wahtye, royal purification priest under Neferirkare: a painted tomb some 10 by 3 metres, with more than fifty statues in niches, five shafts, and reliefs of the family, production and the hunt, intact in a way Old Kingdom private tombs at Saqqara almost never are. Wahtye's tomb, not its announcement, is the fact: a substantially unrobbed mid-Old Kingdom chapel, now a fixed point for the period's art.

2019 — Khuwy; the lion mummies. The tomb of the nobleman Khuwy, of the late 5th Dynasty, was announced in April 2019, its painted antechamber and its owner's mummy among the finds. In November 2019, at the Bubasteion, the Egyptian mission announced a cache including five young lion mummies (Section XVII), mummified leopard and cats, and further cat statuary — the assemblage now displayed in part at the Imhotep Museum on site.

September–November 2020 — the coffin shafts. Working at the Bubasteion-side ground, the Egyptian mission under Mostafa Waziri announced in stages — 13 coffins in September, 59 sealed coffins in October, and in November a total presented as 100 intact, sealed, painted wooden coffins of the 26th Dynasty, with some forty statues of Ptah-Sokar-Osiris and gilded funerary masks — from three shafts 10 to 12 metres deep, the coffins stacked in niches and chambers. The coffins are priestly and official burials of the Saite period, many containing mummies, distributed after announcement among the Egyptian Museum in Tahrir, the Grand Egyptian Museum, the National Museum of Egyptian Civilisation and the New Administrative Capital's museum. The find's real significance is demographic, not artistic: a whole Saite priestly stratum, buried communally and intact, available for study as a population.

January 2021 — the funerary temple of Naert. A mission led by Zahi Hawass, working beside the Teti pyramid, announced the funerary temple of Naert (Naarat), a wife of King Teti whose name and temple had not previously been known, with mudbrick magazines, burials of the New Kingdom in its ground, and further coffins and shabtis — shabtis of the standard types. The announcement extends the Teti complex's plan; its full publication is awaited, and this article reports it as announced.

May 2022 — the Bubasteion cache. The Egyptian mission announced some 250 Late Period coffins with mummies and 150 bronze statuettes of gods — among them Anubis, jnpw [Gardiner: M17-N35-Q3-G43-E16] [𓇋𓈖𓊪𓅱𓃣], Bastet, bst [Gardiner: D58-S29-X1] [𓃀𓋴𓏏] in her cat form, and Osiris, wsir [Gardiner: Q1-D4-A40] [𓊨𓁹𓀭] — from the Bubasteion cemetery ground, with papyri fragments reported among the finds. January 2023 brought, from a separate mission at Gisr el-Mudir's side of the plateau, four Old Kingdom tombs and a mummy covered in gold leaf, of a man named Hekashepes, dated by the mission to about 4,300 years ago; and 2023–25 has continued the pattern — among the announcements, in April 2025, the tomb of a 5th Dynasty prince, son of Userkaf, with a monumental false door. Each is reported here as its mission announced it; peer-reviewed publication, where it exists, is what Sections I–XXII rest on, and the distinction between announced and published is maintained deliberately.

// ON THE MINISTER'S ONE PER CENT //

At the November 2020 announcement, the minister stated that only one per cent of Saqqara's antiquities had been discovered. The figure is rhetoric, not measurement — no census of the undiscovered exists against which a percentage could be computed — and it is quoted in the press as if it were a survey result. This article records it as what it is: an official's way of saying, truly, that the plateau is nowhere near exhausted. The supportable version is in Section XXIV: most of Saqqara has never been excavated to modern standard, and significant zones have never been excavated at all.

XXIV. Conservation and What Remains Unpublished

Saqqara's conservation problems are the physical form of its history. The Step Pyramid's core — small blocks, inward-leaning accretion layers, voids left by ancient and modern tunnelling — required the long consolidation programme that preceded its 2020 reopening, and its subterranean galleries remain a monitored structure, not a solved one. The Serapeum's galleries contend with rock instability and with the salt and humidity load of mass visitation. Painted tombs across the plateau — Wahtye's and Maya's burial chambers among them — face the standard enemies in acute form: bat colonies, salt crystallisation from rising damp, earlier restorations ageing badly, and the microclimate shock of opening a sealed chamber to the modern air. The dog catacombs have suffered gallery collapses that are simultaneously a conservation emergency and a sampling opportunity, and the Cardiff report treats them as both.

Groundwater is the threat with a trend. Modern irrigation has raised the water table across the Memphite floodplain; low galleries and deep shafts that were dry for millennia now stand nearer to damp than at any time in their excavated history. Against it stand the unglamorous instruments: drainage, monitoring, documentation by photogrammetry and laser scanning — the Leiden–Turin mission's current work is as much digital recording as digging — and the site museum, the Imhotep Museum at the plateau's entrance, renewed and reopened in 2023, which holds the site's finds in the site's climate rather than dispersing them.

The larger debt is publication. For every Saqqara monument with a modern memoir — the causeway tombs, the Bubasteion series, the Saite shafts now in progress — there are clearances, some of them major, that exist only as preliminary notes, museum registers and archives: much of Mariette's and his successors' nineteenth-century work; stretches of Quibell's and Emery's seasons whose full records rest in excavation archives, including the Quibell papers now in Milan; animal deposits removed before sampling existed. The field's own verdict, repeated in its mission reports, is that re-excavation of the record — archive work, object rejoining, memoir completion — is now as productive as new digging on this plateau. A Saqqara article written in fifty years will rest, in significant part, on the publication of work already done.

What remains in the ground can be stated without the minister's percentage. Geophysical survey keeps returning anomalies — walls, shafts, enclosures — in ground no spade has entered, Gisr el-Mudir's interior among them. The tomb of Imhotep, sought since Emery, is unlocated. Whole zones — between the known clusters, beneath the later cemeteries, under the sand sheets west of the enclosure — are blank on the excavation map not because they are empty but because they are unexamined. The necropolis that served Memphis for three thousand years has been dug, intensively, for one hundred and seventy-five. The proportion the visitor should carry away is not one per cent. It is that Saqqara is an archive still mostly closed — in the ground, in the stores, and in the unpublished record alike — and that every section of this article is a summary of the opened fraction.

The heart, ib [Gardiner: F34] [𓄣], of the matter is the one the Egyptians themselves stated, in the offering formula carved on a thousand Saqqara chapel walls: the dead endure while their names are spoken and their places kept. The plateau's three thousand years of burial, and its two centuries of excavation, are a single long attempt — by priests, by families, by monks, by missions — to keep the places. The archive's work, and this article's, is the current form of the same obligation: to record what the keeping has found, at the strength the evidence bears, in the place where the next reader will look for it.
