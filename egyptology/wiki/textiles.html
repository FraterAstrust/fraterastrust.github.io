I. Words for Cloth — Linen in the Egyptian Vocabulary

Linen was not one thing in Egyptian, and the vocabulary already grades it. The general word for cloth, for a length or sheet of woven linen, is mnḫt [Gardiner: G17-N35-Aa1-X1] [𓅓𓈖𓐍𓏏] — the word the embalming texts use for the sheets laid over a body, and the word the temple accounts use for the woven product of estate flax, in grades from work-cloth to the god's own garments (see the temple laboratory, where the linen room stands beside the laboratory at Edfu). A garment, clothing as worn, is ḥbs [Gardiner: V28-D58-S29] [𓎛𓃀𓋴]. At the top of the scale stands šs-nswt [Gardiner: N37-S29-A43-X1] [𓈙𓋴𓀲𓏏], "royal linen" — literally the fine linen of the king — the grade the Ritual of Embalming reserves for particular stations of the body and the grade this article's thread counts (Section XIII) try to put numbers on. Bandage words belong to the same family of terms: a bandage is sbn [Gardiner: S29-D58-N35] [𓋴𓃀𓈖], one of several wrapping words the mummification article sets out in full.

Two cautions govern the vocabulary of this article, and both are corrections the modern literature has forced. The first concerns the word "spinning." Older translations and museum labels describe Egyptian thread as "spun," and Egyptian workers with spindles as "spinners," as a matter of routine. The fibre evidence says otherwise for pharaonic Egypt: the thread was made by splicing (Section VII), and the spindle's real job was plying (Section VIII). This article therefore uses "spinning" only when reporting what an older source says, and flags it when it does. The second caution concerns fineness words. Egyptian grade terms — royal linen, fine linen, close-woven linen and their companions in the lists — are real administrative categories, but no surviving text defines them by a thread count. Where this article gives counts, they are modern measurements of surviving cloth, set beside the ancient words as correspondences, not as definitions.

Every technical Egyptian term in this article carries its Gardiner sign codes, and every bracketed glyph string has been composed sign by sign from the archive's V2 Gardiner map, so that the printed transliteration is exactly what the signs decode to. Where a post-audit correction supersedes the map's draft value, the corrected value is used: F13 = wp, M44 = spd, O31 = ꜥꜣ, O33 = srḫ, I14 = ḳꜣb, R20 and R21 = sšꜣt, F21 = msḏr, U38 = mḫꜣt, E10 = bꜣ, E8a = ꞽb. The full sign list is given in the hieroglyph reference.

A last preliminary, parallel to the one the archive's glass article makes. No Egyptian technical manual for weaving survives — no recipe papyrus for cloth, no workshop handbook. The procedural account that follows is reconstructed from four kinds of evidence, kept labelled as it goes: the cloth itself, surviving in Egypt's dry tombs in quantities no other ancient culture can match, and now read fibre by fibre under the microscope; depictions, above all the Middle Kingdom tomb scenes and workshop models that show the chain in sequence; tools and workshops, excavated at settlements such as Amarna, Gurob and Deir el-Medina; and experiment, the modern re-running of the process by the EGYARN project and other textile researchers, which has corrected the textbook account at its most fundamental step. Where a step is a reconstruction rather than an attestation, the text says so.

II. The Evidence — Tomb Scenes, Workshop Models and Surviving Cloth

Textile production is the best-depicted craft sequence in Middle Kingdom art. The tombs at Beni Hasan — Baqet III (no. 15), Khety (no. 17) and Khnumhotep II (no. 3) — show the chain in register: flax pulled in the field, fibre stripped from the stem, splicing performed on the thigh, plying with spindle and bowl, warping, and weaving at the ground loom, with laundering scenes in the same repertoire. Parallel scenes occur at Deshasheh, Meir and Sheikh Said. These are not generic "daily life" decoration to be waved at; read in sequence, and checked against the cloth, they are the closest thing Egypt left to a process manual, and this article cites them step by step.

The Meketra workshop model (c. 1950 BCE), from the Theban tomb of the chancellor Meketra and now in the Metropolitan Museum, gives the same industry in three dimensions: a weaving workshop with ground looms, warping in progress, and the stores of a great household's cloth production rendered as a staffed room. A model is evidence of organisation as much as of technique — it shows who stood where, how many looms a household ran, and that cloth-making was an establishment, not an afterthought.

Above both stands the cloth itself. Egypt's tombs preserve textiles by the tens of thousands, from Neolithic fragments in the Fayum (c. 5000 BCE) to complete New Kingdom wardrobes. Two survivals anchor this article's ends of the story. At the beginning, the Tarkhan dress (Petrie Museum, UC28614B): a tailored, V-necked linen garment with pleated sleeves, excavated by Flinders Petrie at Tarkhan in 1913 from a First Dynasty mastaba, recognised as a garment only in 1977 when the bundle it lay in was sent for conservation, and radiocarbon-dated at Oxford in 2015 to 3482–3102 BCE at 95% probability (Stevenson and colleagues, Antiquity, 2016) — the oldest woven garment known, made before or at the very cusp of the First Dynasty. At the industry's height, the linen chests of the tomb of Hatnefer and Ramose at Thebes, excavated by the Metropolitan Museum in 1935–36, held folded sheets of every grade from coarse mattress cloth to the sheer "royal linen" of Section XIII — a household's whole cloth wealth, buried as funerary provision.

The modern evidence has a fourth leg, and it is the one that changed the field. The EGYARN project (Centre for Textile Research, University of Copenhagen; grant 890144), directed by Chiara Spinazzi-Lucchesi, set out to reconnect New Kingdom spinning tools to New Kingdom textiles by integrating textile, tool, fibre and textual study, with Gurob and above all Deir el-Medina as its case-study assemblages — and, crucially, by doing the work: its experimental programme, carried out with the weaver and spinner Marie Wallenberg and published in the EXARC Journal, rebuilt the splicing process variable by variable and documented its failures as carefully as its successes. Where this article reports a splice angle, a moisture problem or a retting degree, it is reporting that experimental record, not a plausible guess.

// THE SHAPE OF THE EVIDENCE //

c. 5000 BCE — woven flax fragments in the Neolithic Fayum. c. 3600 BCE — a Predynastic platter from Badari (tomb 3802) depicts a horizontal loom: the loom is older than the dynasties. 3482–3102 BCE — the Tarkhan dress, tailored clothing at the dawn of the state. Middle Kingdom — the Beni Hasan sequence and the Meketra model: the full chain depicted. c. 1425 BCE — the vertical loom first depicted (Section XI). c. 1491–1473 BCE — Hatnefer's chests: royal linen at its measured finest. Until at least c. 600 BCE — splicing remains the pharaonic yarn technology (Section VII).

III. The Flax Plant — Cultivation and the Field Year

The plant is Linum usitatissimum — the name itself, "most useful," is earned twice over in Egypt, since the same crop gives fibre from its stem and oil from its seed. The fibre is a bast fibre: it lies in the phloem, the inner bark surrounding the stem's hollow woody core, in bundles of some twenty to forty individual fibres glued together by pectins (the anatomy as set out in the EGYARN experimental literature). Everything in the production chain follows from that anatomy. The worker's problem is never the fibre itself, which is long, strong and fine; it is the glue and the woody core that hold it captive, and every preparation step is a negotiation with pectin.

Flax fitted the Nile year as grain did. It was sown on the basin fields as the inundation water drained away in the autumn, grew through the cool season, and was harvested in the spring, roughly three to four months after sowing — a winter crop in a summer land, occupying the same flood-recession fields as the cereals and legumes whose cycle the archive treats in Food and Drink and in the field system set out in Agriculture; this article takes the standing crop as its starting point. Flax wants water while it grows and punishes neglect with short, coarse stems: fibre length is decided in the field, months before any weaver sees the product, because the usable fibre can never be longer than the stem it grew in.

Sowing density was itself a fibre technology. Flax sown thickly grows tall, straight and unbranched — each plant racing its neighbours for light, putting its growth into stem length rather than seed heads — and tall unbranched stems are exactly what the fibre-worker wants. Flax sown for seed, by contrast, is spaced to branch and flower. Egyptian growers therefore ran, in effect, two crops under one name, and the harvest decision of Section IV — when to pull — was the second half of a choice that began at sowing: a field destined for fine cloth was managed for stem, and a field destined for linseed and oil for capsule. The two products were not rivals so much as grades of one harvest, since even a fibre crop yields seed from its rippling (Section IV), and even a seed crop yields tow-grade fibre.

Where the crop grew mattered to its reputation. The Delta, with its water and its deep soils, was flax country on the largest scale, and Delta and Fayum linens carried regional standing in the textual record; but every estate that could irrigate a flax plot did so, because cloth was a store of value no household could do without (Section XX). The temple estates grew flax as they grew grain — the temple laboratory article traces an estate system that owned the land that grew its flax and the weaving sheds that worked it, issuing finished mnḫt [Gardiner: G17-N35-Aa1-X1] [𓅓𓈖𓐍𓏏] to the god's service by grade.

THE FIBRE CROP

Sown thick, grown tall and unbranched, harvested young or half-ripe (Section IV). Product: long bast ribbons for splicing. The fine-cloth chain begins here, not at the loom.

THE SEED CROP

Sown spaced, allowed to branch, flower and ripen fully. Product: linseed for oil and next year's sowing; the stems yield only coarse, short fibre as a by-product.

THE PLANT ITSELF

A slender annual, roughly a metre tall at harvest, with a hollow woody core, an inner bark carrying the fibre bundles in pectin, and small blue flowers. Nothing about the standing plant announces cloth; the whole industry is extraction.

IV. Harvest — Pulling, Timing and Rippling

Flax for fibre was pulled, not cut. The harvester grasped a handful of stems and drew them up out of the ground roots and all, because every centimetre of stem is fibre: a cut stem throws away the length between blade and root, and in a crop whose whole value is length that waste is unaffordable. The pulling scenes in the tomb paintings show exactly this, handful by handful, the pulled plants laid in bundles to dry. It is slow work and it is the first of the chain's many labour bills — a theme this article does not let go of, because Egyptian linen's fineness was bought with time at every single step.

Timing — the Harvest Is a Grade Decision

When to pull decides what the fibre can become, and the Egyptian evidence, read with the experimental literature, supports the classic three-point scale. Pulled green, while the plant is still in or just past flower and the stems are unripe, flax gives the finest, softest, most pliable fibres — the fibres of the sheerest cloth; the Metropolitan Museum's note on the Hatnefer sheet concludes that its superfine thread must have come from flax harvested when the plants were very young (paraphrased from the Met record for 36.3.111). Pulled at half-ripeness, stems yellowing and capsules forming, the fibre is stronger and coarser, the general-purpose harvest. Pulled fully ripe, the crop maximises seed but the stems have lignified — the woody core has hardened into the fibre's enemy — and the fibre is fit for coarse cloth and cordage. A grower could not have all three from one field. The grades of Section XIII are therefore not created at the loom; they are harvested.

Rippling — Taking the Seed

Dried bundles went next to rippling: the stems were drawn through a wide-toothed comb — a rippling comb or fork — which stripped the seed capsules (bolls) from the stems without breaking the stems themselves. The capsules were collected, threshed and winnowed for linseed: for oil, for food and medicine, and for next year's sowing. Rippling is the chain's first combing operation and must not be confused with the heckling of Section VI: the ripple works on the whole stem, coarsely, to remove seed; it does not dress fibre. After rippling, the stems — now clean rods of bark and core — were bundled again for the retting decision.

The harvest sequence, stated plainly: pull by the roots; bundle; dry; ripple the seed off; keep the stems intact and sorted by length as far as the harvest allowed. Sorting matters more than it sounds. Splicing (Section VII) joins ribbons end to end, root to tip, and a worker splicing from unsorted, mixed-length material produces uneven singles no plying can rescue. The evenness of good Egyptian yarn begins as field discipline.

V. Retting — and the Egyptian Caveat

Retting is the controlled rotting of the flax stem: microbial action, in water or in dew, digests the pectins that glue the fibre bundles to the woody core and to each other, freeing the fibres for extraction. In the later European linen industry retting is the pivotal, dreaded step — under-retted flax will not separate, over-retted flax rots its own fibres — and textbooks long assumed Egypt must have retted as Europe retted, for two to three weeks, as the gateway to all that followed. The Egyptian evidence does not fit that assumption, and this section states the archive's position at its real strength: the classic full retting of the European chain does not fit pharaonic practice, because the Egyptian yarn technology depends on the pectin that full retting destroys.

The reasoning is mechanical. Egyptian thread was made by splicing ribbons — strips of bast in which the fibres still run together in their natural bundles, held by their own pectin (Section VII). A ribbon is precisely what retting is designed to abolish: ret the stem thoroughly and the bundles fall apart into individual fibres, fit for draft spinning but useless for the ribbon-splice. The EGYARN experiments tested the variable directly, working with un-retted, half-retted and fully retted material, and their programme settled on un-retted strips for the successful splicing reconstructions, with partially retted material also workable; the archive's research dossier records the complementary finding that where retting is used at all, a short retting of around ten days was experimentally optimal — a softening, not a dissolution. Light retting, in other words, may have loosened the stem enough to strip; the industry did not ret its fibre free.

What, then, freed the fibre from the core? Chiefly the hands, and water used as a softener rather than a rot-box: soaking and wetting recur in the experimental reconstructions and in the depictions' logic, and a damp ribbon is a pliable ribbon. The honest summary is threefold. Attested: Egypt produced spliced yarn from bast ribbons for three thousand years, which constrains preparation to methods that leave ribbons intact. Experimentally supported: un-retted or lightly retted stems strip and splice well; fully retted stems do not splice in the Egyptian manner. Not established: the exact ancient routine — how long Egyptian stems soaked, where, and in what water — for which no text and no excavated retting installation yet gives a certain answer. Retting pits and ponds belong to the European evidence; projecting them wholesale onto the Nile is one of the assumptions this article exists to retire.

// WHY THE CAVEAT MATTERS //

Retting is not a pedantic footnote. It is the fork in the whole technology. Ret fully, and you must spin — the fibres are individuals, and only continuous drafting and twisting can gather them. Ret lightly or not at all, and you can splice — the fibres arrive as ribbons, and the join, not the draft, makes the thread. Egypt chose the second fork and stayed on it for millennia; the choice explains the tools (Section VIII), the depictions (Section II) and the structure of the yarn itself (Section VII) in a single move.

VI. Stripping the Fibre — Breaking, Scutching and Heckling, Honestly Assessed

Between the stem and the ribbon stands the extraction step, and here the archive must be as honest about absence as about presence. The mature European flax chain interposes three mechanical operations between retting and spinning: breaking, crushing the dried stem in a hinged brake so the woody core shatters while the flexible bast survives; scutching, beating and scraping the broken stems with a blade to knock the shattered core (the shive) away; and heckling (hackling), drawing the fibre repeatedly through beds of iron pins to comb it into parallel, separated strands. The task that set this article specified "scutching/heckling" as steps of the Egyptian chain. The evidence does not support them as such, and the article says so rather than dressing a European chain in Egyptian clothes.

For pharaonic Egypt there is, on the current record, little evidence for breaking and scutching as distinct mechanical operations, and no evidence for heckling. No flax brake, scutching board or heckling comb has been identified in the pharaonic toolkit, and the depictions do not show them. What the Beni Hasan scenes show instead is the fibre being stripped from the stem by hand: the worker takes the (dried, possibly soaked) stem and peels the bast away in long ribbons, the core discarded, the ribbon kept whole. Hand-stripping is slower than breaking and scutching, and it produces exactly the feedstock splicing requires — continuous ribbons running the stem's full length, fibres still in their bundles, pectin still in place. The absence of the European machines is not a missing technology; it is the signature of a different one. A heckled, parallelised, individualised fibre supply would have been worse than useless to a splicer.

Two qualifications keep the claim honest. First, hand-stripping surely shaded into hand-dressing: ribbons could be drawn across an edge, beaten lightly, wetted and worked between the fingers to shed core fragments and split an over-wide ribbon into narrower ones — the EGYARN preparation work includes exactly such low-technology handling, and the boundary between "stripping" and a manual equivalent of scutching is a matter of definition. What is absent is the apparatus and the aim: no machine, and no intention of separating the fibres from one another. Second, the claim is period-bound. Later, foreign-ruled and Roman-period Egypt absorbed Mediterranean practice wholesale, and fibre preparation there is a different question; this article's chain is the pharaonic one, and its honesty about the missing machines is part of what makes the splicing correction of the next section unavoidable. If Egypt had heckled, it would have spun. It spliced — so it cannot have prepared fibre the heckling way.

The product of this section, then, is the ribbon: a strip of bast a stem's length long — up to the better part of a metre from a good crop — containing its twenty-to-forty-fibre bundles entire. Ribbons were kept sorted, coiled or laid in bundles, and carried to the splicer's lap. The thread is about to be made without ever existing as a loose fibre mass at all.

VII. Splicing — the Correction at the Centre of This Article

Here is the correction, stated first in one sentence and then in full. Pharaonic Egyptian linen thread was not made by draft spinning. It was made by splicing: individual bast-fibre ribbons were joined end to end, each join made by overlapping the two ribbon ends and twisting them together at the overlap alone, producing a continuous single thread whose length is the sum of its ribbons. The demonstration is modern and microscopic. Gleba and Harris, "The first plant bast fibre technology: identifying splicing in archaeological textiles" ( Archaeological and Anthropological Sciences 11, 2019, 2329–2346), defined the types of splicing and published an explicit method for identifying spliced thread in archaeological textiles, applying it across Europe, Egypt and the Near East; their conclusion, paraphrased, is that splicing — not draft spinning — was the plant-fibre thread technology of the ancient world they sampled, and that the switch to draft spinning came far later than textbooks assumed. For Egypt specifically the point had been building for decades — Fox noted the peculiarities of Egyptian yarn as early as 1910, and Barber, Cartwright and colleagues, and Kemp and Vogelsang-Eastwood's Amarna work all carried it — but Gleba and Harris gave the field its identification method and its name. Splicing remained the pharaonic technique until at least the beginning of the Persian period, c. 600 BCE (Cartwright and colleagues, as cited in the EGYARN experimental literature).

What Splicing Is, Precisely

Take two fibre ribbons. Lay them end to end, overlapping by a few centimetres, aligned root to tip — the thick root end of one against the tapering tip of the next, so the join keeps the thread's diameter even; a root end left too thick is tapered first. Then twist the two ribbons together at the overlap only — classically by rolling them under the palm on the bare thigh, exactly as the Beni Hasan painters show, the worker seated with ribbons fed from a supply beside them — and move on. The rest of each ribbon carries no twist at all. The resulting single is a chain of untwisted ribbons locked together by short, tightly twisted splices, each splice a few centimetres long. Gleba and Harris distinguish end-to-end splicing, the discrete joins just described, from continuous splicing, in which new fibre is fed in as the thread is formed; the Egyptian evidence and the EGYARN reconstructions centre on the end-to-end form. A splice can be twisted in either direction, conventionally labelled s or z after the diagonal of the twist, and the direction is a real, recordable property of every ancient thread.

How It Differs from Spinning — and Why the Old Reading Failed

In draft spinning, by contrast, fully separated fibres are drawn out continuously from a prepared mass — classically dressed on a distaff — while a rotating spindle twists the emerging stream along its whole length. The two technologies differ in everything: in feedstock (loose fibres against ribbons), in preparation (full retting and heckling against light retting and hand-stripping, Sections V–VI), in where the twist lives (everywhere against only at the joins), and in what the finished single looks like under magnification — which is where the old reading failed. A spliced single, once two of them are plied together with opposite twist (Section VIII), presents a regular diagonal surface that generations of cataloguers recorded as "S-spun yarn." The textbook phrase "S-spun Egyptian linen" is, in large part, a misreading of plied spliced structure: the microscope, at sufficient magnification and with Gleba and Harris's criteria in hand, shows the untwisted ribbon lengths and the discrete joins that the naked-eye description smoothed away. The archive's rule follows: no claim of draft-spun pharaonic yarn is accepted in these pages without fibre-level evidence, and the spinning language of older museum records — including, it must be said fairly, the Metropolitan Museum's own note that the Hatnefer sheet's thread was "spun from flax harvested when the plants were very young" — is reported as the record's wording, while the harvest-timing substance underneath it stands.

The Experimental Record — What Actually Makes a Splice Hold

Splicing is easy to describe and, the EGYARN experiments found, hard to do. Spinazzi-Lucchesi and Wallenberg's programme (EXARC Journal) set out to produce splices that hold without adhesives, without prolonged moisture, and without being kept under tension, with the tight twist visible in the ancient threads — and documented, variable by variable, what governs success. Four findings carry the procedural weight of this article. First, the angle: the angle at which the two ribbons meet controls the number of rotations the rolling palm imparts — a narrow angle gives few rotations and a weak join; a wider angle generates the rotations that make a splice grip, and grip is strength. Second, the overlap: a few centimetres, root to tip, the thick end tapered — too short starves the join, too long wastes fibre and thickens the thread. Third, moisture is a trap: wet ribbons splice easily, but flax fibres swell in water and shrink on drying, so a splice made wet loosens as it dries and gaps open between the ribbons; the earlier experiments that soaked ribbons for a day and glued or tension-dried the joins were compensating for exactly this. Fourth, handling: finished singles keep their splices best coiled loosely rather than wound hard into balls, which disturbs the joins — and the ancient depictions' coils and balls beside the splicer are read in that light. The experimenters' conclusion deserves its place here in paraphrase: the ancient workers spliced vigorously and very effectively — the tight, regular splices of the surviving yarns are a mastered skill, not a makeshift.

DRAFT SPINNING — NOT THE PHARAONIC METHOD

Separated fibres drawn continuously from a mass and twisted along the whole length by a rotating spindle. Requires full retting and combing. The textbook default — and, for pharaonic linen, the error this section corrects.

END-TO-END SPLICING — THE PHARAONIC METHOD

Ribbons overlapped a few centimetres, root to tip, twisted only at the overlap, rolled on the thigh. Twist lives at the joins; the ribbon lengths between are untwisted. Identified by the Gleba and Harris method; reconstructed by EGYARN.

THE SPINDLE'S REAL JOB

Spindle whorls are common finds — and are not evidence of draft spinning. In the splicing system the spindle adds twist for plying two singles together (Section VIII). Presence of whorls proves twisting, not spinning.

One more consequence, because it reorganises the social history of the craft. Splicing needs no distaff, no prepared fibre supply, no equipment beyond a lap, a thigh and a stock of ribbons — and it is interruptible work, done seated, at household scale, by the fireside as readily as in a workshop. The technology that made Egypt's finest export was, at its decisive step, a hand technology of almost embarrassing simplicity, whose entire difficulty was tacit: ribbon preparation, splice angle, moisture judgment, rhythm. Sections XIX–XX return to what that means for who made Egypt's cloth.

VIII. Plying — the Spindle, the Spinning Bowl, and What They Were Really For

A single spliced thread is usable, but it is not yet weaving yarn at its best. A splice, however well made, is a local thickening and a potential weak point, and a single made of untwisted ribbon lengths has little resistance to abrasion in the loom. The Egyptian answer was plying: two spliced singles were twisted together along their whole length, with a twist opposite in direction to the splices' own, so that the singles lock around each other, the splices are trapped and staggered inside the ply, and the finished yarn gains the strength and evenness the warp in particular demands. In the notation modern textile scholarship uses (Rast-Eicher and Dietrich's system, as cited in the EGYARN literature), a yarn of two z-spliced singles plied with an S-twist is written S2*z — ply direction first, number of singles, splice direction last — and its mirror, two s-spliced singles plied Z, is Z2*s. The notation is worth learning because it says in four characters what the old literature's "S-spun" said wrongly in one word.

The tools of plying are the tools the older literature misread as a spinning kit. The spindle — a shaft with a whorl as flywheel and weight — was used in the plying stage: the depictions and the EGYARN reconstructions show spliced singles being twisted together with the spindle's help, the whorl's momentum doing the steady twisting that fingers alone sustain poorly over long lengths. The spinning bowl, a bowl with an internal loop or handle through which yarn was passed, belongs to the same stage: the singles were drawn through the bowl — water in it could damp the yarn as it passed, a controlled touch of moisture at the one stage where damp helps rather than harms — and the bowl's loop kept tension and order while the spindle plied. Read with splicing in mind, the whole familiar toolkit reassembles itself one step later in the chain than the textbooks put it. Nothing is discarded; everything is reassigned.

Plying was also where counts were set. Warp yarn — the lengthwise yarn that takes the loom's tension and the beater's punishment — was plied hard and even; weft yarn, which only has to lie in the shed, could be softer, and fine cloth might use plied warp against lighter weft, one of the asymmetries the thread counts of Section XIII keep meeting (warp counts run higher than weft counts in Egyptian cloth again and again). Singles could also be plied more than twofold for cordage-grade yarn, and the same twisting technology, at larger scale, made the ropes and nets that stand at the coarse end of the fibre economy. The yarn, finished, was wound into balls or skeins — the depictions show both — and issued to the warping stage by count and by weight, in the workshops that kept accounts (Section XIX).

IX. Warping — Preparing the Loom's Skeleton

Weaving begins before the loom, in warping: the laying out of the warp, the full set of lengthwise threads, in order, at length, under even tension, ready to be mounted. Warping is the stage at which the cloth's width, length and warp count are irrevocably decided, and at which an error — a crossed thread, an uneven tension, a miscount — is woven into every subsequent row. The Egyptian evidence shows warping done on pegs driven in the ground or set in a frame or wall, the yarn carried back and forth around the pegs to build the warp as a continuous ordered sheet, with a cross kept in the threads to preserve their order; the Meketra model shows the operation inside a household workshop, and the Beni Hasan sequence includes it in the chain.

Warping also made the cloth's first ornament, before any weaving: the starting border. Egyptian cloth characteristically begins not with a raw warp edge but with a woven heading — a narrow band in which warp and weft are worked together (in some pieces a tablet-woven or multiply-worked heading) that locks the warp threads in their spacing and gives the weaver a firm, finished edge to weave away from. The starting border survives on complete cloths and is one of the features by which a whole cloth, as opposed to a cut fragment, is recognised. At the cloth's other end the warp would be finished with a fringe of the warp threads themselves, plied or knotted (Section XII) — the Met's Hatnefer sheet preserves both: a warp selvage at one end and a plied fringe at the other.

The scale of warping deserves a number. A sheet of the Hatnefer type — 161 cm wide at 46 warp threads to the centimetre — carries on the order of seven thousand four hundred warp threads, each over five metres long, each one spliced from dozens of ribbons and plied, each one laid in order by hand. That arithmetic, not any loom mechanism, is the true measure of Egyptian textile labour, and it is why the Met's judgment that such a length "would have taken months of constant industry to weave" understates the case: the weaving was the last of the months. The splicing was the first of them.

X. The Horizontal Ground Loom, Step by Step

For most of pharaonic history the loom was the horizontal ground loom: the warp stretched flat, at ground level, between two beams, and the weaver working along it at ground level too. It is depicted from the Predynastic period — the Badari platter of c. 3600 BCE already shows it — through the Middle Kingdom scenes and models, and it never entirely disappeared. The sequence below is the standard reconstruction from the depictions, the models and the cloth, in the order of work.

1. Peg Out and Mount the Warp

Two stout beams — the warp beam and the cloth beam — are set parallel on the ground, held by pegs driven in at their ends, at a distance apart equal to the cloth's intended length (long cloths could be woven in stages, the finished portion wound on). The prepared warp (Section IX) is stretched between the beams and tensioned: the pegs and beams, not any ratchet, hold the tension, and the warp lies as a flat sheet a few centimetres above the earth, wide as the cloth and long as the piece.

2. Lay the Shed Rod and the Heddle Rod

Two openings — sheds — must alternate to pass the weft over and under alternate warp threads. The ground loom makes them with two devices laid across the warp. The shed rod, a smooth rod resting over alternate warp threads, holds one set of threads raised, so that a natural shed stands open along the warp. The heddle rod, resting across the warp nearer the weaver, carries loops (heddles) attached to the other set of warp threads: lift the heddle rod, and those threads rise through the first set, opening the counter-shed. Alternate the two and the weft can travel both ways. The whole loom's cleverness is in those two rods; everything else is frame and patience.

3. Weave the Starting Border, Then Open the First Shed

Work begins at the starting border (Section IX), woven first to lock the warp spacing. Then the natural shed — the opening held by the shed rod — receives the first weft pick: a length of weft yarn, carried on a stick or wound as a small package, is passed by hand across the full width of the warp, through the shed, and left with a short tail at the selvage.

4. Beat the Pick Home

Each pick is beaten into the fell of the cloth — the growing edge — with a wooden beater, worked along the width so the weft seats evenly. Beating is where warp-faced cloth is made (Section XII): beat hard and the weft is driven so close that the warp covers it entirely; beat lightly and the weave stays open and balanced. The beater's rhythm is the weaver's signature, and uneven beating is the commonest fault in ancient as in modern hand weaving.

5. Change the Shed and Return

The heddle rod is lifted, the counter-shed opens, and the weft travels back across. Shed, pass, beat; counter-shed, pass, beat: the ground loom's whole cycle is those three acts repeated for the length of the piece, the weaver (or weavers — wide cloth could be worked by two, one at each side) moving with the fell as the cloth grows, the finished cloth drawn toward and wound upon the nearer beam as the work advances.

6. Mind the Selvages

At each edge the weft turns around the outermost warp threads, forming the selvage. Egyptian selvages are characteristically firm — often reinforced with extra or plied warp threads at the edge — because a cloth's edges take its wear, and because complete cloths, not cut yardage, were the product: a sheet or garment cloth came off the loom with four finished edges, two woven (the selvages) and two closed by the starting border and the fringe. The weaver's marks found inlaid at the corners of fine sheets — the Met records a series of them at one corner of the Hatnefer sheet, and a single cross at the other — belong to this stage of the work: workshop or weaver identification woven into the cloth itself.

7. Finish the Piece

When the fell reaches the far beam, the remaining warp is cut or unpegged and secured as the closing fringe — plied, knotted or left as laid — and the cloth comes off the loom complete: washed, bleached and finished as Section XIV describes, or sent first to the garment-makers of Section XVII. No cutting waste, no hemming of raw edges: the ground loom produces cloths, in the plural and the particular, each one an individual.

// WHAT THE GROUND LOOM DEMANDS //

Floor space equal to the cloth's full length; a warp of thousands of threads tensioned by pegs; two rods' worth of mechanism; and one or two weavers working at ground level for days to weeks per piece. Its limits are equally plain: the weaver works stooped over a flat warp, wide cloth strains the reach across it, and long cloth strains the room. The vertical loom of the next section answers those limits — three thousand years into the technology's life.

XI. The Vertical Loom — and the Honest Date of the Change

The vertical loom hangs the warp upright: an upper beam, supported on posts or against a wall, carries the warp threads hanging down under tension — weighted or fixed to a lower beam — and the weaver works standing or seated before a vertical cloth, beating the weft upward into the fell. The type transforms the ergonomics of weaving: the weaver's reach across the width is eased, very wide cloth becomes manageable, the work is visible as it grows, and tapestry technique (Section XVI), which wants the pattern in front of the weaver's eyes, finds its natural machine.

Now the date, at the archive's real strength, because the loom's arrival has been recruited into arguments it cannot support. The vertical loom is first depicted in Egypt around 1425 BCE, in the New Kingdom of Thutmose III's generation — the archive's companion article on exchange across the Hyksos age treats the question in its own right (its Section VII), and this article adopts its adjudication exactly. The vertical loom postdates Hyksos rule: it cannot be listed among the technologies the Hyksos brought, and any account that introduces it as a Hyksos gift is a century and a half out. A Levantine stimulus is argued by some scholars — the type was known in the Levant, and Egypt's closest Levantine contact ran through the Delta — but argued is the word: it is not proven, and the alternative, an internal development from the ground loom's long maturity, remains open. What is certain is the shape of the change once it comes: the vertical loom does not abolish the ground loom, which continues beside it for ordinary cloth; the New Kingdom runs both, the upright loom associated above all with the larger workshop production of the period, including the temple and estate weaving sheds of Section XIX.

The loom change is also a labour change, and the depictions register it. Middle Kingdom weaving scenes — ground loom, splicing, plying — are staffed overwhelmingly by women; New Kingdom scenes of the vertical loom in workshop settings show men at the loom as a matter of course. The archive reports the shift as the depictions give it, with the standing caution that tomb scenes show the workforce their owners wished to display; but the correlation of the upright loom, the big workshop and the male weaver is consistent enough across the New Kingdom evidence to state as the period's pattern, against the Middle Kingdom's equally consistent pattern of women weaving in household settings (Section XIX).

XII. Weaving — Tabby, Selvages, Fringes and Pattern

The weave of Egyptian linen is, with vanishingly few exceptions, tabby — plain weave, weft over one warp thread and under the next, alternating each row. The simplicity is structural, not a failure of imagination: tabby is the strongest, most even cloth a single pair of sheds can make, and Egyptian variety was achieved not by multiplying weave structures but by manipulating tabby's variables — thread fineness, thread spacing, and the ratio of warp to weft.

That ratio gives the family its members. In balanced tabby, warp and weft counts are comparable and both show. Egyptian cloth characteristically leans warp-faced: the warp is set more closely than the weft is beaten, so the surface reads as fine warp ribs and the weft is partly or wholly covered — the counts of Section XIII (46 warp against 30 weft at the extreme) are warp-faced cloth quantified. Warp-facing suits spliced yarn: the stronger, harder-plied yarn takes the visible, wearing surface, and the cloth gains its characteristic smooth, dense hand. At the other end of the range, open, gauze-like tabbies — loosely set in both directions — gave the sheer, transparent fabrics of festival and court dress, whose plausibility in the tomb paintings the Met's sheer Hatnefer sheet directly supports.

Weft-looping adds texture within the tabby ground: loops of weft are drawn up from the surface at intervals as the weaving proceeds, producing a piled, towelling-like face used for cloths where absorbency or a soft nap was wanted. Stripes and bands are woven in by changing the weft (or, in warp-striped pieces like the Tarkhan dress, whose warp carries a natural pale grey stripe, by arranging the warp itself) — pattern at the level of thread supply, needing no loom modification. Fringes close the cloth: warp ends plied into cords, sometimes with an inlaid or knotted fringe added along a selvage, as on the Hatnefer sheet. And embroidery — pattern stitched into finished cloth with a needle, in coloured thread — is attested but restrained in the pharaonic repertoire beside the woven decoration of Section XVI; Egypt's decorative instinct ran to pattern made in the weaving, not applied after it. Colour in woven pattern raises the question of dyed thread, and there this article stops at the pointer it is required to give: dyeing is treated, as a full procedural system, in the archive's Alchemy article at its Section XXIV, on the Theban recipe papyri (Leyden X and the Stockholm papyrus) — it is not part of this article's gap and is not duplicated here.

XIII. Grades of Cloth — Royal Linen and the Thread Counts

Egyptians graded linen as they graded everything the state handled: by name, in lists, with the top grade reserved by its very name to the king. The textual lists of cloth types — the tomb of the vizier Rekhmire is the commonly cited New Kingdom witness — name, among others, royal linen, bleached linen, fine linen and close-woven linen as distinct categories (paraphrased from the published summaries of the Rekhmire cloth list). The grades were administrative facts: they set the price of a sheet, the value of a wage payment, the worth of an offering and the storehouse shelf a length was folded onto. What they were not, so far as any surviving text says, is defined thread counts — and so the counts below are offered as the modern, measured shadow of the ancient categories, the correspondence argued case by case.

The Hatnefer sheet is the grade system's exhibit A, and this article states its claims at exactly the strength of the Metropolitan Museum's record. The sheet came from the storehouses of Queen Hatshepsut as a funerary gift to the parents of her courtier Senenmut. It measures 515 cm in length and 161 cm in width and weighs 140 grams — a bedsheet-sized cloth with the mass of a small apple. Its thread is, in the Met's judgment, superfine, from flax harvested very young (Section IV); the length would have taken months of constant industry to weave; and the museum identifies it as the cloth the Egyptians described as šs-nswt [Gardiner: N37-S29-A43-X1] [𓈙𓋴𓀲𓏏], royal linen, the highest quality — adding the detail this article values most, that the sheet was repaired and laundered in ancient times: it had a working life before its burial, and was worth mending. Its sheerness, the Met notes, lends credence to the New Kingdom paintings of pleated garments through which the body's contours and even the skin's colour show. Royal linen, measured, is not a poet's exaggeration. It is a specification.

Against that verified summit, the popular claims must be marked down. Retellings of Egyptian fineness routinely cite 200 to 500 or more threads per inch, gauze that passed through a ring, and kindred marvels. The archive's research dossier states the position plainly, and this article adopts it: such counts were not verified in any authoritative source read for this article, and they are not quoted here as fact. The classical world's own marvel-report belongs in the same labelled place: Herodotus (3.47) describes a corselet sent by King Amasis, worked with figures in gold thread and tree-wool, whose yarn he reports as made of 360 threads twisted together — paraphrased here from Herodotus as a report, the ancient world's testimony to Egyptian fineness's reputation, not a measurement. The measured record needs no inflation: 46 threads to the centimetre in a spliced yarn, in a cloth five metres long, is marvel enough, and it has the additional virtue of being true.

At the scale's other end, coarse cloth was not failed fine cloth but a product in its own right: mattress and packing cloths, sailcloth and sacking grades, the heavy sheets of Hatnefer's own third chest-companion (a coarser sheet over seventeen yards long, probably a mattress, in the Met's description of the chest's contents). A linen economy is a pyramid: a narrow apex of royal fineness, a broad middle of good household cloth, and a base of coarse utility — and the same field, the same ribbons and the same hands fed all three, sorted by harvest timing and splice fineness from the start.

XIV. Bleaching, Washing and Finishing

Cloth off the loom is not yet linen as Egypt wore it. Fresh flax cloth is greyish, stiff with the residues of its making, and uneven in hand; the finishing chain turned it white, supple and bright — whiteness being, in Egypt, not a fashion but a value (Section XV). The steps, from the depictions and the dossier's process summary, run as follows.

First wash. The new cloth was washed to remove the field and workshop from it — soaked and worked in water with an alkaline washing agent: nṯry, natron, the soda of the Wadi Natrun, heads the list, with plant-ash lye (potash) and soapwort — a saponin-bearing plant whose roots and leaves lather in water — as the attested alternatives and companions. Alkali does the double work of the whole finishing chain: it cleans, and it begins the bleaching.

Beating. Wet cloth was beaten with sticks on a stone or wooden base — the laundry depictions show the posture and the tools — driving the wash water through the fibres, loosening dirt and residual pectin and shive, and softening the cloth's hand with every round. Beating is the finisher's scutching-equivalent, applied to cloth rather than fibre: mechanical cleaning by impact, repeated.

Rinsing and wringing. The cloth was rinsed clean and wrung — the Beni Hasan repertoire shows cloth twisted around a post to wring it, the post taking the strain that hands alone cannot. Wringing a five-metre sheet is a two-person job, and the depictions staff it accordingly.

Sun-bleaching. The washed cloth was spread in the sun — on roofs, on lines, on the ground on clean surfaces — and kept damp through repeated wetting while the Egyptian sun and the residual alkali did the slow photochemistry that European linen-workers would later call crofting. Sun-bleaching is the step that made Egyptian whiteness proverbial, and it costs nothing but time, water and roof-space: the one stage of the chain where Egypt's climate is the machine.

Final finishing. Dried cloth was smoothed — pressed and rubbed to lay the surface — inspected against the grade it was woven for, mended where the loom had faulted, and folded for the storehouse, the chest or the market. Fine cloth destined for garments went on to the pleating and sewing of Section XVII. Cloth for the temple went in by grade and count, into the linen room whose issues the temple laboratory article follows. And cloth was not finished once: linen's whole service life (Section XVIII) is a cycle of washing and re-bleaching, so that finishing is better understood as the first laundering of many.

XV. Wool and the Other Fibres — a Marginal Place, and the Purity Rules

Egypt had sheep, goats and their fibres for as long as it had flax, and used them — at the margins. Wool was worked for blankets, coarse outer garments, sacks and utilitarian cloth, and goat hair for ropes, tents and rough goods; neither ever challenged linen's place as the cloth of Egyptian dress, temple or tomb. The marginality is partly climate — Egypt is hot, and linen breathes, washes and dries as wool does not — and partly the production system itself: a civilisation organised for bast fibre, from field rotation to splicer's thigh, had no infrastructural reason to rebuild itself around the fleece. But the sharpest boundary wool met was not practical. It was ritual.

The classical testimony is explicit, and this article gives it as testimony, paraphrased from its named source. Herodotus (2.81) reports that Egyptian priests wear linen alone, that wool is not worn by them, and that wool is not buried with the dead either — it is not, in his word, holy to do so (paraphrased from Herodotus, Histories 2.81). The same book's account of priestly purity (2.37) sets the linen rule inside a whole regime of washing, shaving and fresh clothing. Later, Plutarch's On Isis and Osiris takes up the priests' linen and their avoidance of wool and rationalises it in the philosophy of his own day — a late source, used here only to show the rule still being explained centuries on, not as evidence for pharaonic reasoning. The reasoning the pharaonic record itself supports is simpler and is visible in the practice: linen is a plant product, washed white and washable again; wool is an animal product, carries the animal's dirt and grease, harbours pests in store, and does not take the bleaching that Egyptian purity made visible. Purity, in the temple, had a colour, and wool could not be made to wear it.

The rule's edges are as instructive as its centre. Wool was not unclean in itself for ordinary life — Egyptians wore and used it where its properties served, and woollen cloth grows commoner in later, foreign-influenced periods. What the purity rule governed was the sanctuary and the corpse: the god's house and the god's image were dressed in linen, the priest served in linen, and the dead — above all the dead being made into gods by the embalmers — were wrapped in linen alone, a consumption of cloth on a scale Section XX reckons. Cotton belongs to the same margin, later still: tree-wool appears in Herodotus's Amasis corselet report (Section XIII) as an exotic ingredient, and cotton as an Egyptian crop is a phenomenon of the latest periods, outside the pharaonic chain this article describes. Leather, papyrus-fibre and palm-fibre goods filled their own niches — sandals, mats, baskets, rope — and the archive treats the plant-fibre crafts generally in its period articles; the textile throne itself was never in dispute.

// THE FIBRE HIERARCHY //

Linen — clothing, temple cloth, funerary cloth: the fibre of purity and of record. Wool — blankets, coarse and outer wear, utilitarian cloth: used, and ritually excluded. Goat hair — rope, tenting, sacking. Cotton — exotic in the pharaonic record; a crop only in the latest periods. The hierarchy is a fact of the finds as much as of the texts: Egyptian tombs are linen archives, not wool archives.

XVI. Tapestry and Decoration — Pattern Without Dye

Tapestry weave is tabby turned pictorial. The warp is set up and then disappears: it is spaced wide and beaten completely under, while the weft — carried in small packages, colour by colour — travels only as far across the warp as its patch of the design requires, turns, and travels back. Because each colour's weft is discontinuous, confined to its own area, the weaver builds the design patch by patch, colour area by colour area, exactly as a painter fills an outline. Slits open where two colour areas meet along a warp thread, and are managed by interlocking or dovetailing the wefts at the boundary, or accepted and sewn. It is the slowest weaving there is — the weaver is drawing with thread — and it is the natural technique of the vertical loom (Section XI), where the growing picture faces its maker.

In pharaonic Egypt tapestry is a New Kingdom presence, used at luxury scale rather than as common cloth: tapestry-woven bands, panels and garment elements from royal and elite contexts, of which the most discussed is the decorative work on a gala tunic of Tutankhamun (Carter 367j) — a fine plain linen tunic, 95 × 113.5 cm, carrying decorative bands whose exact technique is, the archive's dossier notes, still debated among specialists. The debate is itself the honest datum: tapestry, woven-in pattern bands, and applied decoration shade into one another in fragmentary survivals, and this article claims tapestry for the New Kingdom repertoire on the strength of the surviving tapestry-woven pieces catalogued from the period, while flagging the tunic's bands as contested rather than conscripting them. Tapestry's great Egyptian flowering — the pictorial woollen and linen tapestries of the late antique and Coptic centuries — lies beyond the pharaonic frame and belongs to the archive's later-period articles.

Below tapestry's summit, decoration ran down the whole scale of the craft, and this section gathers it in one place. Woven stripes and bands (Section XII) in contrasting thread. Fringes plain, plied, knotted and inlaid. Bead-net and applied ornament — faience and glass bead nets laid over funerary cloth, sequin-like gold applications on royal garments — decoration worn on the linen rather than woven in it. Pleating, treated with garment-making in Section XVII, is decoration by structure. And presiding over the whole subject stands the boundary this article was set: the colour of dyed thread. Egyptian weavers used coloured yarns, and the chemistry that coloured them — mordants, dye baths, the purple work of the Theban papyri — is a procedural system in its own right, already treated in full at Alchemy §XXIV. It is pointed to here, deliberately, and not duplicated: this article's subject is the cloth, and the dye-pot has its own article.

XVII. Clothing Construction — Cut, Sewn and Pleated

Egyptian clothing divides into two construction philosophies, and both begin on the loom. The first is draped clothing: complete cloths, finished on all four edges by the weaving itself (Section X), worn wrapped, belted, folded and pleated on the body — the shawl, the sash, the wrapped skirt and the great pleated overdresses of New Kingdom festival scenes are cloths in this sense, their "construction" a matter of arrangement, renewed at every wearing and leaving the cloth intact for the laundry and the chest. The second is cut-and-sewn clothing: cloth cut to pieces and the pieces joined with needle and thread into a fitted garment — and its antiquity in Egypt is the Tarkhan dress's whole significance.

The Tarkhan dress (3482–3102 BCE by radiocarbon; Section II) is made from three pieces of sturdy hand-woven linen, cut and sewn into a garment with a V-neck, a fitted bodice and sleeves, its bodice and sleeves finished with fine knife pleats, and its warp carrying a natural pale grey stripe (the Petrie Museum's description, paraphrased). The museum's judgment, again paraphrased, is that it remains the earliest known example of complex woven clothing — a cut, fitted and tailored garment, as opposed to one draped or wrapped. Five thousand years ago, at the state's dawn, Egyptian garment-making was already tailoring. The dress also carries its life on it: wear and staining at the underarms show it was worn in life, not made for the tomb — a working garment of good cloth, sewn by hands whose other work has not survived.

The sewn repertoire grew with the dynasties. The tunic — two widths of cloth sewn at the shoulders and sides, with or without sewn sleeves — becomes the standard upper garment of the New Kingdom, for workmen and kings alike; Tutankhamun's burial held tunics among a large and varied wardrobe of shirts, shawls, loincloths, gloves and headcloths, the royal version of a national dress. The triangular loincloth, the kilt in its pleated and starched ceremonial forms, the woman's sheath dress and the wide bag-tunic of the later periods fill out the wardrobe; sewing itself was done with needles of bone, wood and copper, in linen thread, with seams chosen for cloth that would be washed hard and often. Pleating deserves its own sentence as a technology: the fine pleats of New Kingdom dress — pressed into damp cloth, and in the Tarkhan dress already present three thousand years earlier — were set by folding and pressure, and maintained by careful laundering and storage; a pleated garment is a stored fold-pattern, and its crispness in the tomb paintings is a laundry achievement (Section XVIII) as much as a weaving one.

Fit, finally, was social information. The same tunic pattern in coarse warp-faced cloth and in translucent royal linen is two different statements, and Egyptian art reads cloth fluently — the sheer overdress over the opaque underdress, the pleated kilt's acreage of fine cloth, the workman's single loincloth. Clothing construction in Egypt is therefore never only technique: it is the grade system of Section XIII, made wearable. The archive's treatment of dress as status display runs through its period articles, from the Old Kingdom to the New Kingdom; this article's province is the making.

XVIII. Laundering as Practice — the Washermen's Craft

A linen civilisation is a laundry civilisation. Linen's virtues — it washes clean, bleaches white, dries fast — are realised only by washing it, constantly, and Egypt organised the work at every scale: the household wash at the canal bank, the estate laundry, and the professional washermen who served the temples and the great houses. The finishing chain of Section XIV and the laundry chain of this section are the same operations in different tenses: finishing is a cloth's first wash, and every later wash re-runs it.

The practice, from the depictions — laundering scenes are a set piece of the tomb repertoire, Beni Hasan among them — runs in a fixed order. The cloth is soaked in water with natron or plant-ash lye, the alkali loosening body oils, dust and sweat from the fibres. It is beaten with sticks on a stone or wooden slab, the washermen working in pairs and in rhythm, the beating driving the liquor through the cloth. It is rinsed in clean water, wrung — twisted around a post for the great sheets, the post-mounted wringing the scenes make unmistakable — and spread in the sun to dry and bleach, the whiteness renewing itself at every cycle. Stains and wear were mended as part of the same economy of care: the Met's Hatnefer sheet was repaired and laundered in antiquity (Section XIII), and mended, patched and re-hemmed linen is the norm, not the exception, in the excavated assemblages. Good cloth was capital, and capital is maintained.

Laundering was also a trade with a reputation. The satirical and didactic literature of the New Kingdom — the tradition the archive treats in wisdom literature — counts the washerman among the hard and dirty trades in its catalogues of occupations: his work is wet, alkaline, back-breaking and done at the river's edge among the crocodiles of the satirist's imagination. The satire is evidence of familiarity: a trade proverbially miserable is a trade everybody used. At the institution's top end, temple laundries kept the god's wardrobe in its required whiteness on a rota, issuing fresh ḥbs [Gardiner: V28-D58-S29] [𓎛𓃀𓋴] for the daily service from the linen room and returning the used cloth to the wash — the linen circuit the temple laboratory article traces through the Edfu magazines. Cleanliness in Egypt was not next to godliness as a proverb. It was the god's daily logistics, and linen was its material form.

XIX. Labour and Organisation — Households, Estates and Temple Weaving Sheds

Who made the cloth? At the base of the system, the household — and within the household, for most of Egyptian history, women. The Middle Kingdom evidence is consistent: splicing, plying, warping and ground-loom weaving are depicted as women's work, in domestic and estate settings, and the Meketra model staffs its weaving room accordingly. Textile labour was women's labour in the same structural way that field labour was men's, and its product was correspondingly double: cloth for the family's backs, and cloth as the household's most liquid asset — the surplus sheet that pays, gifts, dowries and buries (Section XX). This section is the industrial chapter of the archive's women and family article, which treats the household that this labour sustained; the evidence assembled here — the depictions, the models, the letter below — is the material that article builds on.

Household production was also, already, organised production. A much-cited letter of the late Middle Kingdom or Second Intermediate Period, preserved in the correspondence published by Edward Wente ( Letters from Ancient Egypt, 1990), comes from a woman named Irer, in charge of a weaving establishment attached to a household, writing to her master about the difficulty of running the weaving shop when the food supplies by which its workers were paid had failed to arrive (paraphrased from Wente's translation, as summarised in the published cloth-production literature). The letter's value is its ordinariness: a woman managing weavers, a payroll in rations, a supply failure, a complaint up the chain. Textile management was administrative work, and women held it.

Above the household stood the estate and temple weaving sheds — the workshops of the great domains, where production ran by account: flax issued by weight, yarn and cloth returned by count and grade, weavers and splicers working in teams under overseers, output divided between the institution's own consumption (the god's wardrobe, the staff's clothing, the offering lists) and its stores. The temple laboratory article sets the weaving shed in the estate's workshop row, beside the bakery-brewery and the unguent rooms, drawing the same issued raw materials through the same accounting. The New Kingdom adds the state and palace workshops of the vertical-loom era (Section XI), with their male workshop weavers, and the specialist settlements — Deir el-Medina, whose textile assemblage anchors the EGYARN project, and Gurob, a palace town with a notable textile record — where cloth was made by and for a community of royal craftsmen, in houses whose loom emplacements and tool finds let archaeology watch household and workshop production at once. The archive's Deir el-Medina article treats the village whole.

The labour arithmetic closes the section, because it disciplines every romantic account of the craft. Reconstruct a single fine sheet from Section XIII: a fibre harvest pulled by hand; ribbons stripped stem by stem; some seven thousand warp threads, each spliced from dozens of ribbons — the splicing alone runs to hundreds of thousands of individual joins for one cloth's warp, before the weft is made; plying, warping, weeks at the loom, washing, bleaching. EGYARN's experimenters, skilled modern craftspeople, found functional splicing slow to learn and slow to do. Egyptian fineness was not a secret recipe. It was a labour system — overwhelmingly women's labour, largely unrecorded by name — applied without stint. The cloth is the archive of that labour, and it is nearly the only one there is.

XX. The Linen Economy — Wages, Offerings, Trade and the Tomb

Cloth was money's neighbour. In an economy that ran on grain, cloth stood beside it as the second great store of value: produced in every household, graded by name, issued as rations and wages, paid as tax and rent, given as gifts and dowries, and stockpiled in chests exactly as Hatnefer's family stockpiled it. A length of good mnḫt [Gardiner: G17-N35-Aa1-X1] [𓅓𓈖𓐍𓏏] was wealth you could wear, wash, mend, divide and bury. The offering lists put cloth with bread and beer among the first necessities of gods and dead alike, and the earliest funerary texts already list linen among the principal offerings for the deceased (the Metropolitan Museum's Hatnefer record makes the same point from the finds): to provision a tomb was, in large part, to provision it with linen.

Abroad, Egyptian linen was a prestige export. Fine Egyptian cloth moved through the Levantine trade world the archive maps in Hyksos Exchange and its period articles — the same corridors that carried tin, timber and lapis in the other direction — and Egyptian fineness became the ancient world's byword, the reputation Herodotus's Amasis corselet (Section XIII) still trades on a thousand years after the New Kingdom; the diplomatic and commercial system that carried it is treated in the archive's trade and diplomacy article. Within Egypt, the state's cloth demand was an industry in itself: armies, crews, priesthoods and workforces were clothed by issue, and the weaving sheds of Section XIX existed because institutions, like households, run on cloth.

The tomb was the economy's largest single consumer, and the consumption was structural, not incidental. The embalmers' art is, in its longest act, a textile operation: the archive's mummification article reckons the wrapping stage by stage — individual fingers and toes in fine strips, sheets of mnḫt [Gardiner: G17-N35-Aa1-X1] [𓅓𓈖𓐍𓏏] laid over regions, bandages of sbn [Gardiner: S29-D58-N35] [𓋴𓃀𓈖] wound to fix them, royal linen šs-nswt [Gardiner: N37-S29-A43-X1] [𓈙𓋴𓀲𓏏] reserved for its stations — a single well-appointed mummy consuming many metres of cloth across every grade, down to the reused household linen and old garments that pad the modest burials. Multiply by every burial in every necropolis for three thousand years, add the funerary wardrobes — the chests of folded sheets, the spare tunics, the shrouds — and the funerary sector alone explains a standing national demand for cloth that no fashion cycle could match. Egypt wove for the dead as systematically as it wove for the living, and the dry tombs that consumed the cloth are, by the same act, the archives that preserved it for Section II's evidence. The industry's greatest customer was also its librarian.

XXI. Repeat It — the Full Chain in Numbered Steps

The archive's standing practice for procedural articles is to end the technical account with the process restated as a single numbered protocol, stripped of commentary. What follows is that protocol for a length of plied-yarn, warp-faced linen on the ground loom, condensed from Sections III–XIV. It is a reconstruction — the chain as the cloth, the depictions and the EGYARN experiments jointly support it — and it assumes a fibre crop grown for the purpose.

// LINEN — THE PROTOCOL //

1. Sow flax thickly on flood-recession land as the inundation drains (autumn); grow it tall and unbranched through the cool season.

2. Decide the grade at harvest: pull green for the finest fibre, half-ripe for general cloth, ripe only where seed is the crop.

3. Pull the stems up by the roots, by hand, in handfuls. Bundle and dry.

4. Ripple: draw the stems through a wide-toothed comb to strip the seed capsules; thresh the capsules for linseed.

5. Ret lightly at most — a short softening soak or brief retting (around ten days was experimentally optimal where retting is used). Do not ret the ribbons apart; the splicing depends on their pectin.

6. Strip the bast from the stem by hand, in full-length ribbons. Discard the woody core. Keep ribbons sorted by length. (No brake, no scutching board, no heckle: not the pharaonic chain.)

7. Splice: lay two ribbons end to end, root to tip, overlapping a few centimetres; taper a thick root end; roll the overlap under the palm on the thigh so the twist forms at the join only, at a wide meeting angle for maximum rotations. Repeat ribbon by ribbon to the single's full length. Coil the finished singles loosely.

8. Ply: twist two spliced singles together with the opposite twist (two z-spliced singles plied S gives S2*z), using the spindle for steady twist and drawing the yarn through the spinning bowl. Ply warp yarn harder than weft.

9. Warp: lay the warp out on pegs to the cloth's length and width, keeping the cross; weave or work the starting border to lock the spacing.

10. Mount the warp between the ground loom's two beams; peg and tension. Lay the shed rod and fit the heddle rod to the alternate threads.

11. Weave tabby: open the natural shed, pass the weft, beat it home; lift the heddle rod for the counter-shed, pass, beat. Set warp close and beat to cover for warp-faced cloth. Mind the selvages at every turn.

12. Finish the piece: secure the closing warp ends as a plied fringe; take the cloth from the loom with all four edges finished.

13. Wash in water with natron (plant-ash lye or soapwort as alternatives); beat wet on a stone; rinse; wring around a post.

14. Bleach spread in the sun, re-wetting through the drying, until the cloth reaches its whiteness.

15. Smooth, inspect, mend; fold for the chest — or pass to cutting, sewing and pleating for garments.

And the chain in one sentence, for the record: grow it tall, pull it whole, strip it in ribbons, join the ribbons — do not spin them — ply the joins into yarn, and weave the yarn warp-faced into a cloth the sun makes white.

XXII. Esoteric Analysis — the Cloth That Is Grown, Joined and Made White

// READING THIS SECTION //

What follows is the archive's interpretive reading of the linen material, offered as such. It is not presented as established Egyptological fact, and it rests only on the documented material of the preceding sections: the spliced join, the graded whiteness, and the purity rules of Section XV.

The archive reads splicing as the most philosophically eloquent technique in the Egyptian workshop — eloquent precisely because it refuses the gesture every later textile tradition takes for granted. Spinning dissolves the individual fibre into a mass and draws a new continuity out of it; the fibre's own length is surrendered and replaced. Splicing does the opposite. It honours the length the plant grew, joins length to length at a single point of twist, and lets the thread's continuity be a chain of joinings — a thing made of meetings, each one visible under the microscope, each one a small act of fitting root to tip so that the whole runs even. A plied Egyptian yarn is, structurally, a genealogy: discrete lives of plants, joined in order, twisted into a common strength. That the culture which wrapped its dead in this cloth also understood the person as a composite — body, name, shadow, and the rest, joined and held together by rite, as the archive's composite soul article sets out — is offered as a resonance, and as nothing more: the splicers left no treatise, and this reading is the archive's, not theirs.

Whiteness carries the second reading, and this one the Egyptians did state, in practice if not in prose. Linen's whiteness was not found but made — beaten, alkalied, sunned, and made again at every washing — and it was the visible form of purity for priest, god and corpse alike (Section XV). A purity that must be manufactured and perpetually renewed, that lives in a material grown from the black earth and raised by labour into light: the archive notes the shape of that idea, and its kinship with the transformation sequences treated in Alchemy, without conscripting the washermen into philosophers. The last word belongs to the labour itself. Hundreds of thousands of joins in a single sheet, each made by a palm on a thigh, each holding for five thousand years in the best of the survivals — if any Egyptian craft teaches that the sacred is a property of patient, exact, repeated work, it is this one.

This article's rule has been applied throughout: procedural claims are tied to surviving cloth, published fibre analysis, excavation and depiction, or labelled reconstruction and experiment; translations are paraphrased and named rather than quoted at length; and the popular fineness claims of Section XIII are reported as unverified rather than repeated. The research dossier compiled for the archive's overhaul (repeatable-processes dossier, section A13) underlies the process summary and was verified against the sources below, each of which was read for this article.

The Splicing Correction and the Experimental Record

Gleba & Harris 2019 — Margarita Gleba and Susanna Harris, "The first plant bast fibre technology: identifying splicing in archaeological textiles," Archaeological and Anthropological Sciences 11: 2329–2346 (open access via the University of Glasgow's Enlighten repository): the splicing types, the identification method, and the late date of the switch to draft spinning. Spinazzi-Lucchesi & Wallenberg (EGYARN) — "An Angle on Splicing: Experimental Insights into the Production of Linen Thread in Ancient Egypt," EXARC Journal: the EGYARN project (Centre for Textile Research, University of Copenhagen), the Deir el-Medina and Gurob case studies, and the experimental findings on splice angle, overlap, moisture and handling used in Section VII; with the earlier experimental line of Cooke and colleagues, Leuzinger and Rast-Eicher, and Kemp and Vogelsang-Eastwood's Amarna reconstructions as cited there. Cartwright and colleagues 1998, as cited in the EGYARN literature, for splicing's currency until at least c. 600 BCE.

Cloth, Collections and Counts

Stevenson, Dee and colleagues 2016 — "Confirmation of the world's oldest woven garment: the Tarkhan Dress," Antiquity Project Gallery: the Oxford radiocarbon date (3482–3102 BCE at 95% probability), the three-piece construction, the weave counts, and the Badari loom depiction. UCL Petrie Museum / Digital Egypt (University College London) — the Tarkhan dress record (UC28614B) and the woven-linen counts for the Fayum and Tarkhan material. Plutarch, On Isis and Osiris, used only as a late witness to the linen rule (Section XV). Wente 1990 — Edward F. Wente, Letters from Ancient Egypt: the letter of Irer on the household weaving shop, paraphrased in Section XIX. The Middle Kingdom depiction corpus — Beni Hasan (Baqet III no. 15, Khety no. 17, Khnumhotep II no. 3), Deshasheh, Meir, Sheikh Said — and the Meketra workshop model (Metropolitan Museum), as published in the standard textile literature (Kemp & Vogelsang-Eastwood, The Ancient Textile Industry at Amarna, 2001, for the Amarna industry and its reconstructions).
