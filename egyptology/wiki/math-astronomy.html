I. The Ontology of Measure — Aligning Earth and Sky

In Ancient Kemet, mathematics and astronomy were not viewed as abstract sciences, but as the Applied Language of Ma'at. Measurement (ḥꜣy [𓎛𓄿𓇋𓏏𓏛𓏥]) was a ritual act—a means of ensuring that the human-built world (architecture, irrigation, agriculture) was in perfect alignment with the divine blueprint of the cosmos.

In this research framework, these disciplines represent Precision Tuning. By mastering the ratios of the earth and the movements of the stars, the Egyptians were able to establish stable "Resonant Fields" (temples and pyramids) that facilitated communication with higher-dimensional frequencies. Mathematics was the "Code" and Astronomy the "Clock" of the Kemetic operating system.

II. Thoth and Seshat — Divine Sources of Calculation

Calculation had divine patrons. Thoth (Ḏḥwty), the ibis-headed god of writing, reckoning and the moon, was credited with the invention of the scribal arts and with dividing time; in the judgment scene he records the result of the weighing, and in myth he calculates and restores what has been lost or divided. He is the scribe of the gods, and human scribes worked under his authority.

Seshat (sšꜣt, “female scribe”) is his counterpart for measure in the built and recorded world. Depicted from the early dynasties notching a palm rib to record the king's regnal years, she is “foremost in the House of Books,” keeper of records and ground plans. With the king she performs the Stretching of the Cord (pedj-shes), the foundation rite in which a measuring cord was stretched between stakes to lay out a temple's plan and fix its orientation. Counting, recording and measuring were therefore temple acts before they were school exercises: the scribe's arithmetic and the surveyor's cord both stood under divine warrant. See The Scribal Arts.

III. The Rhind Mathematical Papyrus — Systematic Logic

The Rhind Mathematical Papyrus (British Museum EA 10057 and EA 10058) is the fullest surviving handbook of Egyptian calculation. Its scribe, Ahmes, states in his colophon that he copied it in year 33 of the Hyksos king Apepi (c. 1550 BCE) from an older exemplar of the time of Amenemhat III. The British Museum counts 84 problems, worked as model examples rather than stated as general theorems: divisions of bread and beer, areas of fields, volumes of granaries, slopes of pyramids, and quantities in arithmetic and geometric progression.

The papyrus opens with its essential tool: the 2/n table, which expresses 2 divided by every odd number from 3 to 101 as a sum of unit fractions, the form in which all Egyptian fraction work was done. Problem 50 gives the circle rule treated in section VII below: take away one-ninth of the diameter and square the remainder, a procedure equivalent to a value of π ≈ 3.16. The method throughout is procedural: a worked case with concrete numbers, from which the trained scribe could repeat the recipe on new numbers.

IV. The Moscow Papyrus — Volumetric Precision

The Moscow Mathematical Papyrus (Pushkin Museum, Moscow) is the second great mathematical handbook, a Middle Kingdom collection of 25 problems covering much the same practical ground as the Rhind: rations, areas, and the capacities of containers and structures.

Its Problem 14 is the most celebrated result in Egyptian mathematics. A truncated pyramid (a frustum) is given with base side 4, top side 2, and height 6. The scribe squares the 4 (16), doubles the 4 (8), squares the 2 (4), adds them (28), takes one-third of the height (2), and multiplies: 56. In modern notation the procedure is exactly V = (h/3)(a² + ab + b²) — the correct formula, obtained nowhere else in the ancient record at this date. The papyrus gives the recipe, not a derivation: how Egyptian scribes arrived at it is not recorded, and modern reconstructions of their reasoning remain conjecture.

V. The Wedjat Eye — Mathematical Theology of the Grain

One of the most profound applications of Kemetic mathematics is found in the Wedjat Eye (Eye of Horus [𓂀]). The component parts of the eye correspond precisely to fractions of the heqat grain measure:

  • 1/2: Inner corner (The smell / The nose)
  • 1/4: Pupil (The sight / The light)
  • 1/8: Eyebrow (The thought / The mind)
  • 1/16: Outer corner (The hearing / The sound)
  • 1/32: Curved tail (The taste)
  • 1/64: Tear track (The touch)

The sum of these fractions is 63/64. In the traditional story the missing 1/64 is supplied by Thoth, by magic — the god restores what the torn eye lacks. Two scholarly cautions belong with the scheme itself. First, the neat equation of the eye's parts with the heqat fractions is a modern hypothesis (Möller, 1911, popularised by Gardiner) that has been largely abandoned since Peet (1923) and Ritter (2002): the fractions themselves, and their use in grain accounting, are certain, but the eye-part correspondence may be a modern systematisation rather than an ancient doctrine. Second, the sense-labels in the list above follow the standard scheme as usually printed; they travel with the hypothesis, not independently of it. See Heka — The Primordial Force.

VI. Decimal and Fraction Systems — Mapping the Small

Egyptian numeration was decimal: separate signs stood for 1, 10, 100, 1,000, 10,000, 100,000 and 1,000,000, and a number was written by repeating each sign as many times as needed. The system was additive rather than positional — the value of a sign did not depend on its place — and it had no sign for zero. In cursive hieratic, ligatured forms made the same system fast enough for accounts, rosters and tax records.

Fractions were handled differently. With the standing exception of 2/3 (and, in later use, 3/4), a fraction was written as a sum of unit fractions — fractions with numerator 1 — so that 2/5 appears as 1/3 + 1/15, found by the 2/n table of the Rhind Papyrus. Multiplication proceeded by successive doubling and adding of selected doublings, division by the reverse process. For capacity measure, the heqat was divided by the Horus-eye series 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, 1/32, 1/64 treated in section V above: a binary set of unit fractions built into the grain accounts themselves.

VII. Geometric Hardening — Squaring the Circle and Phi

The title of this section names two claims that must be separated from the evidence. For the circle, the evidence is real and modest: Rhind Problem 50 replaces a circle of diameter 9 by a square of side 8 — the diameter less its ninth, squared — giving an area rule equivalent to π ≈ 3.16. That is a good practical approximation for fields and granaries. It is not a construction for “squaring the circle” in the Greek sense, and no Egyptian text presents it as one.

For phi (φ ≈ 1.618), the golden ratio, there is no Egyptian evidence at all. No surviving Egyptian text names the ratio, states it, or gives a design procedure using it, and the ratio as a defined quantity first appears in Greek mathematics (Euclid's “extreme and mean ratio”). Ratios close to φ can be extracted from the Great Pyramid's dimensions, but the pyramid's slope is fully explained by its chosen seked (section VIII), and the φ reading of it is a modern, nineteenth- and twentieth-century interpretation. It is presented in this archive as a modern claim about the monuments, not as an attested Egyptian design constant.

VIII. Pyramidal Volume — The Mathematics of the Benben

Pyramids posed two distinct problems: setting the slope, and reckoning volumes. The slope was specified by the seked: the horizontal run, in palms, for a rise of one royal cubit (seven palms). Rhind Problems 56–60 work entirely in this measure — given base and height, find the seked; given seked and base, find the height. A seked of 5½ palms, that of the Great Pyramid of Khufu, needs no other constant to explain the building's angle of about 51.8°.

For volumes, the surviving procedures concern the shapes builders and granary officials actually had to fill or empty: cylindrical and rectangular granaries in the Rhind Papyrus, and, supremely, the frustum of Moscow Problem 14 (section IV), whose correct procedure implies a secure practical grasp of how a pyramid's volume scales. No Egyptian text states a general proof for any of these rules; what survives is a tested recipe tradition, accurate enough to raise and provision the monuments it served.

IX. Temple Proportions — The 1:2 and 2:3 Harmonic Ratios

What Egyptian practice demonstrably used is well documented. Figures were drawn on a squared grid under a fixed canon of proportions — in the classical canon, eighteen grid squares from the soles of the feet to the hairline — which let a workshop scale a design from a model to a wall of any size while holding the proportions constant. Slopes were fixed by the seked, foundations were aligned and laid out by the Stretching of the Cord, and dimensions were given in royal cubits, palms and fingers.

The 1:2 and 2:3 “harmonic ratios” named in this section's title are a different kind of claim: that temple plans were designed on the ratios of musical consonances. No Egyptian text states such a programme, and no design drawing survives that encodes it. The idea projects Greek harmonic theory — where 1:2 and 2:3 are the octave and the fifth — back onto Egyptian plans, and belongs to modern speculative literature rather than to the attested record. Where simple whole-number ratios occur in Egyptian buildings, the grid, the cubit system and the seked are sufficient explanation; harmonic-ratio design is labelled here as modern speculation, not Egyptian doctrine.

X. Stellar Observation — The Horizon as Scientific Tool

Egyptian astronomy was observational and calendrical: its instruments were the horizon, the meridian, and generations of recorded risings. Its oldest surviving instruments are texts. The diagonal star tables painted inside Middle Kingdom coffin lids (notably from Asyut, c. 2100–1800 BCE) tabulate, for each ten-day period of the year, which decanal star marked each hour of the night — a star clock the dead could read, built from the same observations by which the living “hour-watchers” told time.

New Kingdom astronomy refined the method. Astronomical ceilings, from Senenmut's tomb onward, set the decan lists beside the northern constellations; the Ramesside star clocks, preserved in the royal tombs of Ramesses IV, VI, VII and IX, replace risings with transits: an observer noted the moment a named star crossed the meridian, located by its position over a seated colleague's body — over the heart, at the left ear, at the right shoulder — in tables drawn up for each half-month. Observation, not theory, is the constant: positions were watched, named and tabulated, and the tables were copied for over a thousand years. See section XI, on the decans themselves.

XI. The 36 Decans — The Measurement of the Night Sky

The Egyptians organised the stars south of the ecliptic into 36 groups known as Decans. Their successive risings through the night marked the hours — the principle of the diagonal star tables and star clocks of Section X — and each decan in turn served as the hour-marker for a ten-day period of the civil year. The decan's heliacal rising — its annual first appearance in the dawn after a period of invisibility — is a different event from that ten-day cycle, and the two must not be fused: the ten-day rhythm is the clock at work, the heliacal rising its yearly reset.

Each decan was associated with a specific deity and was believed to possess a unique vibrational frequency. The timing of rituals was synchronized with the rising of a particular decan to ensure that the Ritual Action was in resonance with the desired cosmic force. The 36 Decans are the Vibrational Modulators of the night, governing the 12 hours of the Duat. See Netherworld Books Wiki.

XII. The Sothic Cycle — Calibration of the Great Year

Kemetic chronology is calibrated against the Sothic Cycle—the periodic heliacal rising of the star Sopdet (Sirius [𓇮]).

Because the Egyptian civil calendar lacked a leap year, it fell out of sync with the solar year by one day every four years. A full Sothic cycle — 1,460 Julian years, comprising 1,461 wandering Egyptian years — was required to return to synchronization. By recording "Sothic Risings" in specific regnal years, the ancient scribes provided modern researchers with Absolute Data-Nodes that permit the precise dating of history. This celestial clock is the ultimate anchor for the Timeline of the Nile Valley.

XIII. The 365-Day Solar Calendar — The Seasonal Engine

The Egyptian civil calendar is the most durable Egyptian invention in daily use anywhere: a year of 365 days, made of twelve months of thirty days plus five added (epagomenal) days, celebrated as the birthdays of Osiris, Horus, Set, Isis and Nephthys. The months were grouped in the three Nile seasons — Akhet (inundation), Peret (growing) and Shemu (harvest), four months each — and every month was divided into three ten-day “weeks” aligned with the decans. A lunar calendar continued alongside it for religious observance, but administration — taxes, rations, regnal dating — ran on the civil year.

The civil year had no leap day, so it drifted against the solar year by about one day every four years: the “wandering year.” A festival fixed to a civil date travelled through all the seasons and returned to its starting alignment only after a full Sothic cycle of 1,460 years (section XII). The defect was understood — the Canopus Decree of 238 BCE proposed adding a sixth epagomenal day every fourth year — but the reform was not taken up in pharaonic practice, and the wandering 365-day year remained Egypt's civil standard into the Roman period.

XIV. The Merkhet — The Physical Tool of the Astronomer

The merkhet (mrḫt, “instrument of knowing”) was the sighting instrument of the Egyptian hour-watcher: a slotted palm-rib sighting bar used together with a plumb line (the bay), whose hanging cord gave a true vertical against the sky. Working in pairs across a north–south line, observers watched a decanal star approach the meridian; the moment it crossed the plumb line's plane was the hour-mark the star clocks were built from.

The same transit technique served on the ground. A meridian line fixed by stellar observation gave true north for the Stretching of the Cord, and the cardinal alignment of pyramids and temples — the Great Pyramid stands within a few minutes of arc of true north — was achieved with instruments of this simplicity: cord, plumb line, sighting slot, and a watched star. Surviving merkhets are late examples of a very old tool, depicted in use long before the extant pieces were made.

XV. The Dendera Zodiac — The Final System Synthesis

The Dendera Zodiac, located on the ceiling of the Temple of Hathor, represents the final synthesis of Kemetic decanal astronomy with Babylonian and Greek zodiacal constellations.

It records a sophisticated understanding of the night sky. It does not record the Precession of the Equinoxes: there is no textual or artefactual evidence that the Egyptians knew of precession, whose discovery is standardly attributed to Hipparchus (c. 128 BCE). The zodiac itself is a Ptolemaic ceiling relief of c. 50 BCE. The Zodiac served as a microcosmic resonator, 'locking' the entire history of the heavens into the architecture of the temple, ensuring that the goddess's terrestrial house remained in sync with the cosmic cycles. The Zodiac is the Total System Backup of the ancient sky. See Ptolemaic Period Wiki.

XVI. Planetary Hours and Ritual Timing — Synchronized Heka

The planetary hours — the scheme in which each hour of day and night is ruled in turn by a planet, cycling through the “Chaldean order” Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, Sun, Venus, Mercury, Moon — are not a pharaonic system. They belong to Hellenistic astrology, formed in the centuries after Alexander from the meeting of Babylonian planetary lore with Greek astronomy, and they are attested in astrological authors of that world, not in temple texts of the dynastic period.

What pharaonic Egypt contributed to the scheme was its frame, not its rulers. Egypt divided day and night into twelve hours each, and those hours were seasonal: a daylight hour was one-twelfth of the time from sunrise to sunset, long in summer and short in winter, and the night hours were told by the decans (sections X–XI). Planetary rulership was later fitted into that Egyptian twenty-four-hour frame in Hellenistic Alexandria. Ritual timing in the temples, by contrast, was governed by the hour-watchers, the decanal clocks and the festival calendar — a stellar and solar discipline, not a planetary one.

XVII. Precession of the Equinoxes — Long-Term Data Cycles

Precession — the slow wobble of the earth's axis that shifts the equinoxes against the stars by about one degree in seventy-two years — is sometimes claimed as an Egyptian discovery, on the strength of Egypt's very long observational record. The evidence does not support the claim. No pharaonic text, table or inscription shows that Egyptian astronomers detected precession: their star clocks and decan lists were copied for centuries without the corrections a knowledge of precession would have required, and changing temple or pyramid alignments show at most that later builders re-observed the sky, not that they modelled the shift.

Egypt's genuine long-term cycle is a different one: the Sothic cycle of 1,460 years (section XII), produced by the civil calendar's drift against the heliacal rising of Sirius — a calendrical effect the Egyptians demonstrably tracked. The discovery of precession itself is credited to Hipparchus in the second century BCE, who detected it by comparing his own stellar positions with those recorded by Timocharis some 150 years earlier, as Ptolemy's Almagest reports. Long records made the discovery possible; making it took the comparative, geometrical astronomy of the Hellenistic world.

XVIII. The Hellenistic Interface — Eudoxus and Hipparchus

Greek authors themselves placed part of their astronomy's schooling in Egypt. Strabo (Geography 17) reports that Eudoxus of Cnidus and Plato stayed at Heliopolis and lived with the priests there, learning, among other things, the Egyptians' reckoning of the year's fraction beyond 365 days; Diogenes Laertius likewise reports Eudoxus studying with Egyptian priests. These are ancient reports about the fourth century BCE, to be weighed as such — but the traffic they describe is real in its effects: Egyptian material, above all the calendar and the decans, passed into Greek astronomy and astrology through Alexandria.

Hipparchus (second century BCE) worked in that transmitted record: Ptolemy's Almagest shows him using older observations, Egyptian and Babylonian among them, for the periods of the sun and moon — the long baselines that Egyptian record-keeping uniquely supplied. The line completes itself in the calendar: the Julian reform of 46 BCE, advised by Sosigenes of Alexandria according to Pliny, adopted the Egyptian 365-day year and added the leap day that the Canopus Decree had proposed for Egypt itself three centuries earlier. The 365¼-day year of the Julian calendar — ancestor of the calendar now in worldwide use — is the Egyptian civil year, corrected.

XIX. Clocks in Practice — Water, Shadow, and Star

The star clocks of Section X had instrument counterparts for cloudy nights and for the day. The water clock (clepsydra) measured the night by outflow: the oldest surviving example, from Karnak and dated to the reign of Amenhotep III, is an alabaster vessel whose interior is graduated with scales for each month — because the night hours are seasonal (Section XVI), the scale must change with the season, and the vessel's twelve monthly scales are exactly that correction made in stone. Water drips through a small hole at the base; the level falling past the marks gives the hour.

By day, the shadow clock read the sun: a horizontal bar with a raised, cross-piece end cast a shadow along a graduated scale, turned at noon to read the afternoon. Surviving examples are late, but the principle is the merkhet's daylight twin (Section XIV) — time told from a watched edge and a marked line.

THREE CLOCKS, THREE SKIES

Star clock: decanal risings and transits — the night told by the stars (Sections X–XI).

Water clock: outflow against monthly scales — the night told without a sky.

Shadow clock: the sun's shadow on a graduated bar — the day told by an edge.

All three divide the light or dark as given: seasonal hours, honestly kept.

None of these instruments measures uniform time, and none was meant to. Egyptian hours were one-twelfth of the available light or dark, and the instruments are honest to that definition: they divide the night and the day as given, not an abstract twenty-four hours. Precision, in this system, means agreement with the sky the instruments were built under.

XXI. Arithmetic in Practice — Doubling, Parts, and the Pesu

Egyptian arithmetic is a small kit of operations used with great fluency. Multiplication proceeds by doubling: to multiply by 13, the scribe doubles the number three times (2, 4, 8), marks the rows that sum to 13 (1, 4, 8), and adds the corresponding products. Division runs the process backward — double the divisor until the dividend is reached, then read off the multiplier. Fractions enter through the unit-fraction system of Section VI and the 2/n table of Section III.

THE RHIND PAPYRUS — A WORKING TABLE OF CONTENTS

The 2/n table: 2 divided by every odd number, 3 to 101, in unit fractions.

Problems 1–6: loaves divided among men — division as fair shares.

Problems on the pesu: the exchange rate of bread and beer against grain.

Area problems: rectangles, triangles, a trapezoid, and the circle (Problem 50).

Volume problems: cylindrical and rectangular granaries, in khar and heqat.

Problems 56–60: the seked — pyramid slope reckoned in palms per cubit of rise.

Progression problems: shares in arithmetic and geometric progression,

including the famous houses–cats–mice–grain sum of Problem 79.

The pesu problems deserve their place in the table: the pesu is the baking and brewing rate — how many loaves or jugs a measure of grain yields — and the Rhind works exchanges between bread and beer of different pesu with the same doubling machinery. This is ration arithmetic for an administered economy: temples and work-gangs (compare the Deir el-Medina grain payments) lived by these conversions, and the school text trains exactly the clerk who would run them.

Alongside the Rhind and Moscow papyri stood smaller handbooks: the Kahun mathematical fragments (UC 32160 and companions, from Petrie's el-Lahun finds) with their 2/n table, and the Berlin Papyrus 6619, whose best-known problem solves simultaneous equations in all but name — a square of area 100 divided into two squares whose sides stand in a stated ratio. The kit was a curriculum, not a single book.

XXII. Land Measure — Rope-Stretchers and the Tax Field

The mathematics with the largest payroll was surveying. Egypt's tax base was its fields, and the inundation annually moved their boundaries: after the waters fell, survey teams — the "rope-stretchers" of Greek accounts, working with the knotted cord of the Stretching of the Cord (Section II) — re-measured the plots and the scribes re-entered the areas in the registers.

The units are the cubit rod of 100 cubits and the setat (aroura) of 100 by 100 cubits — 10,000 square cubits. The area rules of the papyri serve this work directly: rectangles exactly; triangles by half base times height; and, in the Moscow tradition, a rule for the trapezoid. Where a field was irregular, it was divided into the shapes the rules could handle and summed — the procedural style of Section III applied to the landscape.

The surviving land registers — the great Wilbour Papyrus of Ramesses V's reign above all — show the system at full scale: field by field, holder by holder, area by area, with the grain assessment computed from the measurement. The geometry of Section VII's honest negatives belongs here too: a state that measured this much land needed good rules, and its rules for circle and frustum are exactly as good as this work required — which is the right way to honour them.

XXIII. Astronomical Ceilings — Senenmut to Esna

The star clocks came off the coffin lid and onto the ceiling. The earliest surviving astronomical ceiling, in the tomb of Senenmut (TT71, reign of Hatshepsut), sets the decan lists in a great southern panel beside the northern constellations — among them the Bull's Foreleg (our Ursa Major), the Hippopotamus, and the Crocodile — around the circumpolar, imperishable stars that the Pyramid Texts had made the dead king's destination.

Royal tombs of the Ramesside age carried the scheme forward: the ceilings of Seti I and of the Ramesside kings combine decans, constellations, and the Books of the Day and Night (see Netherworld Books), so that the king lay literally under the ordered sky his astronomers had tabulated. Temple ceilings of the Ptolemaic and Roman periods — Dendera (Section XV), Esna, and the pronaos ceilings of the great temples — complete the series, by then carrying the Babylonian zodiac inside the Egyptian decanal frame.

A ceiling is the star clock made architectural: the same risings and transits, fixed in paint and stone at the one place where king or god eternally lies beneath them. The line from Asyut coffin to Esna pronaos is one instrument, enlarged for a thousand years.

XXV. Orientation — Finding True North

The most precisely measurable achievement of Egyptian astronomy is not a text but a set of walls. The Great Pyramid of Khufu is aligned to true north within a few minutes of arc — an accuracy no compass-free culture reaches by accident. The method, reconstructed from the instruments of Section XIV, is a stellar one: observers sighted a circumpolar star (or the cord stretched between two such stars at their simultaneous transit) with merkhet and plumb line, and fixed the meridian on the ground at the Stretching of the Cord.

The small residuals are themselves data. Successive Old Kingdom pyramids show slightly different alignment errors, drifting in a pattern consistent with the slow change of the circumpolar stars' positions — the observation behind Kate Spence's well-known proposal (2000) that the drift could refine pyramid dating. The proposal is debated, and this archive presents it as a proposal. What is not debated is the baseline fact: the builders repeatedly found true north to arc-minute precision with cord, plumb line, and watched stars.

Orientation was theology as much as surveying. A temple aligned to a star, a solstice, or the Nile's course set its god's house inside the order the astronomers tabulated; the foundation rite made the alignment an act of the king and Seshat together (Section II). Measurement, astronomy, and kingship meet on the stretched cord.

XXVI. The Lunar Calendar — Festivals by the Moon

Beside the civil year of Section XIII ran a lunar calendar, and the two coexisted for the whole of pharaonic history. The lunar year of twelve months (about 354 days) began with the first invisibility of the waning moon after the heliacal rising of Sirius's season, and a thirteenth month was intercalated when observation required it, keeping the lunar festivals in their seasons without disturbing the civil count.

The lunar calendar governed what the civil calendar could not: the festivals whose dates are defined by the moon's phases — the monthly feasts of the new and full moon kept in every temple, and great movable feasts such as the Beautiful Festival of the Valley in its lunar aspect. Temple inscriptions routinely double-date: a festival is fixed in the lunar reckoning and recorded in the civil one, and the two dates together are often the modern chronologer's best evidence.

The junction of the two systems is itself an astronomical observation of the first rank: a lunar date that can be tied to a civil date in a known reign fixes that reign against the moon's computable phases, and such junctions — the Sothic risings of Section XII and the lunar dates of the Illahun archive among them — are the load-bearing evidence of Egyptian absolute chronology.

Egypt thus ran a dual time system for three thousand years: a solar-administrative count of perfect regularity, drifting slowly against the seasons, and a lunar count of perfect seasonality, irregular by design. Thoth, lord of the moon and of reckoning (Section II), presides over both — the archive knows no better emblem of Egyptian timekeeping than that double allegiance.

XXVII. Moscow Problems at Work — the Basket, the Rectangle, the Heap

Section IV introduced the Moscow Papyrus through its masterpiece; this section works three more of its twenty-five problems in full, because the Moscow's character is in its working. The roll — the Golenishchev Papyrus, now Pushkin Museum E 4676, a Middle Kingdom manuscript of about 1850 BCE, published by Vasily Struve in 1930 — gives its solutions with far less explanation than the Rhind's (Section XXI): where the Rhind teaches, the Moscow recites, and the modern reader must supply the reasoning the scribe's audience evidently did not need written down.

Problem 10 — the basket. The text, in the standard rendering of Struve's edition, runs in paraphrase of its steps: "Example of calculating a basket (nb.t). You are given a basket with a mouth of 4½. What is its surface?" The scribe's procedure doubles the mouth to 9 and then works on the 9: take 1/9 of 9 — "since the basket is half an egg-shell" — giving 1; the remainder is 8. Take 1/9 of 8, giving 2/3 + 1/6 + 1/18 (that is, 8/9); the remainder of 8 after this subtraction is 7 + 1/9 (that is, 64/9). Multiply 7 + 1/9 by 4½: the answer is 32 — "Behold, this is its area. You have found it correctly."

Read algebraically, the computation is exact and startling: the doubling and the two successive ninths amount to multiplying the diameter by 8/9 twice over, so the result is 2 × (8/9)² × d² — the surface area of a hemisphere computed with the circle constant at 256/81 ≈ 3.16, the same value the Rhind's circle rule implies (Section VII). But the problem's fame now rests on a philological dispute the numbers cannot settle: what shape is a nb.t? Struve and Gillings read a hemisphere; Peet read the curved surface of a semi-cylinder; a further study in the Journal of Egyptian Archaeology (96, 2010) argued from a Karnak measuring vessel and a Beni Hasan tomb model for the semi-cylinder, while a still more recent reading takes the figure as a semicircular segment. The calculation is certain; the basket is not. All three readings are left standing in the literature, and this archive leaves them standing too.

Problem 6 — the rectangle. A rectangular enclosure has area 12, and its breadth is 1/2 + 1/4 (three-quarters) of its length; find both sides. The scribe's method is reciprocal reasoning of an elegance the terse text barely pauses on: take the reciprocal of 3/4, which is 1 + 1/3; multiply the area by it, 12 × 1 1/3 = 16; take the square root of 16, which is 4 — the length; and three-quarters of 4 is 3 — the breadth. In modern dress the scribe has solved l × (3/4 l) = 12 by isolating l² = 16, a quadratic handled without any of the apparatus of algebra, by procedure alone. The identical problem, with the identical steps, stands in the Kahun fragments (Section XXI) — the Moscow and Kahun scribes were working from a shared teaching tradition.

Problem 19 — the heap. The Moscow's Problems 1, 19, and 25 are aha problems, "heap" problems, in which an unknown quantity is to be found from itself and its parts. Problem 19, in paraphrase: "a quantity, taken 1½ times, and 4 added to it, makes 10; what is the quantity?" The scribe subtracts first — 10 less 4 is 6 — and then divides 6 by 1½ to reach 4, which checks: 1½ × 4 = 6, and 6 + 4 = 10. Where the Rhind's heap problems are typically worked by false position — guess, test, scale — the Moscow's master strips the same task to direct inversion of the operations, in the order reverse to that in which they were imposed. Around these three set pieces the rest of the roll ranges from the practical to the enigmatic: Problems 2 and 3 compute parts of a ship (a mast, for instance, at 1/3 + 1/5 of a cedar log of 30 cubits: 10 + 6 = 16 cubits), and several problems, Problem 11 among them, remain only partly intelligible today — the honest residue of a handbook whose classroom is gone.

Taken together, the Moscow problems correct an easy misreading of the corpus. The frustum of Problem 14 (Section IV) is not a lone flash of genius in an otherwise pedestrian literature; it is the summit of a working tradition that could also price a rigging, solve a quadratic by reciprocals, and invert an equation by unwinding it — and that kept its methods in the imperative voice, as recipes, because for its users the proof of a method was that the answer, checked, came out right.

XXVIII. The Wandering Year — the Drift Worked in Numbers

Section XIII stated the defect of the civil calendar; this section does its arithmetic, because the drift is a quantity before it is a curiosity. The civil year counts 365 days; the solar year counts very nearly 365¼. The calendar therefore loses almost exactly a quarter of a day each year against the seasons — one full day every four years, as the ancients themselves reckoned it. Compounded, the loss runs on a scale no administrator could miss: after 40 years the calendar stands 10 days early against the sky; after 120 years, a full month; after 730 years, half a year, so that a festival founded for the inundation season is being celebrated in the season of harvest; and after 1,460 Julian years the loss totals a complete year, and the calendar comes round to its starting alignment.

The cycle's arithmetic is worth setting out in one place, since the two counts of it are often confused. In 1,460 Julian years there are 1,460 × 365¼ = 533,265 days. In 1,461 civil — "wandering" — years there are 1,461 × 365 = 533,265 days. The same total of days, counted two ways: that identity is the Sothic cycle of Section XII, 1,460 Julian years answering to 1,461 Egyptian years, the interval after which the heliacal rising of Sirius (Sopdet) again falls on New Year's Day, 1 Thoth.

The cycle is anchored, unusually for pharaonic chronology, by a dated observation. The Roman writer Censorinus, in De die natali (written 238 CE), records that a century before his time — in 139 CE — the rising of the Dog Star had coincided with 1 Thoth. Counting back by whole cycles gives the previous coincidence years, 1321 BCE and 2781 BCE, and between those fixed piers the recorded Sothic dates of individual reigns can be hung: the most famous is the Illahun papyrus letter noting the rising of Sopdet in year 7 of Senusret III on the 16th day of the fourth month of Peret, which — on the usual reckoning, and subject to the debated question of the observation point — places that regnal year in the 1870s–1860s BCE and with it the backbone of Middle Kingdom chronology. The wandering calendar, useless as a seasonal guide within a single lifetime, turns out to be a superb clock across three thousand years, precisely because it was never corrected.

The correction was nonetheless proposed, and its failure is a document of the first importance. The Canopus Decree of 238 BCE, issued by a synod of priests under Ptolemy III and inscribed in hieroglyphic, Demotic, and Greek, ordered — so that the seasons, in the decree's own concern as paraphrased here, should not wander — a sixth epagomenal day to be added every fourth year. It was not taken up; the priesthoods went on dating by the wandering year, and the reform died with the reign. Only under Augustus was a leap-day calendar imposed on Egypt — the Alexandrian calendar, the Egyptian 365-day frame with the quarter-day restored — and it is that Egyptian frame, by the standard account transmitted through the Alexandrian astronomer Sosigenes into Julius Caesar's reform of 45 BCE, that the Julian and then the Gregorian calendars carried to the modern world. Egypt's most durable export is the shape of everybody's year, drift and all.

Why three millennia of astronomers tolerated a calendar they knew to be wrong by a day every four years is a genuine historical question, and the honest answers are administrative and religious rather than ignorant: the civil year was a counting instrument, superbly regular for taxation and regnal dating; the religious calendar ran on the moon and on observation alongside it (Section XXVI); and a festival that wandered through all the seasons across the great cycle could be read, in the long view the temples took, as a festival that in time was celebrated in every season. The drift was a defect the system had learned to think with.
