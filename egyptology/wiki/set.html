I. The Name — stẖ, stš and Sutekh

No Egyptian god is harder to introduce honestly than Set, because no Egyptian god carries two reputations so completely opposed. The scribes who wrote his name wrote it in several forms, and the variation itself is part of the evidence. The commonest later spelling is stẖ, with an older and very durable alternative stš [Gardiner: E20] [𓃩], in which the whole name is carried by a single sign: the Set animal itself, seated, ears squared, tail erect. The sign is a logogram — the picture is the word — and the archive's V2 transliterator accordingly gives E20 the whole-word value stš. A fuller writing, swtẖ, preserves an initial sw element, sw [Gardiner: M23] [𓇓], that later fell away, and it is from this fuller form that Egyptologists derive the conventional vocalisation Sutekh. Greek writers rendered the name Sēth — the form that survives in English as Seth — and in Coptic the name became Sēt. This article uses Set, the spelling now standard in most museum and university publication, and gives Seth where a cited source or a king's conventional name (Seti, Sethnakhte) requires it.

What the name means is not known, and the archive says so plainly rather than repeating the confident guesses of older handbooks. Proposals have connected stẖ [Gardiner: E20] with roots of separation, of desertion, and of turning aside; none commands a consensus, and the Egyptians left no etymological statement of their own. What can be documented is the name's company. The Set-animal sign does duty in Egyptian writing far beyond the god's name: words for turmoil, confusion, illness, storm and rage are written with Sethian determinatives, so that the sign itself came to mark a semantic field — the field of things disordered, violent, and out of place. The god's name and the vocabulary of disorder grew up together, each reinforcing the other, for three thousand years. A god whose sign means disturbance in ordinary words is a god the language itself has already judged — and yet the same sign stands, unashamed, in the names of kings (Section XIV). The tension is not a modern invention. It is in the writing system.

The full sign list is given in the hieroglyph reference.

One word must be kept distinct from the name from the outset, because confusing them has distorted Set's reputation for two millennia. isft [Gardiner: M17-S29-I9-X1] [𓇋𓋴𓆑𓏏] — "disorder, wrong, chaos" — is the abstract opposite of mꜣꜥt [Gardiner: U1-D36-X1] [𓌳𓂝𓏏], the order-truth the archive treats in Ma'at and Isfet. Set is a god: a person, with a family, temples, priests, and a seat among the Ennead. Isfet is not a person and has no cult. Apep, the serpent of absolute chaos, stands outside the ordered cosmos altogether and is never worshipped. Set stands inside it — dangerously, disruptively, but inside. Holding that distinction is the single most important discipline in everything that follows: Egyptian religion did not confuse its storm god with its devil, because Egyptian religion had no devil, and it is the later conflation of Set with Apep — and then with the Greek Typhon, and then with Satan — that produced the one-dimensional villain of popular retelling. The Egyptian record is stranger and more interesting than that.

II. The Family — Son of Geb and Nut, Brother of Osiris

In the Heliopolitan genealogy of the Ennead — psḏt [Gardiner: N9-X1] [𓇷𓏏], "the Nine" — Set is no intruder from outside creation. He is family at the deepest level the system recognises. The earth god Geb and the sky goddess Nut — nwt [Gardiner: N35-G43-X1] [𓈖𓅱𓏏] — bore five children in the intercalary days of the year: Osiris, wsir [Gardiner: Q1-D4-A40] [𓊨𓁹𓀭]; Horus the Elder, Ḥr-wr [Gardiner: G5-G36] [𓅃𓅨]; Set; Isis, ꜣst [Gardiner: G1-S29-X1] [𓄿𓋴𓏏]; and Nephthys, nbt-ḥwt [Gardiner: V30-X1-O10] [𓎟𓏏𓉡], "Mistress of the House," who became Set's wife. The murder at the centre of the Osiris myth is therefore not an invasion. It is a fratricide inside the ruling house of the cosmos, and Egyptian theology never lets the reader forget it: the plaintiff's uncle, the defendant's brother, the judge's son. Every party to the great litigation of Sections VIII–IX is related to every other.

The pairing of Set and Nephthys repays attention. Nephthys is, by every account, one of the kindest powers in the pantheon: mourner of Osiris, protector of the dead, guardian — with Isis, Neith and Serqet — of the canopic shrine. Her marriage to Set is the systematic counterweight Egyptian theology loves: the barren desert wedded to the faithful mourner, and the union is, in most tellings, childless in the way the desert is childless. A later tradition made Anubis, inpw [Gardiner: C6] [𓁢], the son of Nephthys — by Osiris in the dominant version, by Set in a minority one. Herman te Velde, whose 1967 monograph Seth, God of Confusion remains the standard study of this god, doubted that Set was ever regarded as Anubis's father in genuine Egyptian religion, and the archive follows that caution: where the parentage matters in this article, Osiris is the father named by the sources. Set's recognised consorts in the New Kingdom are instead the foreign goddesses Anat and Astarte (Section XIV) — a theological statement in itself about where Set's affinities were felt to lie.

Set's position among the five siblings is structural. Osiris rules the fertile land and then the dead; Isis rules by heka, ḥkꜣ [Gardiner: V28-D28-G1] [𓎛𓂓𓄿]; Horus the Elder is the sky, pt [Gardiner: N1] [𓇯], whose eyes are the sun, rꜥ [Gardiner: N5] [𓇳], and the moon, iꜥḥ [Gardiner: N11] [𓇹]. Set's portion is everything the others are not: the desert margin, the storm, the foreign country, the night wind, violence itself. The Ennead did not exile him to that portion after the murder; in the oldest strata the portion is simply his, as the sky is Horus's. The myth of the murder (Section VII) explains the enmity, not the office. Confusing those two things — supposing that because Set killed Osiris his whole domain is a punishment — is the second great error in popular accounts, and this article will return to it at the settlement (Section IX), where the gods themselves are shown distinguishing between the crime, which is judged, and the power, which is retained and posted.

THE FIVE CHILDREN OF GEB AND NUT

Osiris, the murdered king; Horus the Elder, the sky falcon; Set, the storm; Isis, great of magic; Nephthys, the mourner. Set is the middle term of the five: neither the firstborn king nor the youngest sister, but the strong brother in between — a position the litigation will exploit, since his claim to succeed Osiris rests on seniority and strength against the son's claim by birth.

SET AND NEPHTHYS

nbt-ḥwt [Gardiner: V30-X1-O10] [𓎟𓏏𓉡]. Husband and wife in the Ennead's pairing, yet Nephthys mourns Osiris, helps Isis recover the body, and guards the dead — her loyalty in the myth runs consistently against her husband's crime and with his victims. Egyptian theology tolerated the contradiction without dissolving it: a marriage is a cosmic pairing, not a political alliance.

SET AND THE FOREIGN GODDESSES

In New Kingdom accounts Set is joined with Anat and Astarte, warrior goddesses of the Levant received into the Egyptian pantheon. The marriages are syncretism written as genealogy: the god of foreign lands takes foreign wives, exactly as the god of foreign lands had already been identified with the foreign storm god Baal (Section XIII).

III. The Paradox — Disorder Within the Cosmic Order

The whole of this article is contained in one sentence of Christiane Zivie-Coche's study of foreign deities in the UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology, here paraphrased because it states the matter exactly: the border-crosser par excellence was Seth, who embodies that role in all his ambiguity — protector of the sun god in the solar barque, murderer of his brother Osiris, and god of the deserts, disturbing and threatening — a trickster. Three portraits in one breath, and all three are documented Egyptian positions, held simultaneously by the same civilisation, often in the same temple. Set is the Osiris cycle's antagonist and murderer. Set is also Ra's indispensable defender, the only god stationed at the prow of the solar barque with the spear against Apep. And Set is the god of the desert, of storms, of foreign lands and foreigners — of everything outside the Nile's green order that nevertheless presses on that order daily and cannot simply be wished away.

The archive's framing, following the analysis in the Apep article, is a threefold distinction. Apep is absolute chaos: uncreated, unpersonlike, outside the cosmos, seeking only its dissolution, never worshipped, never reconciled. Isfet, isft [Gardiner: M17-S29-I9-X1], is disorder as an abstract condition — the sum of wrong, decay and falsehood that mꜣꜥt [Gardiner: U1-D36-X1] perpetually holds down. Set is contained disorder: a created god, a member of the Ennead, whose violence is real, culpable, and punishable — and also usable, because the cosmos itself contains storms, deserts and enemies, and needs a power that is native to them. Only a being of Set's nature can meet Apep's gaze at the prow; the archive's Apep article makes the point in its own terms, that contained chaos is the only weapon against absolute chaos. Set is therefore not order's opposite number in a dualism. He is order's most dangerous employee.

That is why the familiar modern summary — "Set, Egyptian god of evil" — fails on the evidence at every period before the very latest, and fails even then in the ways Section XVI documents. Evil, in the Egyptian frame, is not a person. Persons can be judged, and Set is judged: the tribunal of the gods tries his crime at length and rules against him (Sections VIII–IX). But the same tribunal does not — cannot — vote the storm out of the weather. What Egyptian theology does with Set, across three thousand years, is more subtle than either worship or condemnation alone: it litigates him, binds him, posts him, marries him abroad, names kings for him, and finally, in a changed political world, erases him — and even the erasure is never total. Each of those verbs gets a section. The paradox is not a puzzle to be solved before the article can proceed; it is the article.

// THREE KINDS OF DISORDER //

Apep — ꜥꜣpp [Gardiner: E7-Q3-Q3] [𓃘𓊪𓊪]: the serpent outside creation; absolute chaos; destroyed nightly, returning nightly; no cult, no temple, no reconciliation. Isfet — isft [Gardiner: M17-S29-I9-X1]: disorder as a condition, the abstract contrary of mꜣꜥt [Gardiner: U1-D36-X1]. Set — stš [Gardiner: E20] [𓃩]: disorder as a person — created, related, culpable, indispensable. The first must be annihilated daily; the second must be held down perpetually; the third must be governed. Egypt's whole theology of Set is the technology of that government.

IV. The Set Animal — an Iconography Without a Species

Every other great god of Egypt can be introduced by an animal that exists. Horus is a falcon; Anubis is a jackal; Hathor is a cow. Set's animal does not exist — or, if it once did, no one has identified it — and Egyptologists therefore call it, with deliberate neutrality, the Set animal: the creature written stš [Gardiner: E20] [𓃩] and, in a second common form, [Gardiner: E21] [𓃫]. Its diagnostic features are consistent across three thousand years of representation: a slender, dog-like body; a long head ending in an elongated, downward-curving snout; two tall ears whose ends are cut off square — the feature found in no living animal and therefore the creature's signature; and a stiff, erect tail, in the full form forked at its tip or tipped with a tuft. From its oldest undoubted representations, in the protodynastic period (Naqada IIIA/B), the animal already has exactly this form: dog-like, square-eared, long-snouted, tail erect. The type was fixed before the pyramids and never fundamentally changed.

The candidates proposed for its natural original make a small history of Egyptology in themselves: the aardvark, the African wild dog, the saluki hound, the fennec, the jackal, the donkey, the pig, the oryx and other antelopes, the okapi, and the giraffe — whose flat-topped ossicones were once held to explain the square ears, though the Egyptians depicted giraffes distinctly and knew the difference. A recent re-examination of the iconography (in the journal Egyptological Hypotheses, 2024) restates the difficulty precisely: the body is undoubtedly that of a canid, while the face is anything but dog-like when set beside Anubis or the dogs of the hunting scenes. The honest conclusion, and the one this archive adopts, is that the Set animal is best understood as a composite or conventional creature — an artificial animal for an artificial category, assembled, like the griffin or the sphinx, to be recognisable precisely by being unnatural. A god of the unclassifiable gets an unclassifiable beast. Whether an extinct or locally known animal lies somewhere behind the type cannot now be recovered, and claims to have "solved" the Set animal should be read with the same suspicion this archive applies to the wedjat-fraction story in the Horus article: famous is not the same as demonstrated.

The animal's career in art begins at the summit of the state. On the ceremonial macehead of the predynastic King Scorpion (Naqada III) the Set animal stands on a standard among the standards of the powers of the land. Cylinder-seal impressions from the tombs of Second Dynasty kings show the Set animal standing on top of the king's serekh — the position the Horus falcon usually occupies — in the reign of Peribsen, who set the Seth-animal, not the falcon, above his palace-façade name (Section XI). From the Old Kingdom onward the god also appears in the mixed form that becomes his standard portrait: a man's body with the Set animal's head, wearing the tripartite wig, with or without the Double Crown, and the royal kilt. One of the few preserved full statues of this type, from Medinet Habu, is now in the Egyptian Museum in Cairo (JE 31628). The two forms — beast entire, and beast-headed man — are the same statement at two registers: the beast entire for the god's raw nature, the hybrid for the god in society, striding among gods and kings with sceptre in hand.

Around the central animal clustered a whole Sethian bestiary — the real animals Egyptians associated with Set's domain and temper: the donkey, ꜥꜣ [Gardiner: E7] [𓃘], beast of the desert tracks; the pig, šꜣ [Gardiner: E12] [𓃟]; the jackal, sꜣb [Gardiner: E17] [𓃥]; the oryx of the desert margin; the hippopotamus of the river's violence; the crocodile; and, in the hostile texts of the Late Period, the turtle and the serpent. In the mythic combat Set himself takes hippopotamus form against Horus (Section VIII), and in the Edfu temple drama treated in the Horus article he is harpooned as a red hippopotamus. The bestiary matters for the demonisation (Section XV): as Set's reputation darkened, his animal associations darkened with it, until in Late Period representation he is increasingly shown simply as a donkey or a donkey-headed man — the composite mystery-creature traded in for an animal of stubbornness and lust in the ancient stereotype — and in the Book of the Faiyum he appears, most strangely, with a flamingo's head. The iconography is a graph of the theology, and it can be read in either direction.

SQUARE-TIPPED EARS

The diagnostic feature. No natural animal has them; every Set animal does. Whatever the type's origin, the squared ear became the god's trademark — visible at a glance across a relief, and sufficient by itself to identify a damaged figure as Set's.

THE CURVED SNOUT

Long, thin, and downturned at the end — aardvark-like to some eyes, greyhound-like to others. Combined with the ears it produces a head that is elegant, alert, and slightly wrong: the visual equivalent of the god's character.

THE ERECT, FORKED TAIL

Carried stiffly upright, often forked or tufted at the tip like an inverted arrow. In the hieroglyph the tail is as diagnostic as the head: [𓃩] cannot be mistaken for any natural canid sign in the list.

THE CANID BODY

Slender, deep-chested, long-legged — the body of a desert hunting dog. The composite is therefore not random: desert body, impossible head. Set's animal is built the way his domain is built, out of the margin's real materials recombined past nature.

V. Iconography — the Was Sceptre and the God's Forms

In his anthropomorphic form Set is a man with the Set animal's head, and his furniture is the standard furniture of divine power, each piece of it legible. He carries the ꜥnḫ [Gardiner: S34] [𓋹], "life," and the crook ḥqꜣ [Gardiner: S38] [𓋾] in royal contexts; he wears the White Crown ḥḏt [Gardiner: S1] [𓋑] of Upper Egypt or the Double Crown sḫmty [Gardiner: S5] [𓋖], the "Two Powerful Ones," when his kingship is the point; and above all he carries — and in a real sense is — the was-sceptre: wꜣs [Gardiner: S40] [𓌀]. The was is a tall staff whose top is an animal head with long ears and a curved snout, and whose bottom ends in a fork. Its very name is the word for dominion and well-being: to hold the was is to hold power in its settled, effective form.

The connection between the was and Set is old, close, and — characteristically for this god — debated in its direction. The Metropolitan Museum's record for a Middle Kingdom was-sceptre head (25.3.284h, reign of Amenemhat III) summarises the scholarship with proper care: various interpretations exist of the staff's origins and its relationship to Seth; the staff appears already in Predynastic rock art; some scholars link it to the Seth animal, pointing to its symbolic use in the nome sign of Oxyrhynchus, a city where Seth was celebrated, and to the discovery of a gigantic was-sceptre in the god's temple at Ombos; others propose, conversely, that the god's iconography derived from the staff itself. Gardiner held that the sceptre's head is probably the head of the Seth-animal. The archive states the position as the Met states it: Set is strongly associated with the was, the was-head and the Set-animal's head are at minimum close cousins, and the direction of borrowing is unresolved. What is not debated is the sceptre's cosmic use: was-sceptres stand at the corners of the world holding up the sky, pt [Gardiner: N1] — the dominion of Set's sceptre is, quite literally, load-bearing. A Ramesside prayer could accordingly appeal to Seth himself as a supporter of the sky. The murderer's staff props up heaven. That is the paradox of Section III rendered as furniture.

Set's colour is red. He is the red god as the fertile land's gods are black-earth gods: red hair and ruddy skin are Sethian marks in the texts, red animals are his, and the red hippopotamus of the Edfu drama is his ritual form. The colour belongs to his domain term: the desert is the dšrt [Gardiner: S4] [𓋕], the "Red Land," against the kmt [Gardiner: V31-Aa15-X1] [𓎡𓐝𓏏], the "Black Land" of the flood silt — the same sign dšrt [Gardiner: S4] that writes the Red Crown of Lower Egypt, a coincidence of writing the Egyptians exploited and the modern reader must keep untangled by context. Section VI follows the red into geography. Here it is enough to note the iconographic consequence: where Horus's images run to gold, lapis and the falcon's precise black-and-white, Set's run to red ochre, red stone, and the desert palette — the colour symbolism of the two gods is the map of Egypt in two pigments.

Finally, Set is the god Egyptian art shows acting with his hands more than almost any other: brandishing the great sceptre he boasts of in the Contendings, grasping the spear at the prow, presenting the king, smiting the king's enemies in the company of Horus (Section XI), and — in the Ramesside images of Section XIV — standing in foreign dress as Seth-Baal with spear and shield. Even his erased figures (Section XV) are usually erased mid-action. The iconography never permits him the stillness of a purely contemplative god. He is the god of the verb.

VI. Lord of the Red Land — Desert, Storms and Foreign Lands

Egypt is two lands in more ways than the political one. The kmt [Gardiner: V31-Aa15-X1], the Black Land, is the ribbon of flood silt, in places only a few hundred metres wide, where everything Egyptians ate grew. The dšrt [Gardiner: S4], the Red Land, is everything else: the sandstone and gravel deserts rising on both sides of the valley to the horizons — beautiful, waterless, and lethal. Horus, in the standard theological geography, is lord of the Black Land; Set is lord of the Red. This is not a consolation prize. The Red Land is where Egypt's gold came from, where its desert roads ran, where its weather was made, and where its enemies lived. To be lord of the Red Land is to be lord of the conditions of Egyptian life rather than of its comforts.

Storms are Set's weather. In a land of almost no rain, the storm is not a commonplace but an event: the sudden black wind off the desert, the rare violent rain over the valley, thunder — which the texts hear as Set's voice, and which the settlement of Section IX will make his eternal employment, thundering in the sky in Ra's service. Unseasonal and violent natural events belong to his register generally: eclipses, earthquakes, and the disordering of the luminaries are Sethian disturbances in the sources that catalogue them. The star lore belongs here too: the northern constellation the Egyptians saw as a bull's foreleg was bound, in the astronomical texts, in Set's keeping — the imperishable stars chained so that the Sethian power among them could not run loose. Even in the sky, the technology is the same: not annihilation, but binding and posting.

Foreign lands are Set's country in the political sense. The Egyptian word is ḫꜣst [Gardiner: N25] [𓈉], "foreign land, hill country" — the sign shows the desert hills themselves — and its plural ḫꜣswt [Gardiner: N25-Z2] [𓈉𓏥] names the foreign countries collectively. Set is lord of the ḫꜣswt [Gardiner: N25-Z2] as Horus is lord of the Two Lands: the peoples beyond the frontier — Libyans to the west, the Asiatics of Retjenu and Canaan to the northeast, Nubians to the south — live in his element and, in Egyptian theological logic, under his patronage. This is precisely why he, alone of the great gods, could be identified with foreign gods rather than merely compared to them (Section XIII): Baal of Ugarit and Canaan, Teshub of the Hittites — storm gods of the foreign hills — were recognised by Egyptian theologians as Set wearing foreign dress, and by foreigners in Egypt as their own god wearing Egyptian. Zivie-Coche's border-crosser is a border-crosser because his domain is the border: the desert edge where Egypt ends and the world begins, and where everything dangerous and necessary — gold, incense roads, storms, invaders, and new gods — comes from.

The desert also made Set a god of the oases and of travellers, a fact Section XVI will need when the demonisation comes. The western oases — Kharga, Dakhla, Siwa, Bahariya — are islands in Set's sea of sand, reached only by his roads and watered at his sufferance; their inhabitants had a permanent, practical reason to keep the desert god's goodwill that Nile-valley theology, rewritten by later politics, could not talk them out of. Set as lord of the oasis is attested as a cult title in its own right. Keep the fact in reserve: it is one of the hinges on which the last sections turn.

// THE TWO LANDS OF THE TWO GODS //

The Black Land — kmt [Gardiner: V31-Aa15-X1] [𓎡𓐝𓏏]: silt, crops, cities, the ordered valley; the portion of Horus and of Osiris. The Red Land — dšrt [Gardiner: S4] [𓋕]: desert, storm, gold roads, foreign peoples; the portion of Set. The geography is moral without being dualist: Egypt needed both lands, feared one of them, and assigned the feared one a god of its own rather than pretending it was godless. What a civilisation does with the country it fears is give it a lord and negotiate.

VII. The Murder of Osiris — Set as Antagonist

Against all of the above stands the crime, and this article does not soften it. In the Osiris cycle — the most important myth Egypt produced, treated in full in its own article and in the archive's Osiris article — Set murders his brother. The Egyptian sources, which are allusive for a thousand years before they are narrative, know the core facts early: Osiris is killed by Set's agency, drowned and/or enclosed and cast away, his body recovered by Isis and Nephthys, in the developed tradition dismembered and scattered, and revived — not to earthly life, but to kingship of the dead — through Isis's heka and the offering of the healed Eye of Horus, the wḏꜣt [Gardiner: D10] [𓂀]. Plutarch's Greek retelling ( De Iside et Osiride, c. 100 CE) supplies the famous furniture — the measured coffin at the banquet, the seventy-two conspirators, the body cut into fourteen pieces — and this article, following the archive's standing caution, uses Plutarch only as a late Greek witness to a story whose Egyptian core is far older, never as the story's source.

The Pyramid Texts of the late Old Kingdom — the oldest religious corpus in the world — already hold the two halves of Set side by side, and the archive insists on the point because it disproves the tidy modern narrative in which a once-benign god was later slandered. In the Pyramid Texts Set is already the one who struck Osiris down, already Horus's opponent in the struggle for the Eye and the office — and already, in the same corpus, a power whose strength the dead king needs, a god who can be addressed, propitiated, and enlisted. Enmity and utility are coeval in the sources. There is no recoverable stratum in which Set is simply good, and none, until the very end, in which he is simply evil. Te Velde's study makes the complementary warning against the opposite simplification: later scholars who begin from the demonised Set of the Late Period and read him backward across three thousand years are reading the verdict of one political age as the character of the god.

Motive, in the Egyptian tellings, is constitutional before it is personal. Set claims the throne of Osiris as the elder surviving brother and the strongest of the gods — a claim by seniority and by capability, of a kind Egyptian succession practice itself recognised as arguable; Horus claims it as the son and heir — a claim by birth. The myth is therefore not a morality play about a villain's envy, though envy and violence are in it; it is a succession dispute between two principles of legitimacy that Egypt's own kingship held in permanent tension, prosecuted first by murder and then — and this is Egypt's distinctive turn — by lawsuit. The murder puts Set in the wrong. It does not put his claim outside the court's jurisdiction. Section VIII is the lawsuit.

One more datum belongs to the crime's dossier, because the demonisation will lean on it: the wound Set gives and the wound he receives. In the combat traditions around the litigation, Horus loses his eye — the injury behind the wḏꜣt [Gardiner: D10] and the whole healing theology of the Horus article — and Set loses, in the blunt symmetry of the sources, his testicles: the storm's generative power damaged as the falcon's sight is damaged, each god carrying the other's mark out of the fight. Egyptian myth thinks in such exchanges. Neither party emerges whole; that is why the ending cannot be a simple execution and must be a settlement.

VIII. The Contendings of Horus and Set — Eighty Years in Court

The fullest surviving telling of the succession struggle is a lawsuit. The Contendings of Horus and Seth survives in a single manuscript, Papyrus Chester Beatty I, written in hieratic in the Twentieth Dynasty, in the reign of Ramesses V (c. 1149–1145 BCE), probably at Thebes, and now in the Chester Beatty Library, Dublin. A. H. Gardiner published the editio princeps in 1931; Miriam Lichtheim's translation in Ancient Egyptian Literature, vol. II (1976), is the standard English version. The account of the text given here follows the archive's treatment in the Horus article, with the emphasis reversed: this is the one great Egyptian narrative in which Set is not a walk-on menace but a litigant — present on every page, arguing, boasting, cheating, sulking, and, scene by scene, losing a case he is never simply thrown out of court for bringing.

The story opens with the case already ancient: Horus and Set have been litigating before the Ennead for eighty years. Set's position in court is stronger than a modern reader expects. He is the elder; he is, by common consent, the strongest of the gods — his own argument, pressed repeatedly, is his service: he alone stands at the prow of Ra's barque and slays Apep daily, and he brandishes a sceptre of fantastic weight as the exhibit of his strength. Ra, presiding, favours him and resents the young claimant. Against him, Shu and Thoth — ḏḥwty [Gardiner: G26] [𓅝] — argue the son's right: the office belongs to the son of Osiris by birth. The Ennead is deadlocked, irritable, and — the text is unembarrassed about this — faintly comic: the tribunal consults the ram god of Mendes, writes for an outside opinion to Neith of Sais, receives her verdict for Horus with a proposal of compensation for Set, and watches Ra refuse to accept it and retire in a sulk to be coaxed back by Hathor.

Set's conduct of his own case is a study in the trickster's strengths and limits, the aspect of the god te Velde analysed in his 1968 study of Seth as trickster. He will not plead while Isis is in court, fearing her advocacy, so the tribunal removes to an island and forbids the ferryman to carry any woman resembling her across; Isis crosses anyway, in disguise, and traps Set into condemning himself out of his own mouth with a parable of a widow's son and a stranger seizing his father's cattle — Set, judging the parable, awards the cattle to the son, and the court laughs as he realises he has ruled against his own claim. Argument gives way to ordeal, and Set is formidable in every ordeal: the rivals dive as hippopotamuses to outlast each other submerged; Isis's harpoon wounds her own son, heals him, then strikes Set — who pleads kinship so effectively that she releases him, whereupon Horus, raging at her mercy, strikes off his mother's head, and Thoth replaces it with a cow's head, an episode the text uses to explain an iconography of Isis. Set finds Horus resting and tears out his eyes, burying them on a mountain where they grow as lotuses; Hathor heals Horus with gazelle's milk. Set's attempt to disgrace Horus by a sexual assault is inverted by Isis's counter-trick with a pot of lettuce, so that when Thoth calls the seed to account before the tribunal, it is Set's claim that collapses in the court's laughter.

Read Set's part whole, and a pattern emerges that neither the demonised nor the rehabilitated caricature captures. Set loses every legal test and wins or nearly wins every physical one; he is beaten by advocacy, by parable, by maternal cunning and by documentary evidence — never by superior force. That is not an accident of plotting. It is the theological thesis of the work: strength is a fact, but office is a judgment, and a cosmos ruled by mꜣꜥt [Gardiner: U1-D36-X1] is one in which the strongest god alive can still lose his case. Set's tragedy in the Contendings — the text permits the word — is that his one unanswerable argument, his service at the prow, is an argument for employment and not for kingship, as the settlement is about to show.

// SET'S CASE, AS HE MAKES IT //

Seniority — he is the elder brother, surviving heir of the previous generation. Strength — the strongest of the gods, with the great sceptre as his exhibit. Service — he alone spears Apep at the prow of the solar barque, daily. The presiding judge's favour — Ra backs him for most of the eighty years. It is not a frivolous case. That the court finally rules against it anyway is the point of the eighty years: Egyptian legitimacy is not seniority, muscle, or even service, but succession rightly judged. Set's counsel loses; Set's service is retained. Hold both halves.

IX. Judgment and Settlement — the Divided Land and the Placed God

The Contendings ends, after the stone-boat race — Set's boat genuinely stone, and sunk; Horus's pinewood plastered to look like stone, a cheat the text reports without embarrassment, since trickery in a just cause is Isis's own method — with the gods writing to the one authority not yet heard: Osiris in the realm of the dead. Osiris's answering letter reminds the Ennead that he created the grain that feeds gods and men, and that his messengers can fetch any heart that does evil. What eighty years of advocacy could not settle, the dead king's letter settles. Horus is awarded the office of his father and crowned. The standard treatment of the verdict's royal dimension — the srḫ [Gardiner: O33] [𓊁] serekh, the fivefold titulary, the son performing the Opening of the Mouth, wp-r [Gardiner: F13-D21] [𓄋𓂋], with the corrected value wp [Gardiner: F13], "to open," upon r [Gardiner: D21] [𓂋], "mouth" — is given in the Horus article and is not repeated here. Set's half of the judgment is this article's business, and it is the most instructive verdict in Egyptian literature.

Set is not annihilated. He is not imprisoned in the Duat, not fed to Apep, not unmade. In the settlement as the Contendings and the wider tradition present it, he is placed: given to Ra, to thunder in the sky and to stand at the prow of the barque — bound into service, in a station where his violence is exactly what the voyage needs. Other tellings preserve an earlier or parallel form of the settlement as a division: Geb judges between the two and assigns Set the kingship of Upper Egypt, up to Su, the place of his birth, and Horus the kingship of Lower Egypt, up to the place where his father was drowned, the Division of the Two Lands — a partition later superseded, in the tradition's own telling, by Horus's sole inheritance. The two forms of the ending agree in the essential point: the resolution of the Horus–Set conflict in Egyptian theology is a disposition of powers, not an extinction of one of them. The storm is given a province, a post, and a spear.

The settlement also explains the dual symbolism that runs through early kingship (Section XI): a tradition in which Set once held the South as of right can naturally produce a king whose serekh carries both gods reconciled, and a titulary in which the king is protected by both parties. Egyptian political theology kept the settlement's logic for as long as it kept the myth: every succession is the verdict re-enacted, and every reign is the storm re-posted. The archive's article on the Osiris myth cycle traces the story's other lives; the Weighing of the Heart shows the judgment pattern at the scale of a single soul, where Horus presents the justified dead — the precedent of his own vindication doing duty for everyone else's.

WHAT SET LOSES

The office of Osiris: the kingship of Egypt, the throne, the succession. The verdict is unambiguous and permanent — no later Egyptian source re-opens the question of who should have reigned.

WHAT SET KEEPS

His godhead, his province, his strength, and his service: the Red Land, the storm, the foreign countries, and the prow of Ra's barque. The court's judgment is on the claim, not on the god's existence or power.

WHAT THE COSMOS GAINS

A defender against Apep whose nature fits the task, and a standing doctrine: disorder is to be judged, bounded and posted — not denied, and not enthroned.

X. Defender of Ra — Set at the Prow of the Solar Barque

Every night, in the Egyptian account, the sun god Ra — rꜥ [Gardiner: N5] [𓇳] — sails the solar barque through the dwꜣt [Gardiner: N15] [𓇽], the netherworld, and every night the serpent Apep waits in the darkness to stop the sailing, swallow the light, and unmake the morning. The crew of the barque is a company of gods. At its prow, spear in hand, facing forward into the dark where the serpent is, stands Set. The image is one of the most repeated in Egyptian religious art: the Set-animal-headed god at the very front of the boat of the sun, in the act of driving his spear into the great serpent's head. The netherworld books that map the voyage — the Amduat, the Book of Gates and their companions — preserve the scene in its liturgical fullness, and the archive's Apep article gives the enemy's dossier: in its telling of the night battle it is Set who pierces the serpent's head while Isis works her spells, Set who can meet the serpent's gaze without succumbing, because contained chaos can look at absolute chaos as no wholly ordered being can.

Consider what the posting claims. The defence of the sun — the single most important event in the cosmos, repeated nightly, on which everything else depends — is entrusted not to Horus the vindicated king, not to Thoth the wise, not to Ma'at herself, but to the murderer. Egyptian theology is making a precise statement about the nature of the threat and the nature of the defender. Apep is not impressed by legitimacy, wisdom, or right. Apep is stopped by the one power in creation whose violence is of the same kind as its own and greater in degree, aimed — by judgment, by settlement, by the whole apparatus of Sections VIII–IX — in the right direction. Set at the prow is the doctrine of Section III with a spear in its hands: disorder, contained and posted, is the cosmos's weapon against the disorder that cannot be contained at all.

The posting also retroactively dignifies Set's whole dossier. The storm that wrecks is the same energy that, at the prow, saves; the strength that made his claim to the throne arguable makes his service indispensable; even his boast in court — I alone slay Apep daily — is, the tradition concedes, simply true. Temple and funerary art never tired of the scene, and ordinary Egyptians lived under its implication: the god who defends the sunrise is owed a certain gratitude whatever else he has done, and Set received cult, offerings and royal patronage for most of Egyptian history on exactly that basis (Sections XII–XIV). The demonisation, when it came, had to reckon with this scene — and Section XVI will show it failing, instructively, to erase it: at the Persian-period temple of Hibis in Kharga Oasis, Set is still at the prow spearing Apep, but he does it wearing a falcon's head, in Horus's guise, as if the deed could be kept only by reassigning the doer.

// THE PROW AS A THEOLOGICAL ARGUMENT //

The threat is absolute: ꜥꜣpp [Gardiner: E7-Q3-Q3] [𓃘𓊪𓊪], uncreated, unappeasable. The defender is the strongest violent power inside creation, himself judged and bound by the settlement. The conclusion the Egyptians drew and carved for three thousand years: the cosmos is not defended by purity but by governed force. Remove Set from the prow in the name of moral tidiness, and the serpent wins. Later ages that tried to remove him had to put a falcon's head on his body to keep the boat sailing — the image admits what the erasures deny.

XI. Set and Kingship — the Two Lords and the Strength of Kings

The Horus article establishes the constitutional theory of the state: the living king is Horus, the dead king becomes Osiris, and every accession re-enacts the verdict. Set's place in that constitution is the loyal opposition written into its foundation — and at the beginning, more than opposition. In the Early Dynastic period the kingship could be represented as a duality: Horus and Set together, the Two Lords, patrons of the two halves of the land, whose reconciliation was the unification. King Peribsen of the Second Dynasty set the Set animal, not the falcon, above his srḫ [Gardiner: O33] serekh; his successor Khasekhemwy set Horus and Set together above the serekh, reconciled, in a name proclaiming that the Two Powers are at peace. Egyptologists still debate the politics behind the monuments — schism, civil conflict, reunification — but, as the Horus article notes, their mere existence shows the opposition could be balanced as well as fought, and the serekh was the surface on which the balance was declared.

In the developed royal ideology Set contributes what Horus cannot: strength as such. The king is Horus by office, but when the king smites, conquers, and terrifies, the texts reach for Sethian vocabulary — the king's strength is Set's strength, his onset a storm, his spear-arm the prow-god's. Royal iconography of the New Kingdom repeatedly pairs the two gods in the king's service: Horus and Set together crowning the king, together presenting him, together — in the famous paired scenes — performing the ritual knotting of the plants of Upper and Lower Egypt around the windpipe sign, the sema-tawy, the union of the Two Lands enacted by the two old rivals as a single piece of state liturgy. The image is the settlement drawn: the litigation is over, the verdict stands, and both gods are at work in the state the verdict founded — one as its office, the other as its muscle.

The wꜣs [Gardiner: S40] [𓌀] belongs to this section as much as to Section V, because the sceptre of dominion is a piece of constitutional furniture. Gods and kings carry it; its Sethian head (on Gardiner's reading) means that the standard instrument of legitimate power in Egypt wears the storm god's face. The Sed festival, ḥb-sd [Gardiner: W3-F33] [𓎱𓄢], at which the ageing king's fitness was re-proved, drew on the same reserve: the king must show, among his other proofs, that the strength in him — the Sethian component of kingship — is undiminished. A kingship from which Set had been wholly expelled would have been, in Egyptian terms, a kingship that could no longer fight. The demonisation's political cost, when it finally came, was real enough that the Ramesside age before it had moved in exactly the opposite direction, as the next sections show.

XII. Cult Centres — Ombos, Oxyrhynchus, Sepermeru and the Oases

Set was not a god of texts only. He had temples, priesthoods, festivals and towns, and mapping them corrects the impression — left by the myth's dominance in modern retelling — that he was a purely literary villain. His oldest and most famous seat is Ombos: Egyptian nbt [Gardiner: V30-X1] [𓎟𓏏], Nubt, "the Golden," Greek Ombos, near modern Naqada in Upper Egypt, at the desert edge where the road to the eastern gold country leaves the valley. The very name of the town ties the god to the Red Land's wealth: Set of Nubt, "he of the Golden Town," is among his standing titles, and it is the title the Year 400 Stela will give him in royal form — "Great is the strength of Seth, he of Ombos" (Section XIV). Petrie's excavations at Naqada uncovered the early town and its Seth temple; a door jamb from the temple of Set at Ombos built under Thutmose III survives in the Petrie Museum. A second Ombos, at Kom Ombo far to the south, must be distinguished from the Naqada original — the archive follows the standard distinction between Nubt near Naqada, Set's ancient seat, and the later southern town whose famous double temple belongs to Sobek and Horus the Elder.

In Middle Egypt, Oxyrhynchus kept Set's cult — the city whose nome sign, as the Metropolitan Museum's was-sceptre record notes, is built from was-sceptres, and whose Sethian allegiance put it in ritual opposition to neighbouring Herakleopolis in the late mythological geographies. In the Fayyum and at Sepermeru, a substantial provincial town, Set was worshipped with particular devotion in the Ramesside period: Sepermeru housed paired temples of Set and Nephthys, and its Seth cult is among the best documented local cults of the age — a working town's working god, with estates, personnel and festivals, at exactly the period when the same god was the royal family's patron (Section XIV). Add the Delta cult at Avaris (Section XIII), the oasis cults of the west (Section VI), and a scattering of smaller shrines the length of the valley, and the picture is of a god woven into the religious geography of Egypt at every scale.

The cult's material signature includes one object of wonderful literalness: the gigantic was-sceptre found in Set's temple at Ombos, cited in the scholarship the Metropolitan Museum summarises — the god's own sceptre, at temple scale, in the god's own house. The archive's article on temple architecture gives the general grammar of such houses; Set's temples obey it in full, which is itself a datum: for most of Egyptian history a temple of Set was not an anomaly, a tolerated survival, or a piece of apotropaic insurance. It was a ḥwt-nṯr [Gardiner: O10-R8] [𓉡𓊹], a "house of the god," served by a priesthood, funded by endowment, and woven into the calendar like any other — because the god it housed was, whatever else he was, a nṯr [Gardiner: R8] [𓊹], a god, of the Ennead of Heliopolis.

XIII. The Hyksos Era — Seth of Avaris and the Baal Syncretism

In the Second Intermediate Period (c. 1650–1550 BCE), the West Semitic rulers Egypt remembers as the Hyksos — "rulers of foreign lands" — governed the Delta from their capital at Avaris, modern Tell el-Dab'a. The archive treats the period and its exchanges in the Hyksos article; Set's part in it is decisive for everything after. The Hyksos arrived with a great storm god of their own — the Baal/Hadad type of the Levantine coast and hills — and, following the logic of Section VI, they recognised him in Egypt's god of storms and foreign lands. At Avaris, Set was worshipped as the city's great god, in a cult that Egyptian sources themselves describe in superlatives. The Ramesside-era literary text known as The Quarrel of Apophis and Seqenenre (Papyrus Sallier I, British Museum EA10185) states the Hyksos king's devotion in terms quoted here from the standard rendering of that passage: "King Apophis chose for his Lord the god Seth. He did not worship any other deity in the whole land except Seth." The sentence is an Egyptian scribe's characterisation of a foreign king, written with hostile intent — exclusive worship being, to an Egyptian, itself a foreign eccentricity — and it is nonetheless the strongest single witness to what Avaris was: a Seth city.

The syncretism thus founded long outlived the Hyksos. In the New Kingdom, Egyptian theologians of the Delta worshipped the two storm gods as one: Seth-Baal. Zivie-Coche's UCLA Encyclopedia study, paraphrased here, notes the mechanics: Baal's name is often written with the Seth-animal as its determinative — the sign that could itself serve as the ideogram for Seth's name — so that the writing system enacts the identification; and Ramesside documents show an easternised god in exotic dress, spear and shield in hand, who is always accompanied by the name of Seth: an Egyptian god and a Canaanite god in a single figure, two aspects combined. Set was likewise identified with the Hittite storm god Teshub in the diplomacy and theology of the empire age. None of this was marginal or unofficial. It was the state theology of the eastern frontier, where Egypt's empire met Asia, and where the god of the border did the border's theological work.

The expulsion of the Hyksos by Ahmose I (c. 1550 BCE) did not end the Avaris cult — a fact the demonisation narrative usually omits. The Set cult at Avaris flourished after the liberation; the Egyptian garrison Ahmose stationed there was absorbed into Set's priesthood; and the site remained a Seth centre into the Ramesside age, when it became entangled with the royal family's own origins (Section XIV). The association of Set with foreign rule was therefore double-edged from the start: it could be read, later, as collaboration, or it could be read, as the Ramessides read it, as the god's long lordship over the Delta gateway through every change of regime. Which reading prevailed depended, as Section XV shows, on who was foreign and feared next.

XIV. Ramesside Favour — Seti, Sethnakhte and the Year 400 Stela

The Nineteenth Dynasty — the house of Ramesses — came from the eastern Delta, from the Avaris region itself, and it came as Set's family. The dynasty's second king bears the fact in his name: Seti I — "Man of Set" — father of Ramesses II, builder of the great temple at Abydos and of one of the finest tombs in the Valley of the Kings. His grandson Seti II bore the name again two generations later. Between and around them the dynasty's art shows Set at the summit of the state: Set and Horus together adoring and crowning Ramesses II and Ramesses III in the temple reliefs, Set presenting the king, Set's strength invoked as the type of the king's own. A dynasty that named its kings for the murderer of Osiris, in the full knowledge of the myth, in temples covered with the myth's imagery, is the single strongest proof that the paradox of Section III was a lived Egyptian position and not a modern reconstruction. The Ramesside reading of Set is the prow-god's reading: strength, frontier, storm-power in the state's service.

The dynasty even kept Set's era. The Year 400 Stela, found at Tanis and now in Cairo, was set up under Ramesses II and shows the king offering wine jars to Seth — whose figure was later erased, in the demonisation, from the stone that celebrates him. Behind the king stands an official named Seti, the stela's author: the future Seti I, in his early career. The inscription, in the standard modern reading, dates itself to "Year 400" of a king called Aapehtiseth Nubti — "Great is the strength of Seth, he of Ombos" — who is not a lost human king but Seth himself, furnished with fictitious royal titles, so that the stela commemorates the four-hundredth year of an era of Seth, counted back some four centuries before the Ramesside house — to the age, roughly, when the god's cult order at Avaris was founded in the Second Intermediate Period. The stela is thus a foundation charter disguised as a calendar: the Ramesside family presents its god's Delta cult as four hundred years old, older than the Hyksos expulsion, older than the New Kingdom, an institution of the land itself.

The Twentieth Dynasty opens with the name again: Sethnakhte, "Set is victorious," a military founder from the same Delta world, who seized the throne after a period of anarchy and founded the line of Ramesses III. The UCLA Encyclopedia's study of the dynasty notes that Papyrus Harris I frames his accession against that anarchy, and that his Elephantine Stela presents his election as a personal choice by the god Seth, formalised — on the usual interpretation of its lines — by an oracular ceremony in Seth's temple at Avaris. Set, in short, makes the king who bears his name. Add the period's domestic evidence — the cult at Sepermeru (Section XII), the god's marriages to Anat and Astarte celebrated in the Delta theology, Set's figure throughout the royal and funerary art of the age — and the Ramesside centuries stand as the high-water mark of the god's respectability: antagonist in the myth, patron in the state, simultaneously, without embarrassment. It is the baseline from which the fall must be measured.

SETI I (r. c. 1290–1279 BCE)

"Man of Set." Second king of the Nineteenth Dynasty, father of Ramesses II. Author, before his accession, of the Year 400 Stela; builder at Abydos and Karnak; his tomb among the Valley of the Kings' masterpieces.

The name again, two generations on — evidence that "Man of Set" remained a throne-worthy name at the very end of the dynasty his great-grandfather's line founded.

SETHNAKHTE (r. c. 1190–1186 BCE)

"Set is victorious." Founder of the Twentieth Dynasty, presented in his Elephantine Stela as chosen by Seth himself — the god as kingmaker, in the god's own temple at Avaris.

XV. The Demonisation — Erasure in the Late and Persian Periods

From the end of the Third Intermediate Period and into the Late Period, the balance tipped — and the archive uses te Velde's own term for the process, which Zivie-Coche's UCLA study adopts: the demonisation of Seth. Its mechanism, as te Velde established, is political before it is theological. Set had always been the god of foreigners. In an age when Egypt was successively conquered and ruled by foreigners — Kushites, Assyrians, and above all Persians — the god of foreigners became, by an inexorable association, the god of the oppressors. The border-crosser was re-read as the collaborator; the lord of the ḫꜣswt [Gardiner: N25-Z2] as the patron of the invader. Simultaneously the Osirian cults, in their late flowering, pressed the myth's verdict ever harder: Set was regarded, in Zivie-Coche's paraphrased summary, solely in his role as fearful enemy of Osiris — and, through his identification with foreigners in general, as enemy of Egypt as a unified state and society. The exclusion approached completeness in the official religion of the Nile Valley: Seth's name and figure were hacked out of temple reliefs — the erased Seth of the Year 400 Stela is one famous instance, and erased Seth figures on late New Kingdom reliefs at Karnak another — his animal's sign was avoided or substituted in writing, his statues were recut into other gods, and his temples fell or were rededicated.

The iconography tracked the theology downward, as Section IV anticipated: the mysterious composite animal gives way, in Late Period representation, to the donkey — Set shown as a donkey, ꜥꜣ [Gardiner: E7], or as a man with a donkey's head, the animal now read through the stereotype of stubbornness, lust and stupidity. Greek interpreters completed the identification their own mythology suggested: Set became Typhon, the monstrous storm-giant who assaulted Olympus — a reading Plutarch transmits and elaborates — and through Typhon the road runs onward, in the Greek Magical Papyri and late antique demonology, toward the later conflation of Set with Apep and eventually with Satan. Each step in that chain is a translation, and each translation loses the prow. By the end of the process the figure that survives in Mediterranean common knowledge — Set the villain, Seth the devil — would have been unrecognisable, not in his crimes but in his solitude, to the Egyptians of any earlier millennium: he is a Set with the barque, the verdict, the settlement and the temples all stripped away, leaving only the murder.

The archive's companion article on damnatio memoriae treats the mechanics of Egyptian erasure as a practice; Set's case is its largest theological instance, larger than any king's, because what was being erased was not a reign but a member of the Ennead. Names were cut out, ears — the diagnostic squared ears — were targeted on figures, the Seth-animal sign was in some contexts replaced by safer writings. Yet erasure on that scale is itself evidence: one does not mount a centuries-long campaign against a god nobody fears or needs. Section XVI gives the other half of the record.

XVI. The Limits of the Demonisation — the God Who Would Not Vanish

The demonisation was real, and it was never total. The distinction is not a modern softening; it is what the surviving record shows, and this article states the limits with the same plainness as the fall. First, the oases. Set's cult persisted in the western oases — the desert communities of Section VI, for whom the lord of the Red Land was not a theological embarrassment but the power on whose roads and wells their lives depended — long after the valley's official religion had turned against him. "Seth, Lord of the Oasis" is a cult reality of the late periods, not an archaism. Second, Kharga and Hibis. At the temple of Hibis in Kharga Oasis, decorated under the Persian king Darius I — that is, under precisely the foreign rule that fuelled the demonisation — Set stands at the prow of the barque spearing Apep, in the oldest of his saving roles; but he does it, in the famous relief, with a falcon's head, wearing Horus's face. The compromise is transparent and deeply revealing: the deed could not be surrendered, because the cosmos still needed it done, so the doer was disguised. Egyptian theology, forced to choose between its sunrise and its grudge, kept the sunrise.

Third, the margins of the valley itself. Even in the Late Period, Sethian material persists in the interstices of the official record: in the Book of the Faiyum's strange flamingo-headed Set, in magical practice where a dangerous power is precisely what a spell requires, in the Sethian colour and animal lore of the medical and magical papyri treated in the archive's magical practice article, and in place names, personal names and priestly traditions that outlasted the erasures in the Delta and the south. Fourth, the sheer incompleteness of the erasure itself: hacked figures remain legible as figures — an erased Set on a relief is still, to any informed eye, Set — and the great Ramesside monuments at Abu Simbel, Abydos and Karnak carried the god's paired image with Horus through the whole period of his disgrace, too large and too many to undo. The campaign against Set is best described in the terms Zivie-Coche uses: an exclusion that approached completeness in the official pantheon of the valley. Approached. The archive knows of no period in which Set received no cult anywhere in Egypt, and the historian's honest sentence is that the demonisation was a centre's verdict imposed unevenly on a country whose edges — the oases, the Delta marshes of his old cult, the desert roads — went on negotiating with the storm as they always had.

There is a final limit, and it is literary: the Contendings survived. The text that gives Set his day in court — his arguments, his strength, his service, his self-condemning parable and his placed-not-annihilated ending — was copied, kept, and transmitted by the same scribal civilisation that chiselled his face off the temple walls. Egypt preserved the case for the defence in its libraries while prosecuting the defendant in its reliefs. A modern reader who knows only the erasure knows half the archive; a reader who knows only the defence knows the other half. This article has tried to keep both in view at once, because the Egyptians, demonstrably, did.

// THE DEMONISATION, MEASURED //

What fell: Set's place in the official valley pantheon; his temples' endowments; his name and figure on public relief; his composite animal, replaced by the donkey; his reputation, exported to the Greek world as Typhon. What stood: the prow of the barque (at Hibis, in Horus's mask); the oasis cults; the Ramesside monuments too vast to erase; the Contendings in the libraries; and the doctrine underneath everything — that the storm is a power to be posted, not a rumour to be denied. The demonisation is a chapter in Set's history. The record does not permit it to be mistaken for the ending.

Lord of Foreign Lands ḫꜣswt (his domain) N25-Z2 [𓈉𓏥] Patron of the countries beyond the frontier — the root of both the Baal syncretism and the demonisation.
The Spearman of the Prow — (descriptive) S40 (the wꜣs of his dominion) [𓌀] Set at the prow of the barque of Ra, spearing Apep nightly. A descriptive title in this archive, not a translated epithet.
Seth-Baal stš + Baal E20 (determinative of Baal's name) [𓃩] The New Kingdom Delta syncretism: one storm god in Egyptian and Canaanite dress.
Lord of the Oasis — (cult title) E20 [𓃩] Set of the western oases, worshipped there into the latest periods, beyond the valley's demonisation.
Typhon — (Greek identification) — — The Greek reading of Set as the storm-giant who assaulted Olympus; the channel of his late antique reputation.

Among the standing descriptions the texts return to, the archive notes: Great of Strength — the quality the Year 400 Stela makes his royal style; Lord of the Sky's Thunder, the employment of the settlement; the Ombite, lord of Nubt; Lord of Sepermeru , the Ramesside cult title; Confusion's God only in te Velde's modern title, which this article cites as scholarship, not as an ancient name. His wife is Nephthys, nbt-ḥwt [Gardiner: V30-X1-O10]; his hieroglyphic shadow falls across the vocabulary of storm, turmoil and illness wherever the Set animal serves as determinative — a god whose name became a grammatical marker of disorder itself, and who was nonetheless, for most of three thousand years, prayed to by name.

XVIII. Esoteric Analysis — the Storm Given a Station

// READING THIS SECTION //

What follows is the archive's interpretive reading of Set, offered as such. It is not presented as established Egyptological fact, and it rests only on the documented material of the preceding sections: the litigation, the settlement, the prow, and the record — in both directions — of the demonisation.

The archive reads Set as the Egyptian figure of governed force — the proof, worked out in myth and maintained in cult for three thousand years, that a cosmos is not made safe by expelling its dangerous powers but by judging them and giving them a station. Consider what the tradition refuses to do at every turning. It refuses Horus a private revenge: the heir must sue. It refuses the court a simple execution: the loser is posted, not annihilated. It refuses the barque a purer defender: the spear goes to the murderer, because only his kind of strength answers the serpent's. And it refuses, finally, even the demonisation a complete victory: at Hibis the falcon's head is put on Set's body because the work still has to be done and there is still only one god whose nature does it. At every point the system chooses government over purity. The alchemical reading the archive develops elsewhere finds its exact analogue here, and states it as analogy, not as ancient doctrine: the volatile, violent principle is not cast out of the vessel but fixed by art into service — the storm retained in the work under seal, its energy the work's own. What the opus calls fixation, the Ennead calls a verdict and a posting.

Set is therefore, in this reading, the god of everything in a life or a state that is true, dangerous, and not to be enthroned: rage, appetite, ambition, the desert at the edge of the field, the foreigner at the gate, the strength that makes a claim arguable. The Egyptian discipline toward all of these is one and the same — name it, judge it, bound it, post it, and require its service against the greater dark. The failure modes are also given, with historical precision: enthrone the storm and you get the murder of Osiris; pretend the storm away and you get a barque with no one at the prow. Between those two disasters runs the narrow Egyptian road, and Set — judged, bound, speared serpent nightly, erased and not erased — is its unwilling patron.

// THE SET PATTERN, AS THE ARCHIVE STATES IT //

Admit the storm exists — the Red Land is real and has a lord. Judge the crime, not the power — the tribunal condemns the murder and retains the strength. Give force a station — the prow, the frontier, the thunder: posted violence in a defined direction. Never hand it the throne — service is not sovereignty, and the verdict on the claim is permanent. Expect the erasure to fail — what is chiselled off the wall stands at Hibis in a borrowed face, still holding the spear. This is the archive's interpretive summary of the Set material, offered to be tested against the texts of Section XIX, not as a doctrine to be believed.

XIX. Sources and Primary Texts

The Set material is anchored in primary sources of very different kinds — a literary papyrus, royal stelae, temple reliefs, and the writing system itself — and the archive's rule has been applied throughout: quote only what has been seen in a fetched source, and name the source. The one verbatim ancient quotation in this article is the Papyrus Sallier I sentence on King Apophis and Seth, quoted at Section XIII from the standard rendering of The Quarrel of Apophis and Seqenenre (Papyrus Sallier I, 1.2–3; British Museum EA10185). Everything else ancient is paraphrase of the works below, and is labelled as paraphrase where a modern scholar's formulation is being followed.
