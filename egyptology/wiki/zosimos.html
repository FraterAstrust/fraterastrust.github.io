I. The Name — Zōsimos the Panopolitan

Every other article in this archive that treats a person begins with a god, a king, or a text. This one begins with something rarer in the alchemical literature: an author. The Greek alchemical corpus is crowded with famous names — Hermes, Democritus, Moses, Maria, Agathodaimon — and modern scholarship regards nearly all of them as pseudepigrapha, honorific masks under which anonymous recipe-writers and commentators published their work. Zosimos is the great exception. As Olivier Dufault puts it in his study of early Greek alchemy (University of California, 2019), in contrast to the majority of names in the corpus, "Zosimus of Panopolis" does not appear to be pseudepigraphic: behind the name stands a real writer, working at a real date, in a real city, for a real patroness whose household, rivals, and questions can still be traced in his pages. He is, in the phrase this archive uses throughout, the earliest alchemical author whose works survive substantially — not the first person to practise the art, and not the inventor of its recipes, but the first practitioner-theorist whose own voice reaches us in bulk.

His name in Greek is Zōsimos — a common late-antique name built on the root of the verb "to live," with a sense close to "the living one" or "he who will live." His ethnic, the word that does the identifying work, is ho Panopolitēs, "the Panopolitan," the man from Panopolis. The manuscripts of the alchemical corpus style him regularly with that ethnic, and later alchemists often cite him by the ethnic alone, as "the Panopolitan," the way a modern field might cite "the Leiden professor." Two external witnesses agree with the manuscripts: the Byzantine chronicler Georgius Syncellus calls him a Panopolitan, and Photius, the ninth-century patriarch and bibliographer, calls him a Theban from Panopolis. Against this consensus stands a single dissenting voice, and it is an important one because it comes from the source that preserves the largest claims about his output: the Suda, the tenth-century Byzantine lexicon, calls him an Alexandrian. Michèle Mertens, in the introduction to her standard edition of the Authentic Memoirs (1995) and in her study of alchemy, Hermetism and Gnosticism at Panopolis (2002), works through the discrepancy with characteristic care. The eighteenth-century scholar Fabricius proposed the comfortable reconciliation — born at Panopolis, resident at Alexandria — and most historians of alchemy adopted it. Mertens is less comfortable: the Suda's compiler, she notes, knew how to say "adopted city" when he meant it, as he does for other figures; the likelier explanation is that his sources were silent or incomplete on Zosimos's origin and that Alexandria, the default city of Greek learning in Egypt, filled the gap. This article follows Mertens: Panopolis is the origin the evidence supports; Alexandria is a possibility about his working life, not a fact about his birth, and the distinction is flagged wherever it matters.

A second naming caution concerns the language of this article itself. Zosimos wrote in Greek. His technical vocabulary — the words for his apparatus, his operations, his spirits and tinctures — is Greek, and this article transliterates it as Greek, without Gardiner sign codes, because Gardiner codes belong to the Egyptian hieroglyphic script and forcing them onto Greek words would be a category error. Gardiner codes appear here only where genuinely Egyptian words are in play: the names of his city in its older Egyptian form, the name of the land itself, and the Egyptian gods and institutions that form his background. Those Egyptian terms follow the archive's usual protocol, set out in the reading note below.

One more name belongs in this opening section, because Zosimos himself insists on it: the name of the art. In the treatise transmitted as the Book of Sophe, Zosimos retails an origin story in which the first book of the art was called Chema, after the fallen angels who, in his telling, revealed it — and from that book, he says, the science took its name. The story is myth, and Section XVII treats it as myth. But the etymological instinct behind it is the same one the archive examines in the alchemy article: the Greek chēmeia or khymeia has repeatedly been derived from the Egyptian name of Egypt itself, kmt [Gardiner: I6-G17-X1-O49] [𓆎𓅓𓏏𓊖] — "the Black Land," written with the crocodile-skin sign km [Gardiner: I6], "black," the reed m [Gardiner: G17], the loaf t [Gardiner: X1], and the town determinative niwt [Gardiner: O49]. Whether or not the derivation is linguistically sound — scholars remain divided, and a competing derivation from Greek khymos, "juice," has its defenders — Zosimos's own framing is unambiguous about the geography of his art: it is Egyptian knowledge, revealed in Egypt, guarded by Egyptian adepts, and written down, in his case, by an Egyptian from the upper Nile valley who happened to write in Greek. The bilingual, bicultural character of the work is not an accident of transmission. It is the work's native condition, and it is why a profile of Zosimos belongs in an Egyptology archive at all.

II. Panopolis — Ipu, Khent-Min, City of Min

Panopolis is modern Akhmim, on the east bank of the Nile in Upper Egypt, a few kilometres north-east of modern Sohag. To understand what Zosimos's ethnic meant to his first readers, the city's layers of name have to be unfolded in order, because each layer is a different civilisation's reading of the same place. In ancient Egyptian the town was ipw [Gardiner: M17-Q3-G43] [𓇋𓊪𓅱] — i [Gardiner: M17], p [Gardiner: Q3], w [Gardiner: G43] — rendered in older literature as Ipu or Apu. Its cult name was ḫnt-mnw, "Foremost of Min," written here in a transparent phonetic spelling [Gardiner: Aa1-N35-X1-G17-N35-G43] [𓐍𓈖𓏏𓅓𓈖𓅱]: ḫ [Gardiner: Aa1], n [Gardiner: N35], t [Gardiner: X1], then mnw [Gardiner: G17-N35-G43], the god Min. The town was the capital of the ninth nome of Upper Egypt and the great cult centre of Min, the ithyphallic fertility god whose raised arm and flail made him one of the oldest continuously worshipped gods in Egypt. Greek speakers heard ḫnt-mnw and compressed it into Khemmis or Chemmis; and because they identified Min with their own Pan — the identification resting on the god's generative, wild-country character — they also called the city Panopolis, "Pan's city." Herodotus, who visited in the fifth century BCE, already knows it as Chemmis, with a temple and a curious local cult of Perseus. Strabo, five centuries later, describes a population of linen-weavers and stone-cutters — a craftsman's city, which is exactly the kind of city Zosimos's work presupposes.

The weaving matters. Panopolis was famous in the Roman period for its textiles — for fine linen and, increasingly, for the tapestry-woven wool that would make Akhmim's late-antique and Coptic fabrics famous across the Mediterranean. A city of weavers is a city of dyers, and dyeing is one of the four great crafts around which the whole early alchemical recipe tradition is organised (Section IX). When the Stockholm Papyrus, copied in the same broad period and region, devotes some seventy recipes to mordanting wool and imitating Tyrian purple, it is describing the daily economy of towns like Panopolis. Zosimos's own central technical metaphor — the baphē, the dyeing or tincture that fixes a new colour permanently into a prepared body — is a weaver-dyer's metaphor before it is a philosopher's. The archive does not claim that Zosimos personally stood at a dye-vat; nothing in the evidence places a vat in his hands. It claims the weaker and more defensible thing: the conceptual furniture of his art — preparation, mordanting, immersion, fixation, the colour that goes all the way through and cannot be washed out — is the furniture of the industries his city lived by.

Panopolis was also, by Zosimos's lifetime, a place where the old Egyptian religion, Greek learning, and the newer currents of Hermetism and Gnosticism met in unusual concentration. Mertens's Panopolis study takes its title from exactly this conjunction — alchemy, Hermetism and Gnosticism at Panopolis around 300 CE — and assembles the hints in Zosimos's work for the place those currents held in his town. The pattern outlasted him: a century and a half later the same city produced Nonnus, author of the Dionysiaca, the most extravagantly learned pagan epic of late antiquity, and the region's monasteries and copyists would preserve texts — Hermetic, Gnostic, and Christian alike — that were lost everywhere else. The archive's articles on Hermeticism, Gnosticism and the Corpus Hermeticum treat that wider world; Zosimos is its earliest Panopolitan voice, and the only one who speaks from inside a workshop tradition. His city also stood within reach of the older institutional Egypt: the temple House of Life, pr-ꜥnḫ [Gardiner: O1-D36-S34] [𓉐𓂝𓋹] — "house" written pr [Gardiner: O1], "life" written ꜥnḫ [Gardiner: S34] with its phonetic complement ꜥ [Gardiner: D36] — was the kind of priestly library and scriptorium in which the older Egyptian technical and ritual literature had been kept for millennia. Zosimos never claims membership of such an institution, and this article does not claim it for him. But the guarded, book-centred, initiation-flavoured character of his teaching, examined in Section XVII, is continuous with the culture of those houses in a way his Greek philosophical vocabulary alone cannot explain.

Finally, the city's gods frame his imagination. Min's city was a city of generation — of the god who makes things grow, beget, and come forth. Up and down the valley the same theology was stated in the language of heka, ḥkꜣ [Gardiner: V28-D28-G1] [𓎛𓂓𓄿] — written with ḥ [Gardiner: V28], the ka-sign kꜣ [Gardiner: D28], and the reed ꜣ [Gardiner: G1] — the operative power by which words and rites take effect in matter. Zosimos does not use the Egyptian word; he writes Greek, and his operative power is the pneuma, the spirit that can be drawn out of bodies and fixed into them. But a historian reading him against his hometown notices the family resemblance without needing to force it: in both vocabularies, correctly prepared matter, correctly handled by a correctly prepared operator, at the correct time, undergoes a change that is real, permanent, and charged with more than chemistry. Section XIX returns to that resemblance, labelled as interpretation rather than descent.

III. The Life — What Is Known, What Is Inferred

The honest biography of Zosimos can be written in one short paragraph, and this section will write it — then spend the rest of its length showing the working, because in Zosimos's case the working is the scholarship. What is known: a Greek-writing Egyptian named Zosimos, from Panopolis in Upper Egypt, composed a substantial body of alchemical treatises in the form of letters, commentaries, and technical memoirs, addressed principally to a woman named Theosebeia; he treated the earlier alchemical authorities — above all the Democritus tradition and Maria — as classics to be expounded; he described and had drawn a range of furnaces and stills; and his work was already authoritative enough, within a few generations, to be excerpted, translated, and fought over. What is not known: his birth date, his death date, his family, his occupation in any civic sense, his religion in any confessional sense, and whether he ever set foot in Alexandria. No inscription names him. No contemporary outside the alchemical tradition mentions him. Everything else in the standard biographies — the priest, the Gnostic initiate, the temple chemist — is inference from his texts, of varying quality, and this article marks it as such wherever it appears.

Even his date is a bracket, not a point. Mertens establishes that he must have been active about 300 CE, and Dufault, reviewing the whole evidentiary base, gives the outer limits as roughly 240 to 391 CE: he must postdate the authorities he comments on and the recipe culture of the third century, and he must predate the late-fourth-century writers who already treat the alchemical commentary tradition as established. Within that bracket, "late third to early fourth century, flourishing around 300" is the consensus formulation, and it is the one this article's header uses. The bracket matters for more than pedantry. A Zosimos of about 300 writes after the great age of the Greek Magical Papyri, in the same Theban-Upper Egyptian world that produced those papyri and the recipe books of Section IX; he writes before the Christian empire's suppression of the temples, in the last generations in which a pagan-Hermetic teacher could operate openly from a patroness's household; and he writes at exactly the moment when the older Egyptian temple economy that had sustained practical chēmeia for centuries was beginning its long contraction. His encyclopaedic urgency — the sense, everywhere in his work, that the tradition must be collected, ordered, and explained now, or lost — reads differently once that date is felt.

His social position can be recovered a little more concretely, thanks to Dufault's central argument. Reading the letters to Theosebeia against the sociology of late-antique scholarship, Dufault presents Zosimos as a client scholar: a learned man informally retained by a wealthy patroness to teach, to expound difficult texts, and to produce new work — the alchemical counterpart of the grammarians, physicians, and philosophers who staffed great households across the Roman world. The evidence is circumstantial but mutually reinforcing. Theosebeia's household, as glimpsed in the correspondence, has a library and servants of some standing, including a table-server whose duties imply formal dining; Zosimos is invited to dinner in a household that is hers and others'; treatises are written at her request and in answer to her questions; and his tone to her moves between deference, professorial command, and open polemic against rival teachers competing for her ear — precisely the register of a retained expert whose position depends on remaining the indispensable interpreter. None of this makes him a hired hand in the modern sense. The client-scholar of late antiquity was a friend, a dependent, and an authority at once, and Zosimos's self-presentation — the visionary of Section XVI, the guardian of the ancients' secrets — is the self-presentation of a man whose claim on his patroness's attention rested on being more than a technician. Section IV follows the relationship in detail; here it is enough to fix the category, because the category explains the corpus. Letters, commentaries, encyclopaedic collection, and polemic are what a client scholar produces. A temple's internal manual or a guild's notebook would look quite different — and, in the Leyden and Stockholm papyri, we can see exactly how different (Section IX).

KNOWN

Panopolitan origin (manuscripts, Syncellus, Photius); Greek authorship of an extensive alchemical corpus; Theosebeia as principal addressee; commentary on Democritus-tradition and Maria; apparatus descriptions with drawings; activity around 300 CE.

INFERRED — WITH GROUNDS

Client-scholar relationship to Theosebeia's household (Dufault, from the letters' internal evidence); Hermetic and Gnostic colouring of his religious thought (from his own texts); first-hand familiarity with workshop practice (from the precision of the apparatus treatises).

NOT KNOWN

Dates of birth and death; family and civic occupation; confessional identity (pagan, Hermetic, Gnostic, and Christian colourings all appear in or have been claimed for the corpus); residence at Alexandria (the Suda alone, against the consensus); any event of his life outside the correspondence.

A last biographical trap deserves naming, because the older literature falls into it constantly: there were other Zosimoi. The name was common, late antiquity produced several literary men who bore it — a historian, rhetoricians, a Zosimos of Gaza, a Zosimos of Ascalon — and Byzantine compilers were not always careful to keep them apart. Mertens's edition exists in large part to perform that separation for the alchemical corpus: of the mass of material travelling under Zosimos's name in the Greek manuscripts, she distinguishes the groups that may be attributed to him with certainty from extracts, summaries, and outright false attributions inherited from Berthelot's more inclusive edition. When this article says "Zosimos," it means the Panopolitan alchemist as delimited by that critical work. Where a striking doctrine rests on a text of contested attribution — the Book of Sophe material of Section XVII is the chief case — the article says so at the point of use.

IV. Theosebeia — Patroness, Sister, Addressee

Almost everything Zosimos wrote, he wrote to somebody, and the somebody is overwhelmingly one person: Theosebeia. Her name is Greek and transparent — "god-revering," "pious toward the gods" — and the Suda calls her Zosimos's adelphē, his "sister." No modern scholar takes the word as a statement about parentage. In the religious associations of late antiquity, "brother" and "sister" are the ordinary courtesies between fellow-initiates, and the salutation is evidence — of the kind this archive handles carefully — that teacher and pupil understood themselves as members of a shared circle with a shared commitment. What that circle was has been argued over for a century: Reitzenstein proposed an initiatory group on the strength of the word alone; more recent work, including Christian Bull's and Peter Fraser's, pictures Zosimos and Theosebeia at the centre of a small Hermetic ritual community; and a salutation preserved from the end of one of his letters, quoted by a later Christian author, greets its recipients in explicitly Christian terms, which has fed a parallel line of argument about the circle's Christian colouring. Dufault's review of the whole dossier is judicious: a Syriac translation of a lost work of Zosimos partly supports the circle theory while also suggesting that Zosimos himself stood somewhat apart from Theosebeia's group — its adviser and teacher rather than simply its member. This article presents the "sister" as a religious courtesy inside a real teaching relationship, and the competing reconstructions as reconstructions.

Theosebeia herself emerges from the letters with unusual clarity for an ancient addressee, because Zosimos is constantly reacting to her: to her questions, her other teachers, her impatience, and her requests for the parts of the doctrine he would rather not write down. She was, on Dufault's reading of the household evidence, an aristocratic woman who led a group of like-minded students of alchemy and who commissioned Zosimos's advice on the questions the group was working through. She was not a passive pupil. A Syriac-preserved text confirms that she taught others herself; the treatise On the Letter Omega records, in Zosimos's own defensive formulation, that she had asked for "things that ought not" to be mentioned — the final doctrines, the teleia — and his answer is a small masterpiece of the client-scholar's art: he pleads his predecessors. He had, he says, found no such final doctrines in the ancients; allegory he had interpreted; he had done what was appropriate for a commentary. Reverence for the tradition becomes, in the same gesture, an honourable refusal, a boundary on what patronage can buy, and an advertisement that there is a further teaching which his commentary faithfully serves. The whole letter-corpus runs on that tension: the teacher who must satisfy the patroness's demand for the art's secrets, and the guardian who must not surrender them to writing, to rivals, or to the unprepared.

The rivals are named, and they give the correspondence its polemical heat. Chief among them is Neilos, whom Zosimos calls, with audible irony, Theosebeia's priest — "your priest," ho sos hiereus — a religious expert attached to her circle rather than, so far as the phrasing suggests, to any temple, god, or city. In the treatise On the Treatment of the Body of Magnesia, Zosimos's most explicit attack, Neilos appears marvelling with his disciples before divine statues, and Zosimos's counter-argument is bibliographic as much as theological: Neilos's failure, like Theosebeia's own moments of "imperfection," is diagnosed as insufficient acquaintance with the Democritean corpus — he has not read the ancients properly. Other figures flicker at the edges of the polemic: a certain Taphnouti, against whose company Theosebeia is warned, and unnamed "uneducated men" and women practitioners whose readings Zosimos labours to discredit before he will expound a recipe, warning her against any interpretation that contradicts his own. Read sociologically, this is a retained expert defending his retainer. Read as the history of alchemy, it is something more valuable: the first surviving record of alchemical controversy — competing teachers, competing readings of the same ancient texts, and a patroness whose favour was worth competing for. The art, in Zosimos's pages, is already a tradition with insiders, outsiders, and an orthodoxy under construction.

The doctrinal content of the teaching she received can be sampled from the work Dufault analyses most closely, the Final Count (also rendered Final Quittance). There Zosimos lays out a history of the tinctures and then turns the history into an exhortation: Theosebeia is to stop wandering in search of God from place to place, to cease sacrificing to the local overseer-spirits, to stay home and rectify herself — the Greek verb is dieuthynein, to make straight, to set right — and so avert the daimones and perfect her soul. Doing so, he promises, she will find the "genuine, natural and propitious tinctures." The passage is the whole Zosimian synthesis in miniature, and Section XV unpacks it: laboratory success and spiritual condition are not two topics but one, because the tincture that takes permanently in metal and the rectification that takes permanently in the soul are, for Zosimos, the same operation described in two registers. It is also, read plainly, pastoral counsel from a teacher to a patroness he believed was being led astray by competitors — theology doing the work of a professional boundary. Both readings are true to the text, and the archive keeps both.

The letter form itself deserves a closing word. Zosimos did not write a systematic treatise and then excerpt it into letters; the letters are the work, each one occasional — answering a question, correcting an error, expounding a passage of Democritus or Maria, prefacing a technical memoir — and the system, such as it is, must be reconstructed across them, which is what Mertens's edition and the scholarship of Section XX collectively do. The Arabic tradition understood the form perfectly and preserved it: the Book of Pictures (Section VIII) is cast as a dialogue between Zosimos and Theosebeia in which she complains, with some justice, about the ambiguity of his explanations, and he answers with images. A teacher whose pupil talks back in the text is a teacher being read closely by a real person. Of all the evidence for the historical Zosimos, that may be the most humanly convincing.

V. The Corpus — the Cheirokmēta and the Twenty-Eight Books

Zosimos called his subject-matter by a word that states his whole conception of it: Cheirokmēta (χειρόκμητα), "things made by hand" — hand-wrought products, artefacts, the output of skilled manual work raised to a science. The word is rare and deliberate. Alchemy, in its author's own title for it, is not a speculation about matter but a making, and the treatises that expound it are commentaries on makings: recipes, apparatus, and the authorities who handed both down. The Latin tradition would later garble the word in the most instructive possible way. The early medieval recipe collection known as the Mappae Clavicula — "the little key [and] the cloth," a nonsense title — is now generally identified, following Halleux and Meyvaert, as a Latin translation of a lost Greek recipe corpus whose title joined kleidion, "little key," to cheirokmētōn, "of the hand-made things": a translator who misread the rare Greek word as cheiromaktron, "hand-towel," produced mappa, "cloth," and nonsense with a pedigree. The episode, treated in the archive's research dossier on repeatable processes, is a parable of the transmission this whole article traces: Zosimos's world survived into the Latin West even where its vocabulary was no longer understood.

Against that background, the largest claim in the tradition can be stated and weighed. The Suda credits Zosimos with a great alchemical work in twenty-eight books, organised — in the usual account of the entry — under the letters of the Greek alphabet and addressed to Theosebeia. This article presents that figure exactly as the task of honest scholarship requires: as tradition and attribution, not as a surviving table of contents. No twenty-eight-book work exists. What exists is roughly a hundred pages of Greek under his name, a substantial Syriac corpus, and an Arabic tradition of unexpected richness (Sections VI–VIII) — fragments, extracts, and reworkings from which the shape of a much larger œuvre can be glimpsed but not recovered. Two considerations nevertheless keep the Suda's number from being mere legend. First, the letter-organisation it implies is independently attested in the Syriac transmission, where a surviving treatise on the making of tin is headed as the work of the Letter Ḥet — the eighth letter of the Syriac alphabet — and greets "the queen Theosebeia": a book numbered by its letter, exactly as the encyclopaedia tradition describes. Second, the scale is credible for the genre and the man. Zosimos's surviving excerpts refer backward and forward to other treatises of his own, preface lost works, and summarise lost works; the Final Count presupposes a history of the art in many parts; the commentator's posture itself — expounding a canon — implies an author who thought in corpora. The archive's verdict, following Mertens's caution, is that Zosimos very probably did compose an encyclopaedic lettered collection of which the Suda's twenty-eight is the tradition's count, and that reconstructing its contents book by book is not possible on present evidence. Both halves of that sentence are load-bearing.

What the encyclopaedic ambition consisted in can still be described, because its habits survive even where its volumes do not. Zosimos collects: authorities, recipes, apparatus designs, etymologies, origin-myths, and dream-visions all enter the same corpus and are made to comment on one another. He cross-refers: a tincture expounded in a letter is the same tincture whose preparation the apparatus memoir makes possible and whose meaning the vision dramatises. He argues from precedent like a jurist and from canon like a theologian, citing Democritus against Neilos (Section IV) as a church father cites scripture. And he writes, in the Authentic Memoirs, what amount to the first textbooks of the art's hardware — descriptions meant to be built from. Earlier alchemy, so far as it survives, is recipes and revelation-frames (Section IX; the pseudo-Democritus article of the tradition is treated in the dossier and in the alchemy article). Zosimos adds the third genre that turns a recipe tradition into a literature: the commentary, in which an identified author takes responsibility for what the ancients meant. Dufault's formulation is the sharpest available: Zosimos is the first known commentator of alchemy. The twenty-eight books, whatever their exact contents, were the library that commentary served.

// THE CORPUS AT A GLANCE //

Greek: c. 100–109 pages under Zosimos's name in the Byzantine compendia, delimited by Mertens into four attributable groups (Section VI). Syriac: some 64 pages in three late manuscripts (15th–16th c.), preserving material lost or abbreviated in Greek, including treatises organised by letter. Arabic: the Book of Pictures and the Book of the Keys of the Work, extensive quotation in later authors, and manuscripts catalogued by Sezgin in six libraries (Section VIII). Tradition's frame: the Suda's 28 lettered books, the Cheirokmēta, addressed to Theosebeia. Nothing like the whole survives in any one language; the corpus is a triangulation, and every modern account of Zosimos is an exercise in reading three damaged witnesses against each other.

VI. The Authentic Memoirs — Mertens and the Four Groups

The Greek Zosimos reaches us inside a collective artefact: the Greek alchemical corpus, a compendium of some forty authors probably first assembled at Constantinople in the seventh or eighth century and preserved in codices copied between about 1000 and 1500. The chief witnesses are conventionally cited by letter. M is Marcianus graecus 299, of the tenth or eleventh century, now in the Biblioteca Marciana in Venice — the oldest and most important alchemical codex, and the same manuscript that preserves, on a single famous leaf, the diagrams of Cleopatra the Alchemist (Section XIV). B is Parisinus graecus 2325, thirteenth century; A is Parisinus graecus 2327, fifteenth century; a Laurentian manuscript (L) completes the usual sigla. These are copies of copies, seven hundred to twelve hundred years younger than their author, made by scribes who did not always understand the apparatus they were drawing — a fact with consequences Section XIV states plainly. The first printed edition of the corpus, Marcellin Berthelot and Charles-Émile Ruelle's Collection des anciens alchimistes grecs (1887–88), made the texts generally available and remains, in Dufault's assessment, dated and incomplete yet still the best edition for many works. For Zosimos it has been superseded.

The supersession is Michèle Mertens's life work on the corpus, published as Les alchimistes grecs, Tome IV.1: Zosime de Panopolis: Mémoires authentiques (Les Belles Lettres, 1995), with a long introduction — over a hundred pages of it — that remains the foundation of all modern Zosimos biography and bibliography. Mertens sorted the jumble of the Greek manuscripts into four groups of works attributable to Zosimos with certainty:

1 · THE AUTHENTIC MEMOIRS

Thirteen short treatises, also transmitted under the title On Apparatus and Furnaces. They open with the programmatic On the Letter Omega and contain the apparatus descriptions with their drawings, the discussions of divine water, and the three dream-vision texts known as the Visions or On Excellence (Peri aretēs). The technical heart of the corpus, and the subject of Mertens's edition.

2 · CHAPTERS TO EUSEBIA

A second letter-group, addressed to an Eusebia — whether a second patroness, a variant of Theosebeia's name in transmission, or a distinct circle is debated; the archive notes the group and the debate without resolving either.

3 · CHAPTERS TO THEODORUS

A third letter-group, to a male addressee — evidence that Theosebeia, for all her centrality, was not Zosimos's only correspondent, and that his teaching circulated in more than one household.

4 · THE BOOK OF SOPHE AND THE FINAL COUNT

The most myth-laden group: the Book of Sophe material, with its account of the art's revelation by fallen angels, and the Final Count (or Final Quittance), the summation addressed to Theosebeia analysed in Section IV. Attribution within this group is the most delicate in the corpus, and individual doctrines drawn from it are flagged accordingly in this article.

Two features of Mertens's work deserve emphasis for any reader who will go on to the texts themselves. The first is her apparatus dossier: the edition prints some fifty apparatus diagrams preserved across the manuscripts, making visible at a glance both the richness of the tradition and its instability — the same device drawn recognisably in one codex and decoratively in another. The second is her exclusion list: works Berthelot and Ruelle had printed as Zosimos's are shown to be later exegetes' compositions, and one memoir in the received sequence is argued out of the authentic set altogether. The practical consequence for this archive is a rule of citation: doctrines are attributed to Zosimos here only where they stand in Mertens's attributable groups, in the Syriac corpus under his name, or in the Arabic books whose Zosimian core is argued by their editors — and the visionary and technical material on which this article's centre of gravity rests, the Visions and the apparatus memoirs, stands in the securest group of all.

The Authentic Memoirs reward a word about their double title. As Memoirs they are recollections — of authorities, of visions, of teaching given. As On Apparatus and Furnaces they are a manual. The two titles together are the man: no earlier writer in the tradition insists so strongly that the furnace and the dream belong in the same book, expounded by the same voice, for the same pupil. Sections XI to XVI follow the two halves of that programme in order — first the hardware, then the waters and the visions — because that is the order in which a reader of Zosimos can still check him: the apparatus either works as described or it does not, and its working is the ground on which the visions ask to be read.

VII. The Syriac Transmission

Greek was not the only language in which Zosimos survived antiquity, and for some of his work it is not the best witness. A body of his treatises was translated into Syriac — the Aramaic literary language of the Christian East, and the great intermediary through which Greek science passed toward Arabic — and some sixty-four pages of Syriac Zosimos survive in three manuscripts of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, the most important being Cambridge manuscript Mm. 6.29. Berthelot published the material in the third volume of La Chimie au moyen âge (1893), in R. Duval's French translation, and it has been under-used ever since, partly because Syriac alchemy sat between two better-staffed disciplines. Its value is not supplementary but corrective: the Syriac texts differ from the Greek, and in places they preserve, in fuller form, material that the Greek compendia transmit only in abbreviation. A doctrine that looks like a Byzantine summary in Greek may stand in Syriac as the connected argument it originally was.

Three Syriac witnesses have already done work in this article, and they are gathered here so the reader can see the pattern. The lettered treatises: the treatise on the making of tin headed by the Letter Ḥet and greeting Theosebeia as queen is Syriac evidence, and it is the strongest single support for the Suda's lettered-encyclopaedia tradition (Section V). The teaching circle: the Syriac preservation of a lost Greek work is what confirms that Theosebeia taught others and was more than a pupil — while simultaneously suggesting Zosimos's own semi-detached position toward her group (Section IV). The recipe continuity: Syriac collections beyond the named Zosimos corpus — the manuscripts Egerton 709 and Oriental 1593 in the British Library among them — preserve ink and workshop recipes whose technical information, as Matteo Martelli has shown, matches the gold- and silver-ink formulas of the Leyden Papyrus X (Section IX), demonstrating that the practical stratum of the tradition travelled in Syriac alongside the theoretical stratum. Martelli's related demonstration is finer still: a recipe for dyeing silver sapphire-blue in the Latin Mappae Clavicula (its no. 91) appears in very similar form in the Syriac Zosimos — the same recipe surfacing at both ends of the transmission, in the two languages that carried the tradition west and east respectively.

The Syriac channel also explains the shape of what happened next. When Greek learning entered Arabic in the eighth- and ninth-century translation movement, it came substantially through Syriac — through the Christian scholars of Mesopotamia who had been translating Greek medicine, philosophy, and natural science for centuries. Zosimos arrived in Baghdad already twice-mediated: excerpted by Byzantine compilers, translated by Syriac scholars, each stage selecting for what its culture found usable. That the Arabic Zosimos of Section VIII is in places fuller than the surviving Greek is therefore not a paradox. The Arabic tradition drew on Syriac and Greek exemplars that were themselves older and completer than the codices that happen to survive in Venice and Paris. The manuscript lottery, not the quality of the tradition, decided which witnesses we hold.

VIII. The Arabic Transmission — the Book of Pictures and the Book of Keys

The Arabic Zosimos is the great modern surprise of the field, and it is still being absorbed. The bibliographical ground was old: Ibn al-Nadīm's Kitāb al-Fihrist, the tenth-century index of Arabic books, already mentions translations of four books by Zosimos — though inconsistent transliteration scattered them under garbled names, so that the tradition's own catalogue partly hid its author from itself. Fuat Sezgin, the historian of Islamic science, located fifteen manuscripts of Zosimos in six libraries — Tehran, Cairo, Istanbul, Gotha, Dublin, and Rampur. Mertens, analysing what was known of them for her edition, judged the Arabic tradition "extremely rich and promising" while regretting how little of it was accessible in translation. Two works, edited in the Corpus Alchemicum Arabicum (CALA) series under Theodor Abt's direction, have since shown what she meant.

The Book of Pictures (Muṣḥaf aṣ-ṣuwar) is an Arabic compilation of Zosimos's teaching cast as his dialogue with Theosebeia, organised in thirteen chapters, each introduced by a full image. The pictures are the argument: Zosimos and Theosebeia appear with sun and moon upon their heads, held in the hand of a great three-headed being, standing beside an alchemical oven drawn as large as themselves — a pictorial programme in which the teaching couple embody the solar and lunar principles their work unites. The text incorporates fragments of the Greek-known works, including material from The Sulfurs and from the letters to Theosebeia, re-set in a continuous dramatic exchange in which Theosebeia presses her teacher on his obscurities and he answers in images, bidding her ponder their meaning. B. C. Hallum's review of the facsimile edition, in Ambix (2009), states the compilation's further importance in its title: the tome of images is also a source of the Turba Philosophorum, the great Arabic-Latin dialogue of the philosophers — a direct thread from Zosimos's Arabic afterlife into the Latin West's most influential alchemical text (Section XVIII).

The Book of the Keys of the Work (Kitāb Mafātīḥ aṣ-ṣanʿa) is a commentary in ten chapters on an earlier "Book of the Ten Keys," presented at its opening as Zosimos's last text written for Theosebeia — his summation, offered with the claim that after reading it she would need no other. Its preamble's boast of clarity is, by the standard of the corpus, touching: the Keys commentary remains dense with the same symbolic idiom as everything else he wrote. What matters historically is the claim of finality. The Arabic tradition did not merely preserve Zosimos; it curated him, arranging the letters and memoirs into a curriculum with a beginning (the pictures, for the eye) and an end (the keys, for the understanding), and attributing the arrangement to the author's own design for his pupil. A further Arabic witness surfaced as recently as 1995, when translations of Zosimos were identified inside a copy of the Keys of Mercy and Secrets of Wisdom of the Persian alchemist al-Ṭughrāʾī — incomplete and non-verbatim, but proof that Zosimos was still being copied, excerpted, and absorbed into new syntheses a millennium after his death. English translations of both great Arabic books, by Abt and Wilferd Madelung, have made this stratum of the corpus available to readers without Arabic for the first time; the archive's own Islamic Egyptology article treats the wider civilisation that performed this preservation.

Set the three transmissions side by side and a methodological moral emerges that this article applies throughout. The Greek witness is earliest in language and richest in technical apparatus. The Syriac witness preserves the encyclopaedia's lettered architecture and the social detail of the teaching circle. The Arabic witness preserves the most continuous voice — Zosimos in dialogue, expounding images, summing up for his pupil at the end. No one of them is "the real Zosimos" to the exclusion of the others, and any profile built on the Greek alone — as profiles were built for a century after Berthelot — will systematically understate exactly the things this article most needs: the scale of the lost corpus, the reality of Theosebeia, and the depth of his later influence.

IX. The Workshop World — the Leyden and Stockholm Papyri

Zosimos did not invent the material he theorised over. He inherited a going concern: a practical recipe culture, centuries old by his day, whose purest surviving witnesses were copied in his own lifetime and in his own region. The Leyden Papyrus X and the Stockholm Papyrus (Papyrus Graecus Holmiensis) both come from Thebes, reached Europe through the collector d'Anastasy — the Leyden manuscript sold to the Dutch government in 1828, the Stockholm manuscript bequeathed to Sweden in 1832 — and are dated, on papyrological grounds, to the late third or early fourth century CE, with the Stockholm copy perhaps somewhat earlier over an older core. Lagercrantz and Caley concluded that they were written at the same period and in part by the same scribe. Mertens, introducing Zosimos, makes the connection explicit: the two papyri come from the Theban region and date to about 300 — which means they are probably contemporaneous with Zosimos. They are not his work, and this article never presents them as his. They are his world: the bench-level practice of the Thebaid in the decades when he was collecting, commenting, and visioning in the nome next door. The archive's research dossier on repeatable processes gives their full analysis; what follows is the summary a reader of Zosimos needs.

The first thing to grasp about both papyri is what they do not contain. Earle Radcliffe Caley, who translated them (1926–27), stated it flatly: "neither papyrus contains the mystical symbolism and allegorical indirection so typical of the true alchemical literature. Rather they consist largely of simple, short recipes." L. B. Hunt, the Johnson Matthey metallurgist who assessed them as a handbook, agreed: "a practical compilation… completely down to earth and free from alchemical mysticism." These are workshop aide-mémoires, written in abbreviated form for workers who already knew the processes, and they say so themselves: both papyri explicitly acknowledge that the alloys and gems they describe are imitations and not the real thing. The standard critical edition is Robert Halleux's Les alchimistes grecs, Tome I (1981), which prints the two papyri together — fittingly, as the first volume of the same series in which Mertens later edited Zosimos.

The Leyden Papyrus X comprises ten leaves, sixteen pages of Greek, and 111 recipes in Caley's numbering. Seventy-five concern the purification of metals, alloys, purity testing, the imitation of precious metals, and the colouring of metal surfaces. Fifteen concern writing in gold and silver — chrysography and argyrography, with real metal and with substitutes. Eleven concern dyes and the dyeing of cloth, chiefly purple. The final items (nos. 102–111) are titled extracts from Dioscorides on the very minerals the earlier recipes use — arsenic, sandarach, misy, cadmia, chrysocolla, Sinopian rubric, alum, natron, cinnabar, mercury — proving the compilation postdates the first century CE and was assembled from sources by a compiler who wanted his reference shelf bound into the same roll. Of the metal recipes, Hunt counts thirty-four on gold specifically: refining and testing, carat alloying, solders, gilding, gold inks. The governing criterion throughout is appearance honestly labelled: if an alloy looked like gold, silver, or electrum, it was called asem regardless of its ingredients — and the recipe that boasts of the result says, without embarrassment, that the product "will deceive even the artisans." The deception was a selling point of the imitation, not a secret about it.

The Stockholm Papyrus is the larger and stranger book: fifteen leaves, recipes unnumbered in the original, 154 in Caley's edition (1927), under Lagercrantz's title "Recipes for Silver, Stones and Purple." Only nine recipes concern metals. Some seventy — the long middle section — concern the imitation of precious stones and the improvement of genuine ones: the oldest extended gem-recipe collection surviving from antiquity, with ten recipes for pearls and more than twenty for emerald alone, besides ruby, beryl, amethyst, and sunstone. The remainder, another seventy or so, concern the mordanting and dyeing of cloth, with the emphasis on imitating Tyrian purple. The four crafts of the two books together — gold-making (yellowing), silver-making (whitening), stones, and purple — are exactly the four books of the pseudo-Democritus tradition (Section XVII) and exactly the fourfold division Dufault identifies as the definition of early chēmeia itself: tinctorial processes that colour metals into gold or silver, give stones or glass the appearance of precious stones, and dye textiles. Zosimos's philosophy is a philosophy of these four crafts. A reader who keeps the dye-vat, the alloy crucible, and the gem-colourer's oil-pot in view will not go far wrong in his visions; a reader who loses sight of them will mistake workshop metaphors for free-floating mysticism, which is the single most common error in the popular literature on him.

X. The Recipes Themselves — and What Happens When They Are Repeated

Do the recipes work? The question has a disciplined modern answer, because the papyri have attracted an unusual kind of scholarship: historians of chemistry who take the texts back to the bench. Caley's commentary identifies the chemistry; Hunt assesses the metallurgy; and a line of modern replicators — Lawrence Principe for the Leyden metal-colouring recipes, Pamela Bol for the Stockholm gem recipes, Daniela Schoelzke for the dye recipes — has reported what actually happens when the instructions are followed. The archive's verdict, following theirs, is that the core of the corpus is plain, repeatable workshop instruction, neither fraud nor allegory — with the hazard warning of the panel below kept permanently attached.

The papyri also preserve the period's dangerous operations: amalgam gilding and gold inks that are heated with mercury, copper-whitening with roasted arsenic compounds, silver purification by lead cupellation, and litharge dye-baths. Mercury vapour, arsenic oxides, and lead fume are cumulative poisons, and the archive documents these recipes as history, with their hazards stated — never as practice. The kerotakis of Section XI was likewise operated with mercury, sulfur, and arsenic vapours in a sealed heated vessel. Nothing in this article is a procedure to attempt; the repeatable recipes discussed below are the ones modern scholars replicated under laboratory conditions, and even those are reported here as findings, not instructions.

Cementation refining works. Leyden no. 25, for the purification of gold, directs — in Caley's paraphrase — misy, alum, and salt, four parts each, pulverised with water; the gold is coated with the mixture, sealed in an earthenware vessel luted with clay, heated in a furnace until the substances have melted, then withdrawn and scoured. Hunt's assessment is that the mixture evolves acids that dissolve base metals while silver chloride sublimes into the pot: "a reasonably accurate description of the early method of gold refining known as cementation." The procedure runs forward through Pliny, pseudo-Geber, Albertus, Biringuccio, and Agricola, was observed still operating in Colombia as late as 1833, and its cognate sealed-pot process in Agatharchides was experimentally reproduced in modern times with, in the replicators' judgment, full confirmation of its effectiveness. "Doubling" gold works — as alloying. Leyden no. 17 adds misy and Sinopian red to molten gold and yields, in Hunt's words, "quite a reasonable gold alloy not uncomparable with our modern 9-carat or 14-carat golds": the gold is extended, not multiplied, and the recipe tradition knew the difference. Gold solders work. No. 33 — gold two parts, copper one — has a narrow melting range around 920–930 °C and flows readily: "an excellent brazing alloy comparable with those in use today." Testing recipes detect gross adulteration only: no. 43's reheat test — pure gold keeps its colour; whiter means silver; rougher and harder means copper or tin; blackening and softening means lead — is, per Caley, "sufficiently accurate to indicate gross adulterations," while no. 32's tin-on-papyrus test is fooled by eutectic mixtures. The test battery is a working assayer's screen, not an analysis, and the texts' own confidence in it is correctly calibrated.

The single most instructive replication is Leyden no. 89, the "sulfur water." The recipe — a handful each of lime and sulfur in fine powder, in a vessel of strong vinegar or a child's urine, heated from below until the supernatant liquid looks like blood, then decanted — produces a lime-sulfur liquor, chemically a calcium polysulfide solution. Caley identified it as the only actual preparation of a chemical salt in the collection. Principe prepared it and applied it to polished silver, obtaining "a deep, reddish-golden color" — a sulfide patina that is exactly the "colouring" the recipe promises: silver made to wear the colour of gold, permanently, by a tincture in the strict workshop sense. The experiment matters beyond its recipe. It identifies, in a reagent a modern chemist can hold, the kind of liquid that stands behind the theion hydōr, the "divine water" or "sulfur water," of the theoretical texts — Zosimos's texts included (Section XV). Not every divine water in Zosimos is Leyden no. 89; the term covers a family of volatile sulfur-bearing preparations. But Principe's broader lesson governs all such identifications: ancient materials were impure and place-specific, so a recipe that fails with pure modern reagents may succeed with period-like impure ones, and vice versa. Replication is an argument, not a verdict machine, and its negative results must be reported with the same care as its successes.

The Stockholm recipes replicate in kind. Pamela Bol, working an emerald recipe — ground verdigris in linseed oil, the stones soaked overnight — produced "emerald-like stones," concluding that when ancient sources insist the imitations were visually convincing, "they are probably not exaggerating." Her quench-cracked variant shows the mechanism the text implies: heat the crystal, quench it so a fissure network reaches the surface, then let dye dissolved in oil or resin enter by capillary action; verdigris is optically unique among ancient greens because its refractive behaviour in oil and resin lets the colour live inside the stone rather than on it. Bol argues the whole stone-dyeing technology migrated from textile dyeing — the "opening" of a stone corresponding to the mordanting of wool — which closes the circle of Section IX: one tinctorial art, four materials. Schoelzke's replications of the purple recipes (European Textile Forum, 2022), worked directly from Lagercrantz and Caley, add the necessary humility: both papyri centre on alum, vinegar, and urine, but the texts are vade mecums — "there are always some steps or parts of the recipe missing" — and her madder-and-woad purples proceed by informed reconstruction, corroborated by archaeology, since Coptic textiles of the fourth to sixth centuries show exactly those madder-and-woad purples in the cloth. Even the papyrus's own manufacture of its colourant is repeatable: its verdigris recipe — a cleaned, lightly oiled sheet of Cyprian copper suspended in a closed cask over sharp vinegar without touching it, scraped after half a day, re-oiled and repeated "until the sheet becomes used up" — is straightforwardly workable, and is the ancestor of a pigment process used into the modern period. Against these successes the dossier sets the honest failures of identification: misy, sory, cadmia and the place-named earths and salts cannot be reproduced as single modern reagents, and a recipe whose active ingredient is one of them cannot be claimed as faithfully repeated — at best an argued substitution. That sentence, too, is part of what "the recipes work" means.

Why does any of this belong in a profile of Zosimos? Because it calibrates him. The theorist of the tinctures was commenting on a literature whose best recipes demonstrably do what they say: colour metal through and through, extend gold to a stated carat, part silver from gold by cementation, sink green into crystal. His central claim — that a prepared body can receive a baphē that changes what it is, visibly and permanently — was not a metaphor in search of a referent. It was the daily experience of the workshops of his region and century, stated by him, for the first time, as a general theory of matter and spirit. The visions of Section XVI are strange; the bench they allegorise was not.

XI. Apparatus I — the Kerotakis, Step by Step

The Authentic Memoirs — the treatises also titled On Apparatus and Furnaces — preserve the earliest substantial apparatus literature in the alchemical tradition, and its centrepiece is the kerotakis (Greek kērotakis, κηροτακίς). The name itself is a workshop borrowing: kerotakis was first the triangular palette on which encaustic painters kept their wax pigments liquid over heat, and the alchemical device inherits the word from the painter's bench — one more thread tying the art to the colour-crafts of Section IX. The device's function, stated plainly, is reflux vapour-treatment: to expose thin leaves of metal, for hours at a time, to the rising and condensing vapours of a volatile substance heated below them, so that the vapour acts on the metal's surface continuously — sulfidising it, amalgamating it, blackening, whitening, or yellowing it — in the closed, self-returning cycle that the theoretical texts call dyeing a metal with a spirit. F. Sherwood Taylor's classic reconstruction reads the device as functionally a reflux extractor, an ancestor in principle of the Soxhlet; another scholarly summary calls it "a kind of high-temperature double boiler" in which the operator "could boil mercury or sulfur and use its condensing vapor to heat copper or lead in a pan above." Both glosses are modern and are labelled as such here; the construction that follows is Zosimos's as transmitted, chiefly on the authority he himself cites for it — Maria (Section XIII).

THE KEROTAKIS — CONSTRUCTION AND OPERATION, AS DESCRIBED

1 · The furnace. A small charcoal furnace or brazier forms the base and supplies a steady, governable heat from below. Everything above it is built to sit in its rising warmth for a long operation, not a brief firing.

2 · The lower vessel. Into a vessel at the base, directly over the heat, the operator places the volatile charge — the "spirit" of the operation: sulfur, mercury, or an arsenic compound, alone or in the mixtures the recipes specify. This is the reservoir from which the whole action rises.

3 · The chamber. Above the lower vessel stands the body of the device: a dome or cylinder forming a closed chamber, its joints luted tight. The chamber's purpose is containment — nothing of the vapour is to escape, because the vapour is the worker.

4 · The plate. Inside the chamber, in its upper part, sits the plate that gives the device its name — the kerotakis proper, in some descriptions of copper. On the plate the operator lays the material to be treated: thin leaves or sheets of the metal to be coloured — copper, lead, silver, or a prepared alloy — spread so the vapour can reach their surfaces.

5 · Heating and reflux. The furnace is lit and brought to a steady heat. The volatile charge vaporises, rises, and meets the cooler upper surfaces of the chamber and the metal leaves, where it condenses — on the metal, and on the chamber above it, from which the condensate runs back down toward the heat, to rise again. The cycle is continuous and self-sustaining for as long as the heat is held: vaporise, condense, act, return. This is reflux, described and drawn a millennium and a half before the word existed.

6 · The dyeing. Under hours of this treatment the metal leaves change at and below the surface: they blacken with sulfide, whiten under amalgam or arsenic vapour, yellow toward the colour of gold — the colour-sequence the theory names melanōsis (blackening), leukōsis (whitening), xanthōsis (yellowing), with iōsis (purpling, reddening) completing the received scheme. The operator reads the work by its colours, exactly as the recipe texts read theirs.

7 · Opening and removal. When the colour is judged right, the heat is let down, the chamber opened, and the treated leaves removed — bearing a tincture that is not a paint or a wash but a chemical alteration of the metal itself, achieved by a spirit that has been drawn out of one body and fixed into another. Sections XV and XVI are Zosimos's own meditation on precisely this sequence of events.

Three cautions govern the use of this description, and the archive states them rather than leaving the reader to discover the gaps. First, the hazard is constitutive, not incidental. The charges named for the device — mercury, sulfur, arsenic compounds — produce toxic vapours, and the device's whole design concentrates them in a sealed heated vessel. The description above is historical documentation; it is not, and must not be read as, a buildable instruction set. Second, the forms vary. J. R. Partington's study of the device in Nature (1947), reviewing the manuscript pictures Berthelot had published, notes that the drawings make clear there were several forms of the apparatus, probably destined for different uses; the kerotakis of one memoir is a small brazier with an attachment whose use is not immediately obvious, and the name was also applied to the sand- or ash-bath arrangement Hoefer called a "bain-Marie à kérotakis." Third, the drawings are late. Every surviving picture of the device was drawn by a Byzantine scribe seven or more centuries after Zosimos (Section XIV). What is firm across description and drawing alike is the arrangement — volatile below, metal above, sealed chamber, condensing return — and the function that arrangement performs. Dimensions, the material of every part, and any single "correct" silhouette are not recoverable, and any modern model that presents them as recovered is inventing them.

Zosimos's own understanding of the device reaches beyond the bench, and the extension should be reported in his terms rather than ours. The kerotakis was said, in the tradition he transmits, to replicate the process by which gold is formed in the bowels of the earth: vapours rising through buried metals over immense time, colouring and perfecting them, reproduced in the chamber over hours. The furnace is thus a scale model of the earth's interior, and the operator's art a deliberate acceleration of nature — the same claim the pseudo-Democritean aphorism makes in words ("nature rejoices in nature, nature conquers nature, nature masters nature," in the archive's paraphrase of its three clauses) and the same claim Zosimos's visions make in images, when men are boiled in the bowl-shaped altar and come out spirits. It is not too much to say that the kerotakis is the physical object at the centre of his entire system: the place where his chemistry, his cosmology, and his soteriology are quite literally the same apparatus seen from three sides.

XII. Apparatus II — the Tribikos, the Dibikos and the Ambix

Where the kerotakis treats solids with vapours, the stills treat liquids: they draw a volatile "spirit" out of a mixture by heat and deliver it, condensed, into a receiver — the operation behind every "divine water" of Section XV. Zosimos describes the family with a precision that lets its members be distinguished, and the distinctions are the earliest surviving taxonomy of distillation apparatus.

THE TRIBIKOS — THE THREE-ARMED STILL, AS DESCRIBED

1 · The cucurbit. The liquid to be distilled is placed in the still-pot — the cucurbit — set over the furnace. In the gentler operations the pot may itself sit in a water bath (Section XIII) so that its heat is capped and even.

2 · The head. Over the mouth of the pot is luted the still-head, the ambix (below), a helmet- or cup-shaped cover that gathers the rising vapour into one place instead of letting it escape — the part that turns boiling into distilling.

3 · The three arms. From the head lead three outlet tubes of copper or bronze — the feature that names the device (tribikos, the three-armed). Maria's specifications, preserved by Zosimos, are the oldest workshop tolerances in the literature: the tubes are to be made of metal "as thick as a frying pan," and the joints where tube meets head are to be sealed with flour paste — a lute any workshop could make, applied where the vapour pressure would find any weakness.

4 · The receivers. Each arm delivers to its own receiving flask, set to catch the condensate as it runs down the inclined tube. The distillate — divine water and the other volatile preparations — collects apart from the residue in the pot: spirit separated from body, in the vocabulary of Section XV.

5 · The open question, kept open. Why three arms? The texts do not say. Fractionation (different fractions at different arms), parallel collection, and sheer capacity have all been proposed by modern writers; none is stated by Zosimos or Maria, and this article records the three arms as a fact and the explanations as conjectures. A nineteenth-century account of the same descriptions adds the observation that the flow of vapour was simultaneous through the several beaks, with condensation taking place in two or three receivers at once — a reading consistent with parallel collection, and still a reading, not a text.

The dibikos is the same construction with two arms instead of three — Zosimos distinguishes the two-beaked and three-beaked forms explicitly, and Cleopatra's drawn apparatus (Section XIV) is of the two-armed type. Behind both stands the single part that gave the whole family its later name: the ambix (ἄμβιξ), "cup," the helmet-shaped head that crowns the pot and delivers the vapour to the tube. Through Arabic al-anbīq the word becomes alembic, and "alembic" then expands, in Arabic and Latin usage alike, from the head to the whole still. Dioscorides, three centuries before Zosimos, already describes an ambix arrangement for gathering condensed mercury — a reminder, which Zosimos himself would have endorsed, that the commentator inherited his hardware as he inherited his recipes. What he added was not the still but the literature of the still: named parts, stated materials, specified joints, and drawings — the apparatus made teachable at a distance, in a letter, to a pupil who would supervise its building in another household.

Receivers, furnaces, and baths complete the bench. The receivers of the descriptions are small flasks, one to an arm, of a size to be lifted and poured from; the furnaces are the charcoal braziers of the kerotakis section, governable by draught; and the heating of the pot could be moderated by setting it in water, sand, or ash — the bath family whose named, theorised member is Maria's (Section XIII). No physical example of any of this apparatus survives from Zosimos's century. The evidence is textual and pictorial, and its limits are Section XIV's subject. But the family resemblance of the descriptions to every later still, down to the flour-paste lute — which remained a standard jointing of laboratory glassware into the age of ground glass — is not a resemblance modern readers project backward. It is the resemblance of a working design transmitted by people who built from the descriptions, in an unbroken workshop lineage the dossier traces through the Mappae Clavicula and Theophilus into the medieval craft books.

XIII. Maria Hebraea — the Bain-Marie and the Problem of Attribution

No account of Zosimos's apparatus can be honest for long without becoming an account of his chief authority for it. Maria — Mary the Jewess, Maria Hebraea, in Latin Maria Prophetissa, Mary the Prophetess — is known only through quotation, above all through Zosimos, who calls her "the divine Maria" and counts her among the sages. No treatise of hers survives under her own hand; her dates are placed by scholarly convention somewhere between the first and third centuries CE, in Alexandria; and everything attributed to her reaches us filtered through the commentators who cite her, Zosimos first among them. He is explicit about the debt: in his words as transmitted, many constructions of apparatus were written by Maria — she was his authority specifically on apparatus, as Democritus was his authority on the art's theory and antiquity. The three devices the tradition attaches to her name are the kerotakis (Section XI), the tribikos (Section XII), and the water bath that still bears it.

Maria's city is treated from the Egyptian side in Alexandria, and her apparatus tradition in full in the Greco-Roman hub's Maria the Jewess; this section keeps to what Zosimos's citations themselves support.

The bain-marie — Latin balneum Mariae, "Maria's bath" — is the gentlest device in this article and the most immortal. Its construction can be stated in one sentence and operated by anyone: the vessel holding the material is set inside a larger vessel of water, and the water is brought to the boil, so that the inner vessel is heated by the bath and can never exceed the bath's boiling point. The result is gentle, even, capped-temperature heating — for digestions and slow evaporations that direct fire would scorch, for preparations that must be warmed for hours without violence. The Latin name is first attested in the thirteenth century (Arnold of Villanova), is standard by the seventeenth, and survives unbroken in the modern laboratory water bath and the kitchen double boiler: the dossier's judgment is that this is the single ancient alchemical technology that never needed reconstructing, because it never left use. Water-bath heating as such is older than Maria — Hippocratic medicine already used it in the fifth century BCE — and the archive states the attribution exactly as the evidence supports: hers is the alchemical laboratory form of the device, and the name; single-handed invention of hot-water heating is not claimed for her, here or by the careful scholarship.

That sentence is the model for the whole Maria dossier, and this section now applies it without flinching. Attribution by tradition is not proof of single invention — for the tribikos, the sources themselves say so: it is not known whether Maria invented it, but Zosimos credits her with its first description, including the frying-pan thickness and the flour-paste joints, and description by a named authority is itself a historical event of the first order. For the kerotakis, the association with her name is traditional and strong (Partington notes the invention is attributed to her, citing Berthelot's Zosimos, who there quotes Agathodaimon — a glimpse of how layered the attributions are even in the primary transmission). For the bain-marie, name and form are hers in the tradition, over an older technique. Later tradition added more: stills and reflux condensers generally, a silver-sulfide "divine water" process, and the Axiom of Maria — "one becomes two, two becomes three, and out of the third comes the fourth as the one" — a numerological formula about the structure of the work itself, not apparatus at all, which reached its widest fame through Jung's use of it two millennia later. A claim that she discovered hydrochloric acid circulates in popular accounts; it is not accepted by historians of chemistry and the archive does not repeat it as fact. What survives the pruning is still remarkable: the earliest named woman in the history of chemical technology, known to us because the earliest alchemical author treated her writings on apparatus as canonical — and quoted her tolerances.

Maria also stands at the head of the doctrine of Section XV, and Zosimos's reverence for her is doctrinal as well as technical. The commentators who preserve her fragments credit her circle with the lifelike analysis of metals into bodies, souls, and spirits — the vocabulary in which a metal can be killed, made to suffer, and raised — and the Dialogue of Maria and Aros, an extract preserved by an anonymous Christian philosopher, shows her teaching in direct discourse: the prophetess instructing a prince in the magistery of Hermes. Whether the historical Maria wrote any of the words the tradition gives her is unrecoverable; what is recoverable is the function she performs in Zosimos. She is his proof that the art descends through named, quotable, technically precise teachers — that its authority is a chain of persons, not a mist of antiquity. Section XVII takes up the whole chain; Maria is its strongest link precisely because her link is made of frying-pan thicknesses and flour paste.

XIV. The Drawings — the Earliest Apparatus Literature, and Its Limits

Zosimos did not only describe his apparatus; the tradition he founded drew it, and the drawings — some fifty apparatus diagrams across the manuscripts, printed in Mertens's edition — constitute the earliest surviving body of technical illustration in the chemical tradition. Their most famous single page belongs not to Zosimos but to his fellow authority Cleopatra the Alchemist (third century, Alexandria — not the queen): the Chrysopoeia of Cleopatra is a single leaf, Marcianus graecus 299, folio 188v, carrying diagrams and captions that include a two-armed still of the dibikos type and a kerotakis-type device, and the most reproduced image in all alchemy — the ouroboros, the serpent devouring its own tail, enclosing the words hen to pan, "the all is one." That the leaf survives in the same codex as the fullest Zosimos, drawn by the same scribal culture, makes the Marcianus a kind of picture-gallery of the whole Panopolitan-Alexandrine circle: the still, the reflux chamber, and the serpent, within a few folios of one another.

The limits must be stated with the same prominence as the achievement, and the dossier's formulation is adopted here in full. No physical apparatus survives from Maria, Zosimos, or Cleopatra. The contemporaneous evidence is textual only — Zosimos's descriptions, in copies seven hundred and more years younger than their author. The drawings are Byzantine: Marcianus graecus 299 is tenth- or eleventh-century; Parisinus graecus 2325 is thirteenth; Parisinus graecus 2327 is fifteenth. They are not uniform — one Marcianus kerotakis is comparatively realistic, another clearly stylised — because the scribes were copying shapes belonging to devices they no longer built, the way a copyist reproduces the letters of a language he cannot read. A nineteenth-century observer, comparing the Paris drawings with the descriptions, reported that the figures "agree exactly" with the texts; the modern assessment is more careful, and the disagreement between the two judgments is itself the lesson: exactness is what a tradition claims for its pictures, and family resemblance — function, named parts, the metal-above/volatile-below arrangement, the specified tubes and lutes — is what the pictures actually certify. A reconstruction in period materials, further, is a fire and pressure risk on its own terms (charcoal furnace, luted sealed vessels), fit for display rather than operation; a reconstruction in modern glass and copper works precisely because modern distillation is the drawings' descendant, not because the drawings are engineering blueprints.

None of this diminishes what Zosimos began. Before the Authentic Memoirs, technical knowledge of this kind travelled in persons — master to apprentice, workshop to workshop — and the recipe papyri, for all their precision, assume a reader who already stands at the bench. Zosimos's memoirs are the first surviving attempt to make apparatus portable in text: described part by part, dimensioned in kitchen measures (a frying pan's thickness), jointed with named materials (flour paste), and pictured. That the attempt survives only through a millennium of copyists who degraded its pictures is the normal fate of ancient technical literature, and the right response is the one the scholarship embodies: publish the diagrams comparatively, argue function from converging witnesses, and refuse — as this article refuses — to print any single silhouette as "Zosimos's kerotakis" without its manuscript shelfmark attached.

XV. Doctrine — Bodies, Spirits and the Divine Water

Zosimos's theory of matter can be stated in his own working vocabulary, and this section confines itself to that vocabulary, in paraphrase, because his terms are more precise than any modern substitute. Substances divide, for the practical purposes of the art, into bodies (sōmata) and spirits (pneumata). The bodies in the metal work are the fixed, weighable metals — pre-eminently the four base substrates of the transmutation recipes, lead, tin, copper, and iron — which serve as the hypostasis, the substrate that receives change. The spirits are the volatiles: the vapours and sublimates of sulfur, arsenic compounds, and mercury, which rise under heat, escape from bodies they are heated in, and — this is the operative point — carry qualities with them. A spirit is a tincture in transit. Drawn out of one body and fixed into another, it endows the receiving body with a new, permanent quality: a colour, above all. The scholarly literature (Peter Fraser's analysis, as summarised in the journal literature on Zosimos) names these volatile agents exactly so: "tinctures," from the Greek baphai — dyeings. The whole art, in this analysis, is the controlled separation and re-fixation of spirits: in Zosimos's own programmatic summary of the work, as the opening of the Visions treatise phrases it in paraphrase, the composition of the waters, their movement and growth, the drawing of spirit out of body and the bonding of spirit within body — operations which, he insists, are not the addition of foreign natures from outside but one nature acting upon itself.

Soul stands between body and spirit in the triad this article's brief names — body, soul, spirit — and Zosimos's usage must be handled with the honesty the sources demand. The fully systematised triad, in which every substance possesses a body, a soul, and a spirit with fixed technical senses, belongs to the later tradition that systematised him; in Zosimos's own pages the analysis of metals into bodies and spirits does the heavy work, while psychē, soul, appears supremely in the human register — the operator's soul, Theosebeia's soul, the soul to be perfected — and in the lifelike idiom he inherits from Maria's circle, in which metals suffer, die, and are raised. Where later alchemy would speak of extracting a metal's soul, Zosimos speaks of extracting its spirit and of perfecting the practitioner's soul, and the two operations comment on each other without collapsing into one another. The journal literature on his doctrine of conversion puts the connection exactly: the pneuma involved in the conversion of metals is the same pneuma whose extraction from the human body — the "luminous pneuma" of Dufault's summary — is the goal of his spiritual teaching. One word, two benches, a single art: that is the doctrine in its author's proportions, and this article keeps those proportions rather than importing the tidier triads of his successors.

The agent of the whole system is the divine water — theion hydōr (θεῖον ὕδωρ), a Greek phrase whose genius is a pun: theion means both "divine" and "sulfur," so that the "divine water" is simultaneously the "sulfur water," the volatile sulfur-bearing liquor of the stills (Section XII) and the papyri (Section X). In the theoretical texts the divine water is what is prepared, circulated, and applied: metals are immersed in it, macerated in it, dyed by it; it fixes mercury; it is white and yellow, bubbling, alive. The modern journal literature on Zosimos reads the immersion of metals in divine water and the baptismal immersion of the imperfect human as deliberate analogues in his system: the imperfect metal, immersed, arises bearing an indelible new colour; the imperfect person, immersed in the mixing-bowl — the kratēr of the Hermetic tradition — arises rectified. Section IV quoted the pastoral form of the claim: cease wandering, rectify yourself, and you will find the genuine and natural tinctures. The doctrinal form is the converse: find why a tincture takes — why the prepared body receives the spirit permanently while the unprepared body sheds it — and you have found, stated in matter, the law of his spiritual pedagogy. Preparation is everything, in the vessel and in the pupil. The unprepared metal and the unrectified soul fail in the same way, by the same mechanism, and Zosimos's correspondence is the record of a teacher treating both failures with one medicine: right reading, right apparatus, right timing, and the refusal of shortcuts offered by demons and rivals — his texts warn, in the same breath, that daimonic interference produces a deceitful semblance of the art, and that the genuine procedure requires its absence (Principe's summary of the corpus makes the point a headline of his account).

One further doctrine must be recorded because it structures his whole sense of the art's timescale: transformation as turning inside out. Dufault notes that Zosimos described processes of transformation, the making of gold included, as a "turning" of matter "inside out" — the hidden interior of a body brought outward and made manifest as its new surface and colour. The image is kerotakis-shaped: what was shut inside the sealed chamber emerges as the treated leaf's visible tincture; what was shut inside the metal — its spirit — is drawn out and re-fixed as its glory. Scholarship is still clarifying exactly what he meant by the phrase, and Dufault says so; the archive records the phrase, its apparatus resonance, and the open question together, which is where the evidence leaves all three.

XVI. The Visions — Ion on the Bowl-Shaped Altar

The most famous pages Zosimos wrote are not recipes or apparatus memoirs but dreams. The three vision-texts transmitted among the Authentic Memoirs under the title On Excellence (Peri aretēs) — also known as On the Composition of the Waters — are presented here in paraphrase only, following the rule of this archive: no wording is placed in quotation marks that has not been verified in a named published translation, and the visions' English afterlife is too scattered — F. Sherwood Taylor's pioneering study of 1937, excerpts in G. R. S. Mead's circle, a full text-and-translation Oxford doctoral thesis of 2024 — for any single rendering to be quoted here as the text. What follows is the narrative substance, which modern scholarship (the Oxford edition's analysis of its scientific, religious, and literary contexts) reads in close parallel with the kerotakis process of Section XI.

The first vision. Zosimos, having discoursed on the composition of the waters — the separation of spirit from body and its fixation in body — falls asleep, and sees an altar shaped like a bowl, a phialē, with fifteen steps leading up to it. On the altar stands a priest, who speaks, in the dream's thin voice, of a descent of the fifteen steps into darkness and an ascent of the fifteen steps into light, accomplished by the one who sacrifices and renews him, casting off the grossness of the body: consecrated a priest by necessity, he has become spirit. Asked his name, the figure answers that he is Ion, priest of the inner sanctuaries, and that he has endured unbearable violence: someone came at dawn and cut him apart with a sword, dismembering him according to the rule of harmony, stripping the skin from his head, mingling bones with flesh, and burning them in the fire of the treatment, until through the transformation of the body he learned to become spirit. As Zosimos watches, the figure's eyes turn blood-like; he vomits forth his own flesh and collapses, tearing at himself — and a voice identifies what he sees: the man of copper, chief priest and sacrifice at once, who gives up his own flesh. Zosimos wakes and asks himself whether this is not the composition of the waters — the bubbling white and yellow water, the divine water — and concludes that he has understood rightly. A second sleep brings a further figure, the man of lead, submitting to the same unbearable violence; and a third vision shows the altar's place of operation itself, where men who wish to attain excellence enter, cast off their bodies, and become spirits — the place of the exercise, glossed in the text as the place of maceration, over which an igneous spirit presides amid the bubbling and calcination. A later sequence gives the teaching a second emblem: a serpent guarding a temple, which the operator is told to take, cut apart, separate bone from flesh, and recompose into the first step of the stair by which the temple is entered.

Read as literature, the visions are among the strangest documents of late antiquity: a technical author interrupts his treatise on waters with a dream of a flayed priest on a bowl, wakes inside the dream to interpret it, sleeps again, and interprets again — the text's own exegesis is part of the text. Read as the Oxford analysis reads them, in their scientific context, the bowl-shaped altar is the apparatus: the crucible-altar in which bodies are dismembered (analysed, taken apart), burned, and recomposed as spirits is the kerotakis and the still translated into a sanctuary, and the blackening of the metal — melanōsis — is the death the priest undergoes. Read in their religious context, the same ascent of fifteen steps, the same passage from body to spirit, belongs to the world of Gnostic baptism and spiritual ascent that Mertens's Panopolis study maps. The archive does not choose between these readings, because Zosimos does not: the visions' whole force is that the three registers are one event. The operator who has watched copper blacken, suffer, and rise tinctured in the chamber has, in the dream's logic, watched his own possible transformation — and the treatise that follows the dream returns to waters, weights, and apparatus without changing the subject.

Two disciplines govern quotation from the visions in the wider world, and the reader is warned of both. First, the Jungian literature made Ion famous — the dismembered priest became a set text of analytical psychology, and the Book of Pictures' sun-and-moon couple (Section VIII) a type of the alchemical pair — but Jung's Zosimos is a selection, arranged for a psychology, and should not be mistaken for the corpus. Second, popular accounts habitually quote the visions in unattributed "translations" of no determinate source, sometimes conflating Berthelot's French with modern re-translation. The versions a reader can trust are the named ones: Berthelot and Ruelle's French in the Collection (1888), Taylor's study, Mertens's Greek with her French rendering in the Mémoires authentiques, and the 2024 Oxford text, translation, and commentary. Everything in the present section is consistent with those witnesses and borrowed from none of them verbatim.

XVII. The Lineage He Claims — and the Guarding of the Teaching

Zosimos never presents himself as an inventor. He presents himself as a successor — the latest holder of an art that descends through named ancients, whose writings are the title-deeds of his own. The chain, as he constructs it, has five kinds of link, and distinguishing them is the quickest way into his mind. Hermes stands at the head: Hermes Trismegistus, the thrice-great, in whom the Egyptian Thoth — ḏḥwty [Gardiner: G26] [𓅝], the ibis god of writing and reckoning — had long since been fused with the Greek Hermes; the archive's Thoth-Hermes article traces the fusion. Zosimos cites Hermes as the art's fountainhead and treats Hermetic books as scripture of the discipline. Maria (Section XIII) is the chain's technical link: the authority on apparatus, quoted by tolerances. The Democritus tradition is its literary link: the four books ascribed to Democritus — in reality, as Matteo Martelli's edition establishes, a first-century CE composition descending from Bolos of Mendes (second century BCE), not from the fifth-century atomist — treating the four crafts in order, gold, silver, stones, purple, each recipe closing with a clause of the aphorism ascribed to Democritus's legendary Persian master. Ostanes, that master, is the chain's exotic link: the Persian magus whose refrain — nature delighting in, conquering, and mastering nature, in paraphrase — Principe reads as a compressed theory of chemical affinity, like acting on like. And Agathodaimon, the "good spirit," is its most spectral link: a name that surfaces in the apparatus citations (Zosimos quoting him on the kerotakis, in Berthelot's transmission) as pure authority, a voice from before the record. Around the five stand the lesser cited — Chumes, Moses, Cleopatra, the prophets and kings of the pseudepigraphic crowd — and behind them all, in the Book of Sophe material, the chain's mythic first link: the fallen angels who, desiring the daughters of men, taught them the art, whose first book was called Chema. The myth is cognate with the Enochic watcher-legends, and Zosimos retails it as the art's charter: knowledge this powerful could only have entered the world as a revelation — and a dangerous one, which is why its custody matters.

Custody is the second half of the section, and Zosimos is unembarrassed about it: the teaching is to be guarded. The guarding takes four practical forms in his pages. Obscurity of expression: the ancients wrote in cover-names and allegory, and the commentator's task — his own stated task — is to interpret what was allegorical for a qualified reader, not to publish the key to the crowd; pseudo-Democritus's recipes already end with the deferral "or as you shall hear," reserving the operative detail for oral teaching, and Martelli's and Principe's scholarship is clear that mystika in this milieu means things kept secret — trade secrets — before it means anything mystical in the modern sense. Refusal at the boundary: Theosebeia's request for "things that ought not" to be mentioned is declined, on the record, in the Letter Omega itself (Section IV). Polemic against the unqualified: rivals are dangerous not because they compete but because they misread — Neilos marvelling at statues instead of reading Democritus — and the pupil must be warned off their readings before recipes can safely be expounded. Moral qualification of the operator: daimonic interference spoils the work; rectification of the self is a technical precondition of true tinctures. A modern reader may find the guarding self-serving — it certainly served the client-scholar's indispensability — and the archive records that reading without adopting it as a reduction. The same guarding preserved the corpus: texts treated as secrets are copied carefully, hidden successfully, and translated reverently, which is more than can be said for the century's bestsellers.

The lineage's Egyptian root deserves the final word, because Zosimos states it in his geography even where he does not state it in his genealogy. The art's first book, in his myth, is Egyptian; its fountainhead Hermes is Egypt's Thoth wearing a Greek name; its prophetess Maria works in the Alexandrine Egyptian tradition; its Democritus learned, in the legend the tradition always attached to him, in Egyptian temples; its surviving recipe-books were copied at Thebes; and its commentator is a Panopolitan explaining, to an Egyptian patroness, why the genuine tinctures are found by staying home and rectifying oneself rather than wandering among shrines. The archive's Hermeticism and Chain of Philosophy articles trace the longer transmission of which this is the best-documented early link — documented, unusually, not by later claims but by the link's own letters, still warm with dinners, rivals, and unanswered questions.

XVIII. Transmission and Influence — Jabir, the Turba and the Latin West

Zosimos's afterlife runs through the three transmissions of Sections VI–VIII and out the far side of each. In Greek, he became the corpus's centre of gravity: the commentators who followed him — Olympiodorus, Stephanus, Synesius's circle before them — wrote in a genre he had founded, citing the ancients as he had taught the tradition to cite them, and the Byzantine compendia that preserve him are themselves monuments to his editorial influence. In Syriac, his treatises and the recipe-stratum he theorised travelled together into the Christian East (Section VII). In Arabic, the curation of Section VIII — the dialogue of pictures, the final book of keys — fed directly into the two great formations of Islamic alchemy: the Jabirian corpus, the vast body of treatises under the name of Jābir ibn Ḥayyān which modern scholarship dates to about 850–950 and treats as a school's work rather than one man's, which systematised the sulfur–mercury theory and the classified operations — distillation, sublimation, calcination — on foundations the Greek tradition, Zosimos central within it, had laid (its citations of Democritus and the ancients are real but mediated, often through later literary sources); and the Turba Philosophorum, the "assembly of the philosophers," for which Hallum's study names Zosimos's Arabic Book of Pictures as a source — the Panopolitan's dialogue with his pupil reborn as a parliament of sages. The archive treats this civilisation's Egyptian dimension in Islamic Egyptology and its alchemy in the alchemy article; the present profile confines itself to the thread that is demonstrably Zosimos's.

The Latin West received him by two roads, and the dossier's care in distinguishing them is adopted here. The first road ran through Arabic: the Turba, translated in the twelfth century, carried Zosimian material into Latin under other names, and the Arabic technical vocabulary — alembic (from his ambix, by way of al-anbīq), alkali, elixir, aludel, and "alchemy" itself — fossilised his world in every European language. The second road ran underground and in parallel: the Latin recipe tradition of the Mappae Clavicula (Section V), attested in a Reichenau catalogue of 821 and rooted in the Lucca Compositiones of about 800, transmitted the Greek workshop stratum — the same recipes, sometimes textually identical, as the Syriac Zosimos and the Theban papyri — without Arabic mediation, forward through Theophilus's De Diversis Artibus (c. 1125) and Eraclius (c. 1150) into the medieval craft books. The distinction matters for the history of technology: Europe's practical chemistry descends from Zosimos's world by a Latin manuscript tradition that never passed through Baghdad at all, as well as by the famous Arabic road. Both roads, notably, carried the recipes more faithfully than the commentary: the West knew Zosimos's art for a thousand years before it could read Zosimos, and the Emerald Tablet — which the dossier insists, and this article repeats, is a cosmological text naming no ingredient, quantity, temperature, or apparatus, and therefore not a recipe and not his — filled the vacuum where his theory should have been.

The modern recovery reverses the medieval pattern: the commentary came back first, and the bench followed. Berthelot and Ruelle's Collection (1887–88) printed the Greek; Berthelot's Chimie au moyen âge (1893) added the Syriac in Duval's translation; Halleux's Tome I (1981) gave the papyri their modern edition; Mertens (1995) gave Zosimos his; Martelli (2013) re-founded pseudo-Democritus; the CALA editions gave the Arabic books to modern readers; and Principe's The Secrets of Alchemy (University of Chicago Press, 2013) — the citation verified in this archive — carried the whole story, replications included, to a general scholarly audience. What that scholarship has collectively established is the double character this article has been built to hold: a theorist whose visions require the furnace to be understood, and a technician whose furnace requires the visions to be understood. Section XIX states the archive's own reading of that double character, labelled as reading.

XIX. Esoteric Analysis — the Operator Transformed with the Work

This archive permits itself, in a closing analytical section, the reading it has withheld from the evidence sections — offered as interpretation, tested against the texts of Section XX, and never to be mistaken for biography. The reading is this: Zosimos is the first alchemical author for whom the operator's transformation is not an edifying parallel to the work but a stated technical condition of it. The evidence is not a vague atmosphere of spirituality. It is structural, and it appears at every level of his system. At the level of doctrine, the genuine tinctures are promised to the rectified soul and withheld, by daimonic interference and false reading, from the unrectified (Sections IV and XV). At the level of narrative, the visions make the priest of the work into its material — sacrificer and sacrificed, operator and operand, in one figure (Section XVI). At the level of pedagogy, the teacher's central pastoral act is to turn the pupil from wandering among shrines to staying home and setting herself straight — the laboratory instruction and the spiritual instruction are the same sentence (Section IV). And at the level of the apparatus itself, the kerotakis models the earth conceiving gold in darkness over time: a vessel in which what is shut in, heated, and suffered-with emerges bearing a new and permanent nature (Section XI). A tradition that would later split into "laboratory alchemy" and "spiritual alchemy," and argue for centuries about which was the real one, is in Zosimos not yet split, because he had not yet made the cut. The cut is a later editorial act performed on him; the archive declines to repeat it.

The Egyptian resonance may likewise be stated, in the same labelled spirit. Zosimos wrote Greek and cited Greek authorities, and no line of institutional descent from the Pharaonic temples to his household can be documented — this article has said so at every relevant point. But a Panopolitan of about 300 CE, guarding a book-born art revealed by divine beings, teaching that correctly prepared matter in correctly built vessels undergoes real and permanent transmutation at cosmically right times, and that the operator's own rectitude is part of the preparation, is thinking thoughts whose nearest long-lived neighbours are the thoughts of the land he lived in: the land of ḥkꜣ [Gardiner: V28-D28-G1] [𓎛𓂓𓄿], where the word and the rite act on matter, and of the House of Life, where such knowledge was kept by being copied, guarded, and taught to the qualified. The archive calls this resonance, not descent, and offers it as the reason a profile of a Greek-writing commentator stands, without apology, in an Egyptology wiki: he is the last ancient voice in whom the Egyptian workshop, the Greek commentary, and the Hermetic vision are still one utterance — and the first voice in the long modern alchemical conversation that still argues, on his authority and sometimes in his words, about whether they ever should have been separated. Readers who take up the potable gold tradition or the magical papyri from this page will find his century on every side of them; his is the name that turns their recipes into a philosophy.

// THE ZOSIMIAN PATTERN, AS THE ARCHIVE STATES IT //

Prepare the body — nothing receives a tincture unprepared, in metal or in soul. Draw out the spirit — what is fixed and hidden must be made volatile and visible before it can be governed. Fix the spirit — insight that is not re-embodied evaporates; the work is the bonding, not the glimpse. Read the ancients before you read the furnace — the commentary precedes the operation, and protects it. Keep the vessel closed — reflux, not leakage: what the work generates is returned to the work until the colour changes. Let the colour testify — blackening, whitening, yellowing are read, not wished; the matter reports its own state to the operator who has learned the sequence. This is the archive's interpretive summary of the Zosimian material, offered in that spirit: as a reading to be tested against the texts of Section XX, not as a doctrine to be believed.

Michèle Mertens, "Alchemy, Hermetism and Gnosticism at Panopolis c. 300 A.D.: The Evidence of Zosimus," in Perspectives on Panopolis (Brill, 2002), with her 1995 edition's introduction — for the biography, the Suda discrepancy, and the four groups. Lawrence M. The Visions of Zosimos of Panopolis: Text, Translation, and Commentary (University of Oxford doctoral thesis, 2024) — for the visions' narrative, their kerotakis and melanōsis context, and the editorial history after Berthelot. J. R. Partington, "The Kerotakis Apparatus," Nature 159 (1947). B. C. Hallum, review of the Book of Pictures facsimile, Ambix 56 (2009) — for the compilation and the Turba connection. The journal literature on Zosimos's doctrine of conversion and pneuma (Religions, 2021), drawing on Peter Fraser — for bodies, spirits, and tinctures. Pamela Bol on the Stockholm gem recipes and Daniela Schoelzke (European Textile Forum, 2022) on the dye recipes — for Section X's replications.
