I. Words for Metal — the Vocabulary of the Bench

Egyptian metal vocabulary is workshop vocabulary: its oldest words name materials by how they present to the hand and the eye, not by chemical identity, and several of them cover ranges that modern analysis splits apart. The reader who keeps that in mind will not be misled when a single Egyptian word must be translated "copper" in one text and "bronze" in another. The metal was one material to its makers, graded by hardness and colour; the alloy boundary is ours.

The base metal of the whole system is ḥmt [Gardiner: V28-G17-X1-U24] [𓎛𓅓𓏏𓍍] — copper, and by extension copper alloy, bronze: the word under which tools, weapons, vessels and statues are accounted for across three thousand years. Its determinative is itself a piece of equipment: the sign U24 depicts the distinctive spouted crucible of the Old and Middle Kingdom foundry, so that the writing system files the metal under the vessel it was melted in (Section VI). A later, more specific word for bronze, ḥsmn [Gardiner: V28-S29-G17-N35] [𓎛𓋴𓅓𓈖], comes into use alongside it; the analytical literature notes that both arsenical copper and tin bronze could be subsumed under the one term in the Middle Bronze Age, because to the eye and the file the two alloys, at working compositions, are near relations — hard, golden-tinted, castable copper (paraphrased from the discussion of the terms in Ogden, "Metals," in Nicholson and Shaw, Ancient Egyptian Materials and Technology, and in the more recent literature on Egyptian bronze terminology).

Gold is nbw [Gardiner: N35-D58-G43-S12] [𓈖𓃀𓅱𓋞], written with the gold-collar sign as determinative — the finished jewel standing for the metal. Silver is ḥḏ [Gardiner: V28-D46] [𓎛𓂧], "the white (metal)," a word whose history is itself evidence: as Section XV sets out, silver was at first named as a kind of nbw [Gardiner: N35-D58-G43-S12] [𓈖𓃀𓅱𓋞] — "white gold" — before the short form stood alone. Iron is biꜣ [Gardiner: N34] [𓈔], a word that also carries the sense of the hard, dark mineral world more generally, and whose most famous compound, written here in transliteration only, is biꜣ n pt, "iron of heaven" — the name that already knows where the early metal came from (Section XVII). Lead, the quiet metal of weights, net sinkers and alloy additions, is dḥt [Gardiner: D46-V28-X1] [𓂧𓎛𓏏]. Electrum, the gold-silver alloy of Section XVI, is ḏꜥm [Gardiner: D46-D36-G17] [𓂧𓂝𓅓] from the Fifth Dynasty onward, with the older form ꜥsm [Gardiner: D36-S29-G17] [𓂝𓋴𓅓] beside it.

Two units govern every transaction in this article. Metal was weighed, not counted, and the standard weight of the New Kingdom is the dbn [Gardiner: D46-D58-N35] [𓂧𓃀𓈖], the deben, of about 91 grams, divided into ten qdt [Gardiner: N29-D46-X1] [𓈎𓂧𓏏], kite, of about 9 grams. When a tomb scene shows a scribe recording a pile of rings on a balance (Section XXIII), this is the arithmetic being performed: metal as weighed value, in rings, ingots and scrap, against stone weights on the other pan.

Where a spelling cannot be composed with confidence under that discipline, the article prints transliteration alone and says so in place. The full sign list is given in the hieroglyph reference.

A last preliminary, parallel to the one the archive's glass article makes for its own craft. The alchemy article of this archive rests the Egyptian root of the art on what it calls metallurgical metaphysics: gold as the flesh of the gods, silver as their bones, iron as the metal of heaven. That framing is real, and it is documented there. But metaphysics did not smelt a single ingot. Somebody crushed the ore, somebody's cheeks or feet drove the air, somebody read the slag and decided the charge had run clean. This article is that somebody's record: the procedural bench book of Egyptian metal, written to the archive's standing rule of maximum procedural detail for every repeatable physical process. Where the metaphysics and the bench meet — and they meet constantly, in temple workshops and tomb scenes — the article says so and moves on. The fire comes first here, because in the workshop it did.

II. The Bench Record and Its Limits — How This Article Knows

No pharaonic recipe text for smelting survives. There is no Egyptian equivalent of a foundry manual: no papyrus tells the apprentice how much charcoal, how long a blast, how hot a crucible. The famous recipe papyri of the archive — the Leyden Papyrus X and the Stockholm Papyrus, treated in Alchemy — are Greco-Roman compilations of about 300 CE, and even they address the colouring, testing and imitation of finished metals far more than the extraction of metal from ore. Everything procedural in this article is therefore reconstructed, and the reconstruction rests on four kinds of evidence, which the article keeps labelled as it goes.

1. PRODUCTION DEBRIS

Crucibles and their slagged linings, furnace remains, tuyère nozzles, mould fragments, slag heaps, ore and part-smelted charges, excavated at Buhen, Timna, Qantir, Amarna and elsewhere. Debris is the strongest evidence: it is the process, interrupted and fossilised.

2. THE OBJECTS, READ BACKWARD

Chemical analysis and metallography of finished objects — grain structure, inclusions, composition — from which ore type, smelting practice, alloying intent and working sequence can be inferred. Thousands of such analyses underlie the composition figures quoted in this article, above all the corpora summarised by Lucas and Harris and by Ogden.

3. THE SCENES

Tomb and temple paintings of metalworkers, from Old Kingdom Saqqara to the great New Kingdom workshop registers of Rekhmire (TT100). The scenes are process documentation of a specific, limited kind: they show gestures, tools and sequence reliably, and proportions, temperatures and timings not at all. Section XXIII reads the greatest of them step by step.

4. REPLICATION

Modern experimental smelting — malachite reduced in clay crucibles under banks of blowpipes, furnaces rebuilt and fired — which establishes what the ancient installations could do, at what temperatures, with what yields. Replication proves possibility; debris proves practice. The article does not let the first overwrite the second.

Two standing cautions govern everything that follows. The first is chronological honesty: Egyptian metalworking spans three millennia, and a technique attested in the New Kingdom must not be silently projected back into the Old, nor an Old Kingdom crucible scene read as a Ramesside foundry. Dates are given for techniques, not just for objects. The second is the distinction, observed throughout the archive's First Metals article for the deeper past, between native-metal use, smelting and alloying — three different thresholds, crossed at different times for different metals. Gold begins at the first threshold and never needs the second; copper crosses all three; iron, in Egypt, spends two thousand years at the first threshold before the second is crossed at all. Much confusion in popular accounts dissolves once the thresholds are kept apart.

The deeper-time background — native copper in Anatolia, the first secure smelting at Belovode about 5000 BCE, the Balkan mines, Varna gold — is the subject of First Metals in the Antiquity hub, and the Predynastic threshold of the story, where hammered native copper and the first smelted objects appear in the Nile Valley, is sketched in this archive's Predynastic article (its Section XXV). This article begins where those end: with a going concern, a state that mines by expedition, smelts by the crucible-load, and accounts for every deben.

III. Copper Ores — Malachite and the Green Stones That Smelt

The copper ores of the Egyptian world are, in the zones the early smelters could reach, the bright secondary minerals of the weathered upper parts of copper deposits. Chief among them is malachite, the green copper carbonate — the same mineral Egyptians had ground for eye paint and pigment since Predynastic times, so that the smelter's ore was already a familiar, valued substance before anyone smelted it. Beside malachite stand azurite, its blue carbonate companion; cuprite, the red oxide; chrysocolla and atacamite, the blue-green silicate and chloride of arid-zone deposits such as Sinai's; and, deeper and later, the sulphide ores — chalcopyrite and its kin — which demand roasting before they will smelt and which belong, in Egypt, largely to the later history of the craft (Ogden, "Metals," summarising Lucas and Harris, Ancient Egyptian Materials and Industries).

The choice among these ores was not cosmetic; it decided the whole shape of the smelt. The rich oxide and carbonate ores of the weathered zone are self-fluxing in miniature: heated with charcoal in a reducing atmosphere, malachite gives up its water and carbon dioxide, passes through the black oxide, and is stripped of its oxygen by carbon monoxide to leave metallic copper — a reaction a skilled crew could run in a crucible, at temperatures a bank of blowpipes could reach, leaving little or no slag if the ore was clean (Section V). The sulphide ores, by contrast, carry sulphur that must first be burned off in an oxidising roast, and iron that must be fluxed away into a true slag; they repay their trouble with abundance, but they belong to a more capital-intensive metallurgy. Egypt's long preference for the bright ores of Sinai and the Eastern Desert is thus a technological preference as much as a geological accident.

Beneficiation — the Work Before the Fire

Ore as dug is mostly not ore. The first procedural stage at any mine or treatment centre was beneficiation: hand-sorting the dug material to reject barren rock, then crushing the retained lumps — with stone mauls and grinding stones of the kind recovered at the mining sites — to a coarse, even grade. Ogden's summary stresses that even for the simplest ores some such pre-treatment was important to maximise yield, and the point is worth dwelling on, because it is where much of the labour of ancient metallurgy actually sat. Crushing does three things at once: it liberates the copper mineral from the gangue rock around it, it multiplies the surface area the furnace gases can attack, and it lets the sorter make a final visual pass, picking out the greenest fragments. A charge of well-sorted, evenly crushed malachite will smelt in a crucible with up to ninety per cent recovery under experimental conditions (Section V); a charge of unsorted mine-run rock will not smelt economically at all. The green dust on a workshop floor, unglamorous as it is, is the signature of the craft's first skilled act.

Malachite's double life — pigment and ore — also means that not every ancient Egyptian occurrence of the mineral is metallurgical. The archive's rule, applied throughout this article, is that malachite in a grave or a cosmetic palette is pigment until production debris says otherwise, while malachite in a crucible, with copper prills in a slagged lining, is a smelt. The two uses drank from the same mines: the expeditions of Section IV went out for turquoise — mfkꜣt [Gardiner: G17-I9-V31-G1-X1] [𓅓𓆑𓎡𓄿𓏏] — and for the green copper minerals together, under the patronage of the same goddess, Hathor, Lady of Turquoise, whose shrines stand at both great mining fields.

IV. Where the Copper Came From — Sinai, the Eastern Desert, Timna, Cyprus

Egypt has no copper in the Nile Valley itself. Every gram of the metal arrived by expedition, caravan or ship from the desert margins, and the geography of supply is therefore a geography of state reach. Ogden's survey names the main producing regions accessible to the Egyptians as the Eastern Desert and Sinai, including the Serabit el-Khadim region in the southwest, and Timna in the Wadi Arabah — to which the New Kingdom adds a fourth source, imported Cypriot copper arriving by sea.

Sinai — Wadi Maghara and Serabit el-Khadim

The southwestern Sinai fields are the best documented of the early sources because the expeditions carved their records on the rock. At Wadi Maghara, royal reliefs and inscriptions from the Third Dynasty onward — Djoser, Sekhemkhet and Sanakht among the earliest names, then Sneferu, Khufu and their successors — commemorate mining expeditions sent for turquoise and copper, and the ground matches the texts: copper slags, ores, crucibles, ingot moulds and other working debris of Old and Middle Kingdom date have been found there. Ogden, following Petrie and Lucas, raises the possibility that Maghara functioned in part as a treatment centre for ores won in the surrounding region rather than as a great mine in its own right — a distinction that matters, because it is the pattern of the whole industry: ore was commonly treated at or near the mines, since it is easier to transport and to tally ingots than bulky raw rock, fuel permitting. Some twenty kilometres northeast, Serabit el-Khadim pairs its turquoise mines with the temple of Hathor and with reported tuyères, moulds, crucibles and casting installations suggesting local ore treatment on site — though the inscriptions there speak of turquoise, and the relative weight of copper in the Serabit operation remains, on Ogden's reading, genuinely difficult to determine.

The Sinai working rhythm was expeditionary and seasonal. A royal expedition — officials, soldiers, quarrymen, donkey trains — crossed to the fields, worked a campaign, and returned with the product; the inscriptions are its paperwork. Exploitation lapsed in the First Intermediate Period, restarted in the Eleventh Dynasty and became extensive in the Twelfth, with a growing documentary record. At Bir Nasib in Sinai, slag investigation has shown unfluxed copper production in Predynastic times and the use of iron-ore fluxes by some point in the Old Kingdom — the earliest dated marker, in this article's evidence, for the transition from the clean crucible smelt of Section V to the fluxed smelting of Section VII (el-Gayar and Rothenberg's slag study, as summarised by Ogden).

The Eastern Desert

Copper ores occur along almost the entire length of the Eastern Desert, from the latitude of the Faiyum south into Nubia, and the ancient workings are correspondingly scattered and, in many cases, still poorly dated. Ogden's survey picks out the cluster of deposits inland from Safaga and between Safaga and Quseir, with lead, zinc and nickel associations; ancient mines of uncertain date at el-Atawi and Wadi Sitra, due east of Luxor, plausibly supplying the temple workshops of Thebes; the Hammash area northeast of Aswan, with chalcopyrite ores and associated gold, worked from the Middle Kingdom if not earlier; and the Samiuki district near Ras Banas, whose mines Lucas — following Hume — called the most important copper deposits yet discovered in Egypt, with extensive underground workings, ore crushers and slag at Abu Seyal, "worked extensively" in ancient times, and abundant slag at Quban suggesting treatment there. A Twelfth Dynasty stele records an official, Hor, instructed to collect "copper from the land of Nubia" (paraphrased from Lucas's citation of the stele, in Ogden's survey). The Eastern Desert pattern is the Sinai pattern at larger scale and with thinner paperwork: many small workings, state expeditions at the documented end, and a long tail of exploitation whose chronology analysis has yet to fix.

Timna — the Arabah Furnaces

The Timna valley, in the southwestern Arabah some thirty kilometres north of the Gulf of Aqaba, holds the best-excavated copper industry in Egypt's wider world. Mining and local smelting there run from the fourth millennium BCE onward, through a long development that Beno Rothenberg's excavations first mapped: mines worked as cylindrical shafts linked to underground galleries, smelting camps with furnaces, workshops and slag heaps, and — for the Egyptian phase — the small shrine of Hathor at Site 200, its Egyptian layers carrying cartouches from Seti I or Ramesses II through Ramesses V, roughly 1280–1145 BCE. Copper production at Timna appears to have reached its peak under the Nineteenth and Twentieth Dynasties, in what Ian Shaw's assessment, quoted by Ogden, calls the largest-scale Egyptian copper-mining enterprise so far discovered; the workings have been associated with the copper mines of "Atika" named in Papyrus Harris. The smelted product left as plano-convex — bun-shaped — ingots, which Tylecote calculated at between about three and four kilograms each.

Timna also carries this article's clearest lesson in evidentiary humility. The older literature assigned the whole industry to the Egyptian New Kingdom and, before Rothenberg, to Solomon. Work since 2012 under Erez Ben-Yosef, using radiocarbon dating and archaeomagnetism, has redated the peak of production at the great smelting camps to the early Iron Age, after the Egyptian withdrawal — the industry of local desert peoples, intensifying rather than collapsing when the empire left. Uzi Avner's reconsideration argues likewise that the advanced mining shafts were local work, not Egyptian. The archive therefore states the Timna position in its current, layered form: an Egyptian imperial phase is real — the Hathor shrine, the cartouches, the rock inscription of Ramesses III are not illusions — and it sits inside a much longer, substantially local industry that precedes and outlasts it. The furnaces of Section VI are described from Timna evidence because Timna preserves them best, not because every Timna furnace was an Egyptian furnace.

Cyprus — Copper by Sea

From the New Kingdom, literary references to copper imported from the north appear, and some of that copper undoubtedly originated in Cyprus. The evidence is visual as well as textual: oxhide ingots — the characteristic four-handled slab form of Cypriot copper export — are depicted among the goods arriving in the tomb of Rekhmire (Section XXIII), and the Uluburun shipwreck, lost off the Turkish coast in the fourteenth century BCE with a cargo of copper ingots assumed to be Cypriot metal bound ultimately for destinations including Egypt, shows the trade in cross-section. Cyprus does not replace the desert sources; it supplements them, at the top of the market, in the period of Egypt's greatest bronze demand.

V. Smelting Copper, Step by Step — the Crucible Protocol

The earliest Egyptian smelting, and for centuries the standard one, was crucible smelting: ore reduced inside a clay vessel, over or in a charcoal fire, under a forced draught of blowpipes. The protocol below is the archive's reconstruction of that process for a clean oxide ore — malachite of the kind smelted at Buhen — assembled from the excavated debris, from Lucas and Harris's synthesis, from Ogden's summary of the experimental literature, and from the replication experiments of Paul Craddock and others, which have run exactly this smelt in modern times. Where a step is experimental inference rather than excavated fact, the text says so.

// CRUCIBLE SMELTING OF MALACHITE — THE PROTOCOL //

1. Sort and crush. Hand-pick the ore, rejecting barren rock; crush the retained malachite to a coarse, even grade (Section III). Weigh or measure the charge by basket or bowl — the crucible, not the scales, is the unit of the early smelt.

2. Prepare the crucible. Take a coarse, heavily tempered clay crucible — the fabric must survive thermal shock and a slagging charge. In the developed Old and Middle Kingdom form this is the spouted crucible of Section VI, whose profile the hieroglyph for ḥmt [Gardiner: V28-G17-X1-U24] [𓎛𓅓𓏏𓍍] preserves.

3. Charge. Fill the crucible with alternating or mixed layers of crushed ore and charcoal. The charcoal is both fuel and reagent: burning in restricted air it generates the carbon monoxide that strips oxygen from the ore.

4. Set the draught. Arrange the crucible in or over the charcoal hearth and set the blowpipes — reeds tipped with clay nozzles to keep the reed from burning back — converging on the charge. Experiments show a bank of three or more blowpipes can run the smelt; the crew blows in rotation to keep the blast continuous.

5. Raise and hold the heat. Blow the charge up through the decomposition of the carbonate — water and carbon dioxide driven off, green malachite blackening to copper oxide — and into the reducing zone, where carbon monoxide converts the oxide to metal. The working range is set by the chemistry: reduction proceeds well below copper's melting point of 1083 °C, but the metal must then be melted and gathered, so the crucible's hot zone is driven to and held around or above that point.

6. Judge the smelt. The smelter reads the fire by colour, the behaviour of the charge's surface, and experience. There is no instrument. This judgment — when the metal has reduced, coalesced and settled — is the craft's central skill, and it is invisible in the debris except by its results.

7. Recover the metal. With clean malachite, little or no slag forms: the copper gathers as prills and small masses among the charcoal and crucible debris, and is picked out after cooling, or the charge is tipped and the metal separated by crushing the cooled mass. Experimental smelts of simple oxide ores on this pattern have achieved up to ninety per cent recovery of the copper present (Craddock's experiments, as summarised by Ogden).

8. Consolidate. Remelt the recovered prills and fragments together in a fresh crucible to a single pourable mass — the point at which smelting hands over to the melting and casting operations of Sections XIX–XX.

The evidentiary anchor for the protocol is Buhen, the Egyptian colonial town below the Second Cataract in Nubia, where excavation in 1962 revealed an Old Kingdom copper-working establishment inside a massive, crude stone enclosure wall — a "copper factory," in the excavator Emery's phrase, of a settlement whose jar seals and inscriptions name kings from Khafre and Menkaure of the Fourth Dynasty to Userkaf, Sahure and Niuserre of the Fifth. The smelting evidence spread through the site: cylindrical furnaces, copper ore and slag, pottery moulds and crucibles. Analysis of the Buhen material by el-Gayar and Jones found the ore to be primarily malachite of low iron content — with a reported, and subsequently questioned, high gold content, on which see Section VII — and the combination of abundant crucible fragments, malachite, atacamite and copper prills, with little slag, has been read as the signature of exactly the crucible smelting described above (Craddock's reading, in Ogden's summary). Buhen's position is the industry's logic in one site: a fortified colonial outpost, supplied from the north, smelting Nubian ore at the frontier and sending consolidated metal down the river.

How early does the protocol run? If arsenic and nickel impurities in finished objects are accepted as markers of smelted rather than native copper — the standard reading — then smelted copper, fairly impure, was being produced in Egypt by about 4000 BCE, and the low iron contents of the earliest Predynastic and First Dynasty objects (averaging about 0.03 per cent in Ogden's summary of the analyses) are consistent with primitive crucible smelting of clean ores, before fluxing raised the iron signature (Section VII). The crucible itself is attested remarkably early in developed form: an example of the spouted foundry type was found in a Second Dynasty context, about 2800 BCE, at Elkab in Upper Egypt (Claes and colleagues, 2019). The smelt, in other words, was already a mature, standardised operation when the pyramid age opened.

VI. Furnaces, Crucibles and Air — Blowpipe, Pot Bellows, Foot Bellows

Metallurgy is applied air. Charcoal burning in still air will not melt copper in useful quantity; the whole history of Egyptian furnace technology is the history of forcing more air, more controllably, into the fire. The sequence of air-moving devices is one of the few technological progressions the tomb scenes let us date directly, and Broschat's survey for the UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology ("Gold, Electrum, and Silver," 2024) states its outline plainly: depictions of metalworking run from the Old Kingdom onward, and among the innovations they reveal is the introduction of bellows alongside blowpipes, the practice observed from the New Kingdom onward. The fuller sequence, assembled from scenes and surviving equipment, runs as follows.

THE BLOWPIPE — MOUTH POWER

A reed tube, tipped with a clay nozzle (the tuyère) that resists the fire, through which one worker blows directly into the hearth or crucible. Human lungs give a weak, pulsating blast, but a bank of blowers in rotation gives a workable one — enough, as the experiments of Section V show, to smelt malachite in a crucible and to melt metal for casting. The blowpipe never disappears: it remains the precision instrument for small, hot, local work — brazing, soldering, gold melting — into the New Kingdom and beyond, long after bellows take over the heavy draught.

THE POT BELLOWS — HAND AND FOOT

A wide-mouthed pottery vessel whose top is closed by a loose skin diaphragm, with a pipe near its base leading to the furnace. Raise the skin by its cords and air enters; press it down and the air is driven through the pipe. Worked in pairs, pot bellows give a stronger and steadier blast than lungs. Christopher Davey's corpus study of Near Eastern pot bellows (1979, reviewed 2019) remains the standard treatment. Ogden notes little certain evidence for bellows in Egypt until pot bellows appear in the Middle Kingdom — the point at which smelting at scale, and the melting of large castings, becomes straightforward.

THE FOOT / DISH BELLOWS — THE NEW KINGDOM FOUNDRY

In the great New Kingdom scenes the blower stands with a bellows under each foot — bag or dish bellows worked by body weight — treading them alternately while hauling each collapsed skin up again by a cord in the hand, so that one man's full strength drives a near-continuous blast through reed pipes to the hearth. Rekhmire's foundry (Section XXIII) shows several such blowers fanning four charcoal hearths at once: this is the air supply that melts temple doors. Skin bag bellows belong to the same family of devices; the concertina bellows of the first millennium BCE is a later, Near Eastern introduction.

The Crucible as a Designed Object

The characteristic Egyptian foundry crucible of the Old and Middle Kingdoms deserves a paragraph of its own, because it is a designed solution to a specific handling problem and because a modern analysis has recently reopened the question of what it was for. The type — depicted in Old Kingdom tomb scenes such as Mereruka's at Saqqara, where it is employed in the casting of copper, and fossilised as the determinative of ḥmt [Gardiner: V28-G17-X1-U24] [𓎛𓅓𓏏𓍍] — carries a low-sitting opening or spout near its base, bridged above by the continuing crucible wall. Davey's study of the type showed that such a vessel cannot hold liquid standing upright; it works reclined, and casting is achieved by a rocking or tilting motion that brings the spout forward and down. Later First Intermediate Period and Middle Kingdom examples add a bowl-shaped base that lets the vessel stand and retain a melt upright — the form adapting as foundry practice changed.

In 2021 Davey, Rademakers and colleagues published the microanalysis of a well-preserved crucible fragment of this Middle Kingdom type from the fortress at Buhen, now in the Petrie Museum (Journal of Archaeological Science: Reports 36). The fragment held numerous prills of a very arsenic- and nickel-rich copper alloy in what looks like a smelting slag, and the strongly reducing conditions preserved near the low spout led the authors to judge that this crucible was most likely used for smelting a very rich secondary copper-arsenic ore — not, as the type's depiction in casting scenes might suggest, for the more oxidising work of refining raw copper, nor for simple casting of a ready alloy. The authors state the ambiguity themselves and argue the alternatives in the paper; the archive follows them in treating the Buhen crucible as evidence that the same vessel family served at more than one station of the process, and that a crucible's shape alone does not disclose its last firing. It is a model of how this article treats its best evidence: closely, and at its stated strength.

Furnaces Proper — Shafts, Slag Tapping, and the Qantir Foundry

Where production outgrew the crucible, Egypt and its mining world built furnaces. The simplest smelting shaft furnace, in Craddock and Meeks's description quoted by Ogden, was probably the size and shape of a small upturned bucket lacking its bottom — a short clay shaft over a hearth, charged from above, blown through tuyères at its base. Timna preserves the developed form: shaft furnaces in which fluxed charges (Section VII) were smelted down to plano-convex ingots of three to four kilograms, in smelting camps whose slag heaps record campaign after campaign. The further refinement — a furnace from which the molten slag could be tapped, run off while still liquid rather than cooled and broken out — can probably be dated in Sinai to the middle to late New Kingdom, and it matters because tapped slag is cleaner metal: the average iron content of Egyptian copper-alloy objects falls to about 0.14 per cent by the late New Kingdom, a shift for which Ogden lists the tapped-slag furnace, a change to sulphide ores, and manganese fluxing among the candidate explanations, without pretending the record chooses between them.

The foundry at the other end of the process — melting and casting at industrial scale — is best seen at Qantir, ancient Piramesse, the Ramesside capital in the eastern Delta, where Edgar Pusch's excavations uncovered a late Eighteenth- to early Nineteenth-Dynasty metalworking site covering over 30,000 square metres: crucibles, tuyères, moulds, waste, slag and metalworking tools in quantity, including very large-scale copper-alloy casting, with foreign — including Hittite — armour, weaponry and tools among the products, pointing to foreign craftsmen working alongside Egyptian ones. The same city, in another quarter and another material, provides the archive's proof of primary glass production (see Glass, Section VI); Qantir was an industrial capital in the full sense, and its foundry is the excavated counterpart of the painted foundry of Rekhmire, two centuries older, in Section XXIII.

VII. Reading the Slag — and Refining the Raw Copper

Slag is the smelter's waste and the archaeometallurgist's text. It is the non-metallic fraction of the charge — gangue rock, flux, ash, unreduced ore — fused in the fire into a glassy or stony mass, and its composition records, with a fidelity no tomb painting attempts, what was charged, how hot the furnace ran, how strongly reducing its atmosphere was, and how much metal was lost into it. A slag full of copper prills is a failed or an interrupted smelt; a clean, fully fused slag that ran liquid is a mastered one. The distinctions of Sections V–VI — slagless crucible smelting against fluxed furnace smelting — are read off slag and its absence in exactly this way.

Fluxing is the deliberate addition of material to make the waste fraction melt and separate. For copper, the general early flux was iron oxide: smelt oxide copper ores with iron oxide and the iron minerals gather into a slag mass while the copper reduces — the copper emerging either as prills to be picked from the crushed, cooled mass, or, at higher temperatures held longer, as a puddle at the furnace base that cools to a plano-convex bun ingot. The tracer of the transition survives in the metal itself. Early, unfluxed crucible copper carries very little iron — the 0.03 per cent average of the earliest objects in Section V. With the introduction of fluxing, iron levels in Egyptian copper jump to an average of about 0.33 per cent, a rise Ogden dates to the Second Dynasty on the analytical evidence, with the Buhen smelting site's copper averaging about 0.5 per cent iron, and the Sixth Dynasty copper of Pepi I's great statue (Section XXI) at 0.7 per cent iron with 1.1 per cent nickel — iron enough, in Ogden's phrase, to indicate a true fluxing process. Iron in the object is thus not contamination to be apologised for; it is the fingerprint of the furnace regime that made the metal.

Too much fingerprint ruins the metal. Freshly smelted copper can carry several per cent of iron from its flux — levels up to about ten per cent can enter during smelting — and copper so charged is almost impossible to cast or hammer. It must be refined, and the first refining is disarmingly simple: remelt the raw copper and let the iron oxidise at the surface of the melt, skimming off the oxidised impurities as they form, until the iron content falls to around 0.5 per cent, at which point the metal works. That an ingot found in the Wadi Arabah, not far from Timna, left its smelting site in an unrefined state shows the division of labour plainly: refining could be deferred to the receiving workshop, and raw, iron-rich copper travelled as a semi-finished material. Refining in this sense — the oxidative cleaning of a single metal — must be kept distinct from the cupellation and parting of Section XXII, which separate one precious metal from another; the Egyptian evidence for the first is routine and early, for the second late and contested, and this article does not blur them.

One Buhen result belongs here as a standing warning. The ore samples from the Old Kingdom smelting site were reported by el-Gayar and Jones to carry a remarkably high gold content, and the copper derived from them to include gold particles — a result that, if sound, would make Buhen's copper a precious-metal carrier of the first interest. Ogden records the sequel without drama: in view of the ready solubility of gold in copper, the results have been questioned (Craddock and Giumlia-Mair), and a more southern source for the Buhen ore is in any case likely on other grounds. The archive's verdict follows the literature: reported, questioned, unresolved — and a clean example of an analytical claim held at exactly the strength its critics leave it.

VIII. Arsenical Copper — the First Working Alloy

Pure copper is soft, gummy to cast, and blunt to use. The first material that systematically bettered it in Egypt was copper carrying arsenic. Arsenic enters copper from the ore body itself — many copper deposits are naturally arsenical — and its effects are the classic alloy effects: markedly greater hardness in the wrought metal, a vital property for tools and weapons; easier casting, the molten metal flowing more freely; and, at the surface of a casting, an arsenic-rich, silvery skin produced by inverse segregation as the metal cools, which made arsenical copper attractive for objects meant to shine pale. The Buhen crucible of Section VI, with its arsenic- and nickel-rich prills, shows the alloy in production at a Middle Kingdom frontier fortress; the Dynasty 6 mirror disc from Edfu analysed at 7.5 weight per cent arsenic with about one per cent antimony shows it at the mirror-maker's bench.

Was it deliberate? The question is the Egyptian form of the general problem the archive's First Metals article treats for the wider world, and the Egyptian evidence supports the same layered answer. The working convention in the analytical literature is that copper with more than about one per cent arsenic represents the deliberate use of arsenic-rich ores, or the intentional combining of arsenic-bearing and plain ores; below that line, arsenic is read as accident. Ogden's summary adds the chronological argument: the higher arsenic levels that appear during the Old Kingdom were, on Kaczmarczyk and Hedges's assessment, almost certainly not fortuitous — up to seven per cent was found in axes in the British Museum — and the frequent association of high arsenic with low levels of other impurities argues for intentional selection, since a merely dirty ore would raise everything at once. What analysis cannot yet do, Ogden states plainly, is distinguish definitively between the deliberate choice of a naturally arsenical ore and the deliberate addition of an arsenic mineral to a plain charge. The smiths, on the evidence, learned to prefer and to select for the effect; the exact gesture by which they dosed it is beyond recovery.

The working history that follows is one of long dominance and slow displacement. Arsenical copper is the standard hard copper alloy of the Old and Middle Kingdoms; it persists, in noticeable amounts, into the New Kingdom — arsenic appears in copper-based pigments up to the time of Hatshepsut — and then falls away sharply as tin bronze takes over (Section IX), a succession Ogden reads as itself an argument that the earlier arsenic had been intentional: accidents do not get replaced on schedule. After the New Kingdom, arsenic in Egyptian copper alloys is typically under one per cent and presumably mostly fortuitous, with intriguing exceptions — Late Period figures of the child god Harpocrates recur with high arsenic (and high tin, in other examples), raising the possibility, which Ogden leaves open, of a deliberate pale alloy for a child god's pale skin.

// THE COST IN THE WORKSHOP //

Arsenic is a poison, and arsenical smelting and remelting release arsenical fumes. The Egyptian smiths who ran arsenical charges campaign after campaign worked in that exposure without protection, and the occupational cost — chronic arsenic poisoning is cumulative and disabling — is part of this material's history, as it is wherever arsenical copper was made (compare First Metals, Section XII). The archive records the hazard as history; modern replication of arsenical smelting belongs in controlled laboratory conditions, not in open workshop practice.

IX. Tin and the Bronze Problem — an Imported Ingredient

Bronze proper — copper alloyed with tin — outperforms arsenical copper on almost every bench criterion. Ten per cent of tin drops the alloy's melting point from copper's 1083 °C to about 1005 °C, greatly increases the fluidity of the melt and so the fidelity of castings, and hardens the metal beyond arsenic's gift, without arsenic's toxicity to the smith. The effects are, in Ogden's summary, not dissimilar to arsenic's but more dramatic. Egypt's difficulty was never knowledge of the alloy. It was supply: Egypt has no tin. Cassiterite, the tin ore, does not occur in the Egyptian deserts in workable quantity, and every grain of tin in every Egyptian bronze arrived by trade, from sources that remain — here as across the Bronze Age world — an open research problem, the "tin problem" of the First Metals article (its Section XIII). Bronze in Egypt is therefore an index of trade reach as much as of foundry skill.

The chronology, on the analyses, is a long dawn rather than a revolution. Using the same one per cent dividing line as for arsenic, deliberate tin alloying is attested sporadically very early: several Early Dynastic objects exceed one per cent tin, with the highest early levels — seven and nine per cent — in a ewer and basin from the tomb of the Second Dynasty king Khasekhemwy. A Sixth Dynasty vessel analysed at almost six per cent. Yet true bronzes were still the minority in the Middle Kingdom: the superb hollow-cast figure of a man now in the Louvre (E27153) carries about five per cent tin with about one per cent arsenic, a mixed alloy typical of the transitional repertoire, while an old analysis of a Twelfth Dynasty bracelet fragment from the Dahshur treasure reported just over sixteen per cent tin — a figure the archive repeats as Berthelot's 1895 result, from the heroic age of wet chemistry, rather than as a modern measurement. Through the New Kingdom, copper and arsenical copper still share the stage with bronze — Lucas remarked that there was still more copper than bronze in the tomb of Tutankhamun — and only from the Ramesside period onwards is tin present in the majority of copper-alloy objects. The majority of Egyptian bronzes, across all periods, carry up to about ten per cent tin, with higher levels occasional and very rarely above about sixteen per cent: the classical foundry range, arrived at by a state that had to import the decisive ingredient across deserts and seas.

How the tin was added changed with its availability. Ogden's assessment is that early additions were presumably made in the form of tin ore charged with the copper, while the New Kingdom pattern — tin appearing in glazes and pigments from the reign of Thutmose III, unusual tin alloys multiplying, tin becoming far more readily available — agrees with additions of metallic tin, weighed and melted into the copper at the foundry. The foundry gesture itself, at that point, is simple and is what the Rekhmire scenes show (Section XXIII): copper melted in the crucible under charcoal, the tin added to the melt, the whole stirred and skimmed, and poured before the tin can oxidise away. Alloying, at bench level, is the last five minutes of the melt — which is why the scenes of alloying and the scenes of casting are the same scenes.

Lead Joins the Alloy

A third ingredient completes the classical repertoire. Between one and three per cent of lead in a copper alloy eases casting without costing strength, and lead levels as low as one per cent may sometimes be deliberate; but as a major component, lead is late. Levels over about two per cent are rare before the late New Kingdom; the deliberate large-scale addition of lead — up to twenty-five per cent and more in later examples, dropping a copper-tin alloy's melting range below 800 °C and making the mass production of cast statuettes fluid and forgiving — is generally dated from the Nineteenth Dynasty, and high-lead bronze is characteristically a Late Period and Ptolemaic material. Lead was kept out of alloys meant for edges: a leaded bronze casts beautifully and cuts badly. The alloy menu of the mature Egyptian foundry is thus a set of deliberate trade-offs among hardness, fluidity, colour and cost, made object by object — and the analyses, read in bulk, show the foundries making them consistently.

X. Gold Sources — the Three Golds of the Texts

Gold is the metal Egypt had. Where copper and tin index the state's reach outward, gold indexes its grip on its own deserts and its southern frontier — and the Egyptians themselves organised the supply by origin. Ogden's survey, following Vercoutter, records that the texts define three gold regions. The gold of the Eastern Desert, from the Hammamat to Abbad country, was "gold of Koptos," named for the Nile town that controlled the desert roads and much of the Eastern Desert's produce. Further south came "gold of Wawat," won through the Wadi Allaqi and Wadi Gabgaba systems, its caravan routes starting from the fortress of Quban — a settlement that may itself have been founded to protect those routes and to process the region's produce, copper included. From still further south, from what is now Sudan and toward Ethiopia, came "gold of Kush," the gold of empire and tribute.

The scale of the winning is documented archaeologically as well as textually. The combined Egyptian Geological Survey and Munich University surveys of 1989–1993 studied around 130 ancient gold-mining sites in the Eastern Desert, between roughly the twenty-second and twenty-eighth parallels, and found extensive mining activity from Predynastic times onward, with the kinds of deposit exploited changing as recovery technique improved: alluvial gold first and longest, the gold of wadi gravels gathered and washed; vein gold — gold locked in quartz reefs, demanding crushing and grinding before washing — increasingly from the Middle Kingdom. In the Old and Middle Kingdoms the directly evidenced exploitation is of the Koptos gold; the documentary record makes Wawat's gold vital from the Middle Kingdom into the later New Kingdom; the late New Kingdom focus swings back to the Eastern Desert, the swing to which the famous Turin papyrus map of the gold mines — a Twentieth Dynasty document, among the oldest surviving maps in the world — is usually connected. Greaves and Little's verdict of 1929, quoted by Lucas and repeated by Ogden because nothing has displaced it, closes the survey: no workable deposits of gold have been discovered that the ancient Egyptians overlooked.

The gold country had its own sacred geography, and the archive notes it without leaving the bench. The expeditions moved under divine patronage — Hathor in Sinai, the desert gods on the eastern roads — and the metal they brought back entered the economy already named by its origin, graded by the treasury's reckoners, one of whom, a "reckoner of gold" named Thuthotep, left his graffito at the remote Abrak well some 240 kilometres southeast of Aswan about 2000 BCE. Prospecting at that distance was systematic work: Ogden notes that the prospectors, lacking geochemistry, read landforms, rock colours and even the vegetation associated with deposits — practical geology, transmitted as craft knowledge.

XI. Winning the Gold — Panning, Washing Tables, Crushed Quartz

Gold's winning is a separation by weight, and its procedures are the oldest continuously intelligible processes in this article: they can be watched, essentially unchanged, at artisanal gold workings today. Gold is roughly twice as dense as the copper of Section V and many times denser than sand; water, correctly managed, will carry the sand away and leave the gold. The Egyptian evidence — scenes, installations, and the installations' later classical description — supports the following sequence, given here at the archive's full procedural standard.

// GOLD-WASHING — ALLUVIAL AND VEIN //

1. Gather the feed. For alluvial gold: scoop the sands and fine gravels of a wadi bed or old river bed where gold has concentrated. For vein gold: dig the gold-bearing quartz from the reef, and crush and grind it — laboriously, on stone — to a powder from which the gold can be freed.

2. Pan, for small work. Scoop the sand with water into a pan-like vessel and swirl: the sand and water slosh out over the sides while the far heavier gold particles settle and remain in the bottom of the pan.

3. Wash, for production work. Run the sand and water down over a sloping washing table fitted with grooves, ridges or other arrangements to trap the gold grains as the lighter material washes over and away. Ancient gold-washing tables of this kind have been reported and illustrated by Vercoutter (1959).

4. Collect and clean. Gather the trapped concentrate; re-wash as needed; pick out visible grains and nuggets by hand.

5. Melt to ingots. For security and for the tally, melt the won gold into ingots — unless it is present as relatively large nuggets, which travel as they are. New Kingdom paintings show the transport of both nuggets and ring ingots: the ring is at once ingot, unit of account, and the form in which the treasury's balances receive it (Section XXIII).

The scenes anchor the protocol in time. The earliest representation of gold recovery is in the tomb of Baqt III at Beni Hassan (BH15), of about 1900 BCE, showing gold ore being sorted or washed, perhaps ground, with a vertical object that may be a gravity washing table depicted in plan view (Chappaz's reading, in Ogden's summary). The Ramesside tomb at Saqqara of Khacy — whose title was "goldwasher of the treasury of Pharaoh" — shows the fuller sequence: grinding, a sloping washing table, and melting, the three stations of the protocol in one register. And the Twelfth Dynasty stele of the official Simunt (British Museum EA828) supplies the labour behind the images: Simunt records that he went south to bring back gold for Amenemhat II, and that he set men, women and children to dig out the quartz, crush it and wash it. The archive quotes no words of the stele, which it has not read in the original for this article; the content is paraphrased from Ogden's summary, and the division of labour it records — whole communities set to the crushing and washing — is the human cost line of every gold object in this archive. The Middle Kingdom stele of Sa Hathor, of the same reign, records a southern expedition to supervise gold-mining and gold-washing in one breath: winning and washing were a single operation in the administration's mind.

A classical coda, labelled as such. The fullest surviving description of Egyptian gold mining — the division of labour by age and strength, the crushing, the washing tables — comes from Agatharchides, as preserved by Diodorus Siculus, describing the mines of the Ptolemaic period, worked by prisoners of war, slaves and convicts. Ogden's warning stands and this article repeats it: the Dynastic-period descriptions are far less explicit, and Agatharchides' harrowing picture must not be read backward onto the pharaonic workforce without the label. What the pharaonic record itself shows — Simunt's stele above all — is coerced and communal labour at the crushing stones; how far its regime resembled the Ptolemaic one, the evidence does not say.

XII. Gold as Found — Composition, Colour, and the Unrefined Norm

Native gold is never pure gold. The predominant accompanying element is silver, homogeneously alloyed in the natural metal in anything from mere traces up to fifty per cent or more; copper in as-mined gold is typically under about two per cent, with detectable traces of iron, tin and platinum-group metals besides — the silvery-grey platinum inclusions being extremely common in Egyptian gold objects. The consequence governs all Egyptian goldwork: the metal arrived at the bench already an alloy, of variable and unmeasured composition, ranging in colour from rich yellow through pale electrum whites depending on its silver content, and the goldsmith's first skill was reading it — by colour, by feel, by behaviour under the hammer — since no assay in the modern sense existed to read it for him. Broschat's survey makes the complementary point from the objects: the variety of natural alloys in Egypt shows goldsmiths skilled in distinguishing them by sensory and aesthetic characteristics and by physical properties such as melting point and hardness, and in melting and mixing to create custom alloys tailored to a purpose, or to achieve a metal of a different colour.

On top of nature, the bench added its own alloying. Analysis of gold objects from Predynastic times onward shows copper present at levels far above natural impurity, so that deliberate copper addition is certain — a feature shared with contemporary Sumerian goldwork — presumably, as Ogden argues, for the copper's beneficial effect on the colour of gold-silver alloys, warming a pale electrum toward gold; the deliberate addition of considerable copper to produce a red gold is seemingly an Amarna-period phenomenon. Copper in Egyptian gold can range up to about ten per cent, the upper levels more typical after the middle New Kingdom. The modern classificatory line — metal over seventy-five per cent gold is "gold," below it "electrum," with "aurian silver" proposed for alloys of five to fifty per cent gold — falls, as Ogden notes, most inconveniently at about the median composition of much Egyptian goldwork, and reflects the ancient classification poorly; Section XVI treats electrum on its own terms.

The decisive fact for Section XXII is stated here, where it belongs: because as-mined gold could not be accurately assessed, standardised fineness was impossible until gold could be refined to a known pure state and then alloyed down by weighed additions. On the analytical evidence Ogden surveys — his own largely unpublished analyses included — Egyptian gold objects before the Late Period were generally unrefined: gold as mined, or as recycled, with copper sometimes added. That is the norm against which every claim for early refining must be measured, and Section XXII measures them.

XIII. The Goldsmith's Bench — Sheet, Foil, Wire, Raising and Chasing

Goldsmithing is cold work punctuated by heat. Gold and its alloys are forged, in Broschat's summary of the technique, by "kneading" the metal between hammer and anvil — the wall paintings show fist-sized, unshafted stone hammers for the work — and nearly everything the Egyptian goldsmith made begins as an ingot hammered into sheet. The production of many objects, and the plating of many more, required first the casting of numerous ingots, which were then hammered into sheets and foils of varying thickness: sheet strong enough to support its own weight for vessels and jewellery; foil, in the measured range of surviving examples, between about one and fifty micrometres thick, for gilding; with gold beaters of Tutankhamun's time able to produce foils and sheets at least 57 by 52 centimetres in extent. Forging a flat sheet of uniform thickness is, as Broschat insists, a challenging task demanding great skill despite its monotonous appearance — every hammer blow moves the metal, and the sheet remembers every unevenness.

Raising, Sinking, Chasing, Repoussé

A vessel begins as a flat disc of sheet and becomes a bowl by raising — hammering the disc in overlapping courses over a stake, gathering the metal upward into walls — or by sinking it into a hollow. Decoration is then worked by chasing: blunt chisels and punches driven across the surface to displace and compact the metal — never cutting it away — building relief line by line, the punch walked along an intended contour and struck repeatedly, or driven once to stamp a motif. Repoussé works the relief from the back, the sheet supported on a yielding mass of pitch or putty that hardens when cool and softens when warmed, so the piece can be held, worked, released and re-set; fine detail is then lowered and sharpened again from the front. Broschat's examples fix the techniques in objects: the silver bowl attributed to the general Djehuti, of the reign of Thutmose III, and the animal-fight scene in repoussé on the scabbard of Tutankhamun's golden dagger. True engraving — cutting metal away with a sharp tool — is the exception in pharaonic work, rare before the first century BCE, when effective iron tools made it practical; the pharaonic surface is a displaced surface, not a cut one, and the distinction is diagnostic when objects are examined.

Annealing — the Bench's Breathing

Every shaping process work-hardens the metal: hammered gold, silver and copper alloys stiffen as their crystal structure deforms, until the piece will crack rather than move. The remedy is annealing — heating the workpiece to several hundred degrees, at which the deformed crystals recrystallise, the internal stress dissolves, and the metal becomes pliable again while keeping the shape already achieved — then continuing the hammering. A raised vessel is annealed many times between its disc and its finished form. The operation is so fundamental that the tomb scenes caption it: in the Fifth Dynasty tomb of Wepemnofret at Giza, among the earliest metalworking scenes of all, Ogden identifies probably the earliest reference to annealing, in a hieroglyphic caption he renders, with its uncertainty marked, as "There is no cracking (?) if it is heated excellently" (Ogden, "Metals," quoting the scene's caption in translation). The caption is the bench speaking: heat, rightly judged, is what stands between the work and the crack.

Wire

Wire is sheet's linear form, and Egyptian wire was made, not drawn. Broschat's survey, following the standard literature, distinguishes wires hammered from square rods toward a rounder section from wires made by rolling or twisting thin strips of sheet or rod — the latter recognisable under magnification by the fine diagonal line that spirals along them, visible, for example, in the wire of Tutankhamun's golden dagger. Rare examples show signs of strip-drawing, pulling a strip through a perforated drawplate, but the technique is exceptional in pharaonic work and must not be generalised. Wire became rings and loops joining an object's parts, chains, and the filigree — wire laid in scrolling patterns and fixed to a ground — that together with granulation (Section XIV) forms the summit of the Middle and New Kingdom jeweller's art.

XIV. Joining and Surface — Solder, Granulation, Inlay and Gilding

A complex jewel is an assembly, and assembly is a thermal problem: the joining material must melt below the melting point of the parts it joins, and a piece with several joints must be built in descending order of melting temperature, so that each new soldering does not undo the last. Egyptian goldsmiths commanded two distinct joining technologies, and the analytical literature distinguishes them by their traces.

Hard solder, documented in Egypt since at least the Middle Kingdom, is a true filler alloy — usually a gold-copper alloy — applied as pallions, small pellet-like pieces, at the joint. Heated, the solder melts, wets both faces and freezes into a material bond that fills the seam. Colloidal soldering — also called copper-salt or diffusion soldering — adds no filler at all: a mixture of a copper-bearing mineral or copper salt with an organic binder or glue is painted at the joint and used first as an adhesive to hold the parts in place; in the charcoal fire the copper compound is reduced to metallic copper, which alloys with and diffuses into the adjoining gold surfaces, bonding them with nothing added and at most a slight reddish blush at the seam. Colloidal soldering was the technique for the joints under least stress and of greatest delicacy — filigree wires and granules — because it holds the assembly together before firing and leaves no blob of solder to flood the detail.

Granulation, Step by Step

Granulation — decoration built from tiny spheres of gold fused to a surface — is attested in Egypt by the Middle Kingdom, on the cylinder amulets and the great Dahshur and Lahun jewels, and its protocol, as Broschat's survey reconstructs it from the objects, runs:

// GRANULATION — THE PROTOCOL //

1. Make the granules. Melt small pieces of sheet metal or wire on charcoal; surface tension draws each molten fragment into a sphere. The charcoal bed is the mould — a granule is a droplet that froze round.

2. Sort. Grade the cooled granules by size, a process that may have involved sieving through sieves of various apertures. Uniformity of granule is the visible signature of quality in the finished work.

3. Lay the pattern. Arrange the granules on the object's surface in the design — geometric patterns of triangles, diamonds and zigzags, small hieroglyphs, figurative and floral motifs — held in place by the organic binder of the colloidal solder mixture, painted at the contact points.

4. Fire. Heat the whole piece in the charcoal fire. The binder chars away; the copper compound reduces; copper diffuses into granule and ground alike, and each sphere bonds at its point of contact without melting — the granule keeps its roundness because the bond forms below the gold's melting point.

5. Judge. The failure modes are written on surviving pieces: granules melted by excessive heat, granules lost where the bond failed. Broschat's figures of the Khnumit necklace's star pendants show both — open-work granulation of the highest ambition, with some granules slumped in the firing and others gone.

Inlay, Cloisons, and Gilding

Egyptian polychromy set colour into metal. Inlays of coloured stone, faience and — from the New Kingdom — glass were seated in recesses cut or built into the object, or in cloisons: cells made by soldering thin strips of metal on edge onto a base plate, each cell then receiving its stone or paste, as on the pectoral of Sithathoryunet, whose soldered gold strips still draw the design. The technique is conventionally called cloisonné, with Broschat's caveat that the recess-set form is closer to champlevé and that both terms properly belong to enamel, a hot-glass technique not common in pre-Ptolemaic Egypt; the Egyptian inlay is stone and paste in metal cells, not fused glass. Metals were also inlaid into metals — electrum into silver, precious metals into the artificially blackened copper alloy the texts call ḥmt km, "black copper" (transliteration only; the alloy, a copper-gold alloy patinated to a black surface, is treated in the literature of Giumlia-Mair and Quirke) — the earliest known damascene work including the electrum-inlaid inscription on a silver scarab from the tomb of Wah.

Gilding, finally, is foil put to work: gold sheet and foil fixed to wood, ivory, leather, textile, faience, glass, stone and base metal by glues, by hammering or pressing into scored channels, or over a preparatory layer of gesso — the calcium carbonate or sulphate ground that carries the gold on so many wooden objects, and that is documented even on the copper alloy of Pepi I's statue. The life-size copper statue's gilded fingernails (Section XXI) are the technique at monumental scale. What pharaonic gilding is not is mercury gilding: the amalgam process, in which gold-mercury paste is applied and the mercury driven off by heat, is an import from East Asia of much later date, becoming the Mediterranean standard only in Roman times. No mercury film gilds a pharaonic object, and claims that assume one are an anachronism of the same order as natron glass in a New Kingdom crucible.

XV. Silver — the White Gold That Had to Be Imported

Silver inverts gold's story. Egypt's deserts yield no known workable silver source: Broschat's survey states the position directly — Egypt lacks known mineralogical sources of silver, so much of its silver must have been imported from neighbouring countries — and the documentary record agrees from its beginning. The royal annals of the Fourth Dynasty king Sneferu already mention silver as an imported commodity. The late Sixth Dynasty biographic inscription of the official Iny lists silver imported through the trading hub of Byblos in Lebanon. In the Eighteenth Dynasty Annals of Thutmose III, silver rings appear three times — twice in the tribute from Syria, once from Babylon. A recent study of the silver of Queen Hetepheres' bracelets argues, on isotopic and elemental grounds, for an origin in the Cyclades or the Lavrion mines of Attica: Egyptian silver, from first to last, is other people's geology. The one local hypothesis — silver extracted from Egypt's own argentiferous galena — fails on the ores themselves, whose silver content is too low to support it, and analyses of Egyptian silver objects do not support a galena origin for them either (Gale and Stos-Gale's programme, as summarised by Broschat).

Scarcity priced the metal. The vocabulary records the early valuation: at first the single word nbw [Gardiner: N35-D58-G43-S12] [𓈖𓃀𓅱𓋞] served for gold, silver and electrum alike; in the Fourth Dynasty the phrase nbw ḥḏ, "white gold," was established for silver, abbreviated by the Fifth Dynasty to ḥḏ [Gardiner: V28-D46] [𓎛𓂧] alone, at the same time as ḏꜥm [Gardiner: D46-D36-G17] [𓂧𓂝𓅓] was introduced for the silvery gold alloys. Harris's study of the commodity lists, as summarised by Broschat, shows silver preceding gold in the lists until the Middle Kingdom — the order of honour reflecting the rarer metal's higher standing — while by the New Kingdom the gold-to-silver ratio had settled at about two to one (Černý's figure): a deben of gold worth two deben of silver, the imported white metal now the cheaper of the pair, on its way to becoming the standard material of account in the first millennium BCE. The el-Tod treasure of the Middle Kingdom — a hoard of predominantly silver vessels and ingots, with ten gold ingots, plausibly of Anatolian origin — shows the import trade in physical form: silver arriving as finished plate and as bullion, to be weighed, hoarded and reworked.

Silver was worked exactly as gold was worked — sheet, foil, raising, chasing, casting — with the techniques of Sections XIII–XIV, and its surviving masterworks, from the silver vessels of Thutmose III's foreign wives to the silver coffins of the Third Intermediate Period kings at Tanis, are goldsmiths' work in the white metal. Its scarcity in the record is compounded by its chemistry: silver tarnishes and corrodes where gold endures, so the surviving corpus understates the ancient one by more than loss alone would explain.

XVI. Electrum — Between Gold and Silver

Electrum is gold and silver in one metal — in nature, the commonest form in which "gold" comes out of the Egyptian ground. Egyptian sources of the metal could carry silver up to forty-four weight per cent in the grains analysed from Wadi Hammamat (Osman and colleagues' mine study, cited by Broschat), and the full natural series runs unbroken from silver, through aurian silver and electrum, to gold: there is no line in the geology, only lines in the vocabulary, and the vocabulary drew them late (Section XV). Pliny the Elder's much later definition — electrum as gold with more than a fifth part silver — is a Roman attempt to fence a continuum the Egyptians had lived inside for two thousand years.

The bench treated electrum as a material in its own right, not as spoiled gold. Its pale, luminous colour — between the sun of gold and the moon of silver — was a positive value in the Egyptian system of colour, and electrum was specified for objects that wanted exactly that light: the caps of obelisks, catching first and last sun, in the alchemy article's account; inlaid inscriptions and details where its brightness reads against both parent metals; jewellery in which goldsmiths who could read their alloys by eye (Section XII) deployed the natural range deliberately. Artificial electrum — gold and silver melted together to a chosen paleness — is analytically difficult to distinguish from the natural alloy, a small lead content being the usual, not invariable, indicator of a man-made mixture, since the silver of a man-made alloy was itself smelted metal. The archive's position follows Ogden's: the Egyptians worked the continuum knowingly, named its stations as their administration required, and left the hard boundaries to us.

XVII. Iron from the Sky — Meteoric Iron, Gerzeh to Tutankhamun

Egypt's first iron fell out of the sky, and the Egyptians said so: biꜣ [Gardiner: N34] [𓈔] n pt, "iron of heaven" (the compound is printed in transliteration; its first element carries the bracket). Iron meteorites — the cores of shattered asteroids, delivered as lumps of iron-nickel alloy — need no smelting: they are metal already, harder and more brittle than copper, workable by a smith who already commands hammer and anneal. The analytical signature is the nickel: iron meteorites carry it at levels terrestrial smelted iron almost never shows, together, in unaltered metal, with the Widmanstätten pattern — the intergrown crystal lamellae that form as meteoritic metal cools over geological time, and that no furnace can reproduce.

The Gerzeh Beads — c. 3200 BCE

The earliest known iron artefacts in Egypt are nine small tubular beads from two burials at the Predynastic cemetery of Gerzeh, securely dated to about 3200 BCE (Rehren and colleagues, Journal of Archaeological Science 40, 2013). Their manufacture is a sheet-metal protocol in miniature, established by neutron and X-ray examination of beads so corroded that in some no metal survives at all: the meteoritic iron was hammered — carefully, since the alloy is hard and brittle — into thin sheet, and the sheet was then rolled or bent into tubes. The beads were strung with lapis lazuli, gold and carnelian: meteoritic iron entered the record as a precious material, on a necklace, in the company of the costliest substances its owners knew. A companion study of a bead from Gerzeh tomb 67 (Johnson and colleagues, Meteoritics and Planetary Science 48, 2013) found the surviving metal fragments retaining a chemistry of approximately thirty weight per cent nickel, with thin bands of the nickel-rich mineral taenite preserving a distorted Widmanstätten pattern — distorted by cold-working — in a bead of which only about 2.4 per cent of the volume remains metal at all. Two independent teams, two methods, one conclusion: the first iron in Egypt is meteoritic, and fourth-millennium smiths had already mastered the smithing of a difficult iron-nickel alloy nearly two millennia before iron smelting reached them.

Tutankhamun's Dagger

The famous case is the iron dagger laid against Tutankhamun's right thigh, found in 1925 with its gold hilt, rock crystal pommel and decorated gold sheath — an iron blade, in the fourteenth century BCE, in a tomb where iron was rarer than gold. Its origin was debated for ninety years on conflicting analyses. In 2016 Comelli and colleagues settled the composition by portable X-ray fluorescence, non-destructively, in the Egyptian Museum in Cairo (Meteoritics and Planetary Science 51): the blade is iron with 10.8 weight per cent nickel and 0.58 per cent cobalt — a composition, and a nickel-to-cobalt ratio, that sit squarely with the iron meteorites and against terrestrial smelted iron. In 2022 Matsui and colleagues, mapping the blade's surface chemistry in two dimensions, refined the nickel figure to 11.8 ± 0.5 weight per cent and reported discontinuous nickel-rich bands arranged with a cubic symmetry consistent with a Widmanstätten pattern — the meteorite's crystal architecture still legible in the finished blade, which constrains how the blade was worked, since sustained high heat would have erased it. The archive states the resulting position at its real strength: the dagger's metal is meteoritic beyond reasonable doubt; which meteorite it came from is not established (the 2016 team's comparison found one compatible modern find, the Kharga octahedrite, among twenty candidates — a compatibility, not an identification); and whether the blade was forged in Egypt or arrived as a foreign gift, as other fine iron objects are documented arriving at the Egyptian court in the Amarna Letters, the analyses do not decide.

Between Gerzeh and Tutankhamun the record is a scatter: a handful of small iron objects across two thousand years, some certainly meteoritic, some — like the corroded pieces wholly devoid of nickel that Craddock nevertheless judges almost certainly meteoritic, against Lucas's contrary reading — argued case by case; and the celebrated iron plate from the Great Pyramid's masonry, championed and re-championed since 1837, whose fullest reassessment (Craddock and Lang) concluded that its structure and composition strongly suggest it is of no great antiquity. The archive lists it as it lists the Buhen gold: a famous claim, held at the strength its examiners leave it. Meteoric iron was always a fortuitous supply — a fall, a find, a gift — and no industry can be built on weather. For iron to become a working metal, Egypt needed the second threshold: smelting. Section XVIII explains why it came so late.

XVIII. Smelted Iron's Late Arrival — the Bloomery Problem

The paradox of Egyptian iron is that the ores are abundant and the temperatures were already there. Iron ores — magnetite, haematite, the ores of the Bahariya Oasis and the Aswan sandstones — occur widely in Egypt, but in Dynastic times they were exploited, on Ogden's summary, seemingly seldom if ever for anything beyond pigments and the fluxing of copper smelts. Iron smelting itself requires about 1100–1150 °C — no hotter than a copper furnace already ran. The difficulty is not the fire. It is the product.

A copper smelt ends in liquid metal that gathers, pours and casts. An iron smelt, at any temperature an ancient furnace could hold, ends in a bloom: a spongy, solid mass of iron intergrown with slag, which never melted and cannot be poured. The bloom must be reheated and hammered, repeatedly, to expel the slag and consolidate the metal into wrought iron — labour-intensive forging for a material that, as Ogden states the bench verdict, could be hammered into serviceable tools and weapons but offered little if any advantage in hardness or edge over the copper alloys it would replace; its one noticeable benefit was a tendency to bend rather than break. Only carburisation — holding the iron in charcoal until carbon enters its surface, making a steel that can be quench-hardened and tempered — turns smelted iron into a superior metal, and carburised, quenched and tempered pieces are sporadic in the Near East from the middle of the second millennium BCE, not an Egyptian industry. Against an established, state-supplied bronze system of mines, expeditions, foundries and recycling, wrought iron had no economic case. Egypt's lateness with iron is not ignorance; it is a rational industry declining a worse product until the product improved.

The chronology of the turn, on the evidence, is correspondingly late and at first foreign-flavoured. Fine iron objects, daggers among them, reach the Egyptian court as gifts from foreign rulers in the Amarna Letters; New Kingdom Egyptian examples remain rarities like Tutankhamun's. Petrie identified two Delta sites — Naukratis and Tell Defenna (Daphnae) — as iron-working places of the sixth century BCE, on large quantities of iron slag; Ogden's caution applies and this article repeats it: copper smelting also produces copious iron slag, so the Delta slag is not, alone, definitive proof of iron smelting (the same caution the Gerzeh literature applies). By the middle of the first millennium BCE iron production had increased dramatically around Egypt while Egypt itself lagged — Moorey's observation, quoted by Ogden, is that iron became cheaper than copper in Mesopotamia only around 600 BCE, the era of the Persian conquest, whose armies' iron equipment has been seen as one factor in Egypt's fall. The largest early group of Egyptian iron objects, some two dozen woodworking tools of the seventh century BCE found in the Delta in association with a Western Asiatic bronze helmet — four of them steel — shows the new metal arriving, characteristically, in foreign company. Full iron production on Egyptian soil, at centres such as Meroë to the south, belongs to the post-pharaonic story and lies beyond this article's centre of gravity.

XIX. Casting I — Open Moulds and Piece Moulds

Casting is the foundry's defining act — the Egyptian word for it, wdḥ [Gardiner: G43-D46-V28] [𓅱𓂧𓎛], "pouring," is the same word the glass article meets for the pouring of molten glass — and its simplest instrument is the open mould: a single cavity cut into stone or formed in clay, open to the air, into which metal is poured and from which the cooled casting is lifted. Open moulds make flat-backed, simple things supremely well: ingots first of all — the bun ingots of the smelting sites and the bar and ring forms of the treasury — and blanks, the rough shapes that the hammer would finish. Broschat's survey notes open moulds used primarily for creating blanks that could be further worked into an object; the open mould is the foundry's roughing-out tool, and its products are supposed to be unfinished.

Piece moulds — two or more fitted parts, of stone or clay, enclosing a cavity — cast simple three-dimensional forms with a front and a back: axes, adzes, mirrors' discs, simple figures. The mould parts are keyed to register, bound or weighted together, and poured through a gate; the casting is freed by opening the mould, which survives to cast again, so piece moulds are the technology of repetition for simple shapes. Limestone moulds excavated at Amarna — one carrying a fish motif, beside a finished gold foil fish of similar form — were used for casting jewellery elements or for pressing and chasing gold foil into shape (Broschat's survey, after Kemp); the two uses are cousins, since a mould that shapes hot metal by pouring can shape cold foil by pressure. The pouring itself is a two-man act in the scenes: one worker tips the crucible with tongs while his colleague, in Bernd Scheel's classic description of the Rekhmire imagery in Egyptian Metalworking and Tools, holds back the dross and contamination at the crucible's lip so that clean metal alone enters the mould. Slag in a casting is a ruined casting; the second man's stick is quality control.

Between the open mould and the lost-wax process of Section XX stands the question of scale. Piece moulds and open moulds serve the small and the repeatable. The great castings of the Egyptian record — the temple doors of Rekhmire's foundry, cast in moulds furnished with multiple filling openings so that several crucibles' worth of bronze could enter one vast, thin casting in a single coordinated pour — were one-off engineering: a mould built for one door, one pour, one day on which four hearths, their bellows crews and their crucible teams had to arrive at readiness together (Section XXIII). Large hollow statuary took the other road, the road of wax and core, to which the article now turns.

XX. Casting II — Lost Wax, Step by Step

Most metal statues from Egypt are cast, not hammered, and the majority were made by the direct lost-wax process — cire perdue — in which a wax model is encased in clay, the wax is melted out and lost, and metal takes its exact place. The process is described here in its full sequence, assembled from the Art Institute of Chicago's technical summary of Egyptian copper-alloy statuettes (Emily Teeter, "About Copper Alloy Statuettes," 2025) and Broschat's UEE survey, which agree in every structural particular. Every casting so made is unique: model and mould are both destroyed in the making.

// LOST-WAX CASTING — THE PROTOCOL //

1. Model in wax. For a solid statuette, model the figure entire in wax. For a hollow casting — which saves metal and casts more soundly at size — first form a core of clay, sand and organic material in the rough shape of the figure, smaller than the finished object by the intended wall thickness, and model the wax over the core to full form and final detail.

2. Add the plumbing. Attach wax rods: a sprue or funnel through which the metal will be poured in, and vents through which air will escape as the metal rises. A casting that cannot breathe cannot fill.

3. Pin the core. For hollow work, drive small metal rods — core supports or chaplets — through the wax into the core, so that when the wax is gone the core still hangs exactly centred in the empty mould, held by the pins.

4. Invest. Coat the whole assembly in clay, the investment, building a mould around the wax with the sprue and vent channels preserved.

5. Burn out. Heat the mould in a kiln. The wax melts and runs out — the "lost" wax — and the firing hardens the mould and burns away the last wax traces, leaving a clean cavity that is the figure's exact negative, with the core suspended inside it for hollow work.

6. Pour. Melt the metal in crucibles (Sections VI, IX) and pour it through the sprue into the hot mould, where it flows into the space the wax occupied — for hollow work, into the thin shell between core and investment.

7. Break out. When the metal has cooled and set, break the investment away. The mould dies so the figure can live; there is no second casting from it.

8. Fettle and finish. Cut away sprue, vents and the protruding ends of the core supports; file the chaplet points flush — where the pins were of the same metal as the casting they vanish into its surface; extract the core, commonly through an opening left at the base; chase the surface to final detail (Section XIII); join separately cast parts by solder or by mortise-and-tenon joints, as wooden statuary was joined; inlay, gild and polish as the commission requires (Section XIV).

Two corollaries matter for reading museum collections. First, serial production is possible without any mould surviving: plaster or stone moulds of the Late and Greco-Roman periods, once thought to be for casting metal directly, are now understood to have been used to produce the wax models — near-duplicate waxes, each then invested and cast by the protocol above, so that a temple's hundred identical gods are a hundred individual castings from a family of identical waxes. Second, the chaplets and core are diagnostic: an Egyptian hollow bronze that retains core material, or shows the filed points of its core pins, is telling the examiner exactly how it was made, and technical examination reads those traces as this protocol predicts. The great age of the process in Egypt is the first millennium BCE, when the temple statuette industry reached industrial scale — Garland and Bannister's famous observation, quoted by Ogden, that the draining of the lake at Karnak provided almost a glut of certain varieties of figure, gives the scale — but the process itself, and the hollow casting it enables, are already mature in the Middle Kingdom figure of Section IX.

XXI. Sheet Work — Raising, Annealing, and the Great Copper Statues

Not everything large was cast. The most famous large metal objects of the Old Kingdom were hammered: the over-life-size copper statue of Pepi I from Hierakonpolis (Cairo JE33034), with its smaller companion figure, was built up from copper sheet worked over a core, its parts shaped by hammering and joined, its fingernails gilded — a goldsmith's construction at the scale of a man. Its metal is itself a document: the most reliable analysis (Desch's, as reported by Ogden) gives almost pure copper with 0.7 per cent iron and 1.1 per cent nickel — fluxed-smelting copper (Section VII) of high purity, without tin, worked by the sheet techniques of Section XIII at monumental ambition. A Sixth Dynasty state that could raise, anneal, join and finish copper sheet into a king's image at over life size commanded the whole cold side of the craft as completely as the hot.

Sheet work at this level is a discipline of cycles: hammer until the metal stiffens, anneal until it relents, hammer again (Section XIII). Vessels show the same discipline at domestic scale — the copper and bronze vessels of the Rekhmire registers being beaten out, chased and polished in sequence — and mirrors, whose discs were cast or hammered and then ground and polished to reflect, close the repertoire. The finishing operations deserve their sentence: chasing sharpens the design, grinding and polishing with progressively finer abrasives bring the surface up, and the polished object is the first moment in the whole long process, from ore heap to mirror, at which the metal looks like the substance of the temple treasury. Everything before that moment is matte, blackened, slagged and sooty — which is why the scenes that show polishing show it last, as a separate station with its own workmen (Section XXIII).

XXII. Refining and Cupellation — the Evidence at Its Real Strength

This section is the article's adjudication section, and it is written differently from the rest: claim by claim, with the strength of the evidence stated for each. Refining is where the literature on Egyptian metal most often outruns its evidence, because refining is where metallurgy touches the alchemical imagination — the purification of gold is too good a story to leave unclaimed. The archive's rule applies with full force: the claim is printed at the strength the evidence leaves it, no stronger.

What the Processes Are

Cupellation separates precious metal from base metal by oxidation. The impure metal is melted with lead in a shallow, porous vessel — the cupel — under a strong oxidising blast at around 1000 °C. The lead and the base metals oxidise; the lead oxide, litharge, is skimmed or absorbed into the cupel's porous fabric; the precious metal, which does not oxidise, remains as a bright button. Cupellation cleans gold or silver of copper, lead and their like. What it cannot do is separate silver from gold: both survive the cupel together. That second separation — parting — is achieved by cementation: the gold-silver alloy, beaten thin or granulated to expose maximum surface, is packed in a closed vessel with salt and porous material and held hot for an extended period, during which the silver is converted to silver chloride and absorbed into the cement, leaving refined gold behind. The two processes answer two different questions, and much confusion in the secondary literature comes from using "refining" for both.

Where Cupellation Is Attested

Cupellation is old and, in the wider world, well evidenced. Lucas's synthesis, carried in the standard histories, has silver being produced in the Near East by about 2000 BCE from argentiferous galena by cupellation — the galena smelted to lead, the lead cupelled away, the silver remaining — and production evidence of the fourth millennium BCE, such as the late Uruk silver production at Habuba Kabira in Syria identified by Pernicka and colleagues, pushes the process's attested use earlier still in the region. The earliest unequivocal archaeological evidence for gold parting, by contrast, is the refinery excavated at Sardis in Lydia, of the era of Croesus in the sixth century BCE, where Ramage, Craddock and Cowell documented the cementation process in its installations and waste. Broschat's UEE survey states the current consensus in one sentence, which this article adopts: until further evidence emerges, it must be assumed that the separation or refining of gold was not invented until the seventh century BCE, in Lydia.

The Egyptian Ledger

For pharaonic Egypt, then, the ledger reads as follows. Refining of base-metal content — plausible, indirectly depicted, never excavated. Ogden examines the New Kingdom scene in the tomb of Puyemre (TT39) in which gold is melted with one man working foot bellows while a second stirs the crucible and blows into it through a blowpipe: the blowing, he argues, would help oxidise base metals present, particularly if lead or lead oxide had been added — "true cupellation" in his parenthesis — but with no significant loss of silver, and thus little overall increase in the purity of typical as-mined Egyptian gold. A scene consistent with oxidative refining of gold's base metals is therefore attested; a cupellation installation, a cupel, a litharge cake from a pharaonic context — none is part of the excavated record this article can cite. Parting — not attested for pharaonic Egypt. Broschat's survey is explicit: the knowledge of how to separate silver from gold was not present in Egypt until sometime between the seventh and fifth centuries BCE — which is to say, at the very end of, or after, the pharaonic sequence — and Ogden's verdict concurs from the objects: refining was not used in the day-to-day jewellery industry before the Late Period, and was perhaps introduced by the Persians. The phrases in the texts that speak of "gold of two times" and "gold of three times" have been read since Lucas as suggesting refining; the archive records the reading and the caution together — repeated melting and skimming, which any foundry practised, would justify such phrases without any parting at all.

Two further exhibits are entered with their labels attached. The Amarna Letter EA 10 records a Babylonian king's complaint about Egyptian gold losing weight when put through Mesopotamian fires — often published as evidence of a more efficient, silver-removing refining abroad. Ogden's discussion states the alternatives plainly: the letter must be assumed to refer to such a process, unless the batch sent was one of the gold-copper alloys typical of the Amarna period, whose base-metal content would burn away in any competent remelting and produce exactly the reported loss. The letter proves a complaint; it does not prove a process. And the newest claim: a 2025 study by Albarède, Blichert-Toft and Pinto (npj Heritage Science), reading lead isotopes and elemental patterns in Egyptian and Persian silver objects in the Louvre, argues that salt-cementation parting was already practised in the Middle Bronze Age, over a millennium before Sardis — the silver of some objects bearing, on their interpretation, the signature of silver recovered from gold cementation. The archive reports the study as what it is: a serious, peer-reviewed reinterpretation of analytical data that would, if sustained, rewrite this section; it is not an excavated refinery, it has not displaced the Sardis anchor in the consensus Broschat summarises, and the authors themselves frame parts of the chain — whether the silver chloride of cementation was reclaimed or discarded — as open questions. The claim is logged here, at that strength, for the reader who meets it elsewhere stated as settled fact.

One hypothesis completes the ledger. Antimonial litharge — lead oxide carrying antimony — was used in Egyptian glassmaking from the New Kingdom (Section X of the glass article treats the antimonate opacifiers), and it has been suggested in the literature that a supply of litharge in quantity implies cupellation being practised somewhere in the chain that fed the glass workshops. The suggestion is entered here as a suggestion: litharge can arise from lead processing that is not precious-metal cupellation, and no pharaonic cupellation hearth has been excavated to confirm it. What pharaonic Egypt demonstrably refined, early and routinely, was its copper (Section VII) and its gold's base-metal content at most; the purification of gold from silver, the operation on which the later alchemy of the recipe papyri dreams, belongs in Egypt to the first millennium BCE and after. The bench record says so, and this article lets it.

XXIII. The Scenes as Documents — Rekhmire Read Step by Step

The tomb of the vizier Rekhmire at Thebes (TT100), of the reign of Thutmose III, about 1460 BCE, preserves the most complete metalworking documentation to survive from pharaonic Egypt: registers of the temple workshops of Amun, published by Norman de Garis Davies (1943) and read by every subsequent student of the craft, Ogden and Scheel among them. The scenes are not illustrations of the craft in general; they are a specific production, the casting of bronze doors for the temple of Amun, shown from raw material to finished object. Read in sequence, as the archive now reads them, they are the closest thing Egypt left to the foundry manual it never wrote.

Step one — the metal arrives. The sequence opens with supply: ingots and scrap delivered to the workshop, including the oxhide ingots of the Cypriot trade (Section IV), and gold arriving in the ring form in which the treasury handled it. Step two — weighing. Scribes record the metal on balances — gold rings weighed against stone weights, the deben arithmetic of Section I performed in public, under the vizier's inspection. Nothing enters the melt unweighed; nothing will leave it unaccounted. Step three — the hearths. The foundry register shows four charcoal hearths fired at once, each fanned by dish bellows worked with the feet (Section VI): several blowers, a battery of air, because the melt to come is not a crucible of jewellery metal but the charge for monumental doors. Step four — melting and alloying. Crucibles stand in the hearths; the copper melts; the alloying addition — the tin of Section IX — is made at the crucible; overseers supervise the melt with blowpipe and stirring rod, the gestures Puyemre's parallel scene (TT39) shows in gold. Step five — the pour. Crucible teams converge on the door mould: a great flat mould, furnished with multiple filling openings, receiving molten bronze from several crucibles in one coordinated operation — the military precision of the pour is the register's whole point, since a door casting that freezes halfway is a door lost. Step six — finishing. The final registers move to the cold bench: vessels and objects beaten out, chased with hammer and punch on the anvil, and polished — the smiths of the last station, with brazier, blowpipe and tongs laid out above them in Scheel's famous plate, turning matte castings into temple furniture.

The same tomb's precious-metal registers complete the documentation for gold and silver: craftsmen making, in the accompanying text's paraphrase as published in the secondary literature, vessels for the god in gold and silver — weighing, melting, casting, soldering, hammering, engraving and polishing all depicted, the full verb list of Sections XIII–XIV performed by named trades. And Rekhmire does not stand alone. The Old Kingdom had already fixed the genre: the tombs of Mereruka and Pepiankh show melting with the spouted crucible of Section VI; Wepemnofret's workshop captions the annealing of Section XIII; the tomb of Puyemre supplies the gold-melting scene on which Section XXII's cupellation discussion turns; Baqt III and Khacy document gold-winning (Section XI). Read together, the scenes give the craft's chaîne opératoire across two thousand years — and read critically, with Davies's plates against the excavated debris of Buhen, Timna and Qantir, they check out: the tools match the finds, the gestures match the physics, and where a scene is ambiguous, as at Puyemre, this article has said so rather than resolving it by wish.

What the scenes cannot give is equally definite, and the archive closes the section with it. No scene shows smelting at the mine — the valley's painters painted workshops, not desert camps — so the smelt of Section V rests on debris and replication alone. No scene gives a temperature, a timing or a proportion. And every scene is a vizier's or a noble's monument, showing the workshop as the administration wished it remembered: ordered, supervised, complete. The slag heaps, which show the failures, the re-runs and the waste, are the necessary correction, and this article has used them as one throughout.

XXIV. Workshops and Workmen — Qantir, Amarna, the House of Gold

Egyptian metalworking lived in institutions. At the summit stood the temple and palace workshops — the foundry of Amun's temple at Thebes whose work Rekhmire inspects, the House of Gold, the treasury workshop in which, despite its name, not only gold but other precious materials were worked and stored — and the temple workshops treated in this archive's temple laboratory article, where the production of cult metalwork sat inside the ritual economy of the temple itself. Below the summit, production spread through city workshops and into houses: the excavated record refuses any picture of a craft confined to temple courts.

The two great excavated cases frame the range. At Qantir (Section VI) stands the state foundry at full industrial scale: thirty thousand square metres of crucibles, moulds and casting waste, foreign armour among its products and foreign craftsmen, on the evidence, among its workforce — production as an arm of the Ramesside military state. At Amarna, a generation earlier and at the opposite organisational pole, the domestic workshop complex M50.14–16 in the Main City South — excavated by Woolley in 1922 as a glass workshop and re-excavated by the Amarna Project in 2014 and 2017 — yielded glass, faience and stone working in quantity, and, in the 2017 season, crucibles containing remains of copper alloy: metalworking practised inside a house-sized jewellery workshop, in a suburb, alongside the vitreous crafts. Anna Hodgkinson's publication of the site and Frederik Rademakers's study of its metallurgical remains make M50.14–16 the type-site of the small end of the industry — scrap bronze remelted, small castings poured, in a courtyard economy that the state industry of Qantir presupposes as its base.

For the workmen themselves, the documentary capital is Deir el-Medina, the New Kingdom craftsmen's village of the Theban necropolis, whose ostraca and papyri record a state workforce's rations, absences, disputes and deliveries in unmatched detail. The village's crew served the royal tombs rather than the foundries, and this article does not pretend otherwise; but its records are the archive's best window onto how Egyptian craft production was organised at the level of the working life — gang structure, overseers and scribes, materials issued by weight and accounted on return, skill transmitted inside families — and the metalworkers of the temple workshops belonged to the same administrative world, under overseers of works and overseers of goldsmiths whose titles the inscriptions preserve. The stela of the overseer of artisans Irtisen, which Broschat notes may allude to lost-wax casting while boasting of hereditary expertise passed from father to son, gives the craftsman's own voice to the structure: the knowledge was a patrimony, guarded as one.

Status followed skill. The Timna evidence of Section IV carries the point into the desert: excavation of the great smelting camp on the so-called Slaves' Hill showed, on the excavators' reading of the faunal and material record, metalworkers of high status on a rich diet — specialists the industry could not afford to waste, not the expendable gang of the older imagination. From the reckoner of gold at the Abrak well to the bellows crews of Rekhmire, the bench record of Egyptian metal is a record of valued, organised, hereditary expertise — the human infrastructure without which no protocol in this article runs.

XXV. Repeat It — the Master Protocols in Brief

The archive's standing practice for procedural articles is to end the technical account with the processes restated as numbered protocols, stripped of commentary. The full protocols stand in Sections V (crucible smelting), XI (gold-washing), XIV (granulation) and XX (lost wax). What follows condenses the two remaining master sequences of the Egyptian foundry — the bronze melt and the door-scale pour — from the sections indicated, as the bench summary of the whole article.

// THE BRONZE MELT AND THE GREAT POUR — CONDENSED //

1. Weigh the charge: copper, by ingot and scrap, against the deben weights; record it (Sections I, XXIII). Weigh the tin separately — in the mature foundry, up to about one part in ten for standard bronze (Section IX).

2. Set the hearths: charcoal laid, bellows crews posted — foot bellows for a great melt, one blower to a hearth pair, relieving in rotation (Section VI).

3. Charge the crucibles with copper under a covering of charcoal, which keeps the melt from the air and the tin, once added, from oxidising away.

4. Blow the crucibles to full melting heat — past 1083 °C for the copper, the melt judged by eye and by the rod.

5. Add the tin to the molten copper; stir the alloy together; skim the dross from the surface (Sections IX, XIX).

6. Prove the mould ready: built, dried, its filling openings clear — for a door casting, a mould with multiple gates, so the whole thin casting fills before any part can freeze (Sections XIX, XXIII).

7. Pour in coordination: crucible teams in sequence, each pourer tipping with tongs, each attendant holding back the crucible's dross with a stick so clean metal alone runs (Section XIX).

8. Cool undisturbed; break or open the mould; fettle the casting — gates cut, surface chased, the work passed to the cold bench for beating, chasing and polishing (Sections XIII, XXI, XXIII).

And the whole article in one sentence: Egypt dug bright ores in Sinai and the Eastern Desert and gold in the south, smelted copper in crucibles under blowpipes and then in fluxed furnaces under bellows, hardened it first with arsenic and then with imported tin, washed its gold from gravel and crushed quartz and worked it unrefined by hammer, solder and granule, imported its silver and named it white gold, treasured the iron that fell from the sky for two thousand years before it learned to smelt the iron under its feet — and painted the entire operation, station by station, on the walls of its tombs. The recipe papyri of Alchemy, when they come, inherit every one of those stations. They invent almost none of them.
