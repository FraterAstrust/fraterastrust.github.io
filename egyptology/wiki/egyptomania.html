I. Scope and Method — Two Histories, One Article, Three Grades of Evidence

This article tells two histories that are usually told apart, because each explains the other. The first is the history of Egyptology as a discipline: how a deciphered script became an organised field with museums, an antiquities service, excavation method, university chairs, journals, and eventually an international rescue campaign. The second is the history of Egyptomania: the recurring Western fascination with ancient Egypt that filled those museums' galleries, funded expeditions through subscriptions and ticket sales, and put pyramids, sphinxes, scarabs, and obelisks onto buildings, furniture, jewellery, and cinema screens. Neither history is intelligible alone. The discipline supplied the mania with new objects and new facts at regular intervals (a decipherment in 1822, a royal tomb in 1922); the mania supplied the discipline with money, visitors, political attention, and recruits.

The companion article, Decipherment History, ends where this one begins: with Champollion's Lettre à M. Dacier of 1822 and the first grammar and dictionary. What happened after the script could be read (what was dug, who dug it, where the objects went, and what the public made of it all) is this article's ground. The esoteric reception of Egypt, which has its own long history, is treated in Esoteric Orders, whose §XXI touches Egyptomania from the orders' side; this article treats the public, commercial, and institutional side, and the two should be read as complements, not duplicates.

Because this subject attracts legend the way the tombs attracted collectors, the article grades its own claims, in the archive's usual discipline, and the grading is stated in the text wherever it matters:

Two further rules govern what follows. First, quotations: only quotations that can be traced to a named, checkable source are given as quotations, and each is attributed to that source. Everything else is paraphrase and is written as paraphrase. The most famous line in the whole subject, Howard Carter's answer at the first peep into Tutankhamun's antechamber, is handled under this rule in §XVI, where the published version and Carter's own journal are distinguished. Second, names: this is not a Europeans-only story, and it is not written as one. Egyptian officials drafted the first antiquities law in 1835 (§IX); Egyptian foremen, inspectors, restorers, and scholars run through §§XIV–XV as named individuals wherever the sources name them, and the article says plainly, where the record fails to name the Egyptian workforce, that the failure is in the record and not in the work.

A note on vocabulary. The land the discipline studies called itself, in its own language, kmt [Gardiner: I6-G17-X1-O49] [𓆎𓅓𓏏𓊖], conventionally vocalised Kemet, "the Black Land." Its temples were each a ḥwt-nṯr [Gardiner: O10-R8] [𓉡𓊹], a "mansion of the god," and its temple libraries and scriptoria belonged to the institution this archive treats in House of Life, the pr-ꜥnḫ [Gardiner: O1-D36-S34] [𓉐𓂝𓋹], "House of Life." The modern discipline that studies that world is barely two centuries old. This article is its biography, and its public's.

II. 1798 — An Army with a Library: Napoleon's Expedition and the Savants

Modern Egyptology's conventional birthday is not a scholarly event but a military one. In May 1798 a French expedition under General Napoleon Bonaparte sailed for Egypt, then an Ottoman province governed in practice by the Mamluk beys. The campaign's strategic purpose, disrupting British routes to India, is documented in the expedition's own planning; its military outcome is equally documented: initial victory, the destruction of the French fleet by Nelson at Aboukir Bay (1 August 1798), a failed Syrian campaign, Bonaparte's departure for France in 1799, and the capitulation of the remaining French forces in 1801. What outlasted the army was its passenger list.

Embarked with the troops was a Commission of Sciences and Arts: some 160 to 170 savants (the figure of 167 is the one most often given in the expedition literature), astronomers, engineers, mathematicians, naturalists, chemists, geographers, architects, draughtsmen, linguists, and printers, among them the mathematician Gaspard Monge, the chemist Claude-Louis Berthollet, the naturalist Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire, and the artist and antiquarian Dominique Vivant Denon, whose bestselling illustrated Voyage of 1802 would reach the European public years before the official publication (§III). In Cairo the savants were housed as the Institut d'Égypte, founded in August 1798, where they surveyed, measured, collected, and recorded: antiquities, certainly, but also Egypt's modern towns, crafts, agriculture, and natural history. The expedition was, among other things, an instrument of occupation, and its scholarship was made under occupation conditions, a fact the discipline's historians now state as part of the record rather than as an embarrassment to be passed over.

The expedition's most consequential single find was accidental. In July 1799, French soldiers rebuilding a fort near Rashid (Rosetta) uncovered the trilingual decree stele now called the Rosetta Stone. Its decipherment history belongs to the companion article and is not repeated here; its institutional history belongs to this one. Under the Capitulation of Alexandria (1801), the antiquities collected by the French were surrendered to Britain; the Stone reached London in 1802 and was presented to the British Museum, where the French savants, who had been allowed to retain their notes, drawings, and papers, lost the objects but kept the data. That division, objects to one capital, documentation to another, set the pattern for the collecting century that followed (§V), and the Stone's presence in London remains a live question of the ethics thread (§XXV; see Repatriation Ethics).

WHAT THE EXPEDITION LEFT BEHIND

Three durable things: a surveyed, drawn, and measured Egypt, published as the Description (§III); a European reading public for Egyptian subjects, created by Denon's Voyage (1802) and the returning savants' memoirs; and a demonstration, visible to every European government, that antiquities were spoils worth claiming in a treaty. The first made Egyptology possible as scholarship; the second made Egyptomania possible as fashion; the third made both of them political.

One caution belongs here, because later sections depend on it. The savants' Egypt was recorded at speed, under military escort, in a country whose inhabitants appear in the Description largely as subjects of study rather than as colleagues. Egyptian scholars of the period, the chronicler Abd al-Rahman al-Jabarti most notably, recorded the occupation from the other side, and al-Jabarti's account of the French and their institute is a primary source for the expedition in its own right. The discipline that grew from 1798 spent more than a century telling the story almost entirely from the savants' side of the room; §§IX, XIV, and XV of this article are part of the archive's correction of that habit.

III. The Description de l'Égypte — The Expedition Becomes a Book

The Description de l'Égypte, published in Paris between 1809 and 1829 (the closing date is also given as 1828 or 1830, depending on whether text volumes, plate volumes, or the second edition are counted; the first edition is conventionally dated 1809–1829), is the largest single publication the expedition produced: a multi-volume survey in three great divisions, Antiquités, État moderne, and Histoire naturelle, with roughly 900 engraved plates, many of them elephant-folio. Temples were drawn elevation by elevation and plan by plan; inscriptions were copied at a scale and accuracy no European publication had attempted; and the modern sections recorded Cairo's mosques, crafts, and trades with the same documentary seriousness. For the companion article's subject, the Description mattered because its plates put thousands of hieroglyphic inscriptions in front of scholars who would never see Egypt. For this article's subject, it mattered just as much as a pattern book.

The plates' second life was decorative. Designers who had never left Paris, London, or Vienna could now copy a pylon's battered (sloping) profile, a cavetto cornice, a lotus capital, or a winged sun-disc from an authoritative source, and the "Egyptian" objects and rooms of the Empire period (§XIX) are full of forms traceable plate by plate to the Description and to Denon's earlier Voyage. The Metropolitan Museum's Heilbrunn essay on the Empire style makes the mechanism explicit: Denon, who became director of the Louvre's predecessor museum, was instrumental in attaching Egyptianised design to Napoleon's imperial image, and Egyptian motifs appear across Empire silver, wallpaper, porcelain, furniture, and textiles. Scholarship and style were, from the start, the same publication read two ways.

The Description also fixed an image problem the discipline still works against. Its Egypt is monumental, ancient, and largely emptied of modern inhabitants except as staffage; its plates taught Europe to see Egypt as a museum that happened to have a population. The corrective scholarship of the last half-century, which reads the Description as an artefact of empire as well as of learning (the reading associated in Anglophone debate with Edward Said's Orientalism, 1978, where the expedition is a founding case), belongs to the history of the discipline itself: Egyptology's self-criticism is one of its modern products, and §XXV returns to it.

IV. Legend Checked: Napoleon's Night in the Great Pyramid, and the Sphinx's Nose

No figure in this article attracts more legend per documented fact than Napoleon at Giza, and the legends are worth treating properly, as specimens: they show, in miniature, exactly how Egyptomania manufactures its evidence. Two are adjudicated here.

THE NIGHT IN THE KING'S CHAMBER — LEGEND (UNVERIFIED TRADITION)

The story: Bonaparte spent a night alone inside the King's Chamber of the Great Pyramid, emerged pale and shaken, refused ever to describe what he had experienced, and on his deathbed began to speak of it, then stopped. The adjudication: no contemporary source records any such night. Napoleon's private secretary Bourrienne, who was with him in Egypt, states that Napoleon never entered the pyramid at all; the detailed deathbed scene appears in none of the St Helena memoirs. The Smithsonian's account of the story calls it, in terms, apocryphal, and traces an early version of the pyramid anecdote to W. H. Ireland's Napoleon Anecdotes of the 1820s. The story is therefore presented in this archive as what it is: an unverified tradition that grew in 19th- and 20th-century retelling, valuable as evidence about Napoleon's afterlife in popular culture, and as evidence about 1798, worthless.

THE SPHINX'S NOSE — LEGEND (CONTRADICTED)

The story: French artillery used the Sphinx's nose for target practice and shot it off. The adjudication: the nose was already missing long before 1798. Drawings made decades before the expedition (Frederic Louis Norden's Egyptian views, published 1755) show the Sphinx without its nose, and the 15th-century historian al-Maqrizi already describes the face as damaged, attributing the defacement to a Sufi iconoclast in 1378. Whatever one's judgement of that attribution, the chronological point is decisive: a monument drawn noseless in 1755 cannot have lost its nose to soldiers of 1798. The tale survives because it flatters a story about the expedition that both French glory and French guilt find useful; the drawings are the record, and the record is earlier.

A third item is graded differently, and the difference is instructive. Napoleon's address to his army before the Battle of the Pyramids (21 July 1798), with its famous appeal to the centuries looking down from the monuments, is reported: the wording in circulation comes through later Napoleonic literature rather than a stenographic record of the day, the battle was fought at a distance from Giza at which the pyramids were a horizon feature rather than a looming presence, and historians treat the speech as the expedition's own myth-making, polished in retrospect. This article accordingly does not quote it as a verified utterance. The pattern to carry forward is the one §I promised: in this subject, the better a story sounds, the later its paperwork tends to be.

V. The Collecting Age — Consuls, Firmans, and the Market in Antiquities

Between the French capitulation of 1801 and Mariette's monopoly of 1858, Egyptian antiquities were acquired under a system that was legal in form and extractive in effect. Excavation and export ran on the firman, a permit issued by the Egyptian government, and the men best placed to obtain firmans, labour, and river transport were the European consul-generals. The two great rivals of the 1810s and 1820s define the era: Bernardino Drovetti, the French consul-general, a Piedmontese veteran of Napoleon's armies whose agents worked the Theban necropolis and the temples of Nubia; and Henry Salt, the British consul-general from 1816, who employed Belzoni (§VI) and built the collections that the British Museum and the Louvre would buy. Their agents competed for the same tombs, sometimes violently in the period's own reports, and the antiquities market they fed was centred on Luxor and Cairo dealers as much as on the consuls themselves.

The honest description of this system has three parts, and all three belong in the record. First, it was authorised: firmans were real, sales were real, and the Egyptian state of Muhammad Ali treated antiquities, among other things, as diplomatic currency, presenting major pieces to foreign rulers (§IX records what the same state did in law, which complicates any simple story). Second, it was destructive: objects were prised from their architectural and archaeological contexts with no recording worthy of the name, temples were quarried, and the information destroyed in a single season of consular collecting exceeds what any later science can reconstruct. Third, it built the museums: the founding collections of Turin, the Louvre, and much of the British Museum's Egyptian holding are consular collections, purchased entire (§§VII–VIII). Modern museum histories, to their credit, now say this in their own galleries and catalogues; the ethics thread of §XXV begins here and not, as popular telling often has it, in 1922.

The Champollion–Rosellini and Lepsius expeditions mark the collecting age's transition into something else: state expeditions whose primary product was publication (Champollion's Monuments de l'Égypte et de la Nubie; Lepsius's vast Denkmäler aus Aegypten und Aethiopien, 1849–1859), and whose collecting, though still substantial, was increasingly framed as documentation. The decipherment is what made the difference: once inscriptions could be read, a copied text in a folio was worth scholarly capital that a crated statue, however fine, was not. Egyptology's centre of gravity began, slowly, to move from the crate to the notebook. §XII completes that movement.

VI. Belzoni — Engineer, Showman, Excavator

Giovanni Battista Belzoni (1778–1823) is the collecting age's most vivid figure, and the one its historians argue about most, because he sits exactly on the boundary between removal and discovery. A Paduan barber's son who had worked in London as a stage strongman and hydraulic demonstrator, Belzoni reached Egypt in 1815 to sell Muhammad Ali a water-lifting machine. The machine was rejected; the engineer remained, and on the recommendation of the traveller Johann Ludwig Burckhardt he entered Henry Salt's service. His engineering competence was real and it was immediately applied to the largest objects in Egypt.

The documented sequence of his Egyptian years, 1816–1819, is extraordinary by any standard: the removal of the colossal granite bust of Ramesses II known as the Younger Memnon from the Ramesseum to the Nile and thence to the British Museum, a transport operation of levers, rollers, and rope-work that French agents had attempted and abandoned; the clearing of the sand-choked Great Temple of Abu Simbel in 1817, entering a monument Burckhardt had sighted in 1813; the discovery, in October 1817, of the tomb of Seti I in the Valley of the Kings (KV17, still called "Belzoni's Tomb"), with its alabaster sarcophagus, later acquired by Sir John Soane's Museum in London after the British Museum declined it; and the first modern entry into the pyramid of Khafre at Giza in 1818, where his painted name and date remain in the burial chamber. His Narrative of the Operations and Recent Discoveries (1820) made him famous, and his 1821 London exhibition at the Egyptian Hall, with its full-scale facsimile of two chambers of Seti I's tomb, was a founding event of Egyptomania as public entertainment (§XIX).

The adjudication this archive applies to Belzoni is double, and both halves are documented. As a remover, he worked for a consul-collector in a firman system, broke through tomb walls, and shipped antiquities out of Egypt; the sarcophagus of Seti I in a London house-museum is that system's product. As a recorder, he was better than his market: his Narrative and his exhibition facsimiles documented Seti I's tomb with a care that preserved information the tomb itself has since lost to deterioration and tourism, and his engineering accounts are primary sources for the monuments he moved. The modern scholarly verdict, reflected in museum interpretation at the British Museum and Sir John Soane's Museum alike, holds both: a pioneer of archaeological discovery operating inside, and partly against the grain of, an extractive trade. His wife Sarah Belzoni, who travelled and worked with him and later published her own account of Egyptian women's lives within his Narrative's companion material, belongs in the record beside him; the collecting age was not, in fact, an all-male enterprise, merely an all-male byline.

VII. The Great Formations I — Turin (1824) and the Louvre

The first great Egyptian museum was neither in London nor Paris. In 1824 the King of Sardinia, Charles Felix, purchased Bernardino Drovetti's first collection, some thousands of pieces assembled in Egypt over two decades, for the museum of the University of Turin. The resulting Museo Egizio is conventionally described, including by the museum itself, as the oldest museum devoted specifically to Egyptian antiquities, and the first of the great formations. Champollion, who came to Turin in 1824 to study the collection, worked among its papyri and statues at the moment his system was new; his often-retold remark on arriving among the Turin holdings, that the road to Memphis and Thebes passed through Turin, circulates as a reported saying rather than a documented text, and is given here at exactly that strength. What is documented is the work: the Turin collection, including the Royal Papyrus (the Turin King List), became foundational evidence for the chronology the decipherment had made it possible to build.

In Paris, the Louvre's Egyptian holdings, seeded by Napoleonic-era acquisitions and swollen by purchases including Henry Salt's second collection in 1826, were constituted as a distinct Musée égyptien in 1826 with Champollion himself as its first curator. The appointment matters beyond biography: the decipherer spent his last years arranging objects in galleries, arguing for purchases, and drafting the Louvre's Egyptian catalogues, which is to say that Egyptology's founding scholar was also one of its founding museum-makers, and the discipline's double nature, philology in the study, objects in the gallery, was present in one career from the start. The Louvre's department grew by the mechanisms of §V (consular purchase), by Mariette's early excavations, which were conducted for the Louvre before 1858 (§X), and by the partage divisions of the later 19th and early 20th centuries (§XVI explains the system at its end). Its Egyptian galleries, reopened in expanded form in the 1990s beneath and beside I. M. Pei's Pyramid (§XXIII), remain one of the two or three largest Egyptian collections outside Egypt.

A pattern worth naming: every one of these formations was a state act. Kings, emperors, and khedives bought Egyptian antiquity as national prestige, and the museums they founded were instruments of that prestige before they were instruments of scholarship, which most of them also, genuinely, became. Egyptomania and Egyptology share this root too: the same purchase that thrilled a capital's public also paid for the curators who would spend a century cataloguing what the thrill had bought.

VIII. The Great Formations II — The British Museum and Berlin

The British Museum (founded 1753, opened 1759) held Egyptian objects from its earliest years, but its Egyptian identity was made by the Napoleonic war and the consular age: the surrendered French collections of 1801, including the Rosetta Stone (presented 1802); the Younger Memnon, Belzoni's transport triumph, arriving in 1818; and the purchase of Henry Salt's first collection in 1823. Egyptian sculpture filled the museum's ground-floor galleries through the 19th century, augmented by excavations the museum later sponsored directly and by its share under partage. The museum's own modern interpretation is candid about the imperial frame of these acquisitions, and its Egyptian galleries are among the sites where the repatriation debate of §XXV is most concrete: the Rosetta Stone, the Younger Memnon, and the Parthenon sculptures are argued about in the same building, under the same statute (the British Museum Act 1963, which generally bars deaccessioning), a legal fact that shapes what outcomes are currently possible whatever one's view of the merits.

Berlin's Egyptian collection, now the Ägyptisches Museum und Papyrussammlung, was formed by purchase (including a Drovetti collection) and transformed by the Prussian expedition of Richard Lepsius, 1842–1845, which returned with a vast haul of objects, casts, and squeezes and with the documentation published as the Denkmäler. Berlin became the centre of the discipline's philological wing: Adolf Erman's Berlin school produced the Wörterbuch der ägyptischen Sprache, the great Berlin dictionary project (on which see the companion article, §XVII), and trained a generation of grammarians. The collection's most famous single object arrived later and by excavation: the painted bust of Nefertiti, found by Ludwig Borchardt's German expedition at Amarna in December 1912 and allocated to Berlin under the partage division of the finds, a division Egypt has contested ever since on the ground that the bust's significance was not made plain at the division. The bust, displayed in the Neues Museum from 2009, is, with the Rosetta Stone, one of the two objects around which the modern repatriation argument most often turns; the facts of its finding and division are documented, the fairness of the division is the disputed question, and §XXV states both halves.

PARTAGE, IN ONE PARAGRAPH

From the later 19th century until the system was wound down in the decades after 1922, foreign excavations in Egypt worked under licence from the Antiquities Service and the finds were divided: the Cairo museum took what it judged unique or necessary, and the excavating institution took a share of the remainder for its sponsoring museum. Partage is why university museums from Boston to Berlin to Copenhagen hold coherent Egyptian collections, and why the system's end, as Egyptian law tightened (notably the 1912 law asserting state ownership, the post-1922 assertion of full Egyptian control over Tutankhamun's tomb, and finally Law 117 of 1983), is a central chapter of §XXV. It was, in its day, a legal and regulated system, and it was also, in its effects, a mechanism by which a large fraction of everything dug left Egypt. Both sentences are true, and the archive's ethics articles are built on holding them together.

IX. Egypt's Own First Moves — The 1835 Decree and Rifaa al-Tahtawi

The standard European narrative jumps from the consuls to Mariette, as if Egypt were passive in its own antiquities until a Frenchman organised them. The documentary record says otherwise, and UCL's history of museum formation in Egypt, on which this section draws, lays the record out. On 15 August 1835, the government of Muhammad Ali issued a decree that did three things at once: it prohibited the unlicensed export of antiquities, it ordered the inspection of monuments, and it envisaged a museum in Cairo to receive finds, under an inspector (Yusuf Diya Effendi was placed in charge of the collection that formed in the Ezbekiya quarter). The decree is associated in the scholarship with the advocacy of Rifaa al-Tahtawi (1801–1873), the great Egyptian scholar of the educational missions to Paris, who had seen what European museums were and argued, in print, that Egypt's antiquities were a national patrimony to be kept, studied, and displayed at home. Al-Tahtawi is, on this record, among the founders of Egyptian Egyptology in the institutional sense, a generation before the Antiquities Service.

The 1835 system did not hold. The collection was moved, neglected, and, in episodes the record preserves with uncomfortable clarity, given away by the state itself: Abbas I presented part of it abroad, and Said Pasha presented the remainder to the Austrian Archduke Maximilian in 1855. The same government that banned export in 1835 treated the assembled collection as a diplomatic gift reserve twenty years later. Both facts belong to the honest history, and together they explain 1858 better than any story of European rescue: what Mariette obtained from Said was not the idea of an antiquities service, which Egypt already had on paper, but the budget, the monopoly, and the viceroy's sustained backing to make the paper system real (§X). Histories that begin in 1858 mistake an implementation for an invention.

Al-Tahtawi also stands at the head of a second line this article follows: Egyptians writing Egypt's ancient history for Egyptian readers. His historical works presented the pharaonic past as part of Egypt's own national story at a time when European Egyptology treated that past as a European scholarly possession and many Egyptian intellectuals, for religious and cultural reasons, held it at arm's length. The line runs from al-Tahtawi through Ahmed Kamal (§XV) to the Pharaonism of the 1920s, the cultural movement, contemporary with Tutankhamun's discovery and with Egypt's partial independence in 1922, that placed pharaonic imagery at the centre of modern Egyptian national identity in literature, sculpture (Mahmoud Mokhtar's Egypt Awakening, unveiled 1928, sets a peasant woman beside a rising sphinx), and state symbolism. Egyptomania, in other words, had an Egyptian chapter, written by Egyptians for their own purposes, and it is not a footnote to the European one.

X. Mariette and the Antiquities Service (1858) — The Monopoly That Stopped the Drain

Auguste Mariette (1821–1881) came to Egypt in 1850 on a Louvre mission to buy Coptic manuscripts and stayed to excavate the Serapeum of Saqqara, the subterranean burial galleries of the Apis bulls, whose location he reconstructed from a text of Strabo and a sphinx-lined avenue appearing through the sand. The finds went to the Louvre, as the system of §V prescribed. What changed everything was his return in government service: on 1 June 1858, Said Pasha appointed him director (mamur) of a refounded Antiquities Service (Maslahat al-Athar), on an Egyptian government salary, with authority over excavation and antiquities throughout Egypt. The register of the new museum's accessions begins the same month, its first entries in Mariette's own hand.

Mariette's system inverted the consular age. Excavation by others was suppressed in favour of a Service monopoly; export was restricted so that new discoveries stayed for, in the words of his biographical tradition, the Egyptian nation; Egyptian foremen and workforces were posted at the great sites from Aswan to Giza to clear, guard, and send sculpture to Cairo; and a museum was founded at Bulaq (opened 1863) to hold what the Service found (§XI). His own excavation record was immense and uneven: the Serapeum, the Valley Temple of Khafre and its diorite statue of the king, mastabas across Saqqara, work at Abydos, Thebes, Edfu, and Tanis, conducted at a pace and on a scale that later, slower archaeology criticised for thin recording even as it acknowledged that the alternative in the 1860s was not better recording but the dealers. Mariette also supplied, from his Egyptological learning, the scenario from which Verdi's Aida was developed (§XX), a reminder that the Service's founder moved in the Egyptomania world as easily as in the scholarly one.

Two correctives complete the section. First, Mariette's Service was an Egyptian state institution, funded by the Egyptian government and staffed overwhelmingly by Egyptians below its French director; its successor directors included Egyptians in the inspectorate long before an Egyptian, and the institution survives, through many reorganisations, as Egypt's present antiquities administration. Second, the monopoly's beneficiaries and its costs were both real: it ended the consular free-for-all and kept the finds in Egypt, and it also concentrated authority in one man's hands, with excavation standards to match. Gaston Maspero, who succeeded Mariette (1881), loosened the monopoly into the licensed-excavation and partage system of §VIII, the compromise under which the discipline's heroic age of excavation, Petrie's included, was conducted.

XI. A Museum in Cairo — Bulaq, Giza, Tahrir Square, and the Grand Egyptian Museum

The Cairo museum's itinerary is the discipline's Egyptian history in addresses. Mariette's collection opened at Bulaq in 1863, in a former river-transport building on the Nile; a flood in 1878 damaged the building and its contents, and Mariette, who died in 1881, was buried in the museum's garden, where his tomb and statue remain, a French director commemorated in the courtyard of the institution he founded for Egypt. In 1891 the collection moved to a palace at Giza, and in 1902 to the purpose-built Egyptian Museum on Tahrir Square in central Cairo, the pink neoclassical building that housed, for over a century, the world's greatest single collection of Egyptian antiquities, including the complete contents of Tutankhamun's tomb (§XVI) and the royal mummies.

The Tahrir museum's successor reorders the geography of the whole subject. The Grand Egyptian Museum (GEM), built beside the Giza pyramids and fully opened in November 2025 after a long construction and phased openings, displays the complete Tutankhamun collection together for the first time and rehouses tens of thousands of objects from the Tahrir stores; the National Museum of Egyptian Civilization (NMEC) in Fustat, opened in 2021, received the royal mummies from Tahrir in the widely reported "Pharaohs' Golden Parade" of April 2021, a state procession of twenty-two royal mummies through Cairo that was itself a landmark of 21st-century Egyptian Egyptomania, staged by Egypt, for Egypt, with the discipline's full scholarly apparatus in attendance. The centre of institutional gravity that §§VII–VIII located in Turin, Paris, London, and Berlin has, in the present century, moved home. Any account of the museums that ends in Europe is a century out of date.

XII. The Method Change — Petrie, Recording, and Sequence Dating

If Mariette changed who controlled excavation, W. M. Flinders Petrie (1853–1942) changed what excavation was. Petrie arrived in Egypt in 1880 to survey the Giza pyramids, and his triangulation survey of the plateau, published in 1883, remains a model of measured recording (and a standing refutation, by measurement, of the pyramidology then in fashion). Across sixty years of fieldwork, at Tanis, Naukratis, Amarna, Abydos, Naqada, and dozens of other sites, he imposed a discipline the consular and Mariette eras had lacked: everything found was evidence, pottery above all; contexts were to be recorded, not merely emptied; and publication was prompt, annual, and illustrated, so that a season's work entered the scholarly record within the year.

His signature invention was sequence dating: working on the predynastic cemeteries of Naqada and Diospolis Parva in the 1890s, Petrie seriated hundreds of graves by the changing forms of their pottery, arranging them in a relative chronology (his Sequence Dates) for a period that had no inscriptions, no king lists, and no other clock. Seriation, developed independently in Scandinavian archaeology, became a foundational tool of world archaeology, and it was built, characteristically, out of the material earlier excavators had thrown away. His Methods and Aims in Archaeology (1904) codified the ethic: the excavator as recording instrument, the spoil heap as a confession of failure. Petrie trained, directly or by example, much of the next generation, Howard Carter among his former assistants (§XVI), and his collection formed the Petrie Museum at University College London, where he held, from 1892, the first British university chair in Egyptology (§XIII).

The adjudication, again, is double, because the record is. Petrie's method was revolutionary and his pace remained ferocious; seasons were short, funds were thin, and by later standards his recording, though far ahead of his contemporaries', was summary. His workforce system, built on trained Egyptian foremen from Quft (§XIV), professionalised excavation labour and also embedded a colonial hierarchy in it: the reis directed hundreds, the director published alone. And Petrie's published views on race, including eugenic commitments recorded in his own writings, are part of his documentary record and are stated as such in modern scholarship and museum interpretation; they shaped some of his historical interpretations, notably of the predynastic, in ways the field has since discarded. The method change he led is the reason modern archaeology can criticise him from evidence: he taught the discipline to keep the records by which he himself is now judged.

XIII. Institutionalisation — Societies, Chairs, Journals, and Institutes

Between 1880 and 1940 Egyptology acquired the full apparatus of a modern discipline. The markers are datable, and the table below gives the principal ones. Three features of the pattern deserve emphasis. First, funding by subscription: the Egypt Exploration Fund (1882, later Society) was founded by the novelist and traveller Amelia Edwards, whose bestselling A Thousand Miles up the Nile (1877) had made her the mania's most effective recruiter, and it paid for excavation with the subscriptions of an interested public, distributing finds under partage to the museums of its subscribers' countries. The mania, organised, became the discipline's paymaster. Second, permanent institutes in Cairo: the French institute (founded 1880) and its German, and later other national, counterparts put scholars in Egypt year-round, with libraries, journals, and a presence that outlasted any expedition. Third, Egyptian institutional growth in parallel: Cairo University (founded 1908) built Egyptology teaching in Egypt itself, and the first Egyptian professor of the subject, Selim Hassan, belongs to §XV.

XIV. The Hands That Dug — Reis, Quftis, and the Unnamed Workforce

Every excavation in this article was physically performed by Egyptians. A large dig of the heroic age employed hundreds of men and boys, organised in gangs under a reis (foreman), who hired, paid, disciplined, and, crucially, recognised: the reis's eye for a change in the soil, a mudbrick line, a sealed doorway, was the excavation's primary instrument, and directors who kept honest diaries say so. The most famous workforce of all came from Quft (ancient Coptos) in Upper Egypt, where Petrie, from the 1890s, trained local men in his recording methods; the Quftis, as they came to be known across the discipline, were hired season after season by British, American, French, and German expeditions alike, carried the craft from site to site, and trained their own sons and nephews into it. Families of Quft, and of other digging villages, hold excavation as a hereditary skilled trade, and modern scholarship on the Quftis, including oral-history work with their descendants, has begun to restore individual careers to a record that mostly preserved only the directors'.

The single most consequential moment of the heroic age ran through this workforce. The first step of Tutankhamun's staircase was uncovered on 4 November 1922 by Carter's Egyptian workmen clearing below the tomb of Ramesses VI (§XVI). The story that the step was found by a water boy, Hussein Abd el-Rassul, stumbling or pouring water, is a later tradition, attached to a real Qufti-connected family of Luxor (the Abd el-Rassul family, famous in the record since the royal mummies cache of 1881), and is given here at that grade: the workmen found the step; the named boy is the legend's way of giving the workforce a face, and the honest account gives the workforce its collective credit instead. Reis Ahmed Gerigar, Carter's senior foreman, is among the Egyptian staff named in the excavation records, and the Griffith Institute's archive preserves the names of the reises and staff on whom the clearance depended. Where this article can name, it names; where the period record gives only "the men," the article says so, because that anonymity is itself one of the facts the history has to carry.

THE ROYAL CACHE AND THE ABD EL-RASSUL FAMILY (1881)

The Deir el-Bahri royal cache (TT320), in which priests of the 21st Dynasty had hidden the mummies of Egypt's greatest kings, came to official knowledge in 1881 after objects from it surfaced on the Luxor market; the Abd el-Rassul family of Qurna had known of and drawn on the tomb for years. The Antiquities Service's recovery of the cache, conducted by Emil Brugsch with Egyptian officials, and the transport of the royal mummies to Cairo are documented; so is the family's role, as finders, concealers, and eventually informants. The episode is the workforce history in parable: local knowledge found what the Service had not, the market and the museum divided the proceeds of that knowledge, and the official record long credited the recovery to everyone except the men who had known the tomb was there.

XV. Egyptian Scholars in Their Own Discipline — Kamal, Hassan, Fakhry, Habachi

Egyptian Egyptology as scholarship begins, on the documentary record, with Ahmed Kamal (1851–1923), conventionally described as the first native Egyptian Egyptologist. Trained in the school Brugsch directed in Cairo, Kamal worked for the Antiquities Service and the Cairo museum, published extensively in Arabic and French (including catalogues of the Cairo collection and studies of ancient Egyptian language and religion), and taught Egyptology to Egyptian students, among them the generation that followed. His career also documents the gate of §XIII: advancement within the Service and museum was repeatedly blocked in ways his biographers attribute to the European monopoly of senior posts, and his major recognition came late and largely posthumously. He is the proof that the absence of Egyptian names from the heroic age's title pages is an artefact of institutions, not of talent.

The generation Kamal taught took the field. Selim Hassan (1886–1961), who joined the Egyptian Museum in 1921, studied in Paris, and became the first Egyptian professor of Egyptology at Cairo University, conducted the great Giza excavations of the 1930s (his multi-volume Excavations at Giza remains a standard work) and wrote, in Arabic, a multi-volume history of ancient Egypt for Egyptian readers. Ahmed Fakhry (1905–1973), trained at Cairo, Berlin, Brussels, and Liverpool, served as chief inspector across Egypt and became the discipline's great desert scholar, surveying and excavating the oases of the Western Desert (Bahariya, Siwa, Dakhla) over decades. Labib Habachi (1906–1984), chief inspector of Upper Egypt, excavated and published across the south (Elephantine, Nubia, the Heqaib sanctuary at Aswan) and worked on the Nubian salvage of §XXIV. Zakaria Ghoneim discovered the unfinished pyramid of Sekhemkhet at Saqqara in 1951; Kamal el-Mallakh, an architect-turned-archaeologist and journalist, found the great solar boat of Khufu beside the Great Pyramid in 1954, whose excavation and restoration were led by the master restorer Hag Ahmed Youssef, who had begun as a restorer with Reisner's expedition and became the Egyptian Museum's indispensable conservator of wood. The list is a selection, and its point is structural: by the mid-20th century the discipline in Egypt was Egyptian, and the international field's senior figures increasingly included the Cairo-trained.

XVI. Tutankhamun as Archaeology — KV62, 1922–1932

The tomb of Tutankhamun (KV62, Valley of the Kings) was found by an excavation that was, methodologically, the heroic age's mature product, and its clearance, not its discovery day, is its archaeological significance. Howard Carter (1874–1939), trained under Petrie and seasoned as an Antiquities Service inspector and as excavator for American and British patrons, had worked the Valley since 1917 for George Herbert, 5th Earl of Carnarvon, under a concession whose finds, by the post-war rules, were expected to be divided, intact royal burials excepted and reserved to Cairo. On 4 November 1922 Carter's Egyptian workmen uncovered a cut step below the entrance of Ramesses VI's tomb (§XIV); a staircase and a sealed, stamped doorway followed; Carnarvon was summoned from England; and on 26 November Carter made the small breach in the second sealed doorway and looked, by candlelight, into the antechamber.

The famous exchange of that moment is given here under §I's quotation rule. In Carter's published account, The Tomb of Tut·ankh·Amen (1923, with A. C. Mace), Carnarvon asks whether he can see anything and Carter answers, in the published wording, "Yes, wonderful things." Carter's excavation journal for the day, preserved by the Griffith Institute and published in its online edition of the diaries, records the reply in the shorter form, "Yes, it is wonderful." Both texts are Carter's; the published version is a literary expansion of the journal, and quotation of the moment should name which Carter is being quoted. The distinction is small and it is exactly the kind this archive exists to keep.

What followed was a decade of work that set the standard for excavation recording. The tomb's four small rooms held more than five thousand objects, many fragile beyond precedent; Carter's team, including the engineer-archaeologist Arthur Callender, the Metropolitan Museum's Egyptian expedition staff seconded to the work (among them the photographer Harry Burton, whose glass-plate record is the tomb's visual archive, and the epigrapher Alan Gardiner in an advisory role), and the chemist Alfred Lucas, who ran an on-site conservation laboratory in a neighbouring tomb, cleared, conserved, photographed, drew, and card-indexed every object in situ before removal. The burial chamber was formally opened on 17 February 1923; the sarcophagus and coffins were opened across 1923–1926; the king's mummy was examined in November 1925; clearance was completed in 1932. The full record, Carter's diaries, object cards, and Burton's photographs, is held by the Griffith Institute, Oxford, and its online publication is the primary source for everything in this section and the next two.

Politically, the tomb landed in a transformed Egypt. Egypt had been declared formally independent in February 1922, nationalist pressure on foreign privilege was intense, and the Antiquities Service, under its director-general Pierre Lacau, asserted the position that an intact royal tomb belonged entire to Egypt. Carter's relations with the Service broke down repeatedly; in 1924, after a dispute over access (including the exclusion of Egyptian officials' and excavators' wives from a viewing, and the Service's tightening of visitor control), Carter closed the tomb and litigation followed, the work resuming in 1925 under an agreement that surrendered any Carnarvon-estate claim to the finds. The outcome, revolutionary by the standards of §VIII, was that everything stayed: the complete tomb group went to the Cairo museum, and partage, for a discovery of the first rank, was dead. The discipline's centre of legal gravity had shifted, in one tomb, from the excavator's contract to the source nation's law. §XXV draws the line from here to the present.

XVII. Tutankhamun as Media Event — The Times Exclusive and the Press Pack

KV62 was the first great archaeological discovery of the mass-media age, and it was managed, and mismanaged, as one. In January 1923 Carnarvon sold the exclusive newspaper rights in the discovery to The Times of London, for a reported £5,000 down and a majority share of syndication proceeds. The commercial logic was Carnarvon's; the consequences were the story's. Every other newspaper in the world, the Egyptian press included, on Egyptian soil, had to buy its Tutankhamun news second-hand from a London rival, hours late, or find something else to print. The excluded correspondents, camped at Luxor with deadlines and no access, found something else: atmosphere, omens, interviews with anyone who would talk, and the services of novelists and occultists as instant experts. The University of Manchester's account of the curse's birth, drawing on Joyce Tyldesley's Tutankhamen's Curse (2012), states the mechanism plainly: an exclusive media deal, plus reliance on non-expert comment, fuelled the rumours that followed. §XVIII adjudicates the product; this section records the factory.

The media event also had a mass visual culture. Burton's photographs, syndicated worldwide, made the tomb's golden furniture the most recognisable archaeological imagery ever published; newsreel cameras attended the removals; the slow, object-by-object clearance, season after season for a decade, gave the press a serial story with a golden cliffhanger every winter. Egypt responded in kind and on its own behalf: the discovery was a national event in the Egyptian press, folded immediately into the Pharaonism of §IX, and the Egyptian government's assertion of ownership (§XVI) was conducted partly through the newspapers, Egyptian and foreign, as a sovereignty claim the whole country could follow. Tutankhamun is thus the first case where all of this article's threads meet at once: excavation method, museum politics, national identity, commercial media, and popular fashion (§XXI), in a single four-room tomb.

XVIII. The 'Curse of Tutankhamun' Adjudicated — A Media Construction on the Record

The curse is this article's largest single exercise in §I's grading, because unusually, and delightfully, the evidence permits the legend's manufacture to be watched almost day by day. The documented spine is short. Carnarvon, already in poor health since a serious car accident years earlier, cut his cheek shaving in March 1923; the cut, reportedly from a mosquito bite site, became infected; blood poisoning and pneumonia followed; and he died in Cairo on 5 April 1923, some seven weeks after the burial chamber's opening. Every element of that sentence is documented. Everything that makes it a curse was added in print.

The addition can be dated. The popular novelist Marie Corelli, writing as Carnarvon fell ill, published a warning, quoted in the Manchester account from the press coverage of 24 March 1923, that she could not but think some risks were run by breaking into the last resting place of an Egyptian king. Arthur Conan Doyle, creator of Sherlock Holmes and by then a committed spiritualist, was among the commentators the excluded press consulted, and spoke of malign influences an Egyptologist might disturb. When Carnarvon died days later, Corelli was hailed as a prophet, and the phrase circulated in her name, that death comes on wings to whoever enters a pharaoh's tomb, was rapidly received as a translation of an inscription over the tomb's entrance. No such inscription exists. No curse text of any kind was found in KV62; Carter's records contain none; and the tomb's actual inscriptions are of other kinds entirely. Carter himself, in print in 1927, dismissed the curse stories with open contempt. The reports that Cairo's lights failed at the moment of Carnarvon's death, and that his dog at Highclere howled and died at the same instant, are graded reported: they appeared in the press of the moment; power cuts in Cairo were commonplace, and the dog, on the chronology Tyldesley sets out, died hours later by the clock difference, not simultaneously.

Ancient Egyptian tombs never carried curse formulae. The legend borrowed a real genre and planted it where it does not occur.

The curse's afterlife is cultural, not Egyptological: it fed the mummy fiction of §XXII, it still sells documentaries, and it taught the discipline a permanent lesson about access. A closed tomb and an exclusive contract created an information vacuum, and the vacuum filled with the supernatural, because the supernatural was the one Tutankhamun story the excluded press could publish without The Times's permission. Modern excavation media policy, open days, live blogs, and institutional social media among it, is the curse's unwitting legacy: feed the record, or the legend will feed itself.

XIX. Egyptomania, First Modern Wave — Empire Style, the Egyptian Hall, Cemeteries and Obelisks

Egyptomania did not begin in 1798 (Rome had re-erected obelisks and built Egyptianising monuments for two millennia, and the Renaissance and Baroque had their own Egyptian revivals, traced in the standard reception scholarship of James Stevens Curl and Jean-Marcel Humbert and his collaborators, on which this and the following sections draw for their framework). What 1798 began was the first documented wave: a revival fed by measured drawings, so that its pyramids have the right slope and its pylons the right batter, and powered by the Description and Denon's Voyage as pattern sources (§III). In France the wave rode the Empire style: Egyptian motifs in the decorative arts of Napoleon's court, sphinxes and hieroglyphic ornament on furniture, silver, and porcelain, the emperor's Egyptian campaign commemorated in the visual language of the country he had failed to keep.

In Britain the wave's landmark was commercial: William Bullock's Egyptian Hall in Piccadilly, London (1812), a museum and exhibition building fronted by a full Egyptianising facade of pylons, cavetto cornice, and colossal figures, which housed, among much else, Belzoni's 1821 exhibition of the Seti I tomb facsimiles (§VI), the moment when excavation and entertainment publicly merged. Through the century the Egyptian manner settled where its associations fitted: cemeteries, where Egypt meant permanence against decay (the Egyptian Avenue at Highgate Cemetery, London, 1839, with its pylon entrance and lotus columns, is the type specimen; Egyptianising gateways and obelisk monuments filled the new garden cemeteries of Britain and America); prisons, courthouses, and medical schools, where it meant sublime, deterrent permanence; synagogues, churches, factories, and suspension-bridge pylons, where engineers and architects used its massiveness to reassure users of new technologies; and freemasonry's Egyptian rites, whose separate history is told in Esoteric Orders.

The wave's most literal monuments were ancient ones, moved. Paris received a Luxor obelisk in the Place de la Concorde in 1836, a gift of Muhammad Ali transported and erected as a feat of French engineering. London and New York followed with the pair of Heliopolis obelisks long miscalled Cleopatra's Needles (they are obelisks of Thutmose III, later inscribed by Ramesses II, and have nothing to do with Cleopatra beyond their later Alexandrian address): London's was erected on the Thames Embankment in 1878 after a transport voyage that nearly sank it, New York's in Central Park in 1881. The obelisk gifts were diplomacy in stone, Egypt spending its antiquities for international standing (§IX's pattern again), and the receiving cities understood them exactly so. Washington's great obelisk monument (built 1848–1884) is the American, home-made counterpart: the Egyptian form, by then, was simply what a republic built when it wanted eternity.

XX. Egyptomania, Second Wave — Suez, Aida, and the Exhibition Century

The century's second wave was driven by access. Steam navigation and Cook's tours put Egypt within a middle-class itinerary; the Suez Canal, opened in 1869 under Ferdinand de Lesseps (Mariette's patron and the Antiquities Service's political protector), made Egypt a corridor of world trade and a fixture of the illustrated press; and the great international exhibitions built Egyptian courts and temple facades for crowds in the millions. Photography joined the wave early: from the 1850s, travelling photographers (Francis Frith's Egyptian views of 1856–1859 are the famous English case) sold the monuments to armchair subscribers, fixing the standard views, Sphinx before pyramid, colossi at dawn, that postcards still repeat.

The wave's supreme cultural artefact is an opera. Aida, commissioned by the Khedive Ismail for his new Cairo Opera House, with a scenario developed from a sketch by Mariette (who also supervised the historical accuracy of the designs), music by Giuseppe Verdi, and libretto by Antonio Ghislanzoni, was premiered in Cairo on 24 December 1871. The popular claim that Aida was composed for the opening of the Suez Canal is a legend of the tidy kind: the canal opened in 1869, the opera house opened that year with Rigoletto, and Aida's premiere, delayed by the Franco-Prussian War's trapping of its scenery and costumes in besieged Paris, came two years later. The documented commission is grand enough without the canal: a khedive buying, from Europe's greatest living composer, an Egyptian epic for an Egyptian stage, with Egyptology's own director vetting the antiquities. Pharaonism (§IX) and Egyptomania meet in the production's very payroll.

Tourism industrialised the encounter. Thomas Cook's Egyptian tours (from 1869), the great Nile hotels, the dragoman system, and the winter season at Luxor and Aswan created the social world in which Carnarvon held his concession and Carter his winters; Amelia Edwards's A Thousand Miles up the Nile (1877), the travel book that founded the Egypt Exploration Fund (§XIII), is this world's best document, a tourist's narrative that turns, deliberately, into a preservationist manifesto as its author watches monuments damaged and objects sold. The second wave thus funded the third act of the discipline's history: without the tourists, subscribers, and readers the mania produced, the institutions of §XIII have no income. The dependency was not always comfortable, and §XXIII records its modern form.

XXI. Tutmania — The 1920s Wave in Design, Fashion, and Architecture

The discovery of 1922 detonated the largest Egyptomania wave of all, and the first to be fully mass-produced. "Tutmania" (the word is contemporary in spirit; the press of 1923 already wrote of a Tutankhamun craze) ran through every register of design. In the decorative arts it fused with the emerging Art Deco: the winged scarab, the lotus, the sun-disc, and the stepped pyramid entered jewellery, furniture, and graphics in the stylised geometry the new style wanted, and the great Paris houses produced Egyptianising jewels whose makers studied museum pieces and, in Cartier's case, incorporated genuine ancient elements such as faience fragments into modern mounts, a practice the firm's own histories document and modern taste judges variously. Fashion took the tomb's colours (gold, turquoise, lapis) and its silhouettes; popular song and dance took its name; advertising took everything.

Architecture's monuments of the wave are datable and, mostly, still standing. Grauman's Egyptian Theatre in Hollywood, Sid Grauman's great Egyptianising cinema palace, opened on 18 October 1922 with the premiere of Robin Hood, which is to say weeks before Carter found the first step: the theatre is not a response to Tutankhamun but proof that the wave was already breaking, fed by the post-war Egyptian revival in design, when the tomb arrived to give it a king's face. The distinction matters to this article's method: Tutmania amplified an existing current, in cinema architecture above all, rather than creating one from nothing. Egyptian theatres multiplied across America in the 1920s; Egyptianising apartment houses, garages, and factories followed, of which New York's Fred F. French Building (1927), with its Mesopotamian-and-Egyptianising ornament, is a frequently cited survivor. In Britain the wave's most argued-over monument is the Carreras cigarette factory in Camden, London (1926–1928), fronted by black cats for the goddess Bastet and a full Egyptianising facade, a tobacco company's temple to the sales the craze generated.

Three multipliers were new. Photography and newsreel made the discovery visual everywhere within weeks (§XVII). The objects themselves were jewellery-scale, golden, and photographable, where the earlier waves had traded in colossi and architecture. And the story had a narrative shape the earlier discipline never offered: a sealed door, a boy king, a dead earl, and (§XVIII) a curse. Egyptomania's waves track the media systems of their centuries as faithfully as they track the discoveries: print in 1809, photography and the exhibition in 1869, wire copy and cinema in 1922, television in 1972 (§XXIII), and the internet in the digital present (§XXVI).

XXII. Egypt on Screen — Mummies, Cleopatras, and Epic Spectacle

Cinema inherited Egyptomania whole and added its two most durable inventions: the walking mummy and the screen Cleopatra. Cleopatra came first and often: a one-reel Cléopâtre in 1899, Theda Bara's Cleopatra of 1917 (now lost, and legendary partly for that), Claudette Colbert's DeMille Cleopatra of 1934, and the vast Elizabeth Taylor Cleopatra of 1963, whose cost overruns and publicity made it a media event in the Tutankhamun pattern, the production's own legend outrunning its film. Cecil B. DeMille filmed The Ten Commandments twice, in 1923 and definitively in 1956, building Egyptian sets at a scale that blurred into archaeology's public image of itself. The Egyptian subject in cinema is rarely about Egyptology; it is about the two things Egyptomania always sold, antiquity's glamour and antiquity's danger, and the queen and the Exodus are its twin vehicles.

The danger half crystallised in The Mummy (1932), Universal's horror film with Boris Karloff as the revived priest Imhotep, directed by Karl Freund. The film is the curse of §XVIII converted to plot: an expedition, a forbidden text (the Scroll of Thoth), a reading aloud, a revival, and deaths among the excavators, released ten years after KV62 to an audience that had read the curse into being in its newspapers. Its sequels and remakes (the 1999 The Mummy and its franchise among them) repeat the structure with better effects and the same Egyptology: the discipline appears in these films as a profession that exists to open what should stay shut, a public image real Egyptologists have spent a century alternately exploiting and deploring. Between the poles stand the honourable exceptions and the pure spectacles, Land of the Pharaohs (1955), the Polish Pharaoh (1966), after Prus's novel, Stargate (1994), which built a franchise on Egyptology-flavoured science fiction, and the animated The Prince of Egypt (1998), and the honest summary is that cinema's Egypt is a reception document, to be studied as this article studies the Egyptian Hall: as evidence about the audience, not about the antiquity.

XXIII. Later Waves — Blockbuster Exhibitions, the Louvre Pyramid, and Las Vegas

The later 20th century converted Egyptomania into an institution of its own: the touring blockbuster exhibition. The type specimen is The Treasures of Tutankhamun at the British Museum in 1972, the tomb's 50th anniversary, which drew some 1.6 million visitors, a British museum record of its era, with queues as part of the spectacle; the subsequent American tour of 1976–1979 drew millions more across seven cities and fixed "Tut" as a household name in the United States (Steve Martin's novelty hit "King Tut" of 1978 is the wave's pop fossil). Egypt lent the objects as a sovereign state, for fees and diplomacy, the exact inversion of 1823: the source nation now rented its antiquities to the museums that had once simply taken them, and the lending exhibitions became a funding stream for Egyptian archaeology and museums in their own right. Later Tutankhamun tours, and the great loan exhibitions of the 2000s and 2010s, ran on the model 1972 proved.

Architecture closed the century as it had opened it, with a pyramid. I. M. Pei's glass Pyramid at the Louvre, inaugurated in 1989 as the museum's new entrance, set the Egyptian form, abstracted to pure geometry, at the ceremonial centre of the museum Champollion had curated, above the Egyptian galleries themselves; the controversy it provoked (a pyramid, in the Cour Napoléon, was denounced as a pharaonic intrusion on a French palace) is itself a document of the form's undiminished charge. Las Vegas answered with the Luxor Hotel and Casino (opened 1993), a full-scale hollow pyramid with sphinx and obelisk, Egyptomania as total environment and gaming floor, Curl's "recurring theme in the history of taste" running, by then, on a casino's balance sheet. Between Pei and the Luxor the whole argument of this article is visible at a glance: the same form, one culture's high-modernist shrine and another's themed resort, both unintelligible without 1798.

A soberer late wave deserves its sentence: the exhibition-and-repatriation era's scholarly shows, of which Egyptomania: Egypt in Western Art 1730–1930 (Paris, Ottawa, and Vienna, 1994, catalogue edited by Jean-Marcel Humbert and collaborators) is the landmark, in which the museums turned the reception itself into the exhibit, displaying the sphinx clocks and Egyptian Hall bills beside the antiquities that inspired them. The discipline had begun, formally, to curate its own mania. §XXVIII lists the scholarship that made that possible.

XXIV. The UNESCO Nubian Campaign (1960–1980) — Rescue at Continental Scale

The discipline's largest collective act was a rescue. Egypt's Aswan High Dam, begun in 1960, would impound Lake Nasser across some 500 kilometres of Nubia, submerging the entire archaeological landscape of the Egyptian and Sudanese Nile between the First Cataract and the Sudan, temples, fortresses, churches, cemeteries, and rock inscriptions of five millennia. In 1959 the governments of Egypt and Sudan appealed to UNESCO; on 8 March 1960 Director-General Vittorino Veronese launched the International Campaign to Save the Monuments of Nubia. UNESCO's own history of the campaign gives the totals: twenty years of work (1960–1980), twenty-two monuments and complexes saved, forty technical missions from five continents, a cost of some US$80 million, half of it contributed by around fifty countries. It was the founding demonstration of the idea, codified in the World Heritage Convention of 1972 that the campaign directly inspired, that some heritage belongs to everyone and may claim everyone's help.

The engineering centrepiece was Abu Simbel (§VI's sand-cleared temple, now to be water-cleared). From 1964 the two rock temples of Ramesses II were sawn, block by numbered block, more than a thousand blocks of up to thirty tons, lifted above the rising lake, and reassembled sixty-four metres higher and one hundred eighty metres inland inside an artificial cliff built to reproduce the original setting, the reassembled temples inaugurated on 22 September 1968. Philae, the island sanctuary of Isis, was dismantled and rebuilt on the higher island of Agilkia between 1972 and 1980. Other temples were given by Egypt in gratitude to the nations that had led their rescue, and stand now in Madrid (Debod), Leiden (Taffeh), Turin (Ellesyia), and New York, where the Temple of Dendur, installed in the Metropolitan Museum's Sackler Wing in 1978, is the campaign's most visited monument and a permanent, arguable emblem of its ethics: a gift freely given by the source state, and a temple in a museum nine time zones from its river (see Temple Architecture for the buildings themselves, and Sacred Geography for what a temple's place meant).

The honest account holds the campaign's shadow beside its triumph, as UNESCO's own later assessments do. The salvage excavations, dozens of missions racing the water, recorded an enormous amount and still lost more: most of Nubia's archaeology went under unsurveyed, and what was saved was selected, overwhelmingly, for monumentality, temples over villages, pharaonic over later remains, though the campaign's church and settlement work partly corrects the picture. And the lake displaced the living: on the order of one hundred thousand Egyptian and Sudanese Nubians were resettled from their ancestral riverbank, a human cost that the monument-centred record of the 1960s treated as background and that Nubian writers and later scholarship have restored to the centre of the story. The campaign saved Abu Simbel and drowned Nubia; both clauses are its history, and the World Heritage system it founded inherited both the method and the warning.

XXV. The Ethics Thread — From Firman to Repatriation Claim

This article has carried an ethics thread from §V onward, deliberately: the history of Egyptology cannot be told as a sequence of discoveries with the removals left in the footnotes, because the removals were much of the history. The thread's stations, as documented in the sections above, are these. A firman system under which consuls collected for foreign museums (§V), legally authorised and archaeologically destructive. An Egyptian state that legislated protection in 1835 and gave its own collection away in the 1850s (§IX). A state monopoly in 1858 that kept finds in Egypt under a French director (§X). A partage system that divided licensed finds between Cairo and the excavator's museum (§VIII), legal in its day and the mechanism by which much of the world's Egyptian holdings arrived where they are. A hardening of Egyptian law (state ownership asserted in 1912; the Tutankhamun precedent of 1922–1925 that intact finds of the first rank stayed entire, §XVI) and its completion in Law 117 of 1983, under which antiquities are state property and export is prohibited. And a present in which Egypt formally requests the return of specific, named objects, the Rosetta Stone and the bust of Nefertiti chief among them, while the holding museums cite their statutes, their conservation records, and the universal-museum argument in reply.

The archive's dedicated treatment, Repatriation Ethics, argues the cases object by object, and this article does not duplicate it. What this article adds is the historical observation its narrative has earned: every stage of the thread was, in its own time, defended as the reasonable settlement, and every stage was renegotiated when the balance of power and of professional conscience shifted. The firman was reasonable to the consuls; partage was reasonable to Maspero and Petrie; the 1925 settlement was unreasonable, briefly, to Carter. A history that understands this does not thereby excuse any stage of it. It does something more useful: it shows that the present settlement, whatever it is, is also a stage, and that the discipline's own archive, the registers, firmans, division lists, and sale catalogues preserved in the museums themselves, is the evidence base from which the next renegotiation will be argued. Keeping those records, and publishing them, is the part of the ethics work that is already, unambiguously, the museums' job.

XXVI. The Digital Turn — A Pointer

The discipline's present transformation, digital recording, open-access archives, 3D documentation of threatened sites, and the online publication of the great excavation archives (the Griffith Institute's Tutankhamun records, used throughout §§XVI–XVIII, are the type case), is treated in full in the archive's dedicated article, Digital Scholarship, and is only pointed to here, as this article's scope requires. Two sentences of connection are nonetheless earned by the narrative. First, the digital turn is the latest form of the pattern of §III: a new medium of reproduction (the engraved plate, the photograph, the newsreel, the database) has again changed simultaneously what scholarship can do and what the public can see, and the mania and the discipline are again reading the same publication two ways. Second, the digital archive partly answers §XIV's grievance: excavation records online name the reises, staff, and workforces whom print publication anonymised, and the restoration of those names to the searchable record is digital Egyptology's quietest and most honourable achievement.
