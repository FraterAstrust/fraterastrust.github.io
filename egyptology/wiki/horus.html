I. The Name — Ḥr and the Distant One

Horus is among the oldest gods of Egypt whose name can still be read. His Egyptian name is ḥr [Gardiner: G5], written with a single sign: the falcon on its perch, [𓅃]. The sign is a logogram — the picture is the word — and the V2 transliterator of this archive accordingly gives G5 the whole-word value Ḥr. In fuller writings the name appears as ḥr.w, the final w marking an old ending that later fell silent. Egyptologists reconstruct an early pronunciation close to Ḥāru; by Coptic times the name had become Hōr, and Greek writers rendered it Hōros — the form that survives in English as Horus. The god is therefore, before anything else, simply the Falcon.

What the name means beyond "falcon" is debated, and the debate matters because each proposed meaning describes a different god. The most widely repeated derivation connects ḥr [Gardiner: G5] with the root ḥr in the sense of distance and height: Horus is "the Distant One" or "the One Who Is Above" — the falcon seen from below, a speck circling at the limit of vision, untouchable and overseeing everything. On this reading the name is a description of the sky itself as experienced from the ground: remote, elevated, and all-seeing. A second line of interpretation treats the name as primary and unanalysable — the falcon god's proper name, no more to be translated than the name of any person. The archive presents the "Distant One" derivation as the leading scholarly proposal, not as a settled fact: the Egyptians themselves left no etymological dictionary, and their own scribes were happy to exploit the name's sounds rather than explain them.

Those sounds were productive. Egyptian is rich in ḥr-words, and temple theology played on them deliberately. The word for "face" and the preposition "upon" are both written ḥr [Gardiner: D2] [𓁷] — a different sign, the human face in profile, with the same consonants. The falcon god is thus, by a pun no Egyptian scribe would have missed, the one who is upon all things and whose face is the sky. The word for "day," hrw [Gardiner: N7] [𓇵], belongs to the same sound-world: the day is the period of Horus's visible rule. Such wordplay — the raw material of the archive's study of phonology and voice and of the power of names — is not decoration in Egyptian theology. It is one of the ways theology was done.

Horus was also known by place. As the falcon of Nekhen (Hierakonpolis), the predynastic capital of Upper Egypt whose Greek name literally means "Falcon City," he was the patron of the earliest kings — a local Horus who became, as Nekhen's rulers became kings of all Egypt, a national one. Other towns kept their own falcon gods, many of them gradually identified with Horus or distinguished from him only by a local epithet: Horus of Mesen the harpooner at Edfu, Horus of Letopolis, Horus of Hebenu. The pattern is characteristic of the Egyptian pantheon: one great name, many local faces, and no central authority ever insisting that the faces be reconciled.

The name outlived the temples. It survives compounded in personal names borne by ordinary Egyptians for three thousand years — Harsiese, "Horus, son of Isis," is only the most transparent — and in the Greek form Hōros it passed into the vocabularies of Hermeticism and Gnosticism, where "Horos" could even detach from the falcon entirely and name a boundary or limit. Section XIV returns to that strange afterlife. The falcon on the perch, however, never changed: from the labels of Dynasty 0 to the last Ptolemaic temple walls, [𓅃] means Horus.

II. Two Horuses — the Elder and the Son of Isis

The single most important fact for understanding Horus is that Egyptian theology itself distinguishes two of him. Modern handbooks sometimes present this as a scholarly tidying-up of a confused tradition. It is not: the distinction is ancient, explicit, and load-bearing, because the two Horuses have different parents, different myths, and different jobs.

Horus the Elder — Ḥr-wr

Ḥr-wr [Gardiner: G5-G36] [𓅃𓅨] — "Horus the Great," conventionally "Horus the Elder," Greek Haroeris — is the original sky falcon. In the Heliopolitan genealogy of the Ennead, he belongs to the generation of the children of Geb and Nut: brother of Osiris, Isis, Seth, and Nephthys. The Ennead itself is written psḏt [Gardiner: N9-X1] [𓇷𓏏], "the Nine," and Horus the Elder stands inside it as a cosmic power in his own right, not as anyone's heir. His consort in several local theologies is Hathor; his great cult centre in later periods is Kom Ombo, where he is worshipped as Haroeris alongside the crocodile god Sobek (Section XII). The Elder is the Horus of the sky: his face is the heavens, his eyes the sun and moon (Section IV), and his wings the span of the firmament written pt [Gardiner: N1] [𓇯].

Crucially, the Elder is also the elder brother in the first version of the conflict with Seth. Before the Osiris myth fully crystallised, the rivalry of Horus and Seth appears to have been a duel of near-equals — sky against storm, north against south — rather than an avenger's quest. Traces of that older, more balanced conflict survive in the dual kingship symbolism discussed in Section VII, where one early king could place Horus and Seth together above his name.

Horus, Son of Isis — Ḥr-sꜣ-ꜣst

The second Horus is Ḥr-sꜣ-ꜣst [Gardiner: G5-G38-G1-S29-X1] [𓅃𓅬𓄿𓋴𓏏] — "Horus, son of Isis," Greek Harsiesis (from the Egyptian name itself: Ḥr + sꜣ [Gardiner: G38] [𓅬], "son," + ꜣst [Gardiner: G1-S29-X1] [𓄿𓋴𓏏], "Isis"). He is the child of the Osiris myth: conceived by Isis from the revived body of the murdered Osiris — in Egyptian terms, wsir [Gardiner: Q1-D4-A40] [𓊨𓁹𓀭] — hidden by his mother in the Delta marshes at Khemmis, stung and healed in infancy, and raised in secret until he was old enough to claim his father's throne. His defining epithet states his mission in four signs: Ḥr-nḏ-it.f [Gardiner: G5-Aa27-U10-I9] [𓅃𓐩𓌾𓆑], "Horus, Avenger of His Father" — nḏ [Gardiner: Aa27], "to protect, to avenge," upon it [Gardiner: U10] [𓌾], "father." Where the Elder is the sky, the Son acts in the world: he sues, fights, is wounded, is healed, and finally reigns. He is the Horus of narrative.

The two were never kept in watertight compartments. Egyptian theology preferred accumulation to system: a hymn could praise the cosmic falcon whose eyes are the luminaries and in the next breath call him Isis's child, and no priest experienced this as a contradiction to be solved. At Edfu the temple's god is simultaneously Horus of Behdet, an Elder-type solar falcon, and the son who triumphs over his father's murderer. The honest way to state the position — and the position this archive takes — is that "Horus" names a cluster: a sky-falcon god of great antiquity, an Osirian son generated by the funerary myth, and a royal office into which every living king stepped. The sections that follow keep the strands distinct where the sources do, and say so where they merge.

HORUS THE ELDER (HAROERIS)

Ḥr-wr [Gardiner: G5-G36]. Son of Geb and Nut; brother of Osiris, Isis, Seth and Nephthys within the Ennead. Primordial sky god; his eyes are the sun and moon. Consort of Hathor in local theologies; lord of Kom Ombo with Sobek. The Horus of cosmic order rather than of story.

HORUS, SON OF ISIS (HARSIESIS)

Ḥr-sꜣ-ꜣst [Gardiner: G5-G38-G1-S29-X1]. Posthumous son of Osiris and Isis, hidden at Khemmis, healed of the scorpion's sting, plaintiff and combatant against Seth, and finally king. The Horus of the Osiris myth, of the judgment hall, and of every royal succession.

THE CHILD (HARPOCRATES)

Ḥr-pꜣ-ẖrd [Gardiner: G5-Q3-G1-A18], "Horus the Child," is the young Son of Isis before his vindication: finger to mouth, sidelock of youth, standing on the crocodiles of the healing cippi. Section VIII is devoted to him.

A third axis of distinction is generational mathematics. Egyptian lists of gods could speak of Horus as the last of the divine kings — the god-king who succeeded Osiris on earth and then handed kingship to human rulers, each of whom was Horus in turn. This is why the same god can be older than Osiris's myth and younger than Osiris himself: as office, Horus is the eternal successor; as Elder, he precedes the succession; as Son, he enacts it. Kingship theology (Section VII) runs on exactly this productive ambiguity.

III. The Falcon — Iconography and the God's Forms

Horus is a falcon, a falcon-headed man, a child, a winged disk, and — in one of his most majestic forms — a falcon standing protectively behind or above a king who is himself Horus. The falcon is not a costume. Egyptian artists, as the Metropolitan Museum's educators put it, composed the image of Horus from the properties of the peregrine falcon and the lanner falcon and other falcon species, rather than portraiture of a single species: the lanner, still nesting in the limestone cliffs at the desert margin, supplied much of the bird's familiar profile — the bold dark malar stripe running down from the eye, the sleek head, the long wings — while the peregrine contributed the aura of the fastest creature on earth, stooping from the height of heaven onto its prey. What mattered theologically was the falcon's behaviour as seen from Egypt: it lives in the sky, it sees farther than any other creature, it strikes without warning, and it returns to a perch above the world. Every one of those traits becomes a doctrine somewhere in this article.

In his full animal form Horus stands on a perch or standard, often wearing a crown. The double crown — sḫmty [Gardiner: S5] [𓋖], the "Two Powerful Ones," combining the White Crown ḥḏt [Gardiner: S1] [𓋑] of Upper Egypt with the Red Crown dšrt [Gardiner: S4] [𓋕] of Lower Egypt — identifies the falcon as king of the united land. A small faience amulet in the Metropolitan Museum (Third Intermediate Period, c. 1080–664 BCE) shows exactly this: a brilliant blue falcon wearing the double crown, a suspension loop behind its head, the scalloped feather-pattern engraved on its breast. The god could be carried on the body, at a scale of under four centimetres, without losing any of his kingship. For the broader culture of such objects, see Amulets and Talismans.

As a falcon-headed man, Horus wears the same crowns or the double-plumed headdress šwty [Gardiner: S9] [𓋛], and carries the standard furniture of divine kingship: the crook ḥqꜣ [Gardiner: S38] [𓋾], the wꜣs-sceptre [Gardiner: S40] [𓌀] of dominion, and the ꜥnḫ [Gardiner: S34] [𓋹], "life." In this form he walks in processions, receives offerings, presents kings to other gods, and leads the justified dead by the hand (Section XIII). The hybrid body is the Egyptian solution to a theological problem: the god must be recognisably the sky-bird and yet able to act in the human world of hands, doors, boats, and thrones. The colour symbolism of his images — gold flesh for divinity, the black-and-white precision of the falcon's facial markings — follows the same double logic.

THE FALCON

The god entire: perched, standard-borne, or swooping. Cult statues in this form received the daily temple ritual; the living sacred falcon kept at Edfu was crowned annually as the god's embodiment (Section XII).

THE FALCON-HEADED MAN

The god in society: crowned, sceptred, striding among gods and kings. The default form for mythological narrative in relief and papyrus, and for the Horus who conducts the dead to Osiris.

THE CHILD

Nude, sidelocked, finger to mouth: Horus before his vindication, and therefore the form under which he protects other children and heals the stung and bitten (Section VIII).

THE WINGED DISK

Horus of Behdet as pure emblem: sun disk, falcon wings, twin uraei, set over every temple doorway in Egypt (Section X).

THE FALCON BEHIND THE KING

From the Old Kingdom statue of Khafre — the falcon spreading its wings around the king's head from behind the throne — to the Late Period statue of Horus protecting the small standing figure of Nectanebo II between his legs (Metropolitan Museum), the god literally enfolds his human embodiment.

THE SPHINX-BODIED KING-GOD

At Giza the New Kingdom worshipped the Great Sphinx itself as Horus-in-the-Horizon, Ḥr-m-ꜣḫt [Gardiner: G5-G17-N27] (Section XI): the falcon's kingship translated into lion-bodied stone.

Not every falcon in Egyptian art is Horus. Montu, the war god of Thebes, and Sokar, the necropolis god of Memphis, are also falcon-headed, and Ra himself travels with a falcon's head in his solar forms. Identification rests on context, crown, accompanying inscription, and company kept — a discipline the archive applies throughout, and a useful warning against reading any single attribute as a label. What is unique to Horus is the combination: falcon plus kingship plus the Osirian story. Where a falcon wears the double crown, stands on the srḫ [Gardiner: O33] palace-façade (Section VII), or confronts the Seth-animal, the falcon is Horus.

Real falcons were kept, mummified, and buried by the thousand in the animal cults of the Late Period. A hollow bronze falcon in the Metropolitan Museum, X-rayed in modern times, was found to contain the bones of an actual falcon inserted beneath its tail — votive statue and sarcophagus in one. The bird in the cliffs, the god in the temple, and the sign on the page are three registers of a single presence, and Egyptian religion moved between them without friction.

IV. Lord of the Sky — His Eyes as Sun and Moon

The oldest layer of Horus is the sky itself. In the conception attached above all to Horus the Elder, the god's body is the firmament: his face is the heaven, pt [Gardiner: N1] [𓇯]; his outspread wings cross it from horizon to horizon; the stars are the patterning of his speckled plumage; and his two eyes are the two great lights. His right eye is the sun, rꜥ [Gardiner: N5] [𓇳]; his left eye is the moon, iꜥḥ [Gardiner: N11] [𓇹]. The generic word for "eye," irt [Gardiner: D4-X1] [𓁹𓏏], thus becomes a cosmological term: to speak of the Eye of Horus is to speak of a luminary moving in the sky, liable — in the case of the moon — to injury, waning, and restoration. Section V follows that liability into myth.

This is a theology you can watch. The falcon circles at a height where it seems to hang motionless in the blue; the sun and moon cross the same blue on their fixed paths; the identification requires no argument, only looking up. Egyptian cosmology in the archive's survey of the cosmic gods is full of such observed equations, and Horus's is among the most durable: hymns of every period address him as the one whose eyes illumine the Two Lands, and the phrase "Lord of the Sky" — the title this article bears — translates his commonest great epithet, lord of pt [Gardiner: N1].

The horizon is where the sky-god's two eyes do their most important work. The Egyptians wrote "horizon" ꜣḫt [Gardiner: N27] [𓈌] — the place of becoming radiant, from the same root as ꜣḫ [Gardiner: G25] [𓅜], the "transfigured, effective spirit" of the composite soul. The sign itself shows the sun cradled between two hills. Scribes distinguished it carefully from the near-homophone ꜣḥt [Gardiner: G1-V28-X1-N23] [𓄿𓎛𓏏𓈇], "field, arable land": one letter's difference separates the farmer's plot from the doorway of the sun. Horus is pre-eminently a god of the first word. As Ḥr-ꜣḫty [Gardiner: G5-N27-Z4] [𓅃𓈌𓏭], "Horus of the Horizon" — more exactly, of the two horizons, east and west, the dual ending -ty written with y [Gardiner: Z4] [𓏭] — he is the sun at its rising and setting, the falcon at the two gates of the day. Section XI traces his solar forms from this root.

The moon-eye deserves its own paragraph, because it explains the god's healing aspect. The moon visibly changes: it is bitten away night by night and made whole again across the month. A sky-god whose left eye is the moon is therefore a god whose eye is periodically damaged and periodically restored — and the myth of the Eye torn out by Seth and healed by Thoth (Sections V–VI) is, among other things, the narrative form of the lunar cycle, just as the offering of the healed Eye to Osiris is its ritual form. Egyptian mathematics and astronomy tracked the same cycle in tables; the myth and the table are two notations for one observation.

Day itself, hrw [Gardiner: N7] [𓇵], bears his consonants. The brightest star of the southern sky, Sopdet, spdt [Gardiner: M44-X1] [𓇮𓏏] — written with the "sharp, pointed" sign spd [Gardiner: M44] (post-audit value) — belonged not to Horus but to Isis; the sky, like the family, was shared.

A falcon god also governs height as rank. The Egyptian idiom for a god's or king's glorious manifestation is ḫꜥ [Gardiner: N28] [𓈍], "to appear in glory, to be crowned" — the sign shows the sun rising over a hill, and coronation vocabulary is built from it. When the king "appeared" as Horus on the throne, the language said that the sun had risen. The Distant One of Section I is distant the way the sun is distant: not absent, but too high and too bright to approach — and precisely therefore able to see, judge, and give life to everything below.

V. The Wedjat Eye — the Wounded and Healed Eye

If Horus has a single emblem known beyond Egyptology, it is the Wedjat Eye: wḏꜣt [Gardiner: D10] [𓂀]. The sign is itself a small work of art — a human eye with a falcon's markings: the brow line above, the spiral curl and the descending tear-line below, borrowed from the facial plumage of the lanner falcon. The word derives from the verb wḏꜣ [Gardiner: D10] [𓂀], "to be sound, whole, uninjured, prosperous." The wedjat is therefore not "the eye" in general — irt [Gardiner: D4-X1] is the ordinary word — but the Eye in its restored condition: the Sound One, the Whole One. Its name is its biography.

The biography, told across the Pyramid Texts, the Coffin Texts, the Osiris cycle, and the late mythological papyri, runs as follows. In the combat between Horus and Seth — the subject of Section VI — Seth tore out Horus's eye (in some tellings, both eyes; in Plutarch's Greek retelling the injury is one episode among many) and, in the fullest versions, tore it into pieces. Thoth — ḏḥwty [Gardiner: G26] [𓅝], the ibis god of writing, reckoning, and heka — gathered the pieces, spat upon and reassembled them, and returned the eye to Horus whole. Horus then did the act that fixes the wedjat at the centre of Egyptian funerary religion: he gave the healed eye to his dead father. Osiris received it, and was revived by it. The Eye offered to Osiris becomes, in the offering liturgy of every temple and tomb, the prototype of every offering: each gift of bread, beer, incense, or wine presented to a god or a dead person could be called, and depicted as, the Eye of Horus — the thing once broken, now whole, handed over to restore the one who receives it.

Hence the wedjat's career as Egypt's most ubiquitous protective device. Wedjat amulets in blue-green faience, gold, silver, carnelian, and glass were worn by the living and placed in their thousands among mummy wrappings; the British Museum's quadruple wedjat amulet (Third Intermediate Period, EA13430) multiplies the eye fourfold to multiply its strength. Wedjat eyes were painted on coffins — so that the deceased could see out — on the bows of boats, so that the vessel itself could see its way, and on stelae and temple walls as a standing apotropaic guard. The logic is exact: the eye that was destroyed and made whole is the one power in the cosmos that knows injury from inside and has overcome it. Protection by the wedjat is not the protection of the untouched; it is the protection of the healed. The archive's article on amulets and talismans treats the object's typology; the medical papyri show its clinical neighbours, for the wedjat also presided over prescriptions and the restoration of bodily wholeness.

The Eye and the Fractions — a Necessary Caution

A famous tradition, repeated in many older handbooks, holds that the six parts of the wedjat were used as fraction signs for parts of the heqat grain measure — 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, 1/32, 1/64 — summing to 63/64, with the missing 1/64 supplied by Thoth's magic. The fractions themselves are real, and capacity-measure signs resembling parts of an eye are genuinely attested. But the neat equation of the two systems is now largely abandoned by specialists: T. Eric Peet already objected in 1923 that the supposed eye-part signs appear too late to be the origin of the measure signs, and Jim Ritter's palaeographic study (2002) showed that the measure signs developed separately and only later converged in appearance with the eye. The story is best filed where this archive files all such material: as a later scribal reinterpretation — probably a New Kingdom flourish — rather than as the origin of Egyptian fractions. It is quoted here because it is famous, and flagged here because the archive's standard is that famous is not the same as true. The genuine mathematical culture behind the question is treated in Mathematics and Astronomy.

Distinguish, finally, the Eye of Horus from the Eye of Ra. The two are related — both are wḏꜣt [Gardiner: D10] eyes, both protective — but they are not the same organ. The Eye of Ra is the sun god's own fiery daughter-power, sent out as Hathor or Sekhmet to punish and protect; the Eye of Horus is the moon-eye, the injured and restored left eye of the falcon. Popular usage conflates them constantly; Egyptian usage kept a distinction that this article preserves: Ra's Eye burns; Horus's Eye heals.

VI. Horus and Seth — the Contendings and the Triumph

Every succession in Egypt rehearsed one story: the rightful heir against the violent usurper, order against disorder, ma'at against isfet. The heir is Horus; the usurper is his uncle Seth — written in his older form stš [Gardiner: E20] [𓃩], with the enigmatic Seth-animal as his sign. Seth had murdered Osiris, his own brother, and seized the throne. Horus, raised in hiding, came of age with a single claim, stated in his epithet Ḥr-nḏ-it.f [Gardiner: G5-Aa27-U10-I9]: the son must avenge the father and take the father's office. Egyptian sources tell the ensuing struggle in two very different registers — a legalistic, often comic narrative, and a temple drama of cosmic war — and the archive treats them separately, because they are separate works with separate dates and purposes.

The Contendings of Horus and Seth — Papyrus Chester Beatty I

The Contendings of Horus and Seth survives in a single manuscript: Papyrus Chester Beatty I, written in hieratic in the Twentieth Dynasty, in the reign of Ramesses V (c. 1149–1145 BCE), probably at Thebes, and now in the Chester Beatty Library, Dublin. A. H. Gardiner published the editio princeps in 1931; Miriam Lichtheim's translation in Ancient Egyptian Literature (vol. II) is the standard English version. The story opens with the case already ancient: Horus and Seth have been litigating before the Ennead for eighty years.

The Ennead is deadlocked and irritable. Most of the gods favour Horus — Shu and Thoth argue that the office belongs to the son of Osiris by right — but Ra, presiding, favours the stronger and older Seth and resents the young claimant. The tribunal writes for an outside opinion to Neith of Sais, who answers for Horus and proposes compensation for Seth; Ra refuses to accept the letter's verdict. Isis, barred from one session of the court on an island because Seth fears her advocacy, crosses in disguise, and traps Seth into condemning himself out of his own mouth with a parable about a widow's son and a stranger seizing his father's cattle — the gods laugh, and Seth, realising he has ruled against his own case, is furious. Argument gives way to ordeal. The rivals dive as hippopotamuses to see who can stay submerged longest; Isis, harpoon in hand, wounds her own son by mistake, heals him, then harpoons Seth — who pleads kinship so effectively that she lets him go, whereupon Horus, in rage at her mercy, strikes off his mother's head; Thoth replaces it with a cow's head, an episode the text itself uses to explain an iconography of Isis. Seth later finds Horus resting, tears out his eyes and buries them on a mountain, where they become lotus plants; Hathor heals Horus with gazelle's milk. Seth's attempt to disgrace Horus by a sexual assault is inverted by Isis's counter-trick with a pot of lettuce, so that before the tribunal it is Seth's own claim that collapses when Thoth calls the seed to account. In the final contest the rivals race in boats of stone; Seth's boat is genuinely stone and sinks, while Horus's is pinewood plastered to look like stone — a cheat the text reports without embarrassment, since trickery in a just cause is Isis's own method. At last the gods write to Osiris in the realm of the dead, and Osiris's answering letter — reminding the Ennead that he created the grain that feeds gods and men, and that his messengers can fetch any heart that does evil — settles what eighty years of advocacy could not. Horus is awarded the office of his father and crowned; Seth, in a settlement characteristic of Egyptian rather than modern justice, is not annihilated but placed: given to Ra to thunder in the sky at the prow of the solar barque, a bound and useful power. On the solar barque Seth's violence is exactly what the voyage needs against the serpent Apep.

Two cautions govern the archive's use of this text. First, its tone is genuinely literary — satirical, bawdy, and self-aware; it is a scribal entertainment built on sacred material, not a liturgy, and it should not be quoted as if it were temple doctrine. Second, it is one telling among many. The Pyramid Texts, a thousand years older, know the eye-wounding and the tribunal in allusive fragments; Plutarch, a thousand years later, retells the conflict with Greek moralising and omissions he himself acknowledges. The Contendings is the fullest narrative, not the canonical one — Egypt never canonised a single version of anything.

The Triumph of Horus — the Edfu Drama

The second register is carved on the inner face of the western enclosure wall of the temple of Edfu: the text H. W. Fairman published as The Triumph of Horus (1974, from the studies of Blackman and Fairman in the Journal of Egyptian Archaeology, 1942–44). The inscription is Ptolemaic, of the second century BCE, but its language preserves Late Egyptian features suggesting it was compiled from much older papyrus exemplars kept in the temple library. It was not merely read: it was performed, annually, at Edfu's Festival of Victory — a sacred drama in which Horus of Behdet, played in procession and rite, harpoons Seth in the form of a red hippopotamus. Scene by scene the reliefs show the ten harpoons striking — head, neck, ribs, limbs — the chorus urging the god to hold fast, and finally the dismemberment and distribution of the hippopotamus's body among the gods, a ritual butchery that converts the enemy into offerings. The king played Horus's part in the rite's theology: each performance re-founded his kingship on the victory it re-enacted.

Read together, the two works show the conflict's full range. The Contendings insists that Horus wins by right — by birth, by law, by his mother's advocacy and his father's posthumous authority — and that even the gods must submit to the verdict. The Triumph insists that he wins by force, harpoon by harpoon, and that the force must be renewed every year. Egyptian kingship needed both claims, and made both, in different rooms of the same civilisation, for three thousand years. The wider pattern of such performances is surveyed in the archive's article on ritual practice.

VII. Horus and Kingship — Serekh and the Fivefold Titulary

The living king of Egypt was Horus. This was not a metaphor the court poets reached for on feast days; it was the constitutional theory of the state, written into the king's name, his monuments, his coronation, and his funeral. The dead king became Osiris; the reigning king was Horus; the king's mother occupied the place of Isis. Every accession therefore re-enacted Section VI: the son of the dead king took the office against whatever Seth-forces — rivals, rebels, disorder — the moment supplied, and the gods' tribunal was understood to have ruled, again, for the heir.

The Serekh and the Horus Name

The king's oldest name is his Horus name, and its frame is the serekh: srḫ [Gardiner: O33] [𓊁] — the post-audit value of the sign, which depicts the niched palace façade that the serekh frame itself represents. The name is written inside the rectangle of the palace; above it perches the Horus falcon. The composition says everything at once: the falcon is upon the palace; the god is in residence in the king. Serekhs appear from Dynasty 0 — the falcon above the names of Narmer and Aha, among the very first written artefacts of the Egyptian state — and the Horus name remained the first element of the royal titulary into the Roman period. Early Horus names are programmes in miniature: Aha is "Horus the Fighter," and Khasekhemwy's name proclaims that "the Two Powers are at peace."

That last name carries the great exception that proves the theology's flexibility. The Second Dynasty also produced King Peribsen, who set the Seth-animal, not the falcon, above his serekh — and Khasekhemwy, in whose serekh Horus and Seth stand together, reconciled. Egyptologists still debate the politics behind these monuments — a religious schism, a reunification after civil conflict — but their mere existence shows that the Horus–Seth opposition could be balanced as well as fought, and that the serekh was the surface on which the balance was declared. The Early Dynastic evidence is treated in its own article; the principle matters here: kingship was Horus's, but the wise king, like the Ennead's verdict, found a place for Seth too.

The Fivefold Titulary

By the Middle Kingdom the king's full style comprised five names, and three of the five bind him to Horus and to the falcon's family:

1 · THE HORUS NAME

Written in the srḫ [Gardiner: O33] serekh beneath the falcon Ḥr [Gardiner: G5] [𓅃]. The king as Horus incarnate; the oldest name, and the name by which the earliest kings are still known to history.

2 · THE NEBTY NAME

Introduced by nbty [Gardiner: G16] [𓅒], "the Two Ladies" — Nekhbet the vulture of Upper Egypt and Wadjet the cobra of Lower Egypt on their baskets. The king under the protection of the two tutelary goddesses of the Two Lands.

3 · THE GOLDEN HORUS NAME

Introduced by the falcon upon the gold sign: Ḥr-nbw [Gardiner: G5-S12] [𓅃𓋞], "Horus of Gold" — gold, nbw [Gardiner: S12], being the flesh of the gods and the sign of the imperishable. The reading and sense of this name were already debated by the Egyptians' successors; its falcon is unmistakably Horus again.

4 · THE THRONE NAME (PRENOMEN)

In a cartouche, preceded by nswt-bity [Gardiner: A43-X1-A45] [𓀲𓏏𓀵] — "King of Upper and Lower Egypt," written with the sedge nswt [Gardiner: A43-X1] [𓀲𓏏] and the bee bity [Gardiner: A45] [𓀵]. Assumed at accession; usually compounded with Ra.

5 · THE SON OF RA NAME (NOMEN)

The king's birth name in a cartouche, preceded by sꜣ-rꜥ [Gardiner: G38-N5] [𓅬𓇳], "Son of Ra." The title inserts the king into the solar genealogy: Horus by office, Ra's son by proclamation — the two claims run in parallel, exactly as the two Horuses run in parallel in Section II.

The coronation made the theory flesh. The king's "appearance in glory," ḫꜥ [Gardiner: N28] [𓈍], was staged with the release of falcons to the four points of the sky — Horus's birds carrying the news of Horus's accession to the ends of the world — and the great renewal festival of the reign, the Sed festival, ḥb-sd [Gardiner: W3-F33] [𓎱𓄢], re-proved the ageing king's fitness to remain Horus, typically after thirty years and at intervals thereafter. Even the palace vocabulary is falconine: the throne itself, in the UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology's study of the institution, carries serekh-decoration "probably inspired by the Horus-name of the royal titulary," alluding to the king as "living Horus" and lord of the palace. Horus is not the king's patron. He is the king's job description.

The succession's other half was funerary. When the king died, his son — the new Horus — performed for him the rites the myth prescribes, above all the Opening of the Mouth, wp-r [Gardiner: F13-D21] [𓄋𓂋], written with the corrected value wp [Gardiner: F13], "to open," upon r [Gardiner: D21], "mouth." In the ritual's own words the officiating son speaks as Horus to the father become Osiris: the myth is the script, the funeral its performance, and the new reign its outcome. No other Egyptian god is so completely the grammar of political time — the form in which Egypt said before, now, and next about its own government.

VIII. Horus the Child — Harpocrates, Mammisi and Healing Cippi

Between the hidden birth and the tribunal stands the childhood, and Egyptian religion gave the child his own name and cult: Ḥr-pꜣ-ẖrd [Gardiner: G5-Q3-G1-A18] [𓅃𓊪𓄿𓀖] — "Horus the Child," the last element written with the seated child sign ẖrd [Gardiner: A18] [𓀖]. Greek speakers heard the name as Harpocrates. He is shown as Egyptian art always shows a child: nude, his hair in the sidelock of youth, one finger to his mouth — the hieroglyphic gesture for childhood itself. His mythology is the Khemmis cycle: Isis, hiding in the papyrus marshes of the Delta, bears and rears him in secret while Seth searches; the child is stung by a scorpion (in other tellings, bitten or seized by fever); Isis's cry stops the very barque of the sun in the sky until Thoth descends with the spells that draw out the poison. The story is preserved most fully on the Metternich Stela (below) and is retold, with variations, in the archive's article on Isis.

The theological point of the cycle is vulnerability survived. The future king of the gods begins as the most endangered creature in Egypt — a fatherless infant in a swamp, prey to every venomous thing in the Red Land's bestiary. His survival is not his own doing: it is his mother's heka and Thoth's word. That is precisely why this Horus, and no other god, became the patron of endangered children and of everyone stung, bitten, or fevered: he is the one god who has been the patient.

The Cippi of Horus

The child's cult object is the cippus (plural cippi), the "Horus on the Crocodiles" stela. A cippus shows the naked child Horus in high relief, standing frontally on the backs of two crocodiles, grasping snakes, scorpions, a lion and an oryx by their tails and horns, with the protective mask of Bes above his head and dense magical texts covering the back, sides, and base — the Khemmis spells among them. The Metropolitan Museum describes the use exactly: such stelae "are known as cippi; the ancient Egyptians believed that water poured over a cippus could be used to heal injuries caused by snakes or scorpions." The water ran over the carved spells and the child's triumphant figure, was collected in the basin at the stela's foot, and was drunk by, or poured over, the patient. The British Museum's wooden cippus EA60958, painted black with yellow detail, carries the same programme: Horus the child grasping two snakes and a Seth-animal in one hand, a scorpion in the other, the hieroglyphic text wrapping the object on every face.

The supreme example is the Metternich Stela (Metropolitan Museum, 50.85), made in the reign of Nectanebo II (360–343 BCE) for a priest named Nesu-Atum — the largest, finest, and most complete cippus known, its front dominated by the child Horus triumphant over the dangerous creatures, its surfaces carrying the fullest surviving corpus of the Horus-child spells, including the Khemmis narrative. Cippi range from temple monuments of this scale down to pocket amulets a few centimetres high (the Met's marble example, 664–380 BCE, is under eight centimetres), and they were produced from the Third Intermediate Period to the end of the Ptolemaic age — a thousand years of Egyptians treating snakebite with a story about a god who had survived one. The archive's article on magical practice sets the cippi within the wider technology of Egyptian healing; the clinical tradition beside it is treated under the medical papyri.

The Mammisi — Theology of the Divine Birth

Every great temple of the Ptolemaic period built the child's birth a house of its own: the mammisi or birth house — in Egyptian terms the pr-ms [Gardiner: O1-F31] [𓉐𓄟], "house of birth," with ms [Gardiner: F31], "to give birth." Its reliefs tell how the local child god — at Edfu, Harsomptus, son of Horus and Hathor; at Philae, Horus son of Isis — is conceived, born, suckled, and presented to the gods as the legitimate heir. The mammisi is the Osiris myth's first act turned into temple architecture: a small, columned, freestanding chapel in the temple court where the dangerous, secret birth of the heir was celebrated in public festival. Politically, its message served every ruler who built one, Ptolemies included: the heir is divine from the womb; the succession is written in heaven; doubt is impiety. The mammisi at Edfu and the birth house at Philae are treated with their temples in Section XII.

Isis nursing the child — the Isis lactans type, the goddess enthroned with Horus at her breast — became in the Late and Ptolemaic periods one of the most reproduced images in the Mediterranean world, cast in bronze by the thousand for household shrines. Its later resonance with Christian images of the nursing Virgin is a matter of documented art-historical discussion and is handled, with the necessary care, in the archive's article on Christian influences. What is Egyptian and primary is simpler: the mother who hid the heir in the marshes is shown, at last, enthroned and unafraid, feeding the future king in the open.

IX. The Four Sons of Horus — Canopic Protection

Horus is also a father. His four sons — by tradition, his children by Isis in her aspect of the four protective goddesses' partner — are the guardians of the dead body's most vulnerable treasures: the viscera removed in mummification and stored in the four canopic jars. The British Museum's school and collection resources state the system plainly: the Four Sons "were believed to protect the organs of the deceased during mummification and the afterlife," their images placed on the canopic jars that held the organs. The set is already present on coffins and in the funerary literature of the New Kingdom, and it remained standard for fifteen hundred years.

IMSETY — THE LIVER

Human-headed. Guardian of the liver, protected in turn by Isis, and stationed at the south. The only one of the four in fully human form — the form of Horus's own human kingship among his sons.

HAPY — THE LUNGS

Baboon-headed, protected by Nephthys, at the north. His name is written ḥꜣpy [Gardiner: V28-G1-Q3-Z4] [𓎛𓄿𓊪𓏭]. He is not to be confused with Hapy the Nile inundation, a different god whose name is spelled with ꜥ where the Son's has ꜣ; the archive flags the pair wherever it matters.

DUAMUTEF — THE STOMACH

Jackal-headed like Anubis, whose colleague he is in the necropolis; protected by Neith, at the east. His name decodes exactly: dwꜣ-mwt.f [Gardiner: N14-G14-I9] [𓇼𓅐𓆑] — "He who adores his mother," from dwꜣ [Gardiner: N14], "to adore," mwt [Gardiner: G14] [𓅐], "mother," and the suffix .f [Gardiner: I9], "his."

QEBEHSENUEF — THE INTESTINES

Falcon-headed like his father, protected by Serqet the scorpion goddess, at the west. His name means "He who refreshes his brothers" — the libation-pourer of the family, whose cool water revives the dead as the wedjat revives Osiris.

The four do not appear only on jars. In the judgment vignette of the Book of the Dead (Section XIII) they stand together on a lotus flower before the throne of Osiris — in the Papyrus of Ani (British Museum EA10470) the four are shown on the lotus in the hall where Ani's heart is weighed, Horus's sons presiding over the organ-store of every justified dead person now gathered into one scene. On coffins they occupy the four sides; in the tomb they answer to the four cardinal points, the four supports of the sky, and the four goddesses — Isis, Nephthys, Neith, Serqet — who pair with them. Egyptians also knew them as the four stars or "souls" who assist the dead king's ascent in the Pyramid Texts: Horus's family, like Horus himself, is a piece of cosmic furniture, holding up the sky under which his father reigns below. The funerary system as a whole is mapped in the funerary texts survey.

A note on the organs, in the archive's spirit of revision: nineteenth-century writers assigned the four organs to the four sons in several conflicting schemes, and museum labels of different generations disagree. The assignments given above — Imsety/liver, Hapy/lungs, Duamutef/stomach, Qebehsenuef/intestines — are those of the current British Museum presentation and of Grafton Elliot Smith's anatomical study of 1906, and are the standard modern consensus; where an older source differs, it is the older source's scheme that should be checked, not silently harmonised.

X. Behdeti — the Winged Sun Disk

Stand in any Egyptian temple doorway, look up, and you will see Horus. The winged sun disk — a central disk for the sun, two vast falcon wings outstretched to either side, and, hanging below, the twin uraeus serpents of the Two Ladies — is carved above gates, lintels, and stela tops from the Old Kingdom to the Roman period. It is Horus of Behdet: bḥdty [Gardiner: D58-V28-D46-X1-Z4] [𓃀𓎛𓂧𓏏𓏭], "the Behdetite" — the falcon in the shrine of his Delta town, Behdet. Behdet's location was transferred in Egyptian sacred geography to Edfu in the south — the archive treats the geography under sacred geography — and it is Edfu's priests who told the emblem's charter myth.

The Legend of Horus of Behdet and the Winged Disk, inscribed at Edfu and published by Édouard Naville in his Mythe d'Horus (1870), is set in the 363rd year of Ra's reign on earth. Ra-Horakhty is campaigning in Nubia against rebels who have conspired against him. Horus of Behdet, in Ra's boat, offers to destroy the enemies, and takes the form of the great winged disk, flying up into the sky — in E. A. Wallis Budge's translation of the legend, Ra addresses him as his son and commands him to "overthrow the enemies who are before thee straightway." From the height of heaven Horus falls on the rebels with such violence that they can neither see nor hear and slaughter one another; campaign follows campaign down the Nile, the enemy taking crocodile and hippopotamus form and being harpooned by Horus and his followers, the smiths of Edfu. At the end Ra decrees the aetiology that explains the archaeology: an image of the winged disk is to be set up in every sanctuary of every god and goddess of Upper and Lower Egypt, to overthrow the enemies in every place. The emblem over the door is therefore not a decoration but a garrison: Horus, permanently stationed at the threshold, in the act of falling on chaos.

The legend is late in its inscribed form — Ptolemaic — and the archive reads it as Edfu's priests read their own past: as a charter, gathering genuinely old materials (the winged disk itself is attested a millennium and a half earlier; falcon wings as a heaven symbol are older still) into a single explanatory story. Its theology, however, states something permanent about Horus. Of all his forms, the winged disk is the most reduced: no body, no biography, no family — only sun, wings, and serpents; sight, speed, and kingship. It is Horus as pure function, and it is the form in which he travelled farthest, adapted across the ancient Near East on seals, ivories, and royal monuments far beyond Egypt's borders.

// ANATOMY OF THE EMBLEM //

The disk — the sun, and Horus's right eye (Section IV). The wings — the falcon's, spanning the sky, pt [Gardiner: N1]. The uraei — Nekhbet and Wadjet, the Two Ladies of the nbty [Gardiner: G16] name, breathing fire against the god's enemies. Together: the sky-god, the sun, and the united kingship, compressed into a sign that could be read by the illiterate and the foreigner at a glance — and that still reads instantly today.

XI. Solar Forms — Ra-Horakhty, Hor-em-akhet and the Great Sphinx

A sky god whose right eye is the sun will not stay merely lunar and celestial for long: Egyptian theology drew Horus steadily into the sun itself. The mechanism was the Egyptian habit of fusion — two gods sharing a function could be written as one name — and its supreme product is Ra-Horakhty: rꜥ-Ḥr-ꜣḫty [Gardiner: N5-G5-N27-Z4] [𓇳𓅃𓈌𓏭], "Ra-Horus of the Two Horizons." In this form the falcon-headed god is the sun in its daily course, lord of the eastern horizon where it rises and the western where it sets, while remaining recognisably Horus: the fusion preserves both names because both theologies were true. Ra-Horakhty presides over the New Kingdom solar religion, receives the great hymns of the period, and stands at the head of the tribunal in the Contendings (Section VI) — the sun judging between his falcon heir and the storm. Beside him stands the third member of the daily triad, Khepri, from ḫpr [Gardiner: L1] [𓆣], "to come into being": the sun at dawn, in the act of creating itself, where Ra-Horakhty is the sun in its established course.

Hor-em-akhet — the Sphinx as Horus

One solar Horus is made of stone sixty-six metres long. The Great Sphinx of Giza was carved, on the mainstream Egyptological account, in the Fourth Dynasty as part of King Khafre's pyramid complex — a lion-bodied, human-headed guardian of the necropolis, the king himself in leonine form. No Old Kingdom text names it. A thousand years later, the New Kingdom looked at the colossus with new eyes and worshipped it as a god in its own right: Horemakhet, Ḥr-m-ꜣḫt [Gardiner: G5-G17-N27] [𓅃𓅓𓈌], "Horus in the Horizon" — Greek Harmachis. Amenhotep II built a temple to Horemakhet beside the statue; princes and kings left stelae in its court; and between its paws Thutmose IV set the famous Dream Stela (c. 1401 BCE), recording how the prince, sleeping at midday in the statue's shadow, dreamed that the god — named on the stela in the compounded form Horemakhet-Khepri-Ra-Atum, the whole solar day in one god — promised him the kingship of the Two Lands if he cleared the sand choking the statue's body. Thutmose cleared it, and became king. The stela is thus a Horus-text twice over: the falcon god of the horizon grants a crown, as he does in every accession, and the grantee advertises the grant in granite at the god's feet.

The episode states the rule of Section VII from the god's side. Kings do not merely imitate Horus; they petition him, are chosen by him, and owe him. That the petitioner addressed a statue already ancient — already, in the New Kingdom, a monument of a past more remote from Thutmose than Thutmose is from the building of Edfu's temple — only strengthened the claim: Horemakhet is Horus as antiquity itself, the horizon where the sun and the past meet. Later ages kept the cult alive into the Roman period, and the name Harmachis travelled into Greek and Hermetic literature as a title of the solar god. The Sphinx's modern alternative chronologies are outside the evidence this archive works from and are noted here only to be set aside: the inscriptions, which are the archive's business, are New Kingdom, and they say Horemakhet.

Solar Horus also rides. In the nightly voyage of the solar barque through the dwꜣt [Gardiner: N15] [𓇽], the netherworld, Horus figures among the gods who crew, guard, and steer the boat of Ra — the falcon's sight reading the darkness, his sons among the defenders — while Seth, bound into service by the tribunal's settlement, stands at the prow with the spear against Apep. The books that map that voyage are treated in the netherworld books; what matters here is the pattern, by now familiar: Horus's victory over Seth did not delete the storm god from the cosmos but posted him, and the sun's safe passage is the daily proof that the posting works.

XII. Temples of Horus — Edfu, Kom Ombo and Philae

Edfu — the Complete Temple

The temple of Horus at Edfu, on the west bank of the Nile between Luxor and Aswan, is the best-preserved temple of ancient Egypt and the second largest after Karnak. The standing building is Ptolemaic: founded on 23 August 237 BCE under Ptolemy III and completed, after some 180 years of building and decoration, under Ptolemy XII in 57 BCE. Its pylon rises about 36 metres; behind it the full canonical sequence survives roofed and inscribed — court, hypostyle halls, offering chambers, and the sanctuary with its granite shrine, around which runs the girdle wall carrying the Triumph of Horus and the Legend of the Winged Disk (Sections VI and X). Buried under sand and village houses for centuries, it was cleared from 1860 under Auguste Mariette almost intact — a fortune no other great temple enjoyed, and the reason Edfu is the type-site for the archive's article on temple architecture.

Edfu's god is Horus of Behdet with his consort Hathor of Dendera and their son Harsomptus, Ḥr-smꜣ-tꜣwy [Gardiner: G5-F36-N16-G43-Z4] [𓅃𓄥𓇾𓅱𓏭], "Horus, Uniter of the Two Lands" — smꜣ [Gardiner: F36], "to unite," upon tꜣwy, the Two Lands, from tꜣ [Gardiner: N16] [𓇾], "land." The temple's year enacted the whole Horus theology in festival. At the Festival of the Coronation of the Sacred Falcon, described in the UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology's study of Ptolemaic temple festivals (Coppens, 2009), a living falcon was chosen and crowned in the temple during the first days of the month of Tybi: Horus, as son and legitimate heir of Osiris, assumed the kingship of the Two Lands, and the rite re-enacted at once the god's accession and the reigning king's. At the Beautiful Feast of Behdet, Hathor's statue travelled the 180 kilometres from Dendera by river to be united with Horus at Edfu — the divine marriage that produced the heir whose birth the mammisi celebrated. At the Festival of Victory the Triumph was performed. Birth, marriage, coronation, victory: the calendar is the myth, distributed across the year.

Edfu was also a library. Its inscriptions preserve ritual books, geographical lists, a catalogue of the temple's own book-boxes, and the mythological corpus on its walls — texts the priests said were copied from ancient exemplars in the temple's keeping. The institution behind such keeping, the pr-ꜥnḫ [Gardiner: O1-S34] [𓉐𓋹], House of Life, appears at Edfu in the inscriptions of its own rooms; and the goddess who presided over such writing, sšꜣt [Gardiner: R21] [𓋈] — Seshat, her name given here with the post-audit value of R21 — belongs to the temple's scribal theology. Even the doors preach: the great door-leaves, ꜥꜣ [Gardiner: O31] [𓉿] (post-audit value), of a temple ḥwt-nṯr [Gardiner: O10-R8] [𓉡𓊹], "house of the god," opened only to the purified, and the purity rules of Edfu's priesthood are among its surviving texts.

Kom Ombo — the Double Temple

Forty-five kilometres north of Aswan, Kom Ombo does in architecture what Section II does in theology: it houses the Elder. The temple (mainly 180–47 BCE) is a true double temple, perfectly symmetrical on its axis, with twin entrances, twin hypostyle halls, twin sanctuaries. The southern half belongs to Sobek the crocodile god with Hathor and Khonsu; the northern half to Haroeris, Horus the Elder, with the goddess Tasenetnofret, "the Good Sister," and their son Panebtawy, "Lord of the Two Lands." Two priesthoods, two processions, two households of gods, one roof: the Ptolemaic solution to two ancient cults sharing a riverbank. Kom Ombo's Horus is no Osirian child — he is the primordial falcon of the sky, consort and father in his own right, and his temple is the standing proof that the Elder was never absorbed by the Son.

Philae and the Other Horus Towns

At Philae, the great island sanctuary of Isis in its Ptolemaic and Roman rebuilding, Horus is present first as the son whose birth the mammisi celebrates: the birth house at Philae tells the Khemmis story in relief — the conception, the secret birth, the presentation of the heir — for an island whose whole theology is the Osirian family's. Beyond the three great sites, Horus was lord of towns the length of the valley: Nekhen of the first kings; Behdet in the Delta, whose name he carried; Mesen, "the Harpoonery," the Edfu epithet that remembers him as the harpooner of the hippopotamus; Letopolis, where the local falcon Khenty-khety was identified with him; Athribis, Hebenu, and dozens more, each with its own Horus, its own epithet, and its own claim on the falcon. The map of Horus's cult is, in the end, the map of Egypt: there was no nome without him, because there was no kingship without him.

XIII. Horus in the Funerary Literature — Judgment and the Book of the Dead

The dead Egyptian's hope was to be justified as Horus was justified, and the funerary literature casts Horus in three roles at once: son, who performs the rites for the dead father; advocate, who presents the justified dead to Osiris; and judge's colleague, whose own vindication is the precedent every judgment follows. The funerary corpus — Pyramid Texts, Coffin Texts, and the Book of the Dead — develops all three across two thousand years.

In the Pyramid Texts of the late Old Kingdom, the oldest religious corpus in the world, Horus already does the son's work: he seeks his father, finds him, raises him, and presents to him the healed Eye — the offering of the wḏꜣt [Gardiner: D10] as the instrument of resurrection is a Pyramid Text idea before it is anything else. The dead king is addressed as Osiris; the officiant, his son and successor, is Horus by office; and the texts' great theme — the son's act makes the father live again — is the Osiris myth performed as liturgy a millennium before the Contendings made it a story. The Opening of the Mouth, wp-r [Gardiner: F13-D21], by which the mummy's senses were restored, belongs to this stratum of the tradition: the son-as-Horus touches the father's mouth with the adze and the pesesh-kef, and the father breathes.

In the Book of the Dead the setting democratises: any justified person may now stand where only kings had stood. The great vignette of the judgment — best known from the Papyrus of Hunefer and the Papyrus of Ani, both British Museum, both Nineteenth Dynasty — stages the roles exactly. The deceased's heart, ib [Gardiner: F34] [𓄣], is weighed in the balance, mḫꜣt [Gardiner: U38] [𓍝] (post-audit value), against the feather of Ma'at, mꜣꜥt [Gardiner: U1-D36-X1] [𓌳𓂝𓏏], whose feather is šwt [Gardiner: H6] [𓆄]; Anubis tends the scales; Thoth records the verdict, wḏꜥ [Gardiner: Aa21] [𓐣], "judgment"; and then Horus takes the justified dead by the hand and leads him into the presence of Osiris, enthroned with Isis and Nephthys behind him and the Four Sons on their lotus before him, and presents him. The presenter is the point. Who introduces a stranger into a king's presence? The king's son and heir — the one person whose own acceptance is beyond question, because the tribunal has already ruled for him. The dead borrow Horus's vindication exactly as they borrow his Eye: he is the precedent and the escort in one. The full liturgy of the scene is given in the Weighing of the Heart and in the translated passages collected at Book of the Dead passages.

Horus guards the judgment's sequel as well. The justified dead join the company of the gods, sail in the barque, and take their places among the followers of Horus — the šemsu-Ḥr of the oldest royal ideology, the semi-legendary "Followers of Horus" who were remembered as Egypt's first kings. To die justified was to enlist in the falcon's retinue for eternity. And the instruments of the whole process are the parts of the person the archive treats under the composite soul: the kꜣ [Gardiner: E1] [𓃒] sustained by offerings, the bꜣ [Gardiner: E10] [𓃝] (post-audit value) that moves between tomb and sky, and the ꜣḫ [Gardiner: G25] [𓅜], the transfigured spirit — the state whose name shares its root with the horizon, ꜣḫt [Gardiner: N27], where Horus rises. The funerary literature does not merely mention Horus. It is built on him: no son, no rites; no rites, no resurrection; no precedent, no judgment; no escort, no audience with Osiris.

XIV. Horus in the Late Period and the Greek World — Harpocrates Transformed

The last millennium of Egyptian religion did not diminish Horus; it distributed him. In the Late Period the animal cults filled the catacombs with mummified falcons; healing cippi of Horus the Child stood in houses and temple courts (Section VIII); and the falcon's protection of the king received one of its most beautiful statements in the Metropolitan Museum's statue of Horus as a great falcon sheltering the small figure of King Nectanebo II (Dynasty 30, 360–343 BCE) between his legs — the god in that work named for his local form, Horus of Hebyt. Under foreign rulers — Persian, then Macedonian — Horus remained the theological warrant of kingship itself: each new dynasty presented itself, in the temples it built and restored, as Horus succeeding Osiris, and the Ptolemies' work at Edfu, Kom Ombo, and Philae (Section XII) is the largest single building programme ever undertaken in his name.

The Greek world took Horus in two pieces. The adult god — solar, royal, falcon-headed — was identified with Apollo: the classical author Claudius Aelianus records that the Egyptians called Apollo by Horus's name, and the identification is natural — Apollo too is a radiant young god of the sky, a far-shooter and a healer, son of a great mother. The child travelled farther and changed more. Harpocrates, the Greek hearing of Ḥr-pꜣ-ẖrd [Gardiner: G5-Q3-G1-A18], appears across the Hellenistic and Roman world in terracotta and bronze: a chubby child with the sidelock, a cornucopia, and his finger to his lips. Greek eyes read that finger as a command: the Egyptian gesture that simply says "child" (it is the hieroglyph for childhood, painted on the child's sign itself) became, in the new language, an injunction to silence — and Harpocrates became the god of silence, secrecy, and the mysteries, patron of everything that must not be spoken. The transformation is the single most instructive case in this archive of what translation does to a god: nothing in Egypt made Horus a god of silence, and everything in his Greek afterlife did. The archive's articles on the Greek Magical Papyri and the Corpus Hermeticum show the world that received him — a world in which Egyptian gods were invoked in Greek spells, in which Harpocrates could be called upon on a lotus at sunrise as a form of the young sun, and in which "Horos" could be abstracted, in some Gnostic systems, into a personified Limit or Boundary, a god with neither falcon nor child left in him at all.

Rome completed the distribution. The cult of Isis that spread through the empire carried her child with her; Harpocrates stands in Isiac sanctuaries from Pompeii to the Danube, and the mother-and-child bronzes of Section VIII became a Mediterranean commonplace. When the temples finally closed, the falcon's images passed into the keeping of the Coptic world he had partly shaped — the name surviving in Coptic as Hōr, the mother and child echoing in the iconography the archive examines under Christian influences and Coptic Egypt. What ended was the cult. The figure — the eye above all — never left circulation, and its modern ubiquity, from amulet shops to hospital corridors in Egypt, is a survival, not a revival.

XVI. Esoteric Analysis — the Heir Who Integrates the Divided Kingdom

// READING THIS SECTION //

What follows is the archive's interpretive reading of Horus, offered as such. It is not presented as established Egyptological fact, and it rests only on the documented material of the preceding sections: the tribunal, the serekh, the healed eye, and the settlement that posted Seth to the prow of the barque rather than destroying him.

The archive reads Horus as the Egyptian figure of legitimate integration. Consider what the myth actually requires of him. He may not simply kill Seth and take the throne: private vengeance would make him another usurper. He must sue — submit his claim to a tribunal, endure eighty years of procedure, accept advocacy, trickery, and ordeal, and let the verdict come from outside himself, sealed finally by a letter from the dead. Nor may he annihilate the loser: the settlement employs Seth, binding the storm's violence into the sun's nightly defence. Nor, finally, does he reign as a solitary: he reigns as son — of a dead father whose rites he performs, of a mother whose advocacy won him the case, of a sun who presides over his court. Kingship, in the Horus pattern, is not the victory of one force over the others. It is the verdict that assigns every force its place, including the defeated one. The divided kingdom — sky and storm, north and south, living and dead, the two eyes, the two crowns — is not healed by erasing its divisions but by a judgment that holds them in one frame, as the serekh holds the name beneath the falcon.

The wedjat gives the same doctrine a personal scale, which is why this archive, following the material of Section V rather than any modern system, treats the Eye as the Horus symbol most relevant to practice. The eye that protects is not an eye that was never injured; it is the eye that was torn out, scattered, gathered, and offered. Wholeness in the Horus pattern is a result, not a starting condition — and it is completed by being given away, handed to the dead father, spent on someone else's resurrection. The alchemical reading the archive develops elsewhere — in Alchemy and the Osiris myth as the template of dissolution and restoration — finds in Horus the stage after the restoration: not the reassembly itself, which is Isis's work, but the reign that follows it, the long administration of a wholeness that must be renewed at every festival, every coronation, every dawn. Horus is what the Great Work is for: not the stone in the vessel, but the king on the throne, ruling a kingdom whose Seth is at the prow and whose Osiris is in the hall of judgment, with the heir's own healed eye watching from both.

// THE HORUS PATTERN, AS THE ARCHIVE STATES IT //

Claim by right, not by seizure — the heir sues before he strikes. Accept the wound — the eye is lost in a just war and the loss is admitted, not hidden. Let the scattered be gathered by knowledge — Thoth's part: reckoning, writing, the patient reassembly of the record. Offer the healed thing — restoration completes itself in gift. Post the defeated force — the storm is not denied; it is given the prow, a station, and a spear. Renew the verdict daily — the sun rises, the festival returns, the falcon is crowned again. This is the archive's interpretive summary of the Horus material, and it is offered in that spirit: as a reading to be tested against the texts of Section XVII, not as a doctrine to be believed.
