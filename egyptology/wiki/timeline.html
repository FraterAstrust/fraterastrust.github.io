I. The Ontology of Time — Zep Tepi and Linear Flow

Ancient Egyptian civilization operated on a dual understanding of time. While they recognized the linear progression of human events (rnpwt [𓆳𓏏𓏥] - years), this was always nested within Zep Tepi (sp tpy [𓊗𓏤𓊪𓏭𓇋] - The First Time). History was viewed as a sacred vessel through which primordial archetypes were continuously re-manifested.

In this research framework, the timeline is not a flat sequence of dates but a vibrational data-stream. It undergoes periodic phases of initialization (Kingdoms), decoherence (Intermediate Periods), and restoration. To study the chronology of Kemet is to perform a "System Audit" of how Ma'at was maintained, lost, and reclaimed across five millennia. (Dates follow the conventional chronology of Ian Shaw, ed., The Oxford History of Ancient Egypt.)

c. 4300 – 3100 BCE

II. The Primordial Phase — Predynastic Origins

The Predynastic Period represents the "Assembly of the Hardware." During this phase, the indigenous Neolithic cultures of the Nile Valley (Badarian, Naqada I-III) developed the fundamental symbolic language of Egypt.

Critical developments include the emergence of the Serekh (srḫ [𓊁])—the palace-façade identifier for royal power—and the first "Protoglyphs" found in Tomb U-j at Abydos. This era is the pre-initialization of the Kemetic OS, where the concepts of the Falcon (Horus), the Sun, and the Nun were first encoded into material form.

c. 3100 – 2686 BCE

III. Unification — The Early Dynastic Period

The Early Dynastic Period (Dynasties 1–2) marks the formal "Boot-Up" of the unified state. Traditionally attributed to Narmer (Menes), unification was the successful synthesis of the "Two Lands"—Upper and Lower Egypt.

This period established the city of Memphis (mn-nfr [𓏠𓈖𓉺𓏏𓊖]) as the primary administrative node and introduced the core rituals of Divine Kingship. The King became the Neb Tawy (Lord of the Two Lands), the high-frequency capacitor that balanced the internal energies of the nation.

c. 2686 – 2181 BCE

IV. The Solar Peak — The Old Kingdom

The Old Kingdom (Dynasties 3–6) is the "Primary Initialization Phase." It reached its zenith with the construction of the Giza Plateau. Under the guidance of sages like Imhotep, the state utilized Monumental Hardening to fix its spiritual frequencies into stone.

Key developments: The transition from mastabas to true pyramids; the Solar Revolution of the 5th Dynasty; and the creation of the Pyramid Texts (mdw-nTr [𓌃𓊹]). This was an era of absolute centralization, where the Pharaoh's Ka was the sole engine of national prosperity.

c. 2181 – 2055 BCE

V. Systemic Decoherence — 1st Intermediate Period

The collapse of the Old Kingdom led to the First Intermediate Period. A combination of climate failure (failed Nile floods) and bureaucratic bloat caused the central "Signal" to fragment into regional "Noise."

Metaphysically, this was a Democratization Cycle. The transfiguration technologies once reserved for the King (Pyramid Texts) began to appear in the coffins of local officials, evolving into the Coffin Texts. This period proved that the Kemetic data-stream could survive even when its primary "Hardware" (the central state) failed.

c. 2055 – 1650 BCE

VI. The Classical Restoration — Middle Kingdom

The Middle Kingdom (Dynasties 11–13) is the "Classical Stabilization Phase." Founded by Mentuhotep II, it shifted the royal ideal from the "God-King" to the "Shepherd-King."

This era produced the standard forms of Middle Egyptian literature and language. The rise of Amun (imn [𓇋𓏠𓈖]) at Thebes provided a new, flexible theological synthesis. It was a period of high-bandwidth intellectual production, focusing on the ethical responsibility of the individual to maintain Ma'at.

c. 1650 – 1550 BCE

VII. The Hyksos Encounter — 2nd Intermediate Period

The Second Intermediate Period saw the conquest of Lower Egypt by the Hyksos (ḥḳꜣw-ḫꜣswt [𓋾𓐍𓄿𓋴𓅱𓏏𓈉𓏥] - Rulers of Foreign Lands).

This was a Stress-Test for the Egyptian system. The encounter forced the indigenous data-stream to integrate new "Hardware" (the chariot, vertical loom, composite bow) and new "Software" (the integration of Levantine deities). This trauma paved the way for the imperial evolution of the New Kingdom.

c. 1550 – 1075 BCE

VIII. Imperial Zenith — The New Kingdom

The New Kingdom (Dynasties 18–20) is the "High-Bandwidth Expansion Phase." Following the expulsion of the Hyksos, Egypt transformed into a global empire.

Key figures: Thutmose III (military expansion), Hatshepsut (trade and geometry), and Amenhotep III (monumental saturation). The religious center shifted entirely to Amun-Ra at Karnak. This era saw the compilation of the Book of the Dead (prt m hrw [𓉐𓂋𓏏𓇋𓅓𓇳𓏭𓅱] - Going Forth by Day) and the secret navigation technology of the Valley of the Kings.

c. 1352 – 1336 BCE

IX. The Amarna Anomaly — Akhenaten’s Interlude

Nested within the New Kingdom, the Amarna Period was a radical Systemic Rift. Akhenaten attempted to delete the traditional Ennead and replace it with the exclusive worship of the Aten (itn [𓇋𓏏𓈖𓇳]).

While culturally brilliant, this rift caused a collapse of Egypt’s external imperial stability and was eventually subjected to a total "System Restore" (Damnatio Memoriae) by Horemheb and the early Ramesside kings.

c. 1075 – 664 BCE

X. The Theban Theocracy — 3rd Intermediate Period

The Third Intermediate Period was a "Data-Preservation Cycle." As the central "Imperial Hardware" fractured, the High Priests of Amun at Thebes established a local theocracy.

Faced with looting and economic decline, the priesthood focused on the Archival Protection of royal mummies and sacred texts. This era saw the rise of Libyan and Nubian (25th Dynasty) rulers who sought to restore Ma'at by mimicking the high-frequency styles of the Old Kingdom.

c. 664 – 332 BCE

XI. The Saite Renaissance — Late Period

The Late Period (Dynasties 26–31) is the "Total System Backup." Facing the encroaching empires of Assyria, Babylon, and Persia, the Saite kings (26th Dynasty) initiated a radical program of Archaism.

They systematically copied the art, language, and rituals of the Old Kingdom to ensure that the core Kemetic frequency would survive the impending foreign rule. This period marks the final stand of the indigenous Pharaohs before the Hellenistic transition.

332 – 30 BCE

XII. The Greco-Egyptian Synthesis — Ptolemaic Period

The Ptolemaic Period is the "Translation Phase." Following the conquest of Alexander the Great, the high-frequency knowledge of the House of Life was systematically translated into the philosophical vocabulary of Greece.

This era saw the construction of the Great Temples of Edfu, Dendera, and Philae, which are effectively "Stone Encylopedias" of Kemetic theurgy. This synthesis gave birth to Hermeticism, preserving the spirit of Thoth for the Western intellectual stream.

30 BCE – 641 CE

XIII. The Final Chapter — Roman and Coptic Transition

The Roman Period represents the Dissolution Phase. While material resources were extracted for Rome, the initiatory knowledge retreated into increasingly restricted and subterranean channels.

The final hieroglyphic inscription was carved at Philae in 394 CE. The language evolved into Coptic (using the Greek alphabet), and the metaphysical focus shifted into Gnosticism and monasticism. By the Arab Conquest in 641 CE, the ancient OS had successfully "migrated" its essential data into the Christian and Islamic esoteric traditions.

XIV. Astronomical Sothic Cycles — The Calibration of History

Kemetic chronology is calibrated against the Sothic Cycle—the periodic heliacal rising of the star Sopdet (Sirius [𓇮]).

Because the Egyptian civil calendar lacked a leap year, it fell out of sync with the solar year by one day every four years. A full "Sothic Year" required 1,460 years to return to synchronization. By recording "Sothic Risings" in specific regnal years, the ancient scribes provided modern researchers with "Absolute Data-Nodes" that permit the precise dating of the Middle and New Kingdoms. This celestial clock is the ultimate anchor for the timeline of the Nile Valley.

The most consequential single observation is the Illahun Sothic date. A letter from the temple archive of Illahun (el-Lahun), in the Faiyum, announced in advance that the heliacal rising of Sirius would fall on the 16th day of the fourth month of Peret in a Year 7 of a 12th Dynasty king, identified on palaeographic grounds as Senusret III. Calculated back from the known Sothic coincidence of 139 CE, that observation fixes Senusret III's Year 7 at about 1872 BCE in the standard reconstruction — the pin on which the whole Middle Kingdom chronology hangs (see §XXIII for the dissenting placements). A second Sothic date, on the calendar written on the verso of the Ebers Papyrus for Year 9 of Amenhotep I, performs the same service, less securely, for the early New Kingdom.

Alongside the Sothic observations stand the lunar dates. The Illahun archive also preserves a run of temple lunar dates from the reigns of Senusret III and Amenemhat III. Because a set of lunar dates repeats on a roughly 25-year cycle, lunar data cannot fix a reign by themselves; what they do is choose between Sothic placements spaced 25 years apart, and it is the combination — Sothic year plus lunar pattern — that produces the competing "high" and "low" Middle Kingdom chronologies of §XXIII. Chronology in this archive is therefore a chain of arguments, not a table of discoveries, and its links are named wherever a date depends on them.

XVI. Manetho’s Framework — The Origins of Dynastic Division

Every date in §§II–XIII is expressed in dynasties, and the dynastic framework is the work of one man: Manetho of Sebennytos, an Egyptian priest who wrote his Aegyptiaca, a history of Egypt in Greek in three books, under Ptolemy I and Ptolemy II (see Ptolemaic Period, §XV). Drawing on temple king-lists and annals (§XVIII), Manetho arranged the kings in sequence — preceded by dynasties of gods and demigods — and grouped the human reigns into the thirty dynasties (a thirty-first was added by later editors) that Egyptology still uses as its filing system.

The Aegyptiaca itself is lost, and its transmission is the cautionary tale of §XVIII. Two channels preserve it. Josephus, in Against Apion (1st century CE), quotes extended narrative passages, including the Hyksos account, apparently from a fuller text. Separately, an epitome — the dynasties reduced to names and reign totals — was copied into the Chronographiai of Sextus Julius Africanus (early 3rd century CE) and the Chronicon of Eusebius (early 4th century); Eusebius survives through Jerome's Latin version and an Armenian translation, and both versions are preserved side by side by the Byzantine monk George Syncellus in his Chronicle (c. 810 CE), who set them in parallel columns precisely because they disagree. The fragments are collected in the standard edition, Manetho, translated by W. G. Waddell (Loeb Classical Library, 1940). Where Africanus and Eusebius differ — in spellings, reign lengths, and totals — modern chronology has to arbitrate between them against the native lists, which is why Manetho is used in this archive as a framework to be checked against §XV, never as an authority on his own.

XVIII. Archival Integrity — How the Stream was Preserved

The chronology of §§II–XIII rests on a native evidence base that the Egyptians themselves maintained for three millennia — and maintained redundantly, in stone and on papyrus, in temple and in tomb. The annals tradition, represented by the Palermo Stone (§XV), recorded reigns year by year with the Nile height for each year. The cultic lists were carved for ritual use: at Abydos, the list in the temple of Seti I presents 76 royal names in chronological order, the king and the young Ramesses II offering to their predecessors — a curated succession that omits rulers later judged illegitimate, among them the Amarna kings (§IX) and the Hyksos. The Karnak list of Thutmose III, by contrast, names 61 ancestors in no chronological order, grouped for the ritual of the ancestor chamber — and precisely because it was not a succession document, it preserves kings of the Intermediate Periods whom Abydos excludes.

The administrative stream is the Turin Canon: a hieratic papyrus of the reign of Ramesses II listing gods, spirits, and then every human king in order, with reign lengths in years, months, and days — the only native list that attempts complete chronological data, including the Hyksos rulers Abydos suppresses. It reached Turin in the 1820s shattered into more than 160 fragments, and its reconstruction, begun by Champollion, remains the single most consequential jigsaw in Egyptian chronology: whole dynasties of the Second Intermediate Period are known in order only from it. Behind all four documents stood the temple archives and the House of Life, where lists were copied, corrected, and recopied across centuries. The stream was preserved, in short, the way this archive's own method prescribes: multiple independent copies, in multiple media, whose disagreements mark exactly where the evidence runs out.

XIX. Modern Radiocarbon vs. Traditional Chronology

The traditional chronology followed in this archive — the scheme of Ian Shaw, ed., The Oxford History of Ancient Egypt, declared at §I — was built from the king-lists (§§XV–XVIII), reign lengths, and a small number of astronomical anchor points (§XIV). In 2010 it received its first large-scale independent test. Christopher Bronk Ramsey and colleagues (Science 328, 2010) published 211 radiocarbon measurements on short-lived plant remains — seeds, basketry, plant-based textiles — drawn from museum collections and tied to specific reigns, modelled with Bayesian statistics that incorporate the known order and lengths of those reigns.

The result was broad agreement. The radiocarbon chronology sits closest to Shaw's consensus scheme: it places the start of the New Kingdom between 1570 and 1544 BCE (Shaw: 1550 BCE) and the accession of Djoser in the Old Kingdom between 2691 and 2625 BCE — in both cases within decades of the traditional dates, while ruling out some more radical alternatives. The study also identified a small systematic offset, Egyptian samples reading about 19 radiocarbon years older than the calibration curve, attributed to the Nile valley's different growing season, which later work corrects for. Radiocarbon did not replace the historical chronology; at this precision it cannot. What it established is that the list-based framework of §§XV–XVIII and the physical clock now corroborate one another — which is why this archive retains Shaw's dates, stated as approximations, throughout.

XXII. How a Regnal Date Is Built — Counts, Attestations, Co-regencies

Egypt left no era dating — no "year 1 of the world" from which to count. Every Egyptian date is a regnal date: "Year X under King Y," sometimes with the season, month and day added. A modern chronology is therefore a construction with three storeys, and each storey has its own failure mode.

The ground floor is sequence: which king followed which. The king lists (§XV, §XVIII) supply it, cross-checked against genealogies, the stratigraphy of tombs and temples, and the order in which officials' careers span reigns. The first floor is reign length. Three kinds of evidence compete: the totals the Turin Canon assigns (in years, months and days, but damaged and not always credible — it credits Pepi II with 90+ years); Manetho's totals, transmitted through epitomes that disagree with each other (§XVI); and the highest attested regnal year of each king, the hardest evidence of all, which gives only a minimum. In the Old Kingdom the count was often biennial, tied to the cattle census, so a "Year after the 31st count" may mean year 62 — or, if the count became annual, far less; that ambiguity is the whole Pepi II problem in one line.

The second floor is co-regency: periods when father and son (or two rivals) reigned together, so their regnal years overlap and a simple sum overcounts. The 12th Dynasty is the classic case: double-dated monuments prove some overlaps (a stela dated simultaneously to years of Senusret I and Amenemhat II, for instance), while others are inferred from exactly the arithmetic discrepancies the sums produce. Whether Senusret III reigned 19 years or 39 — the crux of §XXIII — turns on reading a Dahshur control note dated to a Year 30 and on whether a long co-regency with Amenemhat III absorbed the difference.

Only the roof is absolute: the handful of points where the regnal construction touches a calendar modern astronomy can compute — the Sothic and lunar dates of §XIV, the Assyrian eponym canon that fixes the first millennium, Shoshenq I's Palestinian campaign (c. 925 BCE), and from the Saite period onward a dense web of Greek, Persian and Babylonian synchronisms (§XXIV). Between the roof points, every date in §§II–XIII is an interpolation across the three lower storeys, which is why honest chronologies print "c." and why this archive's dates move together if a single anchor is ever re-read.

XXIII. High and Low Chronologies — Where the Schemes Diverge

"High" and "low" chronologies are not rival fantasies; they are the same evidence assembled with different choices at the genuinely ambiguous joints of §XXII. The divergences concentrate in three places.

THE MIDDLE KINGDOM (c. 25–30 YEARS)

The Illahun Sothic date (§XIV) fixes Senusret III's Year 7 near 1872 BCE only if the observation was made where the archive assumes, and the Illahun lunar dates then admit placements a 25-year lunar cycle apart. Shaw's scheme, followed here, sits between the older high placement and Rolf Krauss's low chronology, which brings the 12th Dynasty down by several decades. The knock-on effect reaches everything dated by cross-reference to Dynasty 12 — including the strata at Tell el-Dab'a (§VII's site).

SENUSRET III'S REIGN (19 OR 39 YEARS)

The Turin Canon's figure for Senusret III is damaged between readings of 19 and 39 years' significance; his highest certain attestation long stopped at Year 19, and a building control note from his Dahshur pyramid dated Year 30 re-opened the long reading. Shaw prints c. 1874–1855; the most common textbook dates, 1878–1839, encode the long reign. This archive follows Shaw and flags the variant wherever the king's dates are given (see the Middle Kingdom article, §XII).

THE NEW KINGDOM START (c. 1570–1540 BCE)

The radiocarbon model of Bronk Ramsey et al. (2010) places the New Kingdom's start between 1570 and 1544 BCE (§XIX); the Ebers Sothic date for Amenhotep I's Year 9 can be computed from different observation points and yields placements decades apart. Shaw's 1550 BCE is a considered midpoint, not a measurement.

THERA (THE LIVE DISPUTE)

The eruption of Thera (Santorini) ends the Late Minoan IA phase and should synchronise the Aegean, Egypt and the Levant at a stroke. Archaeological linkage long placed it c. 1530–1500 BCE, early in the 18th Dynasty; radiocarbon has consistently pointed earlier. Sturt Manning's Bayesian re-analysis (PLOS ONE, 2022), integrating the eruption-layer radiocarbon with the Theran and Aegean stratigraphic sequences, gives c. 1606–1589 BCE at 68.3% probability (1609–1560 at 95.4%) — squarely in the Second Intermediate Period. The archive reports both placements and builds no date on either: the dispute is the current state of the science, and §XIX's corroboration of Shaw by radiocarbon does not extend to this one horizon.

Earlier periods carry proportionally larger uncertainties, absorbed by wider "c." margins rather than separate named schemes: the Bayesian radiocarbon model for the Predynastic and First Dynasty (Dee et al., 2013) compresses the run-up to state formation, placing the start of the Naqada sequence later than the traditional framework assumed while confirming Aha's accession close to 3100 BCE (see the Predynastic article, §XXI). From the reign of Psamtik I (664 BCE) the divergences vanish: dated documents in several languages lock the framework to the year, and from Alexander onward, to the day.

XXIV. Synchronisms — Egypt Among Its Neighbours

A synchronism is a documented meeting between two chronologies: a letter from one king to another, a treaty naming both parties, a foreign object in a dated layer. Synchronisms are how the floating regnal construction of §XXII is lashed to its neighbours, and a few carry unusual weight.

The Amarna Letters (§IX) bind the late 18th Dynasty to Babylon, Assyria, Mitanni and Hatti in a single correspondence: Amenhotep III and Akhenaten exchange letters with Kadashman-Enlil I and Burnaburiash II of Babylon, Ashur-uballit I of Assyria, Tushratta of Mitanni and Suppiluliuma I of Hatti, so the middle years of all five chronologies must overlap in the pattern the letters describe. A generation later the Kadesh treaty (Year 21 of Ramesses II, c. 1259 BCE) ties Egypt to Hattusili III's Hatti, and the Hittite and Assyrian sequences in turn reach the Assyrian eponym (limmu) lists, which are anchored absolutely by a solar eclipse recorded in the eponymy of Bur-Sagale, computed to 15 June 763 BCE. From that eclipse, Assyrian chronology counts down year by exact year — and everything synchronised with Assyria inherits the anchor, including the Kushite and Saite kings whose wars the Assyrian annals narrate (§XI, §XXI).

In the other direction, Egyptian objects in foreign layers and foreign objects in Egyptian ones give material synchronisms of varying strength: the alabaster lid of the Hyksos king Khayan found at Knossos links Avaris to the Minoan palace sequence (the same web the Thera dispute of §XXIII strains against); Minoan-style frescoes at Tell el-Dab'a belong to the early 18th Dynasty palace built over the Hyksos city, not to Hyksos rule itself, as their excavator has himself revised (see The Hyksos Exchange, §III). Scarabs, seals and pottery seriation extend the web where texts fail, at correspondingly lower confidence. The archive's rule for all of it: a synchronism is evidence for the overlap of two reigns, not for the absolute date of either, until an anchor of §XXII's roof touches the chain.

XXV. The Civil Calendar in Detail — The Wandering Year

Beneath every date in this article sits the civil calendar: twelve months of thirty days, grouped in three seasons of four months — Ꜣḫt, the inundation; Prt, the growing season; Šmw, the harvest — plus five epagomenal days at the year's end, 365 days in all. It is one of the earliest precisely structured calendars known, and its famous defect is its precision: with no leap day, the calendar year drifts against the solar year by one day every four years, so New Year's Day wanders through all the seasons over the 1,460-year Sothic cycle of §XIV. A festival dated "I Akhet 1" is a fixed point in the civil count and a moving point in the agricultural year, which is why temple lunar calendars ran alongside the civil one for festival timing.

The wandering is a gift to chronology and a trap for the unwary in equal measure. Because the drift is perfectly regular, a record that a seasonal event — above all the heliacal rising of Sirius — fell on a given civil date in a given regnal year can be computed in both directions, which is the entire basis of §XIV. But the computation assumes the event was observed, not predicted or conventionally assigned, and that the observation point (Memphis, Thebes, Elephantine — Sirius rises on different days at different latitudes) is the one the calculation uses. Each assumption moves the result by years or decades, and §XXIII's high/low alternatives are, at bottom, different verdicts on those assumptions. The calendar also explains the Egyptians' own double vision of time (§XVII): a state that watched its New Year migrate through flood, sowing and harvest had empirical proof, renewed every generation, that cyclical time (nḥḥ) and fixed record (ḏt) are different things.

For the reader of the period articles, the practical rules are these. Dates before 664 BCE in this archive are Shaw's conventional placements and carry an implicit margin that grows with age — years in the New Kingdom, decades in the Middle Kingdom, generations in the Predynastic. Regnal-year dates quoted from inscriptions ("Year 33 of Apepi," "Year 20 of Piye") are primary data and are quoted as such, never silently converted. And where two schemes genuinely divide the field — Senusret III, the New Kingdom's start, Thera — the articles name the alternative rather than choosing invisibly. The timeline is a scaffold with its joints showing, deliberately: that is what makes it load-bearing.

XXVI. The King Lists Compared — Six Witnesses, Six Agendas

Sections XV and XVIII introduced the lists; this section sets them side by side, because no two were made for the same purpose and none can be read as a neutral spreadsheet. Every list is an argument about legitimacy wearing the clothes of an archive, and the chronologer uses each one only for what its agenda lets it tell the truth about.

Read together, the six witnesses triangulate. Where Abydos and Turin agree on order, order is settled. Where Turin alone gives a reign length, the length is a claim. Where Manetho's dynastic boundary cuts across a continuous family — as at the 12th/13th and 17th/18th transitions — the boundary is bookkeeping, and the period articles in this archive say so where it matters.

XXVII. A Worked Example — How Ramesses II Gets His Dates

One reign, traced through every storey of §XXII, shows the method better than any summary. Take Ramesses II, printed in this archive as c. 1279–1213 BCE, 66 years.

Sequence is uncontested: the Abydos and Saqqara lists, his own monuments, and the succession of his officials place him between Seti I and Merenptah in a direct father–son line. Reign length rests on his highest attested date, a Year 67 document horizon consistent with a reign of 66 full years, and on the count of his Sed festivals, celebrated from Year 30 at roughly two-to-three-year intervals thereafter — fourteen are recorded, a pattern that fits a reign ending in the mid-60s and no other length.

Absolute placement comes by synchronism (§XXIV). His Year 5 battle at Kadesh against Muwatalli II and his Year 21 treaty with Hattusili III tie his reign to the Hittite sequence; the Hittite sequence is tied, a few generations on, to Assyria; and Assyria is anchored by the 763 BCE eclipse in the eponym canon, from which its kings count down without a gap. A lunar date in Ramesses II's Year 52 (a temple lunar observation preserved at Thebes) then selects, among the placements the synchronisms allow, the one the archive prints: accession in 1279 BCE. The residual uncertainty is a matter of a few years — the margin that the "c." in his dates honestly represents.

The contrast with the deep past is the point. For Ramesses II, four independent evidence chains converge within a decade. For Narmer, one radiocarbon model and a relative sequence must do the same work, and the margin is a century. The master table (§XXI) prints both reigns in the same typeface; §§XXII–XXV exist so that the reader never mistakes the typeface for the certainty.

XXVIII. The First-Millennium Anchors — Assyria, Babylon, Persia

Below the New Kingdom, the scaffold of §XXII acquires something the Bronze Age never had: neighbouring chronologies that are themselves anchored to the year. Three contacts do most of the work, and the period articles of the Third Intermediate and Late Periods lean on all three.

Assyria. The Assyrian eponym canon names an official for every year and is anchored absolutely by the solar eclipse in the eponymy of Bur-Sagale (15 June 763 BCE). Assyrian annals then narrate, with regnal years attached, the events that structure Egypt's 25th and 26th Dynasties: Esarhaddon's conquest of Memphis in 671 BCE, Ashurbanipal's campaigns of 667 and the sack of Thebes in 663 BCE — dates the Egyptian side records without any era to hang them on. Where an Egyptian king and an Assyrian king meet in both records, the Egyptian reign inherits the Assyrian year.

The Hebrew Bible's Shishak. The one synchronism of the early first millennium that Egyptian evidence meets from the other side is the campaign of Shoshenq I, founder of the 22nd Dynasty, whose topographical list on the Bubastite Portal at Karnak names the Palestinian towns his army took, and whom scholarship identifies with the "Shishak" whose invasion the Book of Kings places in the fifth year of Rehoboam — computed to c. 925 BCE. The identification fixes Shoshenq's accession near 945 BCE and with it the whole Libyan-period framework, which is otherwise a tissue of overlapping local lines (§X).

Babylon and Persia. From Nebuchadnezzar II onward, Babylonian chronicles and the astronomical tradition behind Ptolemy's Canon of Kings — a list of Babylonian, Persian, Macedonian and Roman rulers with regnal years, anchored by recorded lunar eclipses — give the Late Period a dated grid in step with the Egyptian one. Cambyses' conquest (525 BCE), the two Persian periods, and Alexander's entry (332 BCE) are fixed in both grids at once. After 664 BCE the archive's chronology is not a reconstruction but a record, and its remaining uncertainties are of identification, not of arithmetic.

XXIX. Genealogical Chronology — Counting by Generations

Where king lists fail, family trees take over. Several Egyptian documents preserve private genealogies spanning centuries: the genealogy of the Memphite high priests of Ptah, set up under Shoshenq V and tracing the office through some sixty generations back toward the New Kingdom; the stela of Pasenhor, which under Shoshenq V lists the Libyan ancestry of the 22nd Dynasty house back to its forebear Buyuwawa; and the long priestly genealogies of Thebes and Memphis that thread the Third Intermediate Period's parallel lines into single family histories.

Genealogies convert to years only through a generation length — conventionally 25 to 30 years for office-holding father-to-son successions — and the conversion is the weakest arithmetic in this article: generations telescope (collateral successions and skipped names shorten the tree), offices pass to sons-in-law, and a genealogy drawn up to glorify its dedicator has every reason to lengthen itself. Used as a check rather than a clock, however, genealogies are powerful: they are how the overlapping 22nd, 23rd and 24th Dynasties are ordered at all, and a genealogy that refuses to fit a proposed royal chronology has repeatedly been the first sign that the chronology, not the family, was wrong.

This closes the archive's account of its own dates. The spine of §§II–XIII rests on lists (§§XV, XVIII, XXVI), reign arithmetic (§XXII), astronomical observations (§XIV), foreign anchors (§§XXIV, XXVIII), radiocarbon (§XIX) and family trees (this section) — six independent kinds of evidence, each named where it bears weight. A date in this archive is not a guess wearing a number; it is the current reading of that combined record, and the record is left visible so the reading can be checked.
