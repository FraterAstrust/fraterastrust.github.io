I. The Names — Kyphi, Kapet, and Senetjer

Kyphi is a Greek word wearing Egyptian clothes. The Greek κῦφι (kyphi, Latinised cyphi) transcribes the Egyptian kꜣp.t [Gardiner: V31-Q3-X1] [𓎡𓊪𓏏] — conventionally vocalised kapet — a word for a compounded, burnable perfume: a prepared mixture of resins, aromatics, wine, honey, and fruit, formed into pellets and laid on the censer. The archive's article on magic in practice already writes the word in this spelling, and Ritual Practice §V uses it for the sunset censing of the daily temple service. This article follows that spelling throughout. Where a source or a modern author writes "kapet," "kuphi," "cyphi," or "kyphi," the same Egyptian word is meant; the variation is transliteration, not substance.

The word matters because it is a category before it is a recipe. No single ancient text owns kyphi. The Egyptian inscriptions at Edfu give two recipes under the name; the temple at Philae gives a third, closely related; the medical papyri give earlier and simpler preparations; and the Greek writers of Sections VII and VIII give versions that overlap with the Egyptian ones without ever matching them exactly. Lise Manniche, whose Sacred Luxuries: Fragrance, Aromatherapy, and Cosmetics in Ancient Egypt (Cornell University Press, 1999) remains the standard modern synthesis of the whole subject, makes the comparative point that governs this article: when the different kyphi recipes are set side by side, there is considerable variation in the plant material added to a stable core, and substitutions occur — cardamom for cinnamon and cassia, for example, and pine in another place. Kyphi is therefore treated here as a family of compounded evening incenses built on one core — raisins, wine, and honey carrying ground resins and aromatics — and each witness is presented in its own right, with its own ingredients and its own sequence, rather than being flattened into a single "true" formula that no ancient source actually gives.

The generic word for incense is broader and older. snṯr [Gardiner: S29-N35-V13-D21] [𓋴𓈖𓍿𓂋] — senetjer — is the standard temple incense word, and its root carries the theology in itself: built on the same consonants as the verb "to make divine," snṯr is the substance whose smoke deifies the air it fills. Creasman and Yamamoto's survey of the African incense trade (African Archaeological Review 36, 2019) collects the Egyptian terminology for general and specific forms of incense and stresses how consistently the aromatics themselves were associated with foreign, southern sources — Nubia and Punt above all. What snṯr denoted as a material in any given period is a harder question, and Section IX takes it up in full: the word is conventionally rendered "frankincense," but the identification is period-sensitive and contested, and this article does not use the translation as if it were a chemical analysis.

The third name of the title is the carrier. mrḥt [Gardiner: G17-D21-V28-X1] [𓅓𓂋𓎛𓏏] — merhet — is oil or fat as a worked substance: the base into which aromatics were macerated, cooked, and compounded to make the unguents and perfumed oils of Sections XI and XII. Manniche's entry "Perfume" in the UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology (2009) states the structural fact in one sentence, paraphrased here: in Egypt, scent was released either as incense, burned, or prepared on a base of oils or fat and applied to the body. There was no third, alcohol-based form: pharaonic Egypt had no distilled perfume, and every Egyptian scent that was not smoke was fat. That single technological boundary organises the whole article. Kyphi is the smoke form at its most elaborate. The unguents are the fat form at theirs. The temple laboratory of Section III made both.

An invented spelling would be worse than none: the lexicographical problem of Section II begins precisely where confident spelling ends.

A last naming caution, and it is binding on everything that follows. One substance in these recipes was translated "myrrh" for generations and is not myrrh at all: ꜥnṯyw [Gardiner: D36-N35-V13-Z4-G43] [𓂝𓈖𓍿𓏭𓅱] — antiu. The Saqqara residue programme (Rageot et al., Nature 614, 2023) showed it to be a manufactured mixture of conifer oils or tars worked into animal fat. This article prints antiu untranslated wherever it appears, gives the full correction in Section X, and follows the archive's treatment in Mummification §XIV exactly. Myrrh itself — a real, imported Commiphora gum-resin — appears in these recipes under its own attestation and is never used as a gloss for antiu.

II. Evidence and Method — Walls, Papyri, Residues, and Greek Witnesses

Four kinds of evidence bear on Egyptian scent, and they answer different questions. Temple inscriptions — pre-eminently the so-called laboratory texts of Edfu, published in Émile Chassinat's great edition of the temple (Le temple d'Edfou), with parallels at Philae and references at Dendera — give recipes: named ingredients, stated quantities, and ordered procedures, carved in the rooms where the products were stored. They are the fullest procedural texts for any Egyptian scent, and Sections IV and V rest on them. Medical papyri — the Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BCE) above all, and the donation lists of Papyrus Harris I — give earlier, briefer preparations and the supply lists that fed the temples; the archive surveys them at Medical Papyri. Classical authors — Plutarch, Dioscorides, Galen, Pliny, Theophrastus — describe Egyptian scents from outside, often in detail, sometimes from lost Egyptian intermediaries such as Manetho's treatise On the Preparation of Kyphi, of which no copy survives. And residues and analyses — the chemical examination of surviving substances, vessel contents, and, most decisively for this archive, the labelled vessels of the Saqqara embalming workshop — test what the words actually denoted in at least one workshop at one date.

Manniche's assessment of this source base, in her paper "Ancient Scent — An Evaluation of the Sources" (Memnonia), is the methodological charter for what follows, and its central warning is quoted here in close paraphrase: the main, at times insurmountable, problem is lexicographical — how the ingredients are to be determined and the words translated. Some names are known from other contexts, or their general nature is known from the hieroglyphic determinative, the classifying sign that marks a word as a resin, a plant, a liquid, and so on. Beyond that, certainty thins quickly. It is through the classical writers, Manniche notes, that we obtain the most detailed descriptions of ancient scents, both their composition and their effect — which is precisely why Sections VII and VIII keep the Greek witnesses visibly separate from the Egyptian inscriptions: the Greeks are detailed, but they are describing a tradition already centuries old, through Greek categories, for Greek readers, and their detail is not the same thing as Egyptian attestation.

The older chemical literature sets the other boundary. A. Lucas's Ancient Egyptian Materials and Industries (first published 1926; fourth edition revised by J. R. Harris, 1962) devotes its fourth chapter to cosmetics, perfumes, and incense, and its method — describe each material, its composition where analysis allows, its use, its place of origin, and its date — is the method this article follows for materials. Lucas and Harris were working before modern residue chemistry, and where their identifications have been superseded — antiu is the standing example — the article says so in place and follows the newer analysis. Where no analysis exists, the article follows Manniche's lexicographical caution: an ingredient is presented with the identification the scholarship proposes, the degree of confidence the scholarship assigns it, and the alternatives where the scholarship divides. Peker, in Section IV, is the type case of an ingredient whose name is certain and whose plant is not.

One rule governs quotation throughout. Words are placed in quotation marks only where the translation named in the same paragraph was actually read for this article: Plutarch in Babbitt's Loeb translation (LacusCurtius); Victor Loret's Le kyphi, parfum sacré des anciens Égyptiens (1887) in its French text, with the English renderings supplied here and marked as this article's translations of Loret; and the short journal and museum statements named in place. Everything else — the Edfu inscription itself, Dioscorides, Galen, the medical papyri — is given in labelled paraphrase following the modern study named with it, usually Manniche's tabulations in Sacred Luxuries and her UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology entry. The Edfu recipe in particular is not quoted as if from the wall: Chassinat's edition is the scholarly basis for the text, Loret's 1887 reduction is the working form in which its quantities are usually cited, and Manniche's tabulation is the modern check on both. Where Loret and Manniche differ — and they do, on identifiable ingredients — the difference is printed, not resolved by silence (Section V).

// A NOTE ON RECONSTRUCTION //

A recipe text is not a smell. Even where every ingredient were identified — and Section IV shows how far from that the Edfu list stands — the proportions of volatile aromatics, the ripeness of the wine, the species behind a resin name, and two thousand years of plant breeding stand between a modern bench copy and the sunset censing of a Ptolemaic temple. Modern kyphi sold or compounded today is, at best, a documented interpretation of one named ancient witness, and honest makers say which witness they follow. This article's standard is the same: every procedure below names its source, and no procedure is presented as "the" Egyptian method where the sources give more than one.

III. The Temple Laboratory — Where the Recipes Are Written

The recipes of Sections IV, V, and XII are not library texts that happen to survive. They are wall texts, carved in a specific room of a specific temple: the small chamber conventionally called the laboratory in the Ptolemaic Temple of Horus at Edfu (built 237–57 BCE), with a parallel set in the Temple of Isis at Philae and further references at Dendera. The archive's dedicated treatment of the room, its inscriptions, and its place in the temple's working economy is The Temple Laboratory; this section gives only what the recipes themselves require the reader to know about where they stand.

Manniche's UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology entry makes the room's practical point, in paraphrase: the Edfu "laboratory," lacking light and ventilation, served in practice rather as a storeroom for the finished preparations, and its inscribed instructions may have been copied from a "Book of Unguent" — a work of that title is listed among the library books stored in the temple of Dendera (Chassinat 1966). The distinction matters for how the texts are read. The wall is an archive and a standard, a permanent reference copy in the room where the products were kept; it is not necessarily the bench at which the grinding and boiling were done, and its recipes are written as standards — stated quantities for a stated yield — rather than as a craftsman's day notes. That is exactly the form Sections IV and V reproduce: a bill of materials and a sequence, in the temple's own proportions.

The repertoire carved at Edfu is wider than kyphi. Aufrère's survey of the Edfu laboratory inscriptions (in A Cultural History of Chemistry: In Antiquity, Bloomsbury, 2022, summarising Aufrère 1998) lists the wall's recipes as including, in paraphrase: the finest ointment of styrax; the hekenu ointment; the medjes ointment; the nine ointments of the Ennead; the precious ointment; the ointment of divine mineral; the kyphi; and the pure natron pellets of the Ennead — together with descriptions of the different varieties of frankincense and styrax. Three preparation types organise the whole repertoire in Aufrère's analysis: fumigations (kyphi's class), fat-based cooked ointments, and resinous ointments for anointing the statues of the gods. The same study counts seventy-one basic ingredients across the Edfu recipes — a formulary, not a single formula — and notes the standard measure in which the liquid recipes are cast: the hin, a volume unit of about 0.48 litres, named at the head of a recipe together with the quantity to be produced. Section XII works one of the oil recipes in that form.

Time is the laboratory's hidden ingredient. Manniche's encyclopedia entry states, in paraphrase, that the entire operation of a temple perfume might take up to a year, even two, if the preparation of the base ingredient was included, and that before the main ingredients were approached, the oil or fat base itself was prepared — a pre-treatment whose details the Edfu texts specify with the same care as the compounding that followed. Set against that scale, kyphi's eleven-to-sixteen days (Section V) make it one of the laboratory's quicker products: a fortnight's manufacture for the censer, against a year or more for the great unguents. The daily service consumed both on the same morning — the oils at the anointing, the kyphi at the evening censing (Ritual Practice §§III and V) — and the laboratory existed to keep that consumption supplied at standard, year after year, reign after reign. Carving the standards in stone is what a temple does when it intends a product to outlast every individual perfumer.

IV. The Edfu Kyphi — The Ingredients, with Their Identifications

Edfu preserves two kyphi recipes. They name the same ingredients and differ in the quantities — two standards for the same product, whether for different grades, different festivals, or different scales of production the inscriptions, in the summaries available to this article, do not say. The ingredient list below is the shared Edfu list, in the order in which the procedure of Section V takes the ingredients up. Quantities are given in Section V in the form of Loret's 1887 working reduction, the reduction in which the recipe is most often cited, checked against Manniche's tabulation in Sacred Luxuries. Identifications follow Manniche and the UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology entry, with Loret's 1887 botanical names given where his reduction is the source of the quantity. Every identification that the scholarship itself marks as uncertain is marked uncertain here. That is not a defect of this table; it is the table's most important content.

Read as a bill of materials, the list divides itself by handling, and the division is the recipe's logic. There is a fruit-and-liquid base — raisins, wine, honey — that supplies bulk, sugar, and the medium in which everything else is steeped and bound. There is a resin group — frankincense (or terebinth resin: Section V), mastic, pine resin, myrrh — that supplies the smoke and the fixative strength. And there is an aromatic group — sweet flag, aspalathos, camel grass, mint, the barks, cyperus, juniper, pine kernels, peker — that supplies the volatile top of the scent and is therefore handled dry, ground fine, and never boiled on its own. The procedure keeps the three groups apart until the latest possible moments, for reasons any perfumer will recognise: the volatiles that make the aromatic group valuable are exactly what prolonged heat destroys. Egyptian temple perfumery, at Edfu, already works by that principle, and writes it into stone as sequence.

V. The Edfu Kyphi — The Preparation, Day by Day

What follows is the Edfu preparation as a workshop sequence. The sequence and the quantities are given in the form of Victor Loret's working reduction, Le kyphi, parfum sacré des anciens Égyptiens (Paris, 1887) — Loret's own summary, in his words as read in the French text, was offered "for anyone curious to have kyphi made in a perfumery laboratory," with the Egyptian weights reduced to French grams — checked at every stage against Lise Manniche's tabulation of the same Edfu recipe in Sacred Luxuries (1999). Loret's heading, in this article's translation of his French, reads: "Recipe to make 10,164 grams of kyphi, twice good, for use in worship." The total yield — just over ten kilograms — is the first fact to hold on to. This is not a pinch of incense for one evening. It is a production batch, sized to a temple's consumption, and its timetable is a production timetable. Where Manniche's tabulation differs from Loret's reduction in a way that affects the work, the difference is flagged in the stage where it occurs and collected in the adjudication note that closes the section.

STAGE 1 — THE SEVEN DRY AROMATICS: POUND, SIEVE, KEEP THE FINEST FRACTION

Materials (Loret's reduction, 270 grams of each; 1,890 grams in all): sweet flag (Acorus calamus), camel grass (Andropogon schoenanthus in Loret's nomenclature), mastic (Pistacia lentiscus), cassia, cinnamon, mint (Loret: Mentha piperita), and aspalathos (Loret: Convolvulus scoparius). Manniche's tabulation of the same first group names mastic, pine resin, sweet flag, aspalathos, camel grass, mint, and cinnamon — pine resin where Loret lists the second bark. The group is seven in both.

Work: Pound the seven together very fine in the mortar and pass the powder through a sieve. In Loret's French, as read for this article: "Pound very fine and sieve. Use only two fifths of the mass, the most fragrant and best-powdered part: 756 grams." The coarse remainder — three fifths of the batch by weight — is set aside from this product. The retained 756 grams is the recipe's aromatic concentrate: the finest, most volatile-rich fraction of the grinding, selected by particle size. Manniche's tabulation, as reproduced in the secondary literature, describes the discarded fraction differently — as a liquid portion amounting to two fifths, leaving three fifths as ground powder. The two reductions cannot both be right about the fraction, and the adjudication note below records the conflict without pretending to settle a hieroglyphic quantity from secondary summaries. What both agree on is the operation itself, and it is the operation that matters for the process: grind, sieve, and grade the powder; do not use the whole grinding indiscriminately.

STAGE 2 — THE SECOND FOUR: CRUSH, MOISTEN WITH WINE, REST ONE DAY

Materials (Loret's reduction, 270 grams of each; 1,080 grams in all): Phoenician juniper (Juniperus phoenicea), sweet acacia (Loret: Acacia farnesiana), henna (Loret: Lawsonia inermis), and cyperus (Loret: Cyperus longus). Manniche's tabulation names this second group as cyperus, juniper berries, pine kernels, and peker — two names in common (cyperus, juniper), and then a divergence that is the sharpest in the whole recipe: where Loret resolves two Egyptian plant names into acacia and henna, Manniche prints pine kernels for one and leaves the other, peker, honestly unidentified (Section IV). The stage is given here in Loret's form because his is the complete working reduction; the divergence is flagged, not hidden, and no modern maker should treat either resolution of peker as settled.

Work: Crush the four substances and moisten them with wine — 1,125 grams of wine in Loret's reduction — in a vessel (Manniche's tabulation specifies a copper vessel). Leave the moistened mass to rest one day: in Loret's French, "Leave for a day." Manniche's tabulation adds the working detail that about half the wine is absorbed by the powder and the rest is to be discarded. The rest is a maceration, not an idle pause: the wine extracts and softens, and the overnight steep is the recipe's first timed stage. Combined with the sieved powder of Stage 1, the eleven dry ingredients are now one wine-dampened aromatic body.

STAGE 3 — THE RAISINS: FIVE DAYS IN OASIS WINE

Materials (Loret's reduction): flesh of raisins, well cleaned — 1,260 grams — and wine of the Oasis — 1,440 grams. Manniche's tabulation gives the same operation at a larger stated scale (raisins and oasis wine each in the region of two and a half pounds in the reproduced summary's mixed units) and adds the preparation the Loret summary compresses: the raisins are steeped in the wine and ground together, with rind and pips removed.

Work: Mix the prepared raisin flesh and the oasis wine with the eleven ingredients of Stages 1 and 2, and leave the whole to rest five days. This is the recipe's long maceration, and its centre of gravity: for five days the wine works on raisin sugar, ground resin, and aromatic powder together, and the mass becomes a single steeped body rather than a mixture of parts. In Manniche's tabulation a boiling belongs to this part of the process — the combined mixture is boiled until it has reduced by one fifth — where Loret's reduction places its stated reduction at the honey stage (Stage 4). Both reductions agree that a reduction by one fifth is a specified, measured operation of the recipe, performed by weight; they differ on the stage to which they attach it. The archive prints the difference (see the adjudication note) and follows Loret for the stage order, since his is the continuous working text.

STAGE 4 — HONEY AND RESIN: COOK DOWN BY ONE FIFTH, COMBINE, REST FIVE DAYS

Materials (Loret's reduction): terebinth resin — 1,200 grams — and honey — 3,000 grams. Manniche's tabulation names the resin of this stage frankincense, at 1,213 grams against the honey, in a cauldron. The quantities agree to within a rounding; the resin's name does not. Section IV has already flagged the point: the Egyptian resin word at this stage of the inscription has been rendered "terebinth resin" by Loret and "frankincense" in Manniche's tabulation, and the archive does not choose between them here, because the choice is a lexicographical judgment about a Ptolemaic resin term of exactly the kind Section II warns against settling from summaries. What is certain is the operation, identical in both.

Work: Mix the resin and the honey and cook them together until the combined weight has reduced by one fifth — from 4,200 grams to the 3,360 grams that Loret's reduction states as the remainder. Cooking honey with resin does three things at once: it drives off water, it melts and disperses the resin through the honey, and it produces the hot, fluid binder that can coat and hold the steeped mass of Stage 3. Work the cooked honey-resin into the macerated aromatics and raisins, and leave the combined kyphi to rest five days more. The second five-day rest is a maturation: the hot binder cools, sets, and marries with the wine-steeped body, and the compound becomes handleable as a single mass.

STAGE 5 — THE MYRRH, LAST AND UNCOOKED: GRIND FINE AND WORK IN

Material (both reductions agree): myrrh, finely ground — 1,143 grams in Loret's reduction; Manniche's tabulation gives 1,155 grams. The agreement, to within twelve grams on a ten-kilogram batch, is the best internal check the two reductions offer on each other.

Work: Grind the myrrh fine and mix it into the rested mass. In Loret's closing line, in this article's translation: "Finely ground myrrh, 1,143 grams. Mix into the rest of the mass, which gives 10,164 grams of kyphi." Myrrh is the only major ingredient that is neither steeped nor cooked: it enters dry, at the end, at full strength — the same principle Manniche's encyclopedia entry states for Egyptian perfumery in general, in paraphrase, that sequence and timing were crucial because the ingredient added last would be the most pungent. The finished mass was then formed into small pellets — the form in which temple kyphi is shown and in which the earliest securely identified surviving incense sample, of late 18th-Dynasty date, was also found as pellets (Lucas and Harris's chapter, summarised in Section IX) — and dried for the censer. A pellet is a dose: one pellet, one censing, of a standard the laboratory had taken a fortnight to make.

// ADJUDICATION NOTE — WHERE THE TWO MODERN REDUCTIONS OF EDFU DIFFER //

This article relies on two modern presentations of the same inscription — Loret (1887) and Manniche (1999) — and they differ in four places: (1) the first group of seven, where Loret lists cassia and cinnamon and Manniche lists cinnamon and pine resin; (2) the sieved fraction of Stage 1, kept as two fifths (756 g) in Loret and described in the reproduced Manniche summary as three fifths retained after a liquid two fifths is discarded; (3) the second group of four, where Loret resolves two plant names as acacia and henna and Manniche gives pine kernels and the unidentified peker; and (4) the resin cooked with the honey, terebinth resin in Loret, frankincense in Manniche, with the boil-down by one fifth attached to different stages. These are differences between modern reductions of a Ptolemaic hieroglyphic text, not differences between ancient recipes, and they are printed here because a reader who meets only one reduction elsewhere should know that the other exists. Settling them requires the hieroglyphic text in Chassinat's edition and the lexicography of the individual plant names, item by item; that work is beyond the summaries read for this article, and the article does not pretend to have done it. The sequence, the rests (one day, five days, five days), the reduction by one fifth, the final uncooked myrrh, and the ten-kilogram yield are common to both reductions and are stated without qualification.

Count the timetable whole. One day for the wine-moistened second group; five days for the raisin maceration; five days for the matured compound after the honey stage — eleven days of stated rests, plus the grinding, sieving, cooking, and pellet-forming days at either end. Ritual Practice §V's summary — "eleven days, minimum, stand between the raw aromatics and the first pellet" — is this timetable counted from the inscriptions' own rests. The evening incense of the daily service was therefore never an improvisation. Behind every sunset censing stood a batch begun a fortnight earlier, by a laboratory working batches in rotation, to a standard carved on its own storeroom wall.

VI. Philae and the Earlier Witnesses — Ebers, Harris, and the Temple Variants

The Philae recipe, inscribed in the laboratory texts of the Temple of Isis, is Edfu's sibling, not its copy. In the summaries read for this article (Manniche's comparative discussion; the University of Pennsylvania's medical-list notes on the Edfu and Philae inscriptions together), the Philae preparation is described in the same operational grammar: equal amounts of sweet flag, aromatic rush (camel grass), terebinth resin, cassia, mint, and possibly aspalathus are ground and sieved together; that powder is then combined with separately prepared concoctions — wine with juniper berries, cyperus, and other plants; raisins with wine; and frankincense with honey — and the addition of finely ground myrrh completes the recipe. The architecture is identical to Edfu's: a sieved dry group, separately prepared wet concoctions, a honey-resin stage, myrrh last. The proportions and the grouping differ in detail. Two temples, one technology, two house standards — which is what "kyphi" as a category (Section I) means in practice.

Behind the Ptolemaic walls stand two much earlier Egyptian witnesses, and both are lists rather than procedures. The Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BCE) contains the earliest recipe under the kyphi name, in the summaries read here (Manniche; Ancient Egypt Online's compilation from Manniche): a preparation for purifying the home and sweetening clothes and breath, whose named ingredients are honey, frankincense (so rendered in the compilation; on the antiu question in such lists see Section X), mastic, sweet flag (the compilation's "genen"), pine kernels, cyperus, camel grass, an ingredient rendered "inektun," and cinnamon — prepared, in the summary's account, by the simple method of boiling the ingredients in honey. There are no raisins in the Ebers list as reproduced, no wine stages, no five-day rests. Whether the raisin base was assumed, as the compilation suggests it may have been, or whether the New Kingdom household preparation genuinely lacked it, the papyrus as summarised does not say. The archive prints the list as a list and the method as a single boiling, and notes the distance — fifteen centuries and a whole technology of staged maceration — between Ebers' honey-boiling and Edfu's fortnight.

Papyrus Harris I, the great donation record of Ramesses III (compiled under Ramesses IV, 20th Dynasty), does not give a recipe at all. It records the delivery, to temples, of six ingredients that the Edfu recipe also names — mastic, pine resin (or pine wood), camel grass, mint, sweet flag, and cinnamon — so that the temples could prepare kyphi. The inference drawn in the compilation, and adopted here as an inference, is that the remaining Edfu ingredients — raisins, wine, honey — were not delivered because the temples could source them locally: the Harris list is a list of what central stores had to supply, and what it omits is as informative as what it names. Read together, the three Egyptian witnesses give the tradition's shape across fifteen hundred years: a New Kingdom household fumigant boiled in honey (Ebers), a New Kingdom temple supply chain for a compounded product whose recipe the papyrus does not need to state (Harris), and the Ptolemaic temple standards in full procedural form (Edfu, Philae). The Greek witnesses of the next section enter the tradition at its Ptolemaic end — and describe, in part, a different product.

EBERS (c. 1550 BCE)

Earliest named recipe. Household and breath fumigant. Nine named ingredients as reproduced in the modern compilations; method a single boiling in honey. No raisin or wine stages in the list as summarised.

HARRIS I (20TH DYNASTY)

No recipe: a supply list. Six Edfu ingredients delivered from central stores to temples for kyphi-making; raisins, wine, and honey absent, plausibly because locally sourced (inference, labelled as such).

EDFU (PTOLEMAIC)

Two full recipes, same ingredients, differing quantities. Sixteen ingredients; staged grinding, sieving, wine macerations of one and five days, a honey-resin cook reduced by one fifth, myrrh last; yield 10,164 g in Loret's reduction.

PHILAE (PTOLEMAIC)

Same operational architecture as Edfu — sieved dry group, separate wet concoctions, honey stage, myrrh last — in its own grouping and proportions. The temple variant that proves kyphi a category, not a formula.

VII. The Greek Witnesses — Plutarch, Dioscorides, and Galen

The Greek witnesses are given separately, in their own section, under their own names, because the archive's rule for this subject is that a Greek description of an Egyptian product is evidence for the Greek reception of that product first, and for the Egyptian product only second, and only where the Egyptian sources corroborate it. Three witnesses matter: Plutarch, who gives the theology and the famous list of sixteen; Dioscorides, who gives the shortest working recipe; and Galen, who preserves, at third hand, the most medicalised one.

Plutarch, De Iside et Osiride 79–80 — The Day in Three Scents, and the Sixteen

Plutarch (c. 46–after 119 CE), writing his essay on Isis and Osiris for a Greek readership, closes it with the incense offerings. The translation relied on and quoted in this section is Frank Cole Babbitt's Loeb translation, as read on LacusCurtius (University of Chicago). In §52, in Babbitt's rendering as read there, Plutarch states the daily scheme in one sentence, here paraphrased closely: every day the priests make a triple offering of incense to the Sun — resin at sunrise, myrrh at midday, and the so-called kyphi at sunset — and he promises the reasons later. Sections 79 and 80 keep the promise, and their reasoning is Plutarch's own, a Greek philosophical physiology of air: the night air, he argues in paraphrase, grows dense and weighs the body down, so at rising they burn resin to revive and purify it; at midday the sun draws heavy exhalations from the earth, and myrrh's heat dissolves and scatters them; and kyphi, the compound, belongs to the night for reasons §80 develops.

The list itself is quoted here from Babbitt's translation as read on LacusCurtius, because it is the most quoted sentence in the whole kyphi literature and the archive will not paraphrase what it can quote exactly: "Cyphi is a compound composed of sixteen ingredients: honey, wine, raisins, cyperus, resin, myrrh, aspalathus, seselis, mastich, bitumen, rush, sorrel, and in addition to these both the junipers, of which they call one the larger and one the smaller, cardamum, and calamus." And on the making, in the same translation: "These are compounded, not at random, but while the sacred writings are being read to the perfumers as they mix the ingredients." Two of Plutarch's details are corroborated by the Egyptian side and are adopted here on that strength: the compounding was ordered, not indiscriminate (the Edfu stages are nothing if not ordered), and it was accompanied by recitation — the lector's voice over the bench is the same pairing of hand and word that the embalming Ritual shows in Mummification §XIII. Plutarch's editor's note on the passage (LacusCurtius, note 398) adds the scholarly pedigree: his source for kyphi is associated with Manetho (fragment 87 in Müller's collection) — the lost Egyptian treatise On the Preparation of Kyphi standing behind the Greek list.

What Plutarch then does with the sixteen is his own work, and is labelled here as his allegory, in paraphrase. The number sixteen, he observes, is the square of a square, and the only square number whose perimeter equals its area — and he immediately discounts the observation himself, in Babbitt's rendering, saying that its contribution to the subject is very slight. The real work of the compound, in his account, is physiological and oneiric: its aromatic ingredients give off a sweet and beneficent exhalation by which the air is changed and the body is moved gently toward sleep, loosening the day's cares "as if they were knots," without wine; the dreaming faculty it brightens "like a mirror." And the use was not only fumigation: "They use cyphi as both a potion and a salve," Babbitt renders him, taken internally as a cleanser of the internal organs. His closing explanation is openly symbolic — resin and myrrh are simple, solar substances, products of the sun's action on trees, fit for the day; kyphi, compounded of ingredients of all sorts of qualities, some delighting in night, dew, and shadow, is fit for nightfall, when the air itself is a composite of many lights. It is a beautiful account. It is also a Greek philosopher's account, written a century and more after the Edfu walls were carved, and Section VIII separates what in it is Egypt from what in it is Plutarch.

Dioscorides, De Materia Medica I.24 — The Ten Ingredients

Dioscorides (1st century CE), in the pharmacopoeia that governed Mediterranean medicine for fifteen centuries, gives kyphi a short chapter (Book I, chapter 24 in the usual numbering; chapter 25 in some editions). This article gives his recipe in labelled paraphrase, following Manniche's tabulation in Sacred Luxuries of the recipe in Lily Y. Beck's translation of De Materia Medica (Olms-Weidmann, 2005): kyphi, he says in paraphrase, is an incense preparation pleasing to the gods, which the priests in Egypt use lavishly; it is also mixed into antidotes and given in drinks to asthmatics; there are many ways of making it, and he gives one. His ten ingredients are: sun raisins, old wine, honey, myrrh, pure (cleansed) resin, plump juniper berries, sweet flag (calamus), camel grass, aspalathus, and cyperus. Manniche's tabulation gives his quantities in modern equivalents — raisins at about five and a half kilograms and resin at about two and a quarter kilograms dominating a batch in which the myrrh is a mere few dozen grams — and his method, in paraphrase: stone the raisins and work them up with the wine and the myrrh; grind and sieve the remaining dry ingredients and mix them in; let the whole steep for one day; boil the honey until it thickens, mix the melted resin carefully into it, combine everything thoroughly, and store the product in an earthenware pot.

Set Dioscorides beside Edfu and the relationship is exact and instructive. The operations are the Edfu operations in miniature — raisin base worked with wine, sieved dry aromatics, a one-day steep, a honey-and-resin cook, combination, storage — compressed from a fortnight to about two days and from sixteen ingredients to ten. The ingredients are the Edfu core with the temple's exotics stripped out: no mastic, no barks, no mint, no pine kernels, no peker. Manniche's judgment, adopted here, is that Dioscorides' version reads as primarily an antidote recipe — a physician's kyphi, valued for what it did in a drink for the asthmatic — rather than the temple's fumigant at full strength. The Greek medical tradition did not misunderstand kyphi; it selected from it, for its own purposes, and the selection is visible in the list.

Galen, On Antidotes II.2 — Rufus, in Verse, at Third Hand

Galen (129–c. 216 CE), in the second book of On Antidotes, preserves a kyphi that reaches him through two intermediaries, and the chain is part of the evidence: the recipe is that of Rufus of Ephesus (1st–2nd century CE), versified by Servilius Damocrates, who cast his formulas in iambic verse to make them memorable, and quoted by Galen from Damocrates. This article gives it in labelled paraphrase following Manniche's tabulation in Sacred Luxuries. Rufus's list runs to fifteen ingredients: raisins (skins and pips removed), a little wine, sufficient honey, burnt resin, bdellium (its "nails"), camel grass, sweet flag, pure cyperus, saffron, spikenard, aspalathus, cassia, and cinnamon — with cardamom permitted as a substitute for the cinnamon, the substitution Manniche's comparative discussion (Section I) takes as characteristic of the whole tradition. His method, in paraphrase: mash the honey and raisins together first; grind the bdellium and myrrh with wine until the mixture has the consistency of runny honey, and combine; grind the remaining ingredients separately and add them; form the paste into pellets for burning. The stated uses, in the tradition Galen transmits, are temple purification and a remedy against snakebite; and Galen's own clinical note, in paraphrase, is that kyphi was given to drink, a drachm at a time, for sufferers in the liver, the lungs, and other internal parts.

Rufus's list is the Greek tradition at its most expansive, and its expansions are diagnostic. Saffron, spikenard, and bdellium are not in the Edfu list at all; they are the prestige aromatics of the Roman-era Mediterranean pharmacopoeia, and their presence marks a recipe that has travelled — from temple fumigant to imperial antidote, gathering expensive ingredients as antidotes do. By the time the later Greek medical tradition (Paul of Aegina, 7th century CE) is classifying a "solar" kyphi of thirty-six ingredients and a "lunar" kyphi of twenty-eight, the Egyptian evening incense has become a category of compound antidote in which the temple original is one ancestor among several. The archive reports that endpoint as the tradition's own trajectory, on the standard summaries, and goes no further into it: the Egyptian article ends where the Egyptian evidence does.

VIII. What the Greek Tradition Added, and What It Misunderstood

The comparison can now be made cleanly, because both sides are on the table. The Greek tradition preserved the core with real fidelity: raisins, wine, and honey in every Greek list; the resin and the aromatic grasses in nearly all; the ordered, recitation-accompanied compounding in Plutarch; the pellet form in Galen's Rufus; the evening and purificatory associations throughout. A reader who knew only the Greeks would still know what kind of thing kyphi was. That preservation is not trivial — Manetho's lost treatise stands behind it, and Egyptian priests in a Hellenised Egypt are its likeliest channel — and this article credits it before it criticises anything.

The additions are equally clear. Plutarch's list contains ingredients with no counterpart in the Edfu reductions as read here: bitumen, seselis (seseli), and sorrel, and a rush distinct from cyperus, together with the doubled juniper (larger and smaller) where Edfu names one. Some of these may render Egyptian words that the reductions consulted resolve differently — the lexicographical caution of Section II applies to Greek lists too — but as the lists stand, the Greek sixteen and the Edfu sixteen are different sixteens that share a core and a count. The count itself then does Greek work: Plutarch's square-of-a-square allegory (Section VII) is an interpretation no Egyptian text offers, offered by a philosopher for whom number was theology, and even he rates its explanatory value as slight. The later medical tradition adds its own layer: saffron, spikenard, and bdellium in Rufus; indications for asthma, liver, lungs, and snakebite in Dioscorides and Galen; the drachm dose. None of that is in the Edfu inscription, whose stated purpose is cultic — kyphi "for use in worship," in Loret's heading — and whose medical afterlife is an Egyptian story too (Section XVI), but a separate one, told by the papyri rather than by the laboratory wall.

The misunderstandings are subtler, and two are worth naming precisely. The first is generalisations of the daily scheme. Plutarch's resin at dawn, myrrh at noon, kyphi at sunset is presented in Ritual Practice §V, following him, as the Ptolemaic matching of the day's three services to three censings — a real late scheme, in a real source. But it is one late scheme, and the archive does not project it backwards over three thousand years of daily cult in which snṯr [Gardiner: S29-N35-V13-D21] [𓋴𓈖𓍿𓂋] censings are attested without Plutarch's tidy solar symmetry. The second misunderstanding is the reverse projection of kyphi the antidote onto kyphi the incense: because the Greek physicians drank it, popular accounts present the temple product itself as a medicine that was also burned. The Egyptian evidence keeps the uses distinct in its sources — the laboratory wall compounds for worship; the medical papyri prescribe for the body — even where the same name, and plausibly the same compound, serves both. Sections XVI holds that distinction in the Egyptian material itself.

The Greeks did not invent kyphi, and they did not simply copy it. They received a temple fumigant, kept its core, allegorised its number, medicalised its use, and enriched its list — and their versions, read carefully, are the best evidence for how a sacred Egyptian product became a Mediterranean commodity.

IX. Incense at Large — Senetjer, Frankincense, Myrrh, and the Road from Punt

Kyphi was the most elaborate incense Egypt made, but it was an elaboration of something far older and far more voluminous: the daily burning of resin as such. Temples burned incense at every service; the offering lists put "incense on fire" at the head of the gifts; the dead were censed on the road to the tomb; and the state's appetite for aromatic resins was, as Creasman and Yamamoto's survey establishes, one of the organising facts of Egyptian foreign trade from the late Predynastic period to the Ptolemaic. Egypt produced almost none of the resins it burned. Its own contribution was the compounding — the laboratory — and the demand. The resins came by trade, overwhelmingly from the south: from or through Nubia, and from Punt, the incense land on the African and Arabian shores of the southern Red Sea, in the region of modern Eritrea, Somalia, and the facing Arabian coast (Manniche, UEE entry; Creasman and Yamamoto 2019). Punt was called Ta-Netjer, "God's Land," and its marvels — the Egyptian word for the products of gift-exchange with lands beyond Egypt's political reach — were headed by its aromatics.

The two great resins must be distinguished as materials, because the texts' failure always to distinguish them is the subject of the paragraph after next. Frankincense (olibanum) is the gum-resin of Boswellia trees — in the southern Arabian and Somali range, Boswellia sacra and its relatives — obtained, as the trade literature describes the harvest, by cutting and peeling the bark and collecting the exuded tears after they have hardened in the air. Myrrh is the gum-resin of Commiphora trees and shrubs, harvested the same way from the same broad region. Both travel well — light, stable, valuable by weight — and both burn with the fragrant smoke that made them, for three thousand years, among the most desired substances in the ancient world. Hatshepsut's famous expedition to Punt (c. 1470 BCE), carved on her mortuary temple at Deir el-Bahari, brought back not only resin by the sack but living incense trees for transplanting to Egypt — the state's attempt to shorten the supply line by moving the source, and, on the usual reading of the sequel, an attempt the Egyptian climate defeated.

Now the identification problem, stated at its proper size. The Egyptian word conventionally rendered "frankincense," snṯr [Gardiner: S29-N35-V13-D21] [𓋴𓈖𓍿𓂋], is a word whose material reference the scholarship itself treats as unstable across periods and contexts. Manniche's encyclopedia entry notes that traditionally frankincense and myrrh came from Punt, but that Syria-Palestine is a known source of Pistacia resin — and a Pistacia resin is among the materials that have been proposed for snṯr in particular contexts, so that the same Egyptian word may cover different traded resins at different dates, as supply and temple vocabulary shifted. Creasman and Yamamoto's survey is built on exactly this instability: it catalogues the Egyptian terms for general and specific incenses together with their proposed modern identifications, proposed being the operative word. The archive's practice follows from that: in this article snṯr is translated "frankincense" only where the conventional rendering is the one the cited source uses, the word itself is printed wherever the identification does work, and no chemical claim is ever built on the translation alone. The earliest material sample described as securely identifiable as incense — small pellets comparable with those shown on the monuments, of late 18th-Dynasty date, in Lucas and Harris's summary — is a salutary reminder of how late even the material evidence begins, for a practice the texts attest a millennium earlier.

The harvest and the road complete the process picture at the supply end. A resin tear is a wound product: the tree is cut, the exudate wells, hardens in the sun and air, and is gathered as tears, sorted by colour and purity — the Ptolemaic inscriptions' fine-grained designations for gums and resins by age, colour, and texture (Manniche, UEE entry) are a grading vocabulary, the temple buyer specifying grades of a traded commodity. From Punt the resins moved by sea up the Red Sea to the Egyptian ports and across the Eastern Desert wadis to the Nile, or by the overland and river routes through Nubia; the spices of the kyphi lists — the barks, cardamom — came by caravan from further afield still, through the same entrepôts. Every pellet of Edfu kyphi therefore embodied a supply web reaching from Somali and Arabian resin groves and Asian spice country to a storeroom in Upper Egypt. The laboratory was the last, smallest, and most documented link in that chain — and the only one that wrote its procedures down in stone.

X. The Antiu Correction — A Manufactured Product, Not Myrrh

This section exists because a translation error of long standing touches the recipes of this article directly, and because the archive has already adjudicated it, on chemical evidence, in Mummification §XIV. The substance ꜥnṯyw [Gardiner: D36-N35-V13-Z4-G43] [𓂝𓈖𓍿𓏭𓅱] — antiu — was rendered "myrrh" in generations of Egyptological translation, on philological conjecture, and older renderings of scent and embalming texts print the equation throughout. The Saqqara embalming workshop (26th Dynasty), excavated by the Saqqara Saite Tombs Project and analysed by Rageot and colleagues (Nature 614, 2023), tested the equation directly, because its vessels carried their Egyptian labels into the laboratory: all five vessels labelled antiu contained a mixture of cedar oil or tar and juniper/cypress oil or tar blended with animal fat. The authors' conclusion, adopted by this archive as binding, is that at Saqqara antiu was a product purposefully manufactured from at least two coniferous oils or tars worked into a fat base — and that its translation as a raw material such as myrrh is excluded.

The consequences for reading scent texts are practical, and this article observes them throughout. First, wherever an older translation of a recipe or a ritual prints "myrrh" for a word that is in fact antiu, the substance in the vessel was, on the Saqqara evidence, a compounded conifer-and-fat preparation — scented, workable, and made in the workshop, not imported as a tear resin. The first anointing of the head in the embalming Ritual, for example, is an anointing with antiu so understood (Mummification §XIII). Second, genuine myrrh — the Commiphora resin of Section IX, imported from Punt and Arabia — is a different substance with its own attestations, and it keeps them: Herodotus's grade-one cavity filling, the Edfu recipe's final uncooked addition (Section V), and Plutarch's midday censing all concern myrrh in contexts where the sources and the tradition support the resin, and none of them is antiu. Third, compilations of the Ebers kyphi list that print "frankincense (antiu)" — equating the two words in a parenthesis, as the widely used online compilation read for Section VI does — reproduce exactly the conflation the residue evidence refutes, whichever of the two substances the Ebers word in fact denotes. This article therefore prints the Ebers item with its uncertainty visible (Section VI) and builds nothing on it.

The antiu result generalises, and Mummification §XIV draws the moral for the embalming workshop: the embalmers' named substances were, again and again, recipes — formulated mixtures, heated and blended in the workshop — and the philological habit of matching each name to one raw commodity was the error. The Saqqara programme found the same pattern in sft [Gardiner: S29-I9-X1] [𓋴𓆑𓏏] — sefet, third of the Seven Sacred Oils (Section XV) — which proved to be a fat-based scented unguent with plant additives. A temple laboratory that manufactures its named preparations from traded raw materials is a different institution from a storeroom that merely issues imported commodities, and it is the institution the Edfu walls describe: seventy-one basic ingredients (Section III), compounded to standard, under names that denote products, not plants.

XI. Unguents and Perfumed Oils — Bases, Maceration, and the Pressing Cloth

An Egyptian perfume that was not burned was a fat that had been taught to smell. The technology has three parts — the base, the extraction, and the compounding — and each is documented well enough to give as procedure. The sources for this section are Manniche's "Perfume" entry in the UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology (2009) for the materials and methods in general, the Edfu laboratory texts for the temple form of the compounding (Section XII), and the tomb depictions of perfume-making for the extraction workshop's choreography.

The Bases — Oils and Fats

Manniche's entry lists the available base materials for scent to be applied to the body, in paraphrase, as follows. Local plant oils: sesame (Sesamum indicum), castor (Ricinus communis), balanos (Balanites aegyptiaca, the desert date), and moringa (Moringa oleifera). In limited quantity, and probably imported: olive oil and almond oil. For a more solid unguent, the fat of ox, sheep, or fowl. And, as a further base the entry notes, the pulp or liquid of the starchy seeds of an as-yet-unidentified Ethiopian tree. The choice among them is a perfumer's choice in the strict sense. A base must dissolve and hold volatile aromatics, spread on skin or statue, resist going rancid for as long as the product must keep, and contribute as little interfering smell of its own as possible. Balanos and moringa oils are valued in the modern literature on Egyptian perfumery for exactly the stability and relative neutrality that made them the prestige bases; castor, thick and heavy, serves medicinal and cheaper preparations; animal fats give the solid unguent its body, the form that fills the small jars and, in the Saqqara analyses, carries the manufactured preparations of Section X. Most surviving analyses of perfume-jar contents available to the older literature, Manniche cautions, specify only "fatty matter" — the bases are better attested as a repertoire than any single jar's contents are attested as a recipe.

Extraction — Maceration, Boiling, and the Wringing Cloth

To move scent from plant to base, in Manniche's summary phrase, the Egyptians used maceration and boiling (Serpico and White 2000, cited in the entry). Maceration is steeping: the flowers, leaves, resins, or ground aromatics stand in the oil or fat — cold or warmed — for the days or weeks the material requires, and the base draws out the soluble aromatics. Boiling, and the gentler cooking of the Edfu recipes, does the same work faster and at higher cost to the most volatile components, which is why the temple recipes reserve cooking for resins and honey and keep the fine aromatics out of the pot (Sections V and XII state the principle in both its homes). The tomb depictions supply the extraction's most distinctive Egyptian operation, in paraphrase of the standard descriptions: the macerated flowers or plants were placed in a cloth, and the cloth was twisted and wrung — in the fullest scenes, stretched between poles and torqued by teams — so that the scented oil was pressed out of the spent material and dripped into a vessel below. Pressing by torsion is the same mechanical family as the sack press of the vineyard (Food and Drink §XV), applied to a far more valuable liquid: what the cloth yields is the extract, and what stays in the cloth is waste.

Later perfumery would call one Egyptian-adjacent technique enfleurage — the capture of flower scent by repeated contact with fat — and popular accounts freely credit Egypt with it. The archive's position is the cautious one the sources support: maceration, warm infusion, boiling, and cloth-pressing are attested Egyptian extractions; a formal cold-enfleurage process, of the kind the 19th-century French industry perfected, is an inference from the maceration evidence rather than a described Egyptian procedure, and this article does not present it as one. The famous lily perfumes of the classical sources — the susinum type, a lily scent on an oil base with myrrh and cinnamon among its companions in the Greek descriptions — belong to the Greco-Roman reception of Egyptian perfumery and are labelled as such wherever this article names them: they show the maceration technology working at industrial flower-counts in the hands of Greek and Roman writers, and they are not pharaonic recipes.

Compounding — Sequence, Timing, and the Last Ingredient

Extraction yields scented bases; compounding yields products. Manniche's entry states the compounding principles in paraphrase, and they are the principles the Edfu walls embody. Apart from the choice of ingredients, sequence and timing were crucial, as the ingredient added last would be the most pungent — the volatile top of a compounded scent is whatever went in last and was cooked least, which is why kyphi's myrrh enters uncooked at the end (Section V) and why the oil recipes stage their additions over days. Some items served as fixatives and developers rather than as scents in their own right: the entry names orris root as a binder of scents, and sweet wine added to myrrh as a developer of its fragrance. Most perfumes were left in their natural colour, though alkanet could dye a product red. And the finished perfume went into vessels worthy of it — calcite (Egyptian alabaster) above all, a cool, non-porous, prestigious stone whose small jars and tubes are among the most recognisable objects of the Egyptian cosmetic repertoire. A perfume, in Manniche's formulation in Sacred Luxuries, had to carry an aura of rarity, exclusiveness, and the divine to achieve fame; the vessel was part of the aura, and the temple laboratory's products carried it by definition.

XII. The Edfu Oil Recipes — Hekenu and the Laboratory Repertoire

Kyphi is the Edfu laboratory's fumigation; its oils are the larger half of the repertoire (Section III), and one of them can be given in the same procedural form. The recipe type is the hekenu ointment — ḥknw [Gardiner: V28-V31-N35-G43] [𓎛𓎡𓈖𓅱], a name the reader will meet again in Section XV as second of the Seven Sacred Oils. Aufrère's study of the Edfu recipes (summarised in A Cultural History of Chemistry: In Antiquity) gives the type's form, in paraphrase: the recipes open by naming the product and specifying the amount to be produced, in the standard liquid measure, the hin (about 0.48 litres); and the recipe for a hin of hekenu is built principally on premium frankincense oil worked with styrax. The meticulousness is the point Aufrère's analysis stresses: the Edfu recipes specify quantities exactly, and specify in advance the reduction that correct cooking will produce, so that the finished weight or volume is itself the test of whether the operation was performed correctly — a built-in quality control, stated in the recipe, of the same kind as kyphi's reduction by one fifth (Section V). A cook who reaches the stated reduction has done the work; a cook who has not, has not, and the wall says what the number should be.

The base preparation is where the oils' true timescale hides. Manniche's encyclopedia entry, in paraphrase, states that before proceeding to the main ingredients the oil or fat was itself prepared, and that the entire operation might take up to a year, even two, if the preparation of the base ingredient was included. The Edfu texts, on Aufrère's reading, organise their production in three classes — fumigations; fat-based cooked ointments; and resinous ointments for the statues of the gods, compounded to make wood and stone carry the appearance the Egyptians expected of divine flesh — and the repertoire list of Section III (styrax ointment, hekenu, medjes, the nine ointments of the Ennead, the precious ointment, the ointment of divine mineral, kyphi, the natron pellets) is the laboratory's product line across those classes. One item in that line deserves its sentence: the ointment of divine mineral, which Aufrère's summary describes as a mixture of precious metals, minerals, bitumen, resins, and oils from Punt and God's Land, said to have been used at Coptos to anoint the statues of Min-Amun. The temple laboratory compounded for statues as seriously as for bodies: anointing the god's image in the daily service (Ritual Practice §III) consumed the laboratory's most precious products, and the recipes for them are liturgical technology, not cosmetics in any modern, merely decorative sense.

What the oil recipes share with kyphi, as a house style, can be listed, because it is the laboratory's signature across every product class: a named product and a stated yield at the head of the recipe; ingredients specified by kind and grade; additions staged in a fixed order over stated days; cooking specified by the reduction it must achieve; and a finished product that goes into the storeroom under its name, to be issued to the daily service by measure. That is a formulary in the full pharmaceutical sense, maintained by a priestly institution over centuries, and it is why the Edfu walls — rather than any tomb painting or Greek description — are the archive's primary evidence for Egyptian scent as a process.

XIII. Unguent Cones — The Melting Perfume and the Wax Cone of Amarna

From the reign of Hatshepsut onward, Egyptian art shows banqueters, worshippers, and the honoured dead wearing a small, pale cone perched on the head, on the hair or the wig, often with a lotus flower at the brow. For as long as no cone had ever been found, the images sustained two opposed readings, and the archive states both, because the discovery that settled the cones' existence did not settle their nature.

The traditional reading: a melting unguent. On this reading, classically presented by Manniche (Sacred Luxuries), the cone is a lump of perfumed unguent — scented fat of the kind Section XI describes — worn on the head so that, melting slowly in the warmth, it released its perfume over the wearer's hair and body through the banquet. The reading fits the art's contexts: cones appear in scenes of anointing and offering, are shown on stands and in bowls and being placed on heads, and unguents in Egyptian thought purified and elevated the wearer in the company of a divinity. It also fits the cones' frequent association with women, with sensuality and sexuality, and with the rebirth symbolism of the tomb — the justified dead, anointed and fragrant, in the presence of the gods.

The material evidence: wax, hollow, and unscented. In 2019, Stevens, Rogge, Bos, and Dabbs published the first material examples ("From Representation to Reality: Ancient Egyptian Wax Head Cones from Amarna," Antiquity 93, 2019): two cones from non-elite graves in the cemeteries of Amarna (ancient Akhetaten, c. 1350 BCE), found in place on the skulls — one on a woman who died in her twenties, over her well-preserved hair; the other on an adolescent or young adult. Spectroscopic analysis (DRIFTS) indicated that the cones' primary constituent is a biological wax — beeswax on the team's reading — and not fat or incense, as had sometimes been speculated; the reconstructed cones are hollow; and no perfume was detected in the tests, though the authors note that any perfume present may have evaporated over three thousand years. A hollow wax shell cannot melt and drip scented fat down a banquet guest, because there is no fat in it to melt. The authors' own tentative suggestion, in their abstract's terms, is that the Amarna cones were symbols meant to enhance the rebirth or personal fertility of the deceased in the afterlife — funerary objects, whatever their living counterparts may have been.

The counter-reading, and the archive's position. Manniche's response to the discovery, as reported in the coverage of the paper, is that the Amarna pair may be dummy cones: inexpensive beeswax substitutes worn in burial by inhabitants who could not afford the real, unguent cones of the elite imagery — a reading that preserves the traditional interpretation for the living while accepting the wax for the graves. The two Amarna individuals were, in fact, buried in non-elite cemeteries, which is consistent with the dummy reading and equally consistent with the symbolic reading. The archive's position is the one the evidence currently supports and no stronger: head cones were real objects — the Amarna finds end the older suggestion that they were a purely artistic convention, a halo-like motif with no material counterpart; the two surviving examples are hollow beeswax, unscented as analysed; and whether elite cones of scented unguent also existed in life, as the traditional reading holds and the dummy hypothesis supposes, remains unproven either way, because fat and unguent preserve far worse than wax and their absence from the record is what the traditional reading always predicted. Both sides are live. Any account that presents either the melting-perfume cone or the purely symbolic cone as settled fact has gone past its evidence — in opposite directions.

XIV. Cosmetics as Production — Mesdemet, Green Eye Paint, and the Grinding Palette

Eye paint was a manufactured product with a supply chain, a bench process, and a retail form, and it belongs in this article because its production is the mineral counterpart of the laboratory's resin work: quarried material, ground to grade, bound in a medium, stored in a vessel, applied with an instrument. The two traditional paints are black and green. The black is msdmt — mesdemet, the word that passes into Greek as stimmi and Latin as stibium, and survives in the modern word kohl (from Arabic) for the same preparation. The green is the paint the offering lists call the green eye paint, conventionally rendered from its material, malachite. This article gives both in transliteration or material name without glyphs, the spellings not being among those the author can compose with the confidence Section I requires.

Materials. Mesdemet's principal material, on the analyses summarised by Lucas and Harris (Chapter IV) and the modern literature, is galena — the dark grey lead sulphide ore — ground to powder. The green paint's material is malachite, the green copper carbonate ore, likewise ground. Both ores were mined in Egypt's borderlands — the Sinai and the Eastern Desert are the standing sources in the literature — and both reached the user as lumps of raw material, as stains on the palettes and pebbles that ground them, as powder in small linen or leather bags, and as prepared paste, in the full range of forms Lucas and Harris catalogue from the graves. Modern analyses of surviving eye paints, notably the Louvre samples studied by Tapsoba and colleagues, add a finding the archive reports as the analysts' result: beside the natural galena, some preparations contain the lead compounds laurionite and phosgenite, which are rare in nature and which the analysts conclude the Egyptians synthesised deliberately, by a wet chemical process, as ingredients in their own right. Eye paint, on that evidence, was not only ground mineral; at its most sophisticated it was manufactured chemistry, compounded — like antiu — to a specification.

Process. The bench sequence is short enough to state entire. (1) Select and crush the ore lump. (2) Grind it on the stone palette — the flat grinding palette, among the commonest objects of Predynastic graves, is this process's first tool — with a rubbing pebble, working the powder finer until it will take a smooth line. (3) Bind the powder: mixed with water, gum, oil, or fat to a paste that adheres to the lid and the applicator. (4) Store the powder or paste in its vessel — tubes, small jars, and the shaped kohl pots of stone, faience, and precious materials that fill the museum cases. (5) Apply with the kohl rod or stick — of wood, bone, ivory, or metal, sometimes itself a piece of jewellery — along upper and lower lids, in the extended line the art makes unmistakable. In the earliest periods the user ground their own on the household palette; in later periods, prepared powder and paste could be bought ready-ground, and the palette itself became increasingly a ceremonial object. Red ochre, ground and mixed with water to a paste for lips and cheeks, and the burn remedy of the Ebers Papyrus compounded from cheek stain, red natron, northern salt, and honey (both in the standard summaries of the cosmetic literature), complete the bench's range: pigment, binder, vessel, applicator.

Use and meaning. The paints were worn by men and women, daily, across the social range that could afford the materials, and their functions were never only decorative in the Egyptian account of them. The dark line cuts the desert glare; the preparations were credited with protecting the eye against disease, dust, and flies; and the painted eye is the sound Eye — the wedjat — of Horus, wholeness worn on the face. The offering lists file the two paints immediately after the Seven Sacred Oils (the Saqqara offering list read for Section XV runs: the seven oils, then two bags of green eye paint, then two bags of black) — which is where the archive files them too: as the last, mineral member of the temple-and-tomb scent-and-anointing repertoire, made by the same grammar of grinding, binding, and storing that made everything else in this article.

XV. The Seven Sacred Oils — From the Tomb to the Daily Cult

The canon of the seven sacred oils is the oldest named product line in this article. It is fixed by the Old Kingdom on offering tablets with a named well for each oil — the British Museum's calcite tablet EA6122, from Abydos, incises the seven names over seven circular oil-wells for use in the Opening of the Mouth, and the National Museums Liverpool offering slab gives the canonical order — and it is treated in its funerary home in Mummification §XIV, whose account this section extends, as the task of a scent article requires, to the oils' other home: the daily temple cult, where the same anointing that completed a mummy also dressed a god every morning. The funerary use is the one the tablets embody: the seven oils anoint the mummy and are applied, in the Opening of the Mouth (Opening of the Mouth), to restore the dead person's faculties, each oil at its station in the rite. The temple use is the same act at the other end of the Egyptian world. In the daily service (Ritual Practice §III), the god's image is stripped of yesterday's dress, washed, purified — and anointed with the sacred oils before it is dressed in fresh linen and presented with the day's offering. The anointing is not an embellishment of the service; it is one of its fixed episodes, the moment at which the laboratory's products meet the god's body, every morning, in every temple that kept the full rite. Funerary and temple anointing are one technology with two recipients: the dead person made divine, and the god maintained in divinity, anointed from the same canon, compounded in the same kind of laboratory, under the same conviction — that fragrance is not the sign of the divine presence but one of its substances. The gods are fragrant; to anoint is to make fragrant; to make fragrant is, in the root sense of snṯr [Gardiner: S29-N35-V13-D21] [𓋴𓈖𓍿𓂋] itself, to make divine.

XVI. Kyphi in Medicine — Fumigation, Draught, and Antidote

Kyphi's medical life is Egyptian before it is Greek, and briefer in the sources than its cultic life. The Ebers preparation of Section VI is already a medical-papyrus product — a fumigant for the house, the clothes, and the breath — and the archive's survey of the Medical Papyri places such fumigations where the papyri place them: among the ordinary instruments of Egyptian practice, in which scented smoke cleanses a space, a body, and a breath by the same act. Perfumes and unguents run through the medical corpus in the other direction as well: scented oils and fats are vehicles and remedies in the prescriptions — for the skin, for the hair, in the compounded preparations the UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology entry surveys under fragrant remedies — because in Egyptian pharmacy the fat base that carries a scent and the fat base that carries a drug are the same base, made in the same way, sometimes in the same room. The temple laboratory and the physician's workshop are neighbouring institutions with a shared formulary, and kyphi, compounded in the one and prescribed in the other, is the substance that walks between them.

The Greek medicalisation of Section VII — Dioscorides' drink for the asthmatic, Galen's drachm doses for liver and lungs, Rufus's snakebite antidote — is therefore an extension of an Egyptian pattern, not a Greek invention imposed on a purely cultic product. What the Greeks added was system and dose: kyphi entered the antidote literature, acquired indications, and was prescribed by weight. What they did not add was the idea that a temple incense could be taken into the body. Plutarch's "potion and salve" (Section VII) and the Ebers fumigant for the breath stand on either side of the same Egyptian fact: the compound was always, among its uses, a thing whose smoke and substance were held to cleanse the interior of a person as they cleansed the air of a temple. The archive states the historical uses as history. It does not recommend them, and Section XVII's hazard note says why in the specific case of kyphi's own ingredients.

XVII. Repeat It — The Edfu Process in Numbered Steps

The whole Edfu kyphi process of Section V, gathered into the numbered form this archive uses for its staple processes (compare Food and Drink §XVIII). Quantities are Loret's 1887 working reduction for a 10,164-gram batch; halve or tenth them only with the rests and the reduction unchanged, since those, and not the scale, are the recipe.

// THE EDFU KYPHI — NUMBERED PROCESS (LORET'S REDUCTION) //

1. Weigh the first seven. Sweet flag, camel grass, mastic, cassia, cinnamon, mint, aspalathos — 270 g each, 1,890 g in all. (Manniche's first seven: pine resin in place of the second bark.)

2. Pound and sieve. Pound the seven very fine together; sieve; keep the finest fraction — 756 g in Loret's reduction — and set the coarse remainder aside from this product.

3. Crush the second four. Juniper, the two disputed plant names (Loret: sweet acacia and henna; Manniche: pine kernels and peker), and cyperus — 270 g each.

4. Moisten and rest one day. Work 1,125 g of wine into the crushed four; combine with the sieved powder of step 2; leave the mass one day. Discard wine the powder has not absorbed.

5. Prepare the raisins. Clean 1,260 g of raisin flesh of rind and pips; steep and grind it with 1,440 g of oasis wine.

6. Combine and rest five days. Mix the raisin preparation into the aromatic mass; leave the whole five days.

7. Cook the binder. Mix the resin of the honey stage (Loret: 1,200 g terebinth resin; Manniche: frankincense) with 3,000 g of honey; cook until the weight has fallen by one fifth, to 3,360 g.

8. Work in and rest five days. Work the hot honey-resin thoroughly into the rested mass; leave the combined kyphi five days more.

9. Add the myrrh last. Grind 1,143 g of myrrh fine; mix it, uncooked, into the mass. Total: 10,164 g.

10. Form and dry. Form the mass into small pellets; dry them for storage in the laboratory storeroom; issue by the pellet to the evening censing.

// HAZARD — READ BEFORE ANY MODERN BENCH WORK //

Kyphi is incense. It is compounded to be burned, and this archive does not present it as a drink, potion, or medicine for modern use, whatever the ancient sources say about ancient use. One of its attested ingredients, sweet flag (calamus, Acorus calamus), can be rich in β-asarone, genotoxic and carcinogenic in rodents; the US Food and Drug Administration banned calamus as a food additive in 1968. Plutarch's Greek list also includes bitumen. Burn any incense only with ventilation, on a heatproof surface, away from anything that can catch fire. Historical attestation is a fact about the past, not a recommendation. (This note follows the hazard box of Ritual Practice §V.)

Eleven days of rests, two grindings, one sieving, one measured cook, one uncooked finish, ten kilograms of product. Set beside the beer process of Food and Drink — malting, the two-part mash, straining, fermentation — the kyphi process shows the same Egyptian signature at the opposite end of the economy: a staged transformation, spread over days, in which heat and time and enzyme-like patience are kept in separate vessels and married in a fixed order, and in which the accounting (here, the reduction by one fifth and the stated yield) is part of the recipe. The brewery fed the workforce. The laboratory scented the god. Both wrote their standards down, and both standards, read closely, are engineering.
