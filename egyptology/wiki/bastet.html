I. The Name — bꜣstt and the Ointment Jar

Bastet's name is one of the oldest divine names in Egypt, and one of the least certainly understood. In its early hieroglyphic form it is written bꜣstt [Gardiner: E10-S29-X1-X1] [𓃝𓋴𓏏𓏏]. The archive's Sekhmet article, in its Section XV treatment of the leonine continuum, has already fixed the spelling this article follows, and the two articles are deliberately consistent: the first sign is read bꜣ [Gardiner: E10] [𓃝] with the corrected, post-audit value the archive assigns that sign, the fold of cloth gives the s, and the two loaves give the doubled feminine ending -tt. The modern form "Bastet" is itself a scholarly convention: Egyptologists adopted it because later writings of the name invite the second t to be read, although in earlier pronunciation that final feminine ending was very likely silent. "Bast" is the same name with the convention stripped back; Greek writers made it Bubastis, and Coptic preserved a form close to Oubaste. The spellings differ across three thousand years. The written core does not.

What the name means is a harder question, and this article states the leading answer at exactly the strength its proposer gave it. Stephen Quirke, in Ancient Egyptian Religion, suggested that the name is a nisba formation, "she of the bꜣs," and that the bꜣs [Gardiner: W2] [𓎰] in question is the ointment jar: the flat-based, wide-mouthed vessel, usually of pale stone, in which precious unguent was kept. On that reading the goddess is "she of the ointment jar" — the power that belongs to, and is carried in, the jar of protective scented oil. The suggestion fits two independent facts: the name is in some writings composed with the jar sign itself, and the goddess's durable associations include perfume, unguents, and the protection that anointing conferred (Section XX). A rival derivation, associated with James P. Allen, takes the name as a nisba from a place, "she of bꜣst," a locality otherwise unknown. The archive prints Quirke's reading as the working one, because the jar is in the writing and the unguent is in the cult, and prints Allen's beside it because the matter is not settled: divine names in Egypt were often deliberately opaque, cult secrets rather than labels, and a goddess attested for nearly three millennia is not obliged to surrender her etymology to either.

One caution belongs at the head of the article, because it governs everything after it. The familiar summary of Bastet — "originally a fierce lioness, she softened over time into a gentle cat" — is not false, but it is a compression, and compressions of this kind smuggle in a story the evidence does not tell: a story of a personality mellowing, as if the goddess had aged out of anger. Sections V to VII set out what the record actually shows, period by period: which forms are attested when, what else was changing at the same dates (the place of the domestic cat in Egyptian life, the political rise of her city, the sorting of the lioness goddesses into complementary postings), and where the lioness demonstrably persists after the cat has arrived. The reader who wants the one-sentence version may keep it, provided it is read as a statement about iconography and emphasis, not about a temperament improving with age.

II. The City's Name — pr-bꜣstt, Bubastis, Tell Basta

Goddess and city carry each other's names. The city the Greeks called Bubastis was in Egyptian pr-bꜣstt [Gardiner: O1-E10-S29-X1-X1] [𓉐𓃝𓋴𓏏𓏏] — "the House of Bastet," or more exactly "the Domain of Bastet," built on pr [Gardiner: O1] [𓉐], "house, estate, temple domain." The Greek form Boubastis is the Egyptian name heard through Greek ears and fitted with a Greek ending, by the same process the archive's article on phonology and voice traces for other Delta names; the Hebrew Bible's Pi-beseth (Ezekiel 30:17) is the same name by a third route; and the modern Arabic name of the mound, Tell Basta, preserves the goddess's name still, worn down by four thousand years of continuous local speech. Few Egyptian goddesses can be followed in an unbroken chain of place-names from the hieroglyphs to a modern map. Bastet can.

The chain matters because it corrects a habit of thought. Bastet is often introduced as a "cat goddess" who happened to have a temple at Bubastis. The names say the opposite: she is the goddess of a place, whose domain the city was, whose temple was the city's reason for fame, and whose festival was the city's gift to the whole country. Herodotus, who knew the city in the fifth century BCE, uses "Bubastis" for the goddess herself as readily as for her town — "Bubastis in the Hellenic tongue is Artemis" (Section XIV) — because in Greek ears the two were one word. Egyptian keeps them distinct by a single sign, the house sign of the domain, and this article keeps the Egyptian distinction: Bastet the goddess, Bubastis the city, Tell Basta the mound that remains of it, on the south-eastern edge of modern Zagazig in the eastern Delta.

Her standing title follows from the same fact. She is nbt [Gardiner: V30-X1] [𓎟𓏏] — "Lady, Mistress" — of Bubastis: "Bastet, the great, Mistress of Bubastis" is the form in which temple and statuette inscriptions name her again and again, and the British Museum's bronze of the goddess found at Bubastis itself carries an inscription of exactly that tenor (Section XXII). At Saqqara, where she had a second great sanctuary, she bears the local title Lady of Ankhtawy, "Life of the Two Lands," the old name of the Memphite necropolis district (Section XIX). The titles are addresses. Each one posts the goddess to a place where her power was understood to be on duty.

III. The Earliest Record — Stone Vessels and the Pyramid Texts

Bastet is not a late goddess who grew out of the pet-keeping of the first millennium. She stands near the beginning of the Egyptian written record. The standard reference summary — Richard H. Wilkinson's The Complete Gods and Goddesses of Ancient Egypt, the work the archive's other deity articles cite for attestation history — places her earliest known images on stone vessels of the Second Dynasty (c. 2890–2686 BCE), where she already appears in the form she would keep for two thousand years: a woman with the maneless head of a lioness. Stone vessels of that age survive chiefly from the royal necropolis at Saqqara, and a goddess named and pictured on royal stoneware of the Second Dynasty is a goddess of the court, not of the village: whatever local Delta cult lay behind her, by the time the record opens she has already been received into the state religion of a unified Egypt.

Her name then enters the first great corpus of Egyptian religious literature. Bastet is named in the Pyramid Texts of the late Old Kingdom, the inscribed utterances of the royal pyramids, and Geraldine Pinch's widely used handbook summary of her career begins exactly there: from the Pyramid Texts onward, Pinch writes, Bastet has a double aspect, nurturing mother and terrifying avenger, and it is the avenging aspect that predominates in the Coffin Texts of the Middle Kingdom and in the Book of the Dead, and that is invoked in medical spells. The archive underlines Pinch's formulation because it is the correct frame for the whole article. The "double aspect" is not a late theological repair job, reconciling an old fierce goddess with a new gentle one. It is present from the first texts that describe her at any length. The mother and the avenger are coeval.

The Pyramid Text role that matters most for her later career is her relation to the king. In the Old Kingdom material Bastet stands close to the king as protector and, in the imagery of royal childhood, as a divine nurse and mother: the lioness who suckles and guards the royal child becomes, by the logic Egyptian theology always applies, the lioness who defends the grown king. That maternal-fierce combination — the nursing mother who is also the most dangerous animal in the landscape — is the seed from which both of her later postings grow: the guardian of the nursery and the house in Section XXI, and the Eye of Ra of Section VIII. Neither is a softening of the other. Both are the lioness with young, read at different distances.

IV. Iconography — the Goddess's Forms at a Glance

Across three thousand years Bastet is shown in four principal forms, and the first discipline of her iconography is not to sort them into a simple before-and-after. The forms overlap in time; the same century, and occasionally the same object, can carry more than one of them; and the choice among them is made by context — temple or tomb, state monument or household amulet, the mood of the goddess the dedicator needed — as much as by date.

THE LIONESS-HEADED WOMAN

Her earliest form, from the Second Dynasty stone vessels onward: a woman's body, a lioness's maneless head, sceptre and ꜥnḫ [Gardiner: S34] [𓋹] in hand. In this form she is visually almost indistinguishable from Sekhmet, and only the inscription beside the figure settles which goddess is meant.

THE LIONESS ENTIRE

In full animal form, striding or couchant, she is the desert-edge power without the mediation of the human body: the form of the Distant Goddess in the desert, and of guardianship at its most literal.

THE CAT-HEADED WOMAN

The standard form of the first millennium BCE: a woman, often in an elaborately patterned dress, with the head of a domestic cat, carrying a sistrum, a lion-headed aegis, and a basket, with kittens sometimes at her feet. The Metropolitan Museum's Late Period example (15.43.5) is the type specimen used in Section VII.

THE CAT ENTIRE

Seated, upright, ears pricked: the goddess as a domestic cat, in bronze, wood, faience, and stone, from small amulets to the 42-centimetre Gayer-Anderson cat of the British Museum (Section XXII). Some examples of this form are not only images but containers, made to hold a mummified cat.

Two attributes cross all four forms and repay attention, because they are her name and her office worn as objects. The first is the sistrum, the rattle whose shaking was the characteristic sound of the pacification of the Eye goddesses: music offered to a dangerous power until the power softens, the instrument Hathor also carries, and the sound Herodotus heard on the festival boats (Section XV). The second is the aegis, the broad protective collar topped with a lioness's head, which Bastet holds before her body like a shield. The Metropolitan Museum's curators describe the aegis of their cat-headed Bastet as protective, and the point deserves emphasis: even in her most domestic form, the goddess carries a lion-headed shield. The lioness is not edited out of the cat period. She is miniaturised, held in the hand, and kept on duty.

V. The Lioness Period — Bastet in the Old and Middle Kingdoms

For roughly the first half of her documented life, Bastet is a lioness goddess of the same company, the same appearance, and much the same business as Sekhmet. The company is the lioness continuum the archive maps in Sekhmet Section XV: Mut, Sekhmet, Bastet, Wadjet, Tefnut, Pakhet, Menhit, and a train of local lionesses, one solar, feminine, feline power differentiated by city and by posting. Bastet's posting in that early system is the north: she is the lioness of the Delta, of Bubastis, as Sekhmet's home ground is Memphis and the south's great temples. The pairing is geographical before it is temperamental.

Her business, in the texts that survive, is the Eye's business. She is a daughter of the sun god — rꜥ [Gardiner: N5] [𓇳] — and one of his defenders, an avenger whose ferocity is turned outward against the enemies of order and, in the medical spells Pinch notes, against the demons of disease on behalf of the patient who invokes her. This last point is worth pausing on, because it complicates the gentle-cat story from inside the fierce period: already in the early material Bastet is invoked protectively, for healing and defence, precisely because she is dangerous. The logic is the one the archive documents for Sekhmet's physician-priests: the sender of an affliction is the authority who can recall it, and an avenging goddess is the natural patron of everyone who needs avenging done on their behalf. Protection in Egypt is not the absence of ferocity. It is ferocity correctly addressed.

The Middle Kingdom adds the evidence of place. At Bubastis itself, excavation has recovered Middle Kingdom building on a substantial scale, including a palace complex and the governors' monuments of the Delta administration (Section XIII), and the goddess's cult is the cult of that city's god throughout: the Delta capital of her domain was a working administrative centre of the Middle Kingdom state, not a village shrine awaiting later promotion. In the Coffin Texts, the great Middle Kingdom corpus that democratised the Old Kingdom's royal afterlife literature, Bastet keeps the double aspect Pinch describes — present to the dead as protector, present to their enemies as terror. The lioness of the Middle Kingdom is not yet a cat. But she is already, in the same texts, a mother and a guardian of the vulnerable dead. Both halves of the later Bastet are documented before the cat arrives.

VI. Lioness to Cat — the Evidence, Period by Period

This section does the work the one-sentence summary refuses to do. The claim under examination is that Bastet changed from a lioness goddess into a cat goddess. The questions are: when, in what kinds of object, alongside what other changes, and with what exceptions? Taken in order, the evidence supports a real and dramatic shift of emphasis across the New Kingdom and the Third Intermediate Period, standardised in the first millennium — and it does not support a clean replacement, a datable moment of change, or a change in the goddess's underlying character.

The Animal Changed First

The domestic cat's own history in Egypt runs ahead of the goddess's iconography. The Egyptian word for the cat is mꞽw [Gardiner: E13-G43] [𓃠𓅱] — an honest onomatopoeia, "he who mews" — and cats of the domestic kind, descended from the African wildcat, were living in Egyptian settlements, tolerated and then encouraged around grain stores, long before they were anyone's icon. Genetic study of mummified cats (Kurushima, Ikram and colleagues, Journal of Archaeological Science, 2012) recovered from three Late Period cat mummies the common domestic-cat maternal lineages still prevalent in Egypt and the Near East, with divergence estimates placing the origin of those lineages thousands of years before the mummies themselves: the temple cats of the first millennium were true domestic cats, of lines already ancient in the region. By the Middle Kingdom, cats appear in tomb scenes in domestic settings; by the New Kingdom they are everywhere in the painted record of daily life — under the chairs of married couples, collared and bejewelled, hunting in the marshes beside their owners (Section XVII). A goddess whose animal was becoming the animal of the house was acquiring, in the house cat, a second body that her worshippers met every day.

The New Kingdom — Both Forms in Play

In the New Kingdom (c. 1550–1070 BCE) the cat enters Bastet's iconography without expelling the lioness. Cat-headed and cat-formed representations of the goddess belong increasingly to these centuries, above all in the smaller, personal media — amulets, small bronzes, faience — while the lioness-headed form continues in formal and temple contexts, and lioness-headed Bastet figures are still being made, in fine materials, long after: museum collections hold faience Bastets with lioness heads of much later date than the supposed transition. The pattern is not succession but specialisation by medium and mood: the cat for the goddess approached closely, in the house and on the person; the lioness for the goddess in her majesty and her anger. Egyptian religion, which kept a god's forms in parallel as a matter of course, had no need to retire either.

The Third Intermediate Period — the City Makes the Difference

The decisive change is political, and it has a date range. In the Twenty-second Dynasty (c. 945–715 BCE) the kings of Egypt were, by Manetho's tradition and their own building record, Bubastite kings: a line of Libyan descent whose power base was Bubastis, who built Bastet's temple up into one of the great temples of Egypt (Sections XI–XII), and whose devotion made their city goddess a national one. The archive's article on the Third Intermediate Period sets out the dynasty and Osorkon II's festival hall in their political frame. Under that royal patronage the cat form becomes the public, standard form of the goddess — the form of the bronzes, the festival, and the pilgrimage trade — because the cat is now the form in which the national cult presents her: approachable, processional, reproducible by the thousand for the pilgrims of Section XXII. What changed in the Twenty-second Dynasty was not the goddess's nature but her scale, and the iconography followed the scale.

What "Softened" Gets Wrong

Three facts forbid the simple mellowing story. First, the double aspect is original: Pinch's nurturing mother is in the Pyramid Texts beside the avenger (Section III), so there is no early period in which Bastet is fierce only. Second, the lioness persists: lioness-headed Bastets, the lion-headed aegis in the cat-headed goddess's own hand (Section IV), and the compound Sekhmet-Bastet dedications of the continuum (Section VIII) all continue into and through the cat's ascendancy. Third, the cat is not, in Egyptian experience, a harmless animal: the house cat of the tomb paintings is a hunter, the Great Cat of Heliopolis is a serpent-killer, and a mother cat's defence of her kittens was proverbial enough for Herodotus to build an ethnographic set-piece on it (Section XVIII). The honest summary is therefore narrower and more interesting than the proverb: between the New Kingdom and the Late Period, the dominant public image of Bastet shifted from the lioness to the domestic cat, as her cult was nationalised by her city's dynasty and her animal became the animal of every house — while the lioness, the anger, and the avenging aspect remained hers, attested, and available.

VII. The Cat Form Standardised — Attributes of the First Millennium

By the Late Period (664–332 BCE) and through the Ptolemaic age, the cat-headed Bastet has a standard iconography so consistent that a museum label can be written from any one example to fit most others. The Metropolitan Museum's record for its cupreous-metal Bastet (15.43.5, Late Period–Ptolemaic, 664–30 BCE) gives the type in the Museum's own terms, and this section follows it closely because it is a description of an object, not a reconstruction. The goddess is cat-headed, "a powerful protective figure who also was known for her fertility." She could be represented with a lion head as well, the Met notes, "but as a cat-headed goddess her peaceful traits were emphasized." She holds a protective lion-headed aegis, probably once held a sistrum in her other hand, and carries a basket on her arm. Statuettes of the type sometimes show kittens in front of her, alluding to her fertility; the suggestion that the basket itself held kittens, the Met judges, seems unfounded — an archive-worthy distinction, kept here, between what the type shows and what has been guessed about it.

The dress deserves its own sentence, because it is hers. Bastet's statuettes wear elaborately patterned garments far more often than those of other goddesses: the Met's example has a complex herring-bone pattern, and its curators note both that the patterning marks the piece's quality and that, as some have suggested, the vertical banding may recall the striped fur of a cat. Her ears are pierced for earrings, probably gold in the original. A worn inscription on the base names the goddess — the archive's standing reminder that in this material the name, not the species, is the identification. And the Met's record closes with the distribution that defines the period: her main cult centre was Bubastis, "where thousands of cat mummies and a large number of cat statuettes were discovered," but the cult extended far beyond it, and statuettes of Bastet, as a cat or cat-headed, "were among some of the most popular dedications of the Late and Ptolemaic Periods."

In full cat form the standard is the seated cat: upright, tail brought round, head high, the pose of an animal listening. The type runs from faience amulets a centimetre high to bronzes of near half a metre, and its jewellery is part of the type, not decoration added to it — broad collars, wedjat-eye pectorals, scarabs on the head and chest, earrings and nose-rings in gold and silver (Section XXII examines the supreme example). A nursing variant sets the goddess-cat on her side with kittens at her teats, the maternal aspect made zoologically literal; a British Museum bronze of c. 900–600 BCE, found at Bubastis, shows her instead as a cat-headed woman with four kittens at her feet, sistrum in hand. The kittens are theology: the goddess of the house is the goddess of its increase, and her fertility is not an abstraction but a litter.

VIII. The Eye of Ra Continuum — Bastet's Posting

The archive's Sekhmet article, Section XV, has already established the framework this section expands, and nothing here replaces it: the lioness goddesses form a continuum, one feline, solar, feminine power — the Eye of Ra — differentiated by city, mood, and distance from the sun, its grammar written in the compound names Mut-Sekhmet, Sekhmet-Bastet, and the fourfold Mut-Sekhmet-Bastet-Wadjet. What follows is the same doctrine read from Bastet's end of the spectrum: what her posting is, what work it does in the system, and what her presence in the compounds tells us that a study of Sekhmet alone cannot.

The Eye itself, in the summary the companion articles share, is the sun's power of action at a distance: irt [Gardiner: D4] [𓁹], the Eye of rꜥ [Gardiner: N5] [𓇳], detachable, feminine, and agentive, able to leave the god's face, rage in the desert, be lost, be wooed, and return. Within that office the archive's Sekhmet article assigns the postings in a single sentence this article adopts verbatim as its starting point: Sekhmet is the fire dispatched; Wadjet is the fire posted; Bastet is the fire at rest in the house; Mut is the fire as the king's mother; Hathor is the fire welcomed home. Bastet's posting is thus defined by distance, the variable the whole continuum runs on. She is what the Eye is when it is not abroad: the same solar power, at hearth-range, in the room, on duty over the cradle and the stores, its claws retracted not because they are gone but because the house is the place it defends.

The Pacification, Read from the Other End

The continuum's central rite is pacification — the Sekhmet article's sḥtp complex, the satisfying of the raging Eye by litany, music, festival, and intoxicating drink until the destroyer comes home as a benefactor. Bastet is that rite's destination. When the New Year tradition recorded in the Sekhmet article has worshippers exchange gifts in the form of Sekhmet or of Bastet, indifferently, as the year's first insurance, the interchangeability is the doctrine in practice: the giver is not choosing between a dangerous goddess and a safe one, but addressing the one Eye-power in the aspect the turning year requires — the arrows still abroad, or the power already home and at peace. Bastet-names and Bastet-images cluster at exactly the points of the calendar and the house where the Eye is being received rather than feared: New Year gifts, festival processions, nursery amulets, the sistrum's sound in a doorway. She is, liturgically, the proof that pacification works: the form the fire takes when the liturgy has done its job.

The Distant Goddess and the Return

The mythic frame for the posting is the Distant Goddess cycle, treated in the archive's Hathor and Sekhmet articles: the Eye, offended, flees to the far south in lioness form and must be persuaded home, returning with the inundation, feasted and adorned, her rage converted to beneficence. Bastet's place in that cycle is structural rather than narrative — the named fleeing goddess of the fullest versions is more often Tefnut or Hathor — and the archive states it as structure, not as a lost Bastet myth: the returned Eye, resident again in Egypt and in its houses, is the condition Bastet's whole cult presupposes. Her festival at Bubastis (Sections XV–XVI) is the cycle's civic form: the goddess approached by river, with music, in joy, in her own city, year after year — the homecoming not remembered but re-performed, as the archive's article on ritual practice sets out for Egyptian festivals generally. Where Sekhmet's cult specialises in the moments before the return — the dangerous days, the arrows, the litany of recall — Bastet's specialises in the morning after it: the feast, the full jars, the cats in the temple courts.

IX. Family and Relations — Daughter of Ra, Mother of Mihos

Egyptian divine genealogies are local and functional, not systematic, and Bastet's are a textbook case: she acquires relations where a cult needs them, and different cities keep different lists. The constant, everywhere, is her solar filiation. She is a daughter of Ra — the filiation her Eye-of-Ra office itself implies, since the Eye goddesses are the sun's daughters as well as his weapon — and the companion articles' presentation of her among the daughters of the sun, with Hathor, Sekhmet, and the rest, follows the sources' own habit. Beyond that constant, the archive proceeds relation by relation, at the strength each is attested.

Her clearest offspring is the lion god Mihos — Egyptian Maahes, "the lion who is true beside her" in one traditional gloss of his name — a lion-headed god of war and of the king's protection, whose cult belongs to the same Delta world as hers: Leontopolis and Bubastis are his cities as Bubastis is hers, and Greek writers knew him in her circle. The pairing is iconographically exact: the cat goddess's son keeps the full lion form she was laying down, mane and all, as if the family's leonine inheritance had passed to the next generation entire. Temple tradition at Bubastis joins mother and son in one cult landscape; Herodotus's processional road from her temple runs to a temple of "Hermes," and the city's sacred geography held other divine households besides (Section XIV).

Two further relations circulate widely and are stated here with their warning labels. Greek tradition, as Herodotus reports it (II.156, in paraphrase), made Bubastis a daughter of Isis and Osiris and a sister of Horus, hidden with him by their mother from Set's violence: that is the Greek interpretive genealogy, generated by the equation of Bastet with Artemis and Artemis's known parentage, and it is not an Egyptian family tree. Egyptian tradition itself offers a different occasional connection, in which Bastet appears among the possible mothers of Anubis — the archive's Anubis article lists the variants plainly: son of Ra, of the cow goddess Hesat, of Bastet, of Ra and Nephthys, of Osiris and Isis, varying by source and period. And at Memphis, where the perfume god Nefertem is the child of Ptah and Sekhmet in the Memphite triad, some traditions give Bastet in Sekhmet's place as the mother — a variant that follows naturally from the continuum of Section VIII and from Bastet's own unguent associations (Section XX), and that this archive reports as a variant, not as the triad's standard form, which the Ptah and Sekhmet articles give as Ptah, Sekhmet, Nefertem.

X. Bubastis the City — Nome Capital of the Eastern Delta

Bubastis stood in the south-eastern Delta, on the Pelusiac branch of the Nile — the easternmost of the river's ancient mouths — at the point where the Delta's waterways met the road east: toward the Wadi Tumilat, the canal corridor to the Red Sea, and the land bridge to Asia. The archive's article on sacred geography places it in the Delta's nome system as capital of its own nome, the eighteenth of Lower Egypt in the standard lists, in the row of eastern Delta cities — Mendes, Bubastis, Tanis — that guarded the kingdom's most vulnerable frontier. A goddess of protection posted at a frontier city is not a coincidence of the record. Bubastis was a gate of Egypt, and its goddess was the gate's warden as well as its householder.

The city's antiquity is stratigraphic, not legendary. Excavation (Section XIII) has carried its history down through the New Kingdom city to a Middle Kingdom palace and administrative quarter, to the ka-temples of the Sixth Dynasty kings Teti and Pepi I, and beneath Pepi I's temple to a large provincial palace of the Fourth and Fifth Dynasties: Bubastis was a seat of provincial government in the Old Kingdom, centuries before its dynasty ruled Egypt. Herodotus's observation that the city had been "banked up" higher and higher, generation by generation, while the temple stayed at its original level (Section XIV), is the Delta's settlement physics exactly: a city on a gezira, a mound of accumulated occupation debris and flood defence, rising out of a floodplain that drowned everything not raised. Tell Basta today is that mound, its red granite temple field at the old level, the modern city of Zagazig grown up around and against it.

XI. The Great Temple of Bastet — Builders and Phases

The great temple of Bastet is known, unusually for so famous a building, chiefly from its ruins' inscriptions: the structure Herodotus praised as the most pleasing in Egypt was destroyed, probably by earthquake, and survives as a vast field of fallen granite blocks whose texts the Tell Basta Project has spent three decades reading in place (Section XIII). The building history those blocks yield, as Daniela Rosenow of the project summarised it in Egyptian Archaeology, the Egypt Exploration Society's magazine, is a history of successive royal rebuildings on one sacred footprint. The temple's great age is the Twenty-second Dynasty: Osorkon I's name dominates the blocks of the main eastern entrance, where the king is shown making offerings to a variety of gods, and the distribution of fallen columns with palm-leaf capitals allows the entrance court to be reconstructed as a peristyle court of about 30 by 50 metres. Osorkon II extended the temple extensively and added its most famous component, the festival hall of Section XII. West of the festival gate, Rosenow's reconstruction continues, a colonnade of papyrus-bundle columns, almost 30 metres long, led to a five-aisled hypostyle hall with Hathor-headed capitals — the cow goddess's heads carrying the roof of the cat goddess's house, the continuum built in stone.

The last great builder was also the last native king of Egypt. Nectanebo II (360–343 BCE), whose temple programme the archive's Late Period article traces from the Delta to Philae, renewed the sanctuary itself at Bubastis, as he renewed sanctuaries at Hermopolis, Abydos, Memphis, and a dozen other cult centres: the holy of holies that Herodotus's near-contemporary city showed its pilgrims was, in its final form, a fourth-century building inside an early-first-millennium temple inside a sacred enclosure far older than either. Earlier royal names occur among the blocks as well — the temple field has yielded material of the New Kingdom, including a monumental pink granite statue of a Ramesside queen uncovered by the project between 2001 and 2003 — and in 2004 the entrance area produced a new duplicate of the Canopus Decree of Ptolemy III (238 BCE), proof the temple was still a functioning, decree-publishing institution deep into the Ptolemaic period. The archive's article on temple architecture supplies the grammar — pylon, court, hypostyle hall, sanctuary, the axis of increasing darkness and restriction — within which Bubastis's scattered blocks are read.

Herodotus's description (Section XIV) adds the element the ruins alone could not have proved: water. His temple stood ringed by two Nile channels that did not join, each a hundred feet broad, so that the sanctuary was effectively an island in the middle of the raised city. Recent geoarchaeological work by the Tell Basta Project has, in the project's own reporting, proven the existence of two water canals surrounding the temple of Bastet — a rare, satisfying case of a classical description confirmed by the spade and the corer rather than merely repeated. The island temple, looked down into from the banked-up streets around it, shaded by its grove, approached by its stone processional road from the east: that is the building the festival of Sections XV–XVI filled, and it is recoverable now from text, blocks, and soil together.

XII. Osorkon II's Festival Hall — the Sed at Bubastis

In the twenty-second year of his reign, Osorkon II celebrated his sed-festival — the royal jubilee of renewal, normally the prerogative of a thirtieth year — and built for it at Bubastis a festival hall fronted by a great granite gateway, whose reliefs are a principal source for the entire festival's liturgy. Édouard Naville published the gateway in a dedicated memoir, The Festival Hall of Osorkon II in the Great Temple of Bubastis (1892), as the second fruit of his Bubastis seasons, and the archive's article on the sed-festival draws on the Bubastis cycle throughout: its sequence of processions, enthronements, and visiting gods is among the fullest festival programmes to survive from any Egyptian temple. The Bubastis hall matters to this article for what it says about the city's rank. A king does not build his jubilee — the rite that renews his kingship before all the gods of Egypt — in a provincial shrine. He builds it where the gods are expected to gather, and in the Twenty-second Dynasty that place was the temple of his own city's goddess.

XIII. Excavation History — Naville to the Tell Basta Project

Bubastis was lost and found by its texts. After the Muslim conquest of the seventh century CE the city was abandoned and its very location forgotten; the French scholars of Napoleon's expedition, working from Herodotus, identified the mound of Tell Basta, beside Zagazig, as the site — Étienne-Louis Malus is credited with recognising the features Herodotus describes in the Delta landscape of the mound. The identification held, but the mound suffered for it: through the nineteenth century the ruins were quarried for stone and dug for sebakh, the nitrogen-rich ancient debris Egyptian farmers used as fertiliser, and John Gardner Wilkinson, visiting in 1843, lamented the destruction already under way. The temple Herodotus called the most pleasing in Egypt entered archaeology as a ruin being actively unmade.

Systematic excavation began with Édouard Naville, the Swiss Egyptologist who worked the temple area for the Egypt Exploration Fund in the seasons of 1887–1889 and published them as Bubastis (1887–1889) (1891) and the festival-hall memoir of 1892. Naville established what has remained the frame of all later work: that the great temple existed in ruins where Herodotus put it, that its fallen blocks carried inscriptions of nearly every major period of Egyptian history, and that the site held, beside the temple, the ka-temples of Old Kingdom kings, New Kingdom tombs, and the cat cemeteries of Section XIX, whose brick-lined pits of cat bones, many burned before burial, he was the first to describe. A lecture report of 1887 preserves the excitement of the first season: Naville had "ascertained that the temple, which for a long time had been considered as hopelessly lost, not only existed in ruins but had already yielded most interesting inscriptions." In 1906, railway workers near the site turned up a hoard of gold and silver vessels and jewellery — the Bubastis treasure, now divided chiefly between Cairo and the Metropolitan Museum — a reminder of how much of the site's history has arrived by accident rather than by design.

Twentieth-century Egyptian excavation, above all the long work of Labib Habachi from the 1930s onward, recovered the older city Naville had not sought: Old Kingdom ka-chapels, the Middle Kingdom palace and governors' quarter, the strata that made Bubastis a document of Delta urbanism and not only of one temple. Since 1991 the site has been the charge of the Tell Basta Project, a joint German–Egyptian mission founded from the University of Potsdam with the Egyptian Supreme Council of Antiquities and continued in recent years under Würzburg direction (Eva Lange-Athinodorou) with the Egyptian ministry. Its method is the opposite of Naville's clearance: epigraphic documentation of the thousands of fallen temple blocks in place, architectural reconstruction from their distribution, geoarchaeology of the canals and the gezira, and, since 2018, excavation of the city's earliest levels beneath the ka-temple of Pepi I, where the Fourth–Fifth Dynasty provincial palace of Section X came to light (published in the Journal of Egyptian Archaeology, 2022). The project's work in the built-up area before the temple's entrance — Saite to Ptolemaic housing and a Roman court along the processional road — has begun to give the festival city its streets. What remains unexcavated, under and around modern Zagazig, is most of the ancient city; the archive states the excavation history, as it states the goddess's early record, as a dotted line honestly drawn.

XIV. Herodotus on the Temple — Histories II.137–138 Read Critically

Herodotus of Halicarnassus visited Egypt in the fifth century BCE, probably in the decades around the middle of the century, and his second book is the earliest surviving connected account of the country by an outside observer. For Bubastis he is a witness of the first importance and a Greek witness, and this section and the next read him in that order: first for what he saw, then for the frame through which he saw it. All quotations in these two sections are from the translation of G. C. Macaulay (1890), read in full for the chapters quoted; where this article summarises rather than quotes, it says so.

The temple description (II.137–138) opens with the verdict the whole passage exists to justify. Of the temple at Bubastis Macaulay's Herodotus says: "there are other temples which are larger and built with more cost, none more than this is a pleasure to the eyes." He then gives the reason, and it is a surveyor's reason, not a poet's: the city around the temple had been banked up over the centuries — he attributes the raising of Egyptian towns to convict labour under the Ethiopian king Sabacos, gangs sentenced to heap earth before their home cities — while the temple "has not been moved from the place where it was at the first built," so that the sanctuary sits in a hollow and "is looked down upon from all sides as one goes round." Archaeology confirms the mechanism exactly (Sections X–XI): Delta cities rise on their own debris, temples on old sacred ground do not. Herodotus's eye for the telling physical detail is, here, the eye of a good field reporter.

The description proper (II.138) is quoted here in Macaulay's words, in part, because its particulars have been tested against the ground: "Except the entrance it is completely surrounded by water; for channels come in from the Nile, not joining one another, but each extending as far as the entrance of the temple, one flowing round on the one side and the other on the other side, each a hundred feet broad and shaded over with trees." Within the carved stone enclosure wall, he continues, stood "a grove of very large trees planted round a large temple-house, within which is the image of the goddess," the whole precinct "a furlong every way"; the gateway stood ten fathoms high with figures six cubits high; and opposite the entrance a stone-paved road, about three furlongs long and four hundred feet broad, ran east through the market-place to "the temple of Hermes" — Hermes being his Greek name for the god the Egyptians called Thoth. The two canals are now geoarchaeologically proven (Section XI). The grove, the island setting, the sunken precinct, and the processional road all cohere with the excavated site. On the temple, Herodotus is a reliable witness, and the archive treats him as one.

The frame, however, is Greek, and two of its features must be discounted before the testimony is used. The first is the equation that opens the chapter in Macaulay's rendering: "Bubastis in the Hellenic tongue is Artemis." This is interpretatio graeca, the Greek habit of filing foreign gods under Greek names — Bastet filed under Artemis because both are virginal huntress-powers in Greek logic, an equation that tells us about Greek filing, not about Bastet, whose maternity, fertility, and festival drunkenness are precisely what Artemis is not. The second is his measurement habit: furlongs, fathoms, and hundred-feet are round, converted, and paced-off numbers, the estimates of a traveller, valuable as scale and worthless as survey. Read with those two discounts applied — Greek names translated back, Greek numbers read as orders of magnitude — II.137–138 remains what the excavators found it: the best single description of the temple ever written, by the only classical author who describes it as a building in a living city rather than as a marvel in a list.

XV. Herodotus on the Festival — Histories II.59–60 Read Critically

If the temple passage is Herodotus the reporter, the festival passage is Herodotus the ethnographer at full stretch, and it is the most famous account of any Egyptian festival to survive from antiquity. Its setting is a ranking (II.59): the Egyptians, he says, hold solemn assemblies not once a year but often, and "the principal one of these and the most enthusiastically celebrated" — Macaulay: the chiefest and most zealously kept — is the festival of Artemis, that is Bastet, at Bubastis, ahead of the festival of Isis at Busiris, of Athena at Sais, of the Sun at Heliopolis, of Leto at Buto, and of Ares at Papremis. The ranking is the first fact to keep: whatever discount the details require, a well-travelled Greek of the fifth century BCE, listing Egypt's great pilgrimage feasts from his own enquiry, put Bubastis first.

The journey (II.60) is the heart of the passage, and Macaulay's translation is quoted here because every later account descends from it. The pilgrims go by river, "a great multitude of each sex in every boat"; some of the women have rattles and rattle them, some of the men play the flute through the whole voyage, and the rest sing and clap their hands. At every riverside town the boats come to land and the performance turns outward: some women keep up the rattling, "others cry aloud and jeer at the women in that city, some dance, and some stand up and pull up their garments. This they do by every city along the river-bank." On arrival, in Macaulay's words, "they hold festival celebrating great sacrifices, and more wine of grapes is consumed upon that festival than during the whole of the rest of the year." The rattles are sistra, the goddess's own instrument (Section IV); the flutes, singing, and clapping are the sound of the pacified Eye being escorted, in public, by water, exactly as the Distant Goddess frame of Section VIII predicts. The jeering and the skirt-lifting are the festival's licensed obscenity, the ritual insult and exposure that Greek and Egyptian festival practice both used to provoke laughter, fertility, and the goddess's favour — the archive notes the parallel, in Egyptian myth itself, of the goddess whose display of her body makes a grieving god laugh and return to his duties, and treats Herodotus's shock as part of the evidence: the rite was meant to be unseemly by the standards of the bank.

Then the number. The pilgrims assemble, Macaulay translates, "even to the number of seventy myriads of men and women, besides children" — seventy myriads being 700,000. Three critical facts govern its use. First, Herodotus himself attributes it: "so say the natives" — it is a figure he was told, by interested local informants, not a count he made, and he flags it as hearsay inside the sentence that reports it. Second, it is a round myriad-number of exactly the kind Greek festival rhetoric trades in: its function is to say "beyond counting, the greatest crowd in Egypt," and it performs that function perfectly whether the true figure was 700,000 or a tenth of it. Third, it is demographically implausible as a literal census of a single Delta festival in a country whose total population in the period is usually estimated in the low millions: a fifth or a quarter of the nation, less children, cannot have been on the river to Bubastis at once. The archive therefore prints the number as rhetoric that measures fame, not attendance — evidence, of the strongest kind, for the festival's reputation in its own century, and no evidence at all for its head-count. The wine comparison is of the same kind and gets the same treatment: "more than in the whole rest of the year" is a superlative in the grammar of praise, and it is quoted here as the festival's ancient publicity, not as its cellar book.

XVI. The Festival in Egyptian Terms — What Can Actually Be Known

Stripped of Greek framing, what was the festival of? No Egyptian liturgical calendar for Bubastis survives with its programme intact, and the archive begins by admitting that the festival's date, duration, and running order are not certainly known. What can be reconstructed is its theological shape, from the three bodies of evidence that do survive: the Eye-of-Ra cycle, the architecture, and the votive record.

From the cycle (Section VIII), the festival is a homecoming feast of the Eye: the goddess approached in her pacified aspect, escorted with the music that pacifies — sistrum and flute and song, precisely the instruments Herodotus heard — and feasted with the intoxicating drink whose mythic charter is the night of the red beer in the Book of the Heavenly Cow, where the pacification of the raging goddess by drink is ordained to be repeated "at the seasonal feasts of the year, forever," as the archive's Sekhmet article documents from Hornung's translation. The parallel Festival of Drunkenness of Hathor at Dendera and of Mut at Thebes, treated in the companion articles, is the same rite in other cities: communal, musical, alcoholic celebration as a technology for keeping the Eye in its beneficent mood. The Bubastis festival is that technology at its largest attested scale, in the city of the continuum's house posting — the feast of the fire at rest, given by the whole country to itself, on the river.

From the architecture, the festival was a processional event: the stone road east from the temple gate through the market-place (Section XIV), the island precinct entered at one point only, the courts and colonnades built by the Bubastite kings to stage assembly and movement, and Osorkon II's sed hall (Section XII) proving the city's temples were designed for crowds in ordered motion. From the votive record, finally, the festival was an economic event of the first rank: the bronzes of Section XXII and the cat mummies of Section XIX are its material residue, bought and dedicated by pilgrims in numbers that required workshops, breeding programmes, and a burial industry. A festival that leaves behind it a temple rebuilt by kings, a bronze trade, and cemeteries of hundreds of thousands of animals does not need Herodotus's 700,000 to be believed as a mass event. The ground supplies the scale; the Greek supplies the sound.

XVII. The Domestic Cat — the Animal Behind the Goddess

The Egyptian domestic cat descends from the African wildcat, and its road into the house ran through the grain store: cats that hunted the mice and rats of Egypt's granaries earned toleration, then feeding, then a place under the chair. The painted tombs of the New Kingdom are the great visual archive of that arrival. Cats sit collared and sometimes jewelled beneath the chairs of married couples, a fish or a bowl before them; a cat in the tomb of Nebamun (British Museum EA 37977, c. 1350 BCE) hunts birds in the marshes in the fowling boat with its master, gripping birds in its claws and teeth in the most famous cat image of the ancient world; other cats wear the broad collar and the earring that mark a valued household animal. The word for the animal, mꞽ [Gardiner: E13] [𓃠], is a sound-picture of its cry, and individual cats could be named and mourned as persons of the house: the crown prince Thutmose, eldest son of Amenhotep III, buried his cat Ta-miu, "the She-Cat," in her own small limestone sarcophagus at Saqqara, its sides carved with the cat herself, collared, before an offering table, and with the goddesses who would protect her in the afterlife. A pet with a sarcophagus is not livestock. By the New Kingdom the cat was family.

The cat's behaviour, observed at Egyptian closeness for millennia, supplied the goddess's attributes with unusual literalness. The mother cat's defence of her litter — the quality Herodotus's ethnography seizes on, in his account of tom cats killing kittens and mothers' fierce devotion to their young — is the zoological root of Bastet's nursery protection (Section XXI). The cat's night vision and silent hunting made it the natural enemy of the serpent and the scorpion in the house's dark corners, and the Great Cat of Heliopolis, who in Book of the Dead Spell 17 is the sun god himself in cat form, knife in paw, cutting off the head of the serpent Apep beneath the sacred ished-tree, raised the house's mouser into a cosmic serpent-slayer: the archive's Apep article treats the serpent whose defeat the cat performs nightly. And the cat's fertility — litter after litter, with a fecundity no other household animal matched — underwrites the kittens at the goddess's feet in Section VII. Bastet's theology is, to an extent true of no other Egyptian deity, natural history theologised: what the cat does in the house, the goddess does in the world.

XVIII. Cats in Religious Practice — Herodotus II.66–67 and Diodorus

Between the festival chapters and the temple description, Herodotus gives the cats of Egypt their own ethnographic passage (II.66–67), and it is quoted and paraphrased here from Macaulay's translation, as in Sections XIV–XV. When a fire breaks out, he reports, the Egyptians stand in a line caring more for the cats than for quenching the burning, while the cats slip through or leap over them into the fire — a scene he presents as the strangest of Egyptian prodigies, and which the archive reads as a traveller's heightening of a real duty: the sacred animal was not to be left to burn if men could prevent it, and cats, being cats, defeated the cordon. When a cat dies a natural death in a house, "the occupants of a house where a cat has died a natural death shave their eyebrows and no more" — where a dog has died, the head and whole body are shaven, the graduated mourning of a household that ranked its animals. And then the sentence on which Section XIX is built, in Macaulay's words: the cats when they are dead "are carried away to sacred buildings in the city of Bubastis, where after being embalmed they are buried." Dead cats, from all Egypt in Herodotus's presentation, had one address: the goddess's city. The cemeteries Naville opened twenty-three centuries later are that sentence's archaeological footnote.

Four centuries later the Sicilian historian Diodorus, who visited Egypt in the years around 60–56 BCE, reports the same sacrality from the side of its enforcement, in a passage (Library of History I.83) whose translation by C. H. Oldfather for the Loeb Classical Library the archive follows here in paraphrase, with one short quotation. Whoever kills a cat, Diodorus says, even unintentionally, is certainly put to death by the crowd; and he tells, as an eyewitness, of the Roman who killed a cat by accident while a Roman embassy was in Egypt and the king — Ptolemy XII, not yet recognised by Rome as "friend" — was courting Roman favour with every means at his command. Neither the king's officials sent to beg the man off "nor the fear of Rome which all the people felt were enough to save the man from punishment, even though his act had been an accident." Diodorus closes with the historian's oath: this he relates "not from hearsay," but as something seen with his own eyes. The archive treats the story as Diodorus's testimony about Ptolemaic popular religion — a single, vivid, self-authenticating anecdote, not a statute book — and notes what it proves even at anecdotal strength: in the first century BCE the cat's sacrality could override a king's diplomacy and a superpower's name, in public, in the capital's streets.

Read together, the two Greek witnesses frame the practice from its two ends: Herodotus's mourning household and embalmed dead carried to Bubastis; Diodorus's crowd enforcing the living animal's immunity with lethal violence. Between them lies the whole economy of the next section — an animal protected in life by taboo, honoured in natural death by mourning, and, in the votive system, bred, killed, mummified, and buried by the hundred thousand as a prayer. The contradiction between the taboo and the killing is real, was real to the Egyptians who maintained both, and is not resolved by calling either side hypocrisy: the taboo governed the cat as the goddess's creature in the house; the votive killing belonged to the temple, where the animal's death and mummification were the mechanism that delivered the dedicator's prayer to the goddess forever. Section XIX examines that mechanism as procedure.

XIX. Cat Cemeteries and Mummification — Procedure and Scale

The cat cemeteries are Bastet's largest surviving archive, and they exist at two principal addresses. At Bubastis itself, Naville's excavations found the burial grounds Herodotus's sentence predicts: pits, some lined with brick or hardened clay, filled with masses of cat bones — many of them, at Bubastis distinctively, burned before burial, a local rite that consumed the embalmed body and committed the remains, with beads, bronzes, and small statues of the goddess among them, to the pit. At Saqqara, the necropolis of Memphis, her second sanctuary — the Bubasteion, where Bastet was Lady of Ankhtawy — occupied the cliff edge south-east of the pyramid of Teti — the necropolis surveyed in the archive's Saqqara article — where New Kingdom nobles had cut their tombs in the rock; the French mission of Alain-Pierre Zivie, working there from 1980 and organised from 1986 as the Mission Archéologique Française du Bubasteion, uncovered the complex: a temple of Bastet, its enclosure, and the New Kingdom tombs reused, from the second half of the first millennium BCE, as catacombs for mummified cats. Egyptian excavations further west along the same cliff, reported by Mostafa Waziri and Mohammad Youssef (Prague Egyptological Studies XXIII, 2019), found the pattern repeated — Old and New Kingdom tombs reused in the Late Period for cat burial, with wooden and bronze cat statues, coffins, and amulets among the deposits. The archive's article on mummification treats the Saqqara necropolis and its animal catacombs in their wider setting; this section keeps to Bastet's cats.

The Procedure, as the Bodies Report It

The Bubasteion cats have been studied as bodies, not only as bundles, above all by Zivie with the radiologist Roger Lichtenberg ("The Cats of the Goddess Bastet," in Ikram's Divine Creatures, 2005), and their findings give the votive procedure at procedural strength. More than a hundred complete cat mummies and thousands of cat bones had been recovered at the time of their report. The cats died young: radiography showed the majority killed at a few months to about a year old, by strangulation — the neck dislocated — or by a violent blow to the skull; two manners of mummification follow, a simple one in which the legs and tail are bound and wrapped close to the body, and a more elaborate one in which head, body, legs, and tail are wrapped separately, some mummies finished with modelled eyes and ears of linen. The mummification itself was, by human standards, basic: the animal was dried out, without evisceration — desiccation, anointing, and wrapping, the core sequence the archive's mummification article establishes for animal mummies generally, at about forty days for the full process in experimental replication. Some Bubasteion cats were buried in wooden or stone sarcophagi, some with kittens, jewellery, amulets, and sculptures; and some packets, radiography showed, contained only a few bones, or no cat at all — clay and pebbles in a cat-shaped wrapping. The archive reports that last finding as it reports the parallel finding for animal mummies generally in the companion article: in a mass pilgrimage market the workshops graded output to price, and the bundle's efficacy, in the votive logic, rode on its dedication and form. It is a fact about an industry, and it is stated without the modern sneer it sometimes attracts.

The Categories and the Scale

Salima Ikram's classification of animal mummies, set out in her UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology article "Mummification" (2010) and used throughout this archive, sorts the cats of the cemeteries into their true category. They are not, in their overwhelming majority, pets — though pets were mummified too, and Ta-miu (Section XVII) is the type of that separate, affectionate practice. Nor are they sacred animals in the technical sense, the single chosen creature maintained as a god's living embodiment, as the Apis bull was: Bastet had no single incarnate cat. They are votive mummies: animals bred or obtained, killed, mummified, sold to a pilgrim, and dedicated to the goddess, so that — in Ikram's formulation — the donor's prayers would be taken to the god and addressed throughout eternity, the mummy thereafter buried in the goddess's sacred cemetery. The mummy is a prayer made permanent and physical, and the cemetery is its archive: every bundle a filed petition.

Scale must be stated with the caution the companion article applies. No ancient count exists, and the cemeteries were robbed, burned, and — in the nineteenth century — industrially destroyed: Ikram's UEE article records that cat mummies were used as ballast for ships leaving Egypt and then as fertiliser in Europe, a fate that consumed the Beni Hasan cat cemetery almost entire in a single notorious shipment and that makes every surviving total a floor, not a figure. What can be said: the practice ran at Bubastis, at Saqqara, and at other Bastet shrines for centuries, at its height in the Late and Ptolemaic periods; the Bubasteion alone yielded its hundred-plus mummies and thousands of bones from a fraction of its catacombs; the Bubastis pits held cats in masses Naville could describe only as pits full of bones; and the system that supplied them — breeding, killing at a standard young age, bench mummification, wrapping grades, sale, dedication, burial — was an industry with premises, personnel, and a pilgrim market, of exactly the kind the archive's mummification article documents for animal mummification at industrial scale across more than thirty necropolis sites. Bastet's share of that industry was, with the ibis of Thoth and the dog of Anubis, among the largest in Egypt.

XX. Perfume and Unguents — the Goddess of the Jar

Section I left the goddess's name hanging on a jar. This section follows the jar into the cult. If Quirke's derivation is right — Bastet as "she of the bꜣs [Gardiner: W2] [𓎰]," the ointment jar — then perfume is not one of her interests but her etymological essence, and the associations that gather around her read as the working out of a name: the unguent jar of pale stone on the dressing table, the perfumed oil with which a body was protected, beautified, and made fit for a god's company, the scented fat of the festival cone, the anointing of statues and of the dead. Even if the derivation is wrong — and Section I keeps Allen's alternative on the record — the association is independently real: the goddess of the house is the goddess of the house's luxury and hygiene, and in Egypt luxury and hygiene were both, to a first approximation, scented oil.

The archive's article on kyphi, incense, perfume and unguents gives the technology in full procedural detail, and this section borrows only its frame. Egyptian perfume was not distilled alcohol — spirit distillation is not in the ancient Egyptian record — but fat and oil, charged with aromatics: incense pellets of the kyphi type for the censer, compounded over a fortnight's manufacture in the temple laboratory; unguents proper, scented oils and fats macerated with resins, flowers, and spices and expressed through the pressing cloth, the great preparations maturing for a year or more; the seven sacred oils of the ritual canon; and the unguent cone worn melting on the head at banquets, scenting the wearer through the evening. Anointing, in that world, was protection in material form: oil sealed the skin against the desert air, scent marked the body as cleansed and god-ready, and the same preparations served the living at their festivals, the statues in their shrines, and the dead in their wrappings — the Saqqara embalming workshop's labelled vessels, analysed in the companion article, held unguents of exactly this family. A goddess named for the unguent jar stands at the point where cosmetics, medicine, and liturgy are one substance in one vessel.

Her family keeps the theme. Nefertem, the god of the lotus and of perfume itself — the divine perfumer who brings the lotus to the nose of Ra — is Sekhmet's son in the Memphite triad and, in the variant tradition Section IX records, Bastet's: on either genealogy the continuum's house-and-festival posting is the perfume god's mother. The festival of Sections XV–XVI completes the circuit: a crowd anointed and scented for the goddess's day, wine within and unguent without, music over all — the senses addressed together, as Egyptian festivity always addressed them. The archive therefore treats Bastet's perfume association as load-bearing, not decorative: the jar in the name, the unguent in the rite, and the perfumer in the family are three witnesses to one fact about her posting. The fire at rest in the house smells of scented oil, because scented oil is what a protected, festal, well-kept house smells of.

XXI. Bastet in the Home — Women, Children and the Household Gods

Bastet's largest temple was Bubastis, but her commonest address was the house. The personal religion of Egypt — the religion of amulets, household shrines, and small bronzes, as distinct from the state liturgy of the great temples — placed her among the small company of gods who specialised in the dangers of domestic life: pregnancy and childbirth, infancy and childhood, sickness in the house, the threats that come by night. Her credentials for the posting are her animal's: the mother cat's defence of the litter (Section XVII), the house cat's war on the serpent, the scorpion, and the rat in the stores. A Bastet amulet on a child's body, a Bastet bronze on a household shrine, a cat with kittens in faience worn at the throat: these are not souvenirs of the Bubastis pilgrimage, though the pilgrimage trade sold them. They are the goddess posted, in miniature, where her work was.

The household world she guarded is treated in full in the archive's article on women, family, marriage and childhood. Her household colleague is Bes, the bandy-legged, lion-maned dwarf god whose image guarded the same thresholds — the bed, the birth chamber, the mirror and the cosmetic jar — and whose article in this archive traces the domestic apotropaic system in full. The pairing is systematic, and the archive's Bes article supplies its most charming material witness: a colossal Bes head, said to be from Bubastis itself, nearly forty centimetres high for the head alone — the house god present, at monumental scale, in the cat goddess's own city. Bes and Bastet divide the nursery between them by temperament: Bes frightens evil away with his ugliness, his noise, and his dance; Bastet watches it away, the still, ears-forward vigilance of the cat on the threshold. Egyptian houses, which kept both, evidently did not consider either method sufficient alone. Around them gathered the wider domestic pantheon the archive treats in its articles on amulets and talismans and on dream incubation — where the cat appears among the dream animals, carrying Bastet's associations into the night world of omens — and Taweret, the hippopotamus goddess of childbirth, completes the birth chamber's usual company.

For women specifically, the sources support a careful statement. Bastet's cult was not a women's cult — Herodotus's festival boats are mixed, men and women together, and her dedicators in the bronze inscriptions are of both sexes — but her domains cluster around the parts of life Egyptian culture assigned to women's keeping and women's risk: the house, the stores, childbirth, the nursing of children, perfume and adornment, music and dance. The festival's most notorious vignettes are women's performances — the rattling, the singing, the jeering, the dancing, the lifted skirts of the river progress — performed by women, at women in the towns passed, before a goddess. The archive states the pattern as the evidence gives it: a goddess of the house's prosperity and increase, honoured by the whole community, in whose rites women are not attendants but principals. In the first millennium, when the personal votive record is richest, that combination made her one of the most-dedicated-to deities in Egypt — the Met's verdict on her statuettes (Section VII) is the household cult's footprint in museum numbers.

XXII. Late Period Bronzes and the Votive Industry

The Late Period bronze is Bastet's characteristic monument, and the industry that made it is as much her history as the temple is. The British Museum's account of the type's supreme example frames the whole phenomenon in two sentences the archive adopts: the Gayer-Anderson cat is a bronze figure depicting one form of the goddess Bastet, usually shown as a cat-headed woman or as a cat, whose principal cult centre was Bubastis; and it "probably comes from a temple — thousands of bronze figures of gods, in varying sizes and forms, were dedicated in temples throughout Egypt." Thousands is the operative word. The first millennium BCE in Egypt is, materially, an age of bronze dedication: hollow-cast by the lost-wax method, in workshops attached to or supplying the temples, in sizes from amulet to altar-piece, at prices that graded from the pilgrim's small faience cat to commissions in precious metal. Bastet, with Osiris and a handful of others, stands at the top of that trade's output.

The Gayer-Anderson Cat

The most famous single Bastet in the world is a cat: the British Museum's Gayer-Anderson cat (EA 64391), a hollow bronze of the Late Period, dated by the Museum to around 600 BCE, 42 centimetres high, named for Major Robert Grenville Gayer-Anderson, the collector who kept it in his Cairo house — now the Gayer-Anderson Museum, where a copy remains — and donated the original to the British Museum in 1939. The Museum's description gives the object its full splendour: the cat wears golden earrings and a golden nose ring, and a silver protective pectoral; the scarab on its head and the scarab on its chest symbolise rebirth, and the silver wedjat eye — wḏꜣt [Gardiner: D10] [𓂀] — on the pectoral invoked protection and healing. X-radiography, reported in the Museum's literature, adds the object's private history: the casting is cracked almost through around the body's middle, and an internal system of strengthening — ancient repair or the collector's, the reports discuss both — alone keeps the head in place. Even the masterpiece of the votive industry is a repaired thing, kept whole by hidden work: the archive notes the fact because it is the industry's honest emblem, splendour and patch in one skin.

The Range of the Trade

Below the masterpiece, the trade's range is the evidence for the cult's breadth. The Metropolitan Museum's Bastets include the patterned-dress cat-headed goddess of Section VII (15.43.5) and, at the industry's most ingenious, a Ptolemaic cat statuette (332–30 BCE) made as a container for a mummified cat: image and offering in one object, the bronze cat carrying the real cat to the goddess, its pierced ear once holding a gold ring, a wedjat-eye necklace at its throat. The Met's curatorial summary of such pieces states the system plainly, in the form this archive adopts: mummified cats were given to Bastet at her temples, above all at Bubastis and Saqqara, sometimes inside fine bronze or wooden cases, and the mummies were then buried within the temple grounds. Bronze and cemetery, Sections XIX and XXII, are one economy seen from its two ends: the pilgrim's money became metal or mummy, the temple kept both, and the goddess kept the prayer.

The industry's geography follows the cult's. Workshops at the great sanctuaries supplied their own pilgrims; fine pieces travelled; a bronze Bastet with four kittens, found at Bubastis and dated by the British Museum to 900–600 BCE, shows the type already standard at the cult centre in the Twenty-second Dynasty's aftermath, the goddess cat-headed, sistrum in hand, her litter at her feet, the inscription naming her "Bastet, the great, Mistress of Bubastis" (Section II). Production on this scale, over this span, in this range of materials and prices, is the material signature of a mass cult — and it is the strongest single argument, stronger than any text, for reading Herodotus's festival superlatives as fame honestly earned. Industries do not grow to this size around minor feasts.

XXIII. Bastet Abroad and in Later Reception

Bastet travelled, but she travelled as Greek and Roman writers carried her, more than as her cult was planted abroad, and the archive keeps the two kinds of travel apart. Her cult's genuine foreign footprint is the Delta's own cosmopolitanism: the Phoenician spelling of her name, ʾbst, attests her recognition in the Levant trade world that used Bubastis's roads, and Greek settlers in Egypt from the seventh century BCE onward met her at Naucratis's doorstep and filed her, in their system, as Artemis (Section XIV). That equation governed her classical afterlife. Under Artemis's name she acquired a Greek genealogy (Section IX), a place in the classical lists of Egyptian gods, and, in the Greek form Ailuros — "Cat" itself, as a divine name — a shadow existence as the cat-goddess of the Greek imagination.

The classical literature gives her one famous scene, which this article reports in paraphrase, not quotation: in Ovid's Metamorphoses (Book 5), when the gods flee the monster Typhon into Egypt and hide in animal forms, the goddess who is Diana to the Romans — Bastet's Greek-Roman counterpart — becomes a cat. The story is a Greco-Roman aetiology, invented to explain why Egyptian gods have animal shapes, and it is evidence for Bastet's reception, not for her theology; the archive files it accordingly, beside Ezekiel's Pi-beseth, where the city appears in Hebrew prophecy among the Egyptian shrines doomed to fall — evidence, from the other side, of Bubastis's international fame in the sixth century BCE. A city known to Greek historians, Hebrew prophets, and Phoenician traders by its goddess's name was, by any ancient measure, a famous place.

XXV. Esoteric Analysis — the Fire at Rest in the House

// READING THIS SECTION //

What follows is the archive's interpretive reading of Bastet, offered as such. It is not presented as established Egyptological fact, and it rests only on the documented material of the preceding sections: the name and its jar, the period-by-period iconography, the continuum, the festival, the cemeteries, and the bronzes.

The archive reads Bastet as the Egyptian theology of domesticated power — and insists, on the evidence of Sections V and VI, that domesticated is a statement about address, not about essence. Consider what the sources refuse to do. The iconography refuses to disarm her: the cat-headed goddess carries a lion-headed shield, and the seated cat wears the wedjat and the scarab of a solar power. The continuum refuses to separate her from the fire: the same dedication names Sekhmet-Bastet, and the same New Year rite gives her image and the plague goddess's indifferently. The festival refuses to make her safe by making her solemn: her feast is the loudest, drunkest, most licentious in Herodotus's Egypt, the pacified Eye celebrated with the very instruments — music, wine, sexuality, laughter — that pacified it. And the cemeteries refuse the sentimental reading altogether: the goddess of the beloved house cat was served by an industry that bred and killed her animal by the hundred thousand, because in the votive logic the prayer's permanence was worth the animal's life. At every level the system says the same thing it says of Sekhmet, from the other end: the power is real, it has not been abolished or sentimentalised, it has been given a home — and a home, in this theology, is not where power stops. It is where power agrees to stay.

That is why the archive, following the material, treats the ointment jar of her name as her deepest symbol, deeper even than the cat. The jar holds a precious, volatile, protective substance, made by long art — the archive's kyphi and unguents article gives the year-long maturations and fortnight manufactures of the temple laboratory — sealed in stone, opened at the festival and the sickbed and the burial, effective precisely because it is concentrated and precisely because it is kept. The alchemical reading the archive develops in Alchemy finds the same grammar throughout the tradition Egypt seeded: the volatile fixed in a vessel, the poison become medicine by dose and keeping, the fire banked in the hearth it serves. Bastet is that grammar with a face — the face, appropriately, of the animal that walked out of the desert into the grain store of its own accord, and stayed, and was worshipped for staying: the wild thing that chose the house, and whose choice the house has been celebrating, with rattles and wine, ever since.

// THE BASTET PATTERN, AS THE ARCHIVE STATES IT //

Power is posted, not abolished — the fire at rest is still fire; the house posting is a duty, not a retirement. Gentleness is a discipline — the cat's restraint is the lioness's, practised at close range, over young she would die defending. Protection smells of work — the jar is filled by a year's maceration; safety in a house is manufactured, maintained, and renewed. Joy is a rite, not a mood — the festival's music and wine are the scheduled re-performance of a pacification that must never be left to chance. The prayer outlives the pray-er — bronze and mummy are petitions filed in permanent materials, addressed to a goddess whose whole office is to remain on duty. This is the archive's interpretive summary of the Bastet material, offered in that spirit: as a reading to be tested against the texts and objects of Section XXVI, not as a doctrine to be believed.
