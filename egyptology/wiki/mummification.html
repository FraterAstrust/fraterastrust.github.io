I. The Words for the Work — Mummy, Embalmer, and Workshop

The English word "mummy" is not Egyptian. It descends, through medieval Latin and Arabic mūmiyā — a word for bitumen and for a bituminous medicinal substance — from the dark appearance of many excavated bodies, whose resinous coatings were mistaken for bitumen long before chemical analysis showed how rarely true bitumen was used at all. The Egyptians had no single word that means exactly what "mummification" means in English, because for them the process was not one act but a sequence of distinct crafts — washing, opening, drying, anointing, wrapping — each with its own vocabulary, its own personnel, and its own place. This article follows their vocabulary, because their vocabulary is the process map.

The finished product — the preserved, wrapped, ritually completed body — is the sꜥḥ [Gardiner: S29-D36-V28] [𓋴𓂝𓎛]. The word carries a double sense that English cannot reproduce: sꜥḥ is both the mummy as an object and a title of dignity, "the noble, the august one." The wrapped dead person is not refuse awaiting disposal; the word itself promotes them. The body as mere physical frame, by contrast, is the ẖt [Gardiner: F51] [𓄹] — the belly, the trunk, the material substrate on which the work is done. Egyptian funerary language keeps the two registers apart with complete consistency: the ẖt [Gardiner: F51] is what decays; the sꜥḥ [Gardiner: S29-D36-V28] is what the craft makes of it.

The craftsman is the wt [Gardiner: G43-X1] [𓅱𓏏] — literally "the wrapper," from the verb wt [Gardiner: G43-X1], "to wrap, to bandage." The choice of defining act is telling. Greek and modern accounts fixate on the dramatic opening of the body; the Egyptian professional title names the patient, repetitive, textile work of the second half of the process, the work the Ritual of Embalming (Section XIII) actually describes in detail. The place of the opening and drying work is the wꜥbt [Gardiner: G43-D36-D58-X1] [𓅱𓂝𓃀𓏏] — the "pure place," conventionally rendered "embalming workshop" — and the place of preliminary washing is the ibw [Gardiner: M17-D58-G43] [𓇋𓃀𓅱], the purification tent. A further name, pr-nfr [Gardiner: O1-F35] [𓉐𓄤], "the House of Beauty" (or "House of Perfection"), attaches to the embalming establishment in several periods: the place where the dead are made nfr — beautiful, complete, good — again.

The full sign list is given in the hieroglyph reference.

Above the whole establishment stands a god. Anubis — jnpw [Gardiner: M17-N35-Q3-G43-E16] [𓇋𓈖𓊪𓅱𓃣] — is the divine embalmer: in the Osiris myth it is Anubis who first performs the work, on the body of Osiris himself, and every human chief embalmer thereafter works in his person. His epithets are job descriptions. He is ḥry-sštꜣ [Gardiner: V28-D21-Z4-S29-N37-X1-G1] [𓎛𓂋𓏭𓋴𓈙𓏏𓄿], "Master of Secrets" — the secrets are the workshop's. He is Imy-wt, "He Who Is in the wt [Gardiner: G43-X1]," in the place of wrapping. The archive's article on Anubis treats the god in full; this article treats his craft. The two cannot finally be separated, and Section IV returns to the moment in each working day when the chief embalmer put on the god's mask and the distinction formally dissolved.

A last preliminary distinction. "Embalming" and "mummification" are used interchangeably in popular writing. In this article embalming names the active treatments — excerebration, evisceration, desiccation, anointing — and mummification names the whole transformation, treatments plus wrapping plus ritual completion, that turns a corpse into a sꜥḥ [Gardiner: S29-D36-V28]. The distinction matters because the surviving Egyptian manual literature divides along exactly that line: the Greek historians describe the embalming bench, while the Egyptian Ritual describes the mummification liturgy, and neither source alone gives the whole (Section III).

II. Why Preserve the Body — Soul, Recognition, and the Transfigured Dead

Egyptian anthropology did not locate the person in a single immortal soul that could simply vacate a disposable body. The person was a composite — the archive's full treatment is the composite soul — and several of its components remained bound to the preserved body. The kꜣ [Gardiner: D28] [𓂓], the vital double and sustaining presence, needed a recognisable home to return to and a mouth that could receive offerings. The bꜣ [Gardiner: E10] [𓃝] — written here with the post-audit value of the saddle-billed stork sign — the mobile, individual manifestation of the dead person, travelled out into the world by day and returned to the body in the tomb by night. If the body was destroyed, the circuit was broken. Preservation was therefore not sentimentality about remains; it was infrastructure for a continuing existence that was understood to be, in part, located.

The heart makes the point most sharply. The ib [Gardiner: F34] [𓄣] was the seat of thought, memory, emotion, and moral record — the organ with which an Egyptian, in his own idiom, thought. It was therefore the one major organ the embalmers normally left exactly where it lay (Section VIII), because the dead person would need it at the judgment, where it was weighed against the feather of mꜣꜥt [Gardiner: U1-D36-X1] [𓌳𓂝𓏏] — truth, order, rightness — in the rite the archive treats as the Weighing of the Heart. The brain, by the same anthropology, had no such role, and its routine removal (Section VII) shocked Greek observers precisely because Greek assumptions ran the other way. Neither culture was being careless. Each was preserving what its own model of the person said mattered.

The goal state has a name too. The dead person who has been correctly embalmed, wrapped, furnished, and judged becomes an ꜣḫ — a transfigured, effective spirit — and the wrapped body is the anchor of that state. This is why the process closes not at the workshop door but with the Opening of the Mouth — wp-r [Gardiner: F13-D21] [𓄋𓂋], written with the corrected value wp [Gardiner: F13], "to open," upon r [Gardiner: D21], "mouth" — the rite that restored breath, sight, speech, and eating to the completed sꜥḥ [Gardiner: S29-D36-V28] before the tomb was sealed. Embalming preserved the instrument; the Opening reactivated it. The funerary texts buried with the dead are the script for what the reactivated person then does.

It is equally important to say what mummification was not. It was not universal. Across three thousand years, the full process described in this article was always a purchased service, graded by price (Sections V–VI), and the great majority of Egyptians were buried simply — in sand, in a mat, in a plain coffin — relying on the desert's own desiccation, which had suggested the artificial process in the first place: predynastic bodies buried in hot, dry sand above the flood line are frequently preserved to this day, skin, hair, and nails intact, with no embalmer ever having touched them. The craft began as an imitation of the desert and became, for those who could pay, an improvement on it. Nor was the full process static: the techniques of the Old Kingdom, the classical perfection of the New Kingdom, and the elaborate surface artistry of the Ptolemaic and Roman periods differ in detail at almost every step, and this article flags period difference wherever the evidence requires it.

// THE OSIRIAN PATTERN //

Every mummification rehearsed one precedent. In the myth, Osiris — wsir [Gardiner: Q1-D4-A40] [𓊨𓁹𓀭] — is murdered and dismembered; Isis gathers the body; Anubis embalms it; and Osiris rises, not back into earthly life, but into kingship of the dead. The embalmers' client was therefore not merely being preserved but being Osirified: the first grade of Herodotus's price list (Section V) was said by the embalmers themselves to belong to the god whose name Herodotus declines, on grounds of piety, to speak in that context — and every reader in antiquity understood the unspoken name. The workshop procedure in Sections V–XV is, in the Egyptians' own framing, a technology for repeating that first, divine embalming on human material.

III. How We Know — Texts, Workshops, and the Mummies Themselves

No single source describes the whole process, and the first duty of a craft article is to say plainly what each kind of evidence can and cannot establish. Five kinds survive. Greek and Roman literary accounts — above all Herodotus, Histories 2.86–89 (c. 450 BCE), and Diodorus Siculus, Library 1.91 (1st century BCE) — describe the embalming bench from outside: the grades, the incision, the natron. They are outsiders' reports of a closed, hereditary craft, generally accurate on sequence and sometimes wrong or garbled on mechanism, and they are used in this article as reports to be checked against the bodies, not as bench manuals. Egyptian ritual manuals — the Ritual of Embalming and the older Papyrus Louvre-Carlsberg manual — describe the work from inside, but only its second, liturgical half (Sections XII–XIII). Labelled workshop material — pre-eminently the inscribed vessels of the Saqqara workshop (Section XVI) — names substances and the body parts they were for. The mummies themselves, examined by imaging and chemistry rather than by the destructive unwrappings of earlier centuries, are the final court of appeal for every claim in the other four. And modern replication (Section XVIII) tests whether a described procedure, performed as described, actually does what the texts say it does.

The UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology's treatments of funerary ritual — notably the entries on funerary rituals for the Pharaonic and for the Ptolemaic and Roman periods — supply the frame within which the archive reads these sources: embalming as one stage in a longer funerary sequence that begins at death and ends at the sealed tomb, with the workshop phase itself subdivided by the Egyptians into purification, treatment, and wrapping. The British Museum's synthesis of the process for its collections makes the same structural point in museum terms: roughly forty days of the seventy belong to drying in natron, and the balance to washing, anointing, and wrapping — a division this article adopts as its working reconciliation in Section XII, while presenting each source's own numbers separately rather than flattening them into one.

Two cautions govern everything that follows. First, period. Herodotus watched (or heard described) the craft of the 26th Dynasty and later; the Louvre-Carlsberg manual was written around 1450 BCE; the Saqqara workshop is 26th Dynasty; the Ritual of Embalming survives in Roman-period copies of an older composition. A detail true of one is not automatically true of another, and the popular composite "Egyptian mummification" — brain hook plus canopic jars plus seventy days, all at once, for three thousand years — is a museum-label fiction that this article avoids by dating its claims. Second, grade. The sources overwhelmingly describe the best work money could buy. The third grade of Herodotus — a purge and a natron bed, nothing more (Section VI) — was probably the experience of far more bodies, and the archaeological record of modest burials confirms it.

Where a claim in this article rests on a single famous retelling, it is flagged as such in place. Where the evidence genuinely divides — dry natron against natron solution is the standing example (Section XI) — both positions are presented with their best evidence and neither is declared the winner. That is the archive's standard, and embalming, of all subjects, needs it: the subject's literature is unusually rich in confident sentences that dissolve on contact with a CT scan.

IV. The Workshop and Its People — Roles at the Embalming Table

Embalming was a hereditary, temple-adjacent profession, practised in a purpose-built establishment set apart — for reasons at once ritual and practical — near the necropolis. Diodorus, in paraphrase, stresses the embalmers' honour: they were admitted to temples, ate with priests, and were treated as pure men whose work, however it touched the dead, did not pollute them. The single exception in his account proves the rule by inversion, and Section VI gives it in full: the one worker who made the flank incision was ritually driven off with stones and curses, the community's horror at the cutting of a human body discharged onto the one pair of hands that performed it, so that the rest of the team could proceed with clean status.

The Ritual of Embalming names the senior staff of the second half of the process, and its hierarchy is exact. At the head stands the ḥry-sštꜣ [Gardiner: V28-D21-Z4-S29-N37-X1-G1] [𓎛𓂋𓏭𓋴𓈙𓏏𓄿], the Master of Secrets, who supervises the whole. Under him for the most delicate work — the head, inside and out — serves the ḫtmw-nṯr [Gardiner: Aa1-X1-G17-G43-R8] [𓐍𓏏𓅓𓅱𓊹], the god's sealer or divine seal-bearer. The ḫry-ḥbt [Gardiner: Aa1-D21-Z4-V28-D58-X1] [𓐍𓂋𓏭𓎛𓃀𓏏], the lector — literally "he who carries the ritual book" — speaks the glorifications that pair with each manual act. And the wt [Gardiner: G43-X1] [𓅱𓏏] proper, the wrappers, do the bandaging itself. The division of labour is a division of kinds of knowledge: hand, seal, and voice. The manual chapters of the Ritual read, as Töpfer notes, almost like medical prescriptions in their dry, unemotional phrasing; the glorification spells paired with them do the theological work. Both were necessary. Neither was decoration for the other.

The chief embalmer's identification with Anubis was performed, not merely asserted: workshop scenes and later tradition show the presiding figure masked as the jackal god at the bier, and the god's own title — ḥry-sštꜣ [Gardiner: V28-D21-Z4-S29-N37-X1-G1], Master of Secrets — is the human chief's title too. The body, meanwhile, arrived by a fixed route. It was first carried to the ibw [Gardiner: M17-D58-G43] [𓇋𓃀𓅱], the purification tent, where it was washed — Herodotus's sequence presupposes this washing, and the Louvre-Carlsberg manual places purification first, before excerebration and evisceration — and only then taken into the wꜥbt [Gardiner: G43-D36-D58-X1] [𓅱𓂝𓃀𓏏] for the opening work. One practical detail the replication literature recovered (Section XVIII) explains a feature of the archaeology: embalming tables are strikingly wide, because the natron bed that covered the body had to be accommodated beside and beneath it. The table was sized for the salt, not for the corpse.

Herodotus adds the commercial front office, and it deserves quoting as procedure rather than colour: the embalmers kept wooden models of corpses, painted to show the finished result of each grade, and showed them to the family, agreed a price, and only then began. Diodorus, again in paraphrase, adds an itemised price-list laid before the relatives. Embalming was a craft, a priesthood-adjacent office, and a retail business at the same time, and the sources that forget any one of the three misdescribe it.

V. Herodotus, Grade One — the Most Perfect Way, Step by Step

Herodotus, Histories 2.86, in A. D. Godley's Loeb translation as read on the Perseus Digital Library, gives the fullest surviving bench sequence for the most expensive grade. What follows is his sequence, step by step, in his order, with each step checked against the other evidence in its own paragraph. Short quotations are Godley's; everything else is labelled paraphrase. Herodotus prefaces the sequence with the models and the price agreement (Section IV) and with the remark that the most perfect manner "belongs, they say, to One whose name it would be impious for me to mention in treating such a matter" — the unspoken Osiris of Section II.

STEP 1 — THE BRAIN, THROUGH THE NOSTRILS

In Godley's rendering, the embalmers "first draw out part of the brain through the nostrils with an iron hook, and inject certain drugs into the rest." The iron hook, the nostril route, and the two-part method — mechanical extraction for what the hook can reach, chemical treatment for the residue — are all Herodotus's. Section VII takes up the anatomy (the route passes through the ethmoid region into the cranial cavity), the wide variation the mummies themselves display, and the modern replication finding that liquefying and draining worked where scooping failed.

STEP 2 — THE FLANK INCISION, WITH THE ETHIOPIAN STONE

Next, in paraphrase of Godley's text: making a cut near the flank with a sharp knife of "Ethiopian stone," they take out all the contents of the abdomen. The stone is conventionally understood as obsidian or a fine flint — a lithic blade retained, in a bronze- and iron-using culture, for this one cut, which is itself a datum about the rite's conservatism. Diodorus adds that a scribe first marked on the left flank how much was to be cut, and that the cutter — the paraschistes — fled at once under ritual pelting (Section VI). The left-flank position is confirmed by the incisions preserved on innumerable mummies, frequently covered afterward by an embalming plate.

STEP 3 — CLEANSING: PALM WINE AND POUNDED SPICES

The emptied cavity is then cleansed — in Godley's words, "rinsing it with palm wine and bruised spices." Palm wine is mildly alcoholic and acidic; the pounded aromatics are antiseptic in effect whatever their intended rationale. The viscera themselves received parallel treatment — washed, dried, anointed, and wrapped — detailed in Section VIII. Nothing in this step is symbolic excess: a rinsed, spiced, emptied cavity is precisely what a body about to spend weeks in a desiccant bed requires if the desiccation is to start from a clean field.

STEP 4 — FILLING AND SEWING

Then, in Godley's rendering of the sequence, they fill the belly with pure ground myrrh and casia (cassia) and other spices — "save only frankincense" — and sew up the incision. The exclusion of frankincense is one of those details no outsider invents: it is a workshop rule, reported without explanation, and the article preserves it without inventing one. The filling served mechanically as well as aromatically, giving the shrunken cavity a pack that supported the body wall through drying; later practice filled with linen, sawdust-like materials, resin-soaked cloth, and the treated organs themselves returned as packages (Sections VIII and XIII).

STEP 5 — THE NATRON BED

The body is then concealed in natron for the appointed period. Herodotus's own figure belongs to the total: his text, in Godley's version, speaks of the body kept in the natron ("saltpetre" in the older rendering of the Greek litron) for the seventy days of the whole process, "no longer time being allowed." The reconciliation of that figure with the roughly forty days of drying given by museum synthesis and the 35 + 35 structure of the Egyptian manual is the subject of Section XII; the natron itself, of Section X. What Herodotus establishes beyond dispute is the principle: a full bed of the desiccant, inside and out, for a fixed, ritually bounded term.

STEP 6 — WASHING, WRAPPING IN GUMMED LINEN, RETURN

When the term ends, in Godley's sequence, they wash the body and wrap the whole of it in bandages of fine linen cloth smeared with gum — "which the Egyptians mostly use instead of glue" — and return it to the family, who enclose it in a hollow wooden figure in human form and stand it upright in the burial chamber. The wrapping Herodotus compresses into one sentence is, in Egyptian practice, the whole second half of the seventy days (Sections XIII and XV); his compression is the clearest sign that he is reporting the bench work he could learn about, not the liturgy he could not.

Read as a whole, Herodotus's grade one is a sound industrial process: remove the two great putrefactive reservoirs (brain and gut contents), sterilise the cavity, pack it with aromatics, desiccate the whole under controlled conditions, then seal the result in resinous, gummed textile. Every step has a material function a modern preservation scientist can state, and every step also has the ritual framing the Egyptian sources supply. The article holds both readings together throughout, because the workshop did.

VI. Grades Two and Three — Cedar Oil and the Purge; Diodorus and the Price List

Herodotus's second grade (2.87), in paraphrase of Godley's translation, abolishes the knife entirely. Syringes are filled with oil of cedar — the Greek kedria, most plausibly a cedar or juniper tar-oil of the kind the Saqqara residue programme later found saturating that workshop's vessels (Section XVI) — and the oil is injected into the belly through the anus, the passage then being stopped so it cannot run back. The body lies in natron for the appointed days. On the last day the oil is allowed to escape, and Herodotus claims for it a remarkable power: that it brings the stomach and intestines out dissolved with it, and that the natron meanwhile has so worked on the flesh that nothing remains but hide and bones. That dissolution claim is presented here exactly as what it is — Herodotus's report, not a demonstrated chemical result. No modern replication has reproduced the dissolution of the viscera by retained cedar oil, and the archive does not upgrade his report into a mechanism.

The third grade (2.88) is the economy service entire: in Godley's rendering, "they cleanse the belly with a purge, embalm the body for the seventy days and then give it back to be taken away." No incision, no evisceration, no spices, no fine wrapping — a clyster, a natron bed, and return. Herodotus adds at 2.89, here paraphrased, the workshop's handling rule that the wives of notable men and women of great beauty were not handed to the embalmers until three or four days after death, and at 2.90 the special rule for the drowned and those taken by crocodiles, who were embalmed by the priests of the Nile town where the body came ashore and buried in sacred ground, untouchable by lay hands.

Diodorus (1.91), writing four centuries later and used in this article in paraphrase throughout, supplies what Herodotus omits: figures and theatre. The first-class preparation, he says, cost a talent of silver; the second, twenty minae — a third of a talent; the third, very little. The scribe marked the incision line on the left flank. The slitter cut with the Ethiopian stone and fled instantly, pelted with stones and cursed by those standing by — the rite's way of expelling the sacrilege of the cut from the community onto its necessary performer. The evisceration itself was done by hand through the opening, sparing, in Diodorus's account, the heart and the kidneys — a detail Herodotus does not give. Another worker washed each viscus in palm wine and spices. And Diodorus describes the finished body dressed for over thirty days, first with cedar oil and other preparations, then with myrrh, cinnamon, and such spices as both sweeten the smell and preserve — a thirty-plus-day anointing phase that aligns strikingly with the 35-day wrapping half of the Egyptian manual (Section XII). He describes neither excerebration nor a natron heap in this passage; his silence is a reminder that each literary source is a window, not a wall.

The economic structure deserves one plain sentence: desiccation was never the luxury. Every grade, including the cheapest, received the natron bed and its term, because desiccation is what makes a mummy at all. What money bought was everything around it — the surgery, the aromatics, the oils, the linen, the liturgy, and the case. The Saqqara vessels (Section XVI) and the Ritual (Section XIII) both describe the expensive end of that market; the archaeology of ordinary cemeteries describes the other, and a complete craft history has to hold the price list and the purge in the same field of vision.

VII. Excerebration — the Brain, the Ethmoid Route, and What the Evidence Actually Shows

Excerebration — the removal of the brain — is the step modern readers find most startling, and the step on which the evidence most punishes generalisation. Herodotus's first-grade method (Section V) enters through the nostrils with an iron hook: part extracted mechanically, the rest treated with drugs. Anatomically, the route runs up the nasal cavity and through the thin bone of the ethmoid region — the cribriform area at the roof of the nose — into the cranial cavity; CT studies of mummies routinely show the breach exactly there. Once inside, the hook could be worked to break up the tissue, which was then extracted piecemeal, drained, or rinsed out. Resin or linen was sometimes introduced into the emptied skull, and in a number of mummies a resinous mass fills part of the cranial cavity, poured or settled in after treatment.

But the mummies refuse to make excerebration a rule. Imaging surveys find the full range: skulls emptied through the nose; skulls emptied through a breach elsewhere; skulls in which the brain was never removed at all and survives inside, shrunken and desiccated, where the natron reached it through the body's general drying. The 2011 replication team (Section XVIII) left the brain in place deliberately, noting that X-ray evidence shows about half of 18th-Dynasty royal mummies retaining theirs. The honest statement is therefore period- and grade-sensitive: transnasal excerebration is real, well attested, and characteristic of fine work in several periods — and it was never universal, even for kings. Popular accounts that present the hook as the invariable first act of every mummification overstate the record by exactly the margin the scanners measure.

Why remove it at all, if the heart was the thinking organ? For a bench reason, not a theological one: the brain is a soft, fatty, water-rich mass sealed in a bone box, and left untreated inside a drying body it liquefies and putrefies on its own schedule, staining and compromising the head — the most recognisable, portrait-bearing part of the sꜥḥ [Gardiner: S29-D36-V28]. Where it was left, the embalmers relied on the body's general desiccation reaching it; where the grade paid for certainty, they took it out. The replication evidence supports the practical reading: Brier and Wade's trials on donated heads found that patient scooping with a hook was slow and incomplete, whereas whisking the tissue to a slurry with a rod and irrigating with water, then draining the liquid through the nose with the head positioned for it, cleared the cavity effectively — twenty minutes of whisking and about an hour and a half of draining, in one published retrospective account of their trials. The Egyptian toolkits that survive, and the clean ethmoid breaches in the scans, are consistent with a mature version of exactly that combined technique.

The eye — irt [Gardiner: D4] [𓁹] — belongs to this section's anatomy. The Louvre-Carlsberg manual lists the collapsing of the eyes among the operations completed by day four, before the natron begins: the globes, unsupported once their fluids are gone, sink, and the lids close over them. Later fine work sometimes restored the face's fullness with packing, with painted or inlaid eyes on the mask or coffin, or — in the Roman period's famous portrait mummies — with a painted panel likeness set over the face of the wrapped body. The wḏꜣt [Gardiner: D10] [𓂀], the sound, restored Eye that the archive treats under amulets and talismans, was among the forms placed with the dead: the organ that had collapsed in the workshop returned, in amuletic form, whole.

VIII. Evisceration and the Treatment of the Organs — and the Heart Left in Place

Through the left-flank incision — in fine modern replication, an opening of roughly three to three and a half inches sufficed, worked with patience — the embalmer's hand removed, in the order the anatomy presents them, the intestines, the stomach, the liver, and the lungs. These four are the wettest, most bacteria-laden tissues in the body, the engines of putrefaction; their removal is the single most consequential act of the whole process after desiccation itself. Each organ was then treated as a small mummy in its own right: washed — Diodorus says in palm wine and spices — dried in natron, anointed, and wrapped in linen into a compact package. The Ritual of Embalming catches the packages mid-process (Section XIII): placed in a faience vessel, covered with the ointment called fdt-nṯrw [Gardiner: I9-D46-X1-R8-Z3] [𓆑𓂧𓏏𓊹𓏪], "perspiration of the gods," and stored temporarily in a box, awaiting their final disposition.

That disposition changed over time, and the change is one of the clearest period markers in the whole craft. In the classical canopic arrangement (Section IX), the four packages went into four jars under the protection of the Four Sons of Horus, and the jars went into the tomb in a chest beside the coffin. In later practice — already characteristic of many Third Intermediate Period burials and standard in the world the Ritual describes — the treated packages were returned to the body cavity before the final wrapping: chapter x+VI of the Ritual is precisely this act, the anointed organs placed back into the thoracic and abdominal cavities and the body then filled with substances called pḫrt [Gardiner: Q3-Aa1-D21-X1] [𓊪𓐍𓂋𓏏], "remedy" or "medicament." Dummy canopic jars — solid, symbolic, containing nothing — often continued to be buried alongside, the form outliving the function. Neither arrangement is a decline from the other; they are two theologies of completeness, one keeping the parts guarded beside the whole, the other restoring the whole before sealing it.

The heart — ib [Gardiner: F34] [𓄣] — was left in place. The Metropolitan Museum's note on the heart scarab of the queen Manhata states the doctrine in one sentence: the Egyptians regarded the heart as the seat of the spirit, and it was not removed during mummification. It had a future engagement: the weighing against mꜣꜥt [Gardiner: U1-D36-X1] [𓌳𓂝𓏏] in the hall of judgment, treated in the Weighing of the Heart. Over it, in the wrappings, the embalmers placed the heart scarab, inscribed with Book of the Dead Spell 30B, which asks the heart not to stand against its owner at the judgment; the Met's Third Intermediate Period example, found within the wrappings of the mummy of Henettawy over her chest, shows the placement exactly. Diodorus's additional detail — that the kidneys too were spared — is reported here as his account; the heart's exemption is the one the bodies themselves confirm as doctrine. Where a heart is occasionally absent or displaced in a scanned mummy, the record shows accident or period variation, not a counter-doctrine.

The emptied body was then packed for drying: the cavity filled with bags of natron (the replication count is given in Section XVIII), with temporary aromatic packing, or with linen, according to period and grade, and the incision left open, covered, or sewn at different stages — the Ritual is explicit that during the back treatment of chapter x+V the incision was still being kept open, because the organs had not yet been returned. Sequence, in this craft, is not pedantry. A sewn incision before the return of the organs would be a workshop error, and the manual legislates against it by the order of its chapters.

IX. Canopic Jars and the Four Sons of Horus

The four treated organ packages belonged, in the classical arrangement, to four guardians: the Four Sons of Horus — Ḥr [Gardiner: G5] [𓅃] — each son protecting one organ, each himself protected by a goddess, each assigned a cardinal direction. The jars that held the packages — "canopic" is a modern label, borrowed from the Canopus jar-form of Osiris and kept by convention — began as plain vessels with simple or human-headed stoppers and acquired, by the later New Kingdom (from about the 19th Dynasty), the familiar four distinct heads. The British Museum's early guide to its mummy rooms already describes the canonical set exactly as it is still taught: Amset the man-headed, Hapi the ape-headed, Tuamautef the jackal-headed, and Qebehsenuef the hawk-headed, placed under or near the bier. The canopic chest stood in the tomb near the sarcophagus, and the jars' stoppers were sealed with resin. Excavated sets range from plain limestone and pottery to the alabaster and gold of royal burials. Their inscriptions name the guardian and speak in his voice, each son announcing his protection of the organ and of Osiris — the title every justified dead person bore. The jars are therefore texts as well as vessels, and the funerary corpus includes their formulae alongside the papyri.

X. Natron — the Divine Salt: Composition, Sources, and How Desiccation Works

Natron is the Egyptian process in a substance. Its Egyptian name states its status: nṯry [Gardiner: R8-Z4] [𓊹𓏭] — "the divine (salt)," built on nṯr [Gardiner: R8] [𓊹], "god." A second common term, ḥsmn [Gardiner: V28-S29-G17-N35] [𓎛𓋴𓅓𓈖], names the material in its cleansing use more broadly. Chemically — and here this article follows the analytical literature, including the Raman spectroscopy study of Wadi Natrun natron published in Analytical and Bioanalytical Chemistry (Edwards et al., 2007) and A. Lucas's classic analyses — historical natron is a naturally occurring evaporite mixture, not a single compound: principally sodium carbonate decahydrate with a substantial fraction of sodium bicarbonate (about 17% in the textbook definition), and variable impurities of sodium chloride (Lucas's Wadi Natrun samples range roughly 2–27%) and sodium sulphate (from a trace to about 39% across his samples). There is no one "natron formula" for ancient material, and any recipe that prints one is describing a modern substitute — such as the documented experimental blend of six parts hydrated sodium carbonate to three of sodium chloride, one of sodium sulphate, and one of sodium bicarbonate — not the harvested mineral.

Natron is not manufactured; it is harvested. It forms as a transpiro-evaporite crust on the beds of alkaline lakes as they dry seasonally, and it was scraped and dug from the surface. The principal ancient source was the Wadi Natrun, the depression about 100 kilometres north-west of Cairo lying some 23 metres below sea level, whose very name preserves the material; a second ancient source lay at El-Kab in the south, and the Pyramid Texts already distinguish northern from southern natron. Fifty-two large jars of natron were stored in the tomb of Yuya and Thuya — the workshop's bulk chemical, buried as equipment. It was also a ritual substance in its own right: natron pellets purified mouths and altars, priests chewed them for purity, and the Opening of the Mouth is effected in part with natron — the desiccant of the bench and the purifier of the rite are the same salt, which is precisely the kind of double identity this craft runs on.

The mechanism is threefold, and each fold matters. First, hygroscopic desiccation: the carbonate mixture draws water out of tissue osmotically and holds it, dropping the tissue's water activity below the level at which bacteria and the body's own enzymes can operate. Putrefaction is, chemically, a water sport; natron ends it by drought. Second, alkalinity: a 1% sodium carbonate solution stands at about pH 11.2, an environment hostile to microbial life in its own right. Third, saponification: the alkali attacks body fats, converting a portion of them into soap-like compounds, degreasing the tissue and removing the substrate most prone to rancid, anaerobic decay. The result of the three together, sustained for the drying term, is the leathery, stable, dramatically lightened body the replication weighed in Section XVIII. Desiccation is the essential process — that much the whole literature, ancient and modern, agrees on. Whether desiccation alone explains three thousand years of survival is a different question, and the answer is no: the oils, resins, and waxes of Sections XIV–XV polymerise into waterproof, microbe-inhibiting skins, and the Saqqara programme (Section XVI) shows them formulated deliberately, body part by body part. Natron makes the mummy; the coatings keep it.

// NATRON, NOT SALT //

An older generation of scholarship held that the embalmers used common salt. The question was settled by the refuse: embalmers' waste contains natron, not salt, and Brier and Wade's published experiment — pointedly titled, in its journal form, "The Use of Natron in Human Mummification" — was designed in part to demonstrate the distinction in practice. Pure salt as the desiccant belongs to early Christian-era embalming, not to the pharaonic craft. When Herodotus's Greek litron is rendered "saltpetre" in older translations, the word misleads; the substance in the bed was natron.

XI. Dry Packing or Natron Bath? — an Honest Account of a Live Debate

One question in the craft remains genuinely open, and this article presents it open: was the natron applied dry — heaped over and packed into the body, as Herodotus's account and the Louvre-Carlsberg manual's explicit "dry natron, inside and outside" describe — or, at least for elite work in some periods, as a solution bath in which the body lay immersed? The dry method has the texts. The Louvre-Carlsberg manual (c. 1450 BCE) is unambiguous for its own tradition: dry natron, begun on day four, after purification, excerebration, evisceration, and the collapsing of the eyes. Lucas's and Sandison's tissue experiments in the twentieth century likewise supported dry application as the method that matched the histology of ancient tissue.

The bath has the crystals. Stephen Buckley's case, built over years of residue and tissue analysis of 18th-Dynasty royal mummies, rests on internal salt structures — delicate "snowflake" natron formations within the tissues — which, he argues, could only have grown from solution saturating the body, and on the practical observation that a bath delivers the desiccant evenly into deep tissue in a way a heap cannot. His 2011 mummification of Alan Billis (Section XVIII) was designed as the bath's proof of concept: the body first coated in sesame oil, beeswax, and pine resin to protect the skin from the caustic solution — natron at bath strength is harsh enough, in Buckley's phrase, to take a layer of skin — then immersed for 35 days. The outcome, on imaging, matched 18th-Dynasty mummies closely.

The correct scholarly posture — and the archive's — is that each method now possesses exactly one successful full-scale human proof of concept (dry: Brier and Wade, 1994; bath: Buckley and Fletcher, 2011), a textual witness for dry application in the oldest manual, and a microstructural argument for solution in one dynasty's royal work. The likeliest historical reality is plural: a craft practised for three millennia in many workshops had more than one house method, and "dry or wet?" may be a question with a different true answer at Thebes in 1450 BCE and at Saqqara in 600 BCE. What is not defensible is printing either method as the method. This article's step sequences therefore say "the natron term" where the sources divide, and name the method where a specific source or experiment supplies one.

XII. The Seventy Days — Reconciling the Timetables

"Seventy days" is the most quoted number in the subject and the most carelessly repeated. Herodotus (2.86) gives the full seventy days to the natron covering itself — never longer, he insists — and his own sequence puts washing and wrapping after the seventy days, outside that term. The Egyptian manuals, by contrast, let the total be decomposed. The Papyrus Louvre-Carlsberg manual (c. 1450 BCE), as published from Sofie Schiødt's University of Copenhagen research, divides the seventy into a 35-day drying period and a 35-day wrapping period, worked in four-day intervals — seventeen working intervals — with the mummy finished on day 68 and the last days given to the rites that set the deceased living in the afterlife. The natron began on day four. The British Museum's synthesis, working from the collections, gives about forty days in natron within the seventy — the same structure with rounder numbers. And the Ritual of Embalming, on Töpfer's analysis, covers almost forty days of anointing and wrapping: the second half of the total, counted from the other end. Three independent reckonings, one shape: roughly half the seventy for drying, roughly half for everything after.

The four-day rhythm is the timetable's most revealing detail. Embalming was not continuous labour: the body rested, covered, between working intervals, and the embalmers returned to it on a fixed cadence — the same cadence at which the face treatment of Section XV was renewed. The seventy days are therefore best pictured not as seventy days of work but as a ten-week liturgical and chemical calendar, most of whose days are waiting — which is, of course, what desiccation is. The ritual calendar and the drying curve are the same curve, read in two vocabularies, and the Egyptians, characteristically, kept both sets of books.

XIII. The Ritual of Embalming — the Twelve Chapters of Anointing and Wrapping

The Ritual of Embalming is the only Egyptian text that describes mummification from inside the workshop, and its limits must be stated as plainly as its riches. It survives in hieratic on three Roman-period papyri — Papyrus Boulaq 3, Papyrus Louvre 5158, and Papyrus Durham 1983.11 joined with Papyrus St. Petersburg 18128 — copies of the end of the 1st to the beginning of the 2nd century CE, all descending from one earlier composition; Susanne Töpfer's edition (2015) is the modern scholarly basis, and her study of its instructions against the archaeological evidence is the source this section follows. The beginning of the text is lost — its title with it; "Embalming Ritual" is Maspero's modern designation. The preserved text contains no evisceration and no natron dehydration: its twelve chapters cover the second half of the seventy days, almost forty days of anointing and wrapping, and it was not a bench crib — it was recited during the Hourly Vigil on the final night before burial, the twelve nocturnal hours answering its twelve chapters, and then deposited in the tomb so that the work, once spoken, would stand written forever. Inscribed jars from Saqqara, Thebes, and above all Abusir, labelled with materials and day numbers, independently confirm that a schedule of this kind was actually worked to.

Each chapter pairs a manual — dry, exact, prescription-like: bandage counts, measures of unguents and plants, named body parts — with glorification spells that lift the same act into the sacred register. The sequence runs head to toe, and it is given here entire, because the sequence is the article's subject.

CH. x+I — PURIFICATION (MANUAL LOST)

The manual of the first chapter is lost; fragments of its liturgical section indicate that the purification of the deceased was its subject — the threshold act of the second half, answering the washing of the first.

CH. x+II — THE HEAD, FIRST ANOINTING

Anointing the head with ꜥnṯyw [Gardiner: D36-N35-V13-Z4-G43] [𓂝𓈖𓍿𓏭𓅱] — the substance long translated "myrrh" and shown at Saqqara to be a manufactured conifer-oil-and-fat product (Sections XIV and XVI). The head is treated first, and it will be treated again, at greater length than any other part.

CH. x+III — THE WHOLE BODY

The body anointed from the shoulders down to the soles of the feet with a mixture of ten different oils of vegetable and animal composition — the general anointing on which all later, local treatments build.

CH. x+IV — THE VISCERA

The organs placed in a faience vessel and covered with the ointment called fdt-nṯrw [Gardiner: I9-D46-X1-R8-Z3] [𓆑𓂧𓏏𓊹𓏪], "perspiration of the gods" — perhaps, Töpfer suggests, an allegory for a real body fluid — and stored temporarily in a box (compare Section VIII).

CH. x+V — THE BACK

The body turned onto its stomach; the back rubbed with mrḥt [Gardiner: G17-D21-V28-X1] [𓅓𓂋𓎛𓏏] ointment; sheets of mnḫt [Gardiner: G17-N35-Aa1-X1] [𓅓𓈖𓐍𓏏] and ḥbs [Gardiner: V28-D58-S29] [𓎛𓃀𓋴] linen applied to the upper body. The flank incision is deliberately kept open: the organs are not yet back inside.

CH. x+VI — THE RETURN OF THE ORGANS

The already anointed — and perhaps already wrapped — organs are placed inside the thoracic and abdominal cavities, and the body is filled with substances called pḫrt [Gardiner: Q3-Aa1-D21-X1] [𓊪𓐍𓂋𓏏], "remedy." Only now is the body whole.

CH. x+VII — FINGERS AND TOES: GOLDEN NAILS

Artificial nails of gold and electrum fitted to the fingers and toes, which are then wrapped with fine strips of red linen — the detail by which whole groups of Ptolemaic and Roman mummies, with their separately wrapped digits, are recognised as worked to this text.

CH. x+VIII — THE HEAD, AT LENGTH

By far the most extensive chapter. The ḥry-sštꜣ [Gardiner: V28-D21-Z4-S29-N37-X1-G1] [𓎛𓂋𓏭𓋴𓈙𓏏𓄿] is named for the first time, supervising the ḫtmw-nṯr [Gardiner: Aa1-X1-G17-G43-R8] [𓐍𓏏𓅓𓅱𓊹], who anoints the head and face inside and out. Four mnḫt [Gardiner: G17-N35-Aa1-X1] cloths are placed on forehead, face, ears, and back of head and fixed with bandages; rolls of linen — mdꜣt [Gardiner: G17-D46-G1-X1] [𓅓𓂧𓄿𓏏] — are laid on and around ears, forehead, cheeks, chin, back of head, eyes, and in the mouth and nose; and the head is bound with a bandage two fingers wide — the only textile dimension the whole Ritual gives.

CH. x+IX — THE HEAD ANOINTED AGAIN

A further anointing of the head, with ꜥnṯyw [Gardiner: D36-N35-V13-Z4-G43] and fat — perhaps the same bovine fat used in the preceding chapter. The head receives three chapters of the twelve: the theology of the face (Section XV) is written in the manual's own proportions.

CH. x+X — THE LEFT HAND

The fist anointed; a mixture of ꜥnḫ-imy seeds, natron, and bitumen placed in the palm; the fingers extended for a golden ring, then the hand re-formed into a fist; snb [Gardiner: S29-N35-D58] [𓋴𓈖𓃀]-plant fibres fashioned into thirty-six knots, a pod, and a piece of ꜥrw [Gardiner: D36-D21-G43] [𓂝𓂋𓅱] acacia fixed with its own gum; a sjꜣt [Gardiner: S29-M17-G1-X1] [𓋴𓇋𓄿𓏏] bandage bearing the image of Hapi on the hand's outer face, and one bearing Isis, folded six times, within — the whole then covered and bound with a sbn [Gardiner: S29-D58-N35] [𓋴𓃀𓈖] bandage.

CH. x+XI — THE RIGHT HAND

Hand and fingers anointed with mrḥt [Gardiner: G17-D21-V28-X1]; the seed-natron-bitumen mixture set in the palm; a sjꜣt [Gardiner: S29-M17-G1-X1] bandage with Isis and Nephthys painted in red ink outside; within the palm, a bandage with Ra and Min folded twelve times — Ra's image in orpiment, qnyt [Gardiner: N29-N35-Z4-X1] [𓈎𓈖𓏭𓏏], Min's in ochre, sty [Gardiner: S29-X1-Z4] [𓋴𓏏𓏭] — then cloth and bandages to close.

CH. x+XII — LEGS AND FEET; THE TEXT CLOSES

Soles and legs anointed with mrḥt [Gardiner: G17-D21-V28-X1], then with an unguent of aromatics, bitumen, and ground precious stones; toes and forefeet bound in fine pyr [Gardiner: Q3-Z4-D21] [𓊪𓏭𓂋] yarn; two jackals in antithetic pose painted on sjꜣt [Gardiner: S29-M17-G1-X1] bandages and laid on the feet; a mnḫt [Gardiner: G17-N35-Aa1-X1] sheet; the feet rebound in pyr [Gardiner: Q3-Z4-D21] yarn and royal linen, šs-nswt [Gardiner: N37-S29-A43-X1] [𓈙𓋴𓀲𓏏]; the seed-natron-bitumen mixture at the knee, fixed with ebony gum; twelve knots of snb [Gardiner: S29-N35-D58] fibres on the right leg and a ḥrt [Gardiner: V28-D21-X1] [𓎛𓂋𓏏] bandage in twelve knots on the left; a last anointing. The preserved Ritual ends there — anointed, limb-wrapped, gilded, organ-complete. Mask, cartonnage, and outer shroud it does not mention; archaeology supplies them (Section XV).

Töpfer's comparison gives the text its mummies: a recognisable group of Ptolemaic-to-Roman bodies — in London, Leiden, Liverpool, Berlin, Paris, and elsewhere — with limbs wrapped separately, fingers and toes bandaged individually and once gilded, faces modelled with resin-soaked linen rolls exactly where chapter x+VIII puts them, visceral packages back inside the cavities as chapter x+VI directs, and thick resin layers at back and legs where the manual anoints. Text and tissue match closely enough that the group can be described as worked to this Ritual — the strongest text-to-bench correspondence in the whole subject, and the model for how every claim in this article wants to be verified.

XIV. Anointing, Resins, and the Seven Sacred Oils — and What Antiu Really Was

Anointing is the second half's first language. The general word for the ointment in the Ritual is mrḥt [Gardiner: G17-D21-V28-X1] [𓅓𓂋𓎛𓏏] — oil or fat as a worked substance — and behind the individual treatments stands the canon of the seven sacred oils, fixed since the Old Kingdom on offering tablets with a named well for each: the British Museum's calcite tablet EA6122, from Abydos, incises the seven names over seven circular oil-wells for use in the Opening of the Mouth, and the Liverpool offering slab M13815 gives the canonical order, right to left. In that order they are: sṯy-ḥb [Gardiner: S29-V13-Z4-W3] [𓋴𓍿𓏭𓎱], "festival scent" (conventionally rendered setj-heb); ḥknw [Gardiner: V28-V31-N35-G43] [𓎛𓎡𓈖𓅱], heknu; sft [Gardiner: S29-I9-X1] [𓋴𓆑𓏏], sefet; nḫnm [Gardiner: N35-Aa1-N35-G17] [𓈖𓐍𓈖𓅓], nekhnem; twꜣ [Gardiner: X1-G43-G1] [𓏏𓅱𓄿], tua; ḥꜣtt-ꜥš [Gardiner: V28-G1-X1-X1-D36-N37] [𓎛𓄿𓏏𓏏𓂝𓈙], "best cedar/pine oil"; and ḥꜣtt-ṯḥnw [Gardiner: V28-G1-X1-X1-V13-V28-N35-G43] [𓎛𓄿𓏏𓏏𓍿𓎛𓈖𓅱], "best Libyan oil." The British Museum's standing caution is printed with the objects and adopted here: the precise identification of most of these oils is not certain — the names are certain, the substances mostly are not. Some periods list ten oils rather than seven. The Ritual's ten-oil mixture for the whole body (chapter x+III) and its named unguents belong to that wider, working repertoire, of which the seven are the liturgical canon.

Functionally, the anointing system does four kinds of work at once. The oils and fats restore pliability and appearance to desiccated skin that would otherwise crack in handling and wrapping. Dissolved resins and heated fat-resin mixtures seal: they polymerise into hydrophobic, microbe-resistant films — the varnish that, with the natron, explains the mummies' longevity (Section X). The aromatics perfume, which in this culture is not cosmetics but ontology: the gods are fragrant, the transfigured dead share their nature, and the workshop's success could be smelled. And each application is spoken: the glorification paired with the chapter makes the anointing an act performed on a person, not a treatment performed on an object. The archive's article on alchemy follows these same oils into the later laboratory tradition; here they stay at the bier, where they were compounded.

// THE ANTIU CORRECTION — READ CAREFULLY //

The substance ꜥnṯyw [Gardiner: D36-N35-V13-Z4-G43] [𓂝𓈖𓍿𓏭𓅱] — antiu — was translated "myrrh" for generations on philological conjecture, and older literature (including the first anointing of the Ritual's head chapter, in some translations) still prints the word. The Saqqara residue programme (Rageot et al., Nature 614, 2023) tested it directly: all five vessels labelled antiu in the workshop contained a mixture of cedar oil or tar and juniper/cypress oil or tar blended with animal fat. At Saqqara, the authors conclude, antiu was a product purposefully manufactured by the embalmers from at least two coniferous oils or tars worked into fat — and translation of the word as a raw material such as myrrh is excluded. Myrrh itself existed in the embalmers' world — Herodotus's grade-one filling names it, and the trade in aromatics from Punt and Arabia is real — but antiu is not its name. This article therefore prints antiu untranslated, or as "the manufactured conifer-oil preparation," wherever the Saqqara and Ritual contexts are in view, and flags the correction wherever older translations are quoted. It is the single clearest case in the subject of chemistry correcting philology, and the archive treats it as binding.

Its companion substance fell the same way. Sft [Gardiner: S29-I9-X1] [𓋴𓆑𓏏] — sefet, third of the seven sacred oils, usually rendered "a sacred oil" and left there — proved in the Saqqara analyses to be a fat-based scented unguent with plant additives: an animal-fat base carrying juniper/cypress oil or tar. The pattern generalises. The embalmers' named substances were, again and again, recipes — formulated mixtures, heated and blended in the workshop (Section XVI gives the analytical evidence for deliberate heated mixing) — and the philological habit of matching each name to one raw commodity was the error. The workshop was a manufactory, not a storeroom.

XV. Wrapping — Linen, Amulets, the Face, the Mask, and the Coffin

Wrapping is the craft's longest act and its namesake: the wt [Gardiner: G43-X1] [𓅱𓏏] is the wrapper. The raw material is linen — Egypt's defining textile, produced by the splicing technique the archive treats in its own right — in every grade from reused household cloth and old garments, which pad many a modest mummy, to the fine šs-nswt [Gardiner: N37-S29-A43-X1] [𓈙𓋴𓀲𓏏], "royal linen," that the Ritual reserves for the feet. The wrapping proceeds, in the Ritual's order, part by part rather than in one spiral: individual fingers and toes in fine strips; the head built up with cloths and resin-soaked rolls that model the features; the hands furnished and bound; sheets — mnḫt [Gardiner: G17-N35-Aa1-X1] [𓅓𓈖𓐍𓏏] and ḥbs [Gardiner: V28-D58-S29] [𓎛𓃀𓋴] — laid over regions; bandages — sbn [Gardiner: S29-D58-N35] [𓋴𓃀𓈖], sjꜣt [Gardiner: S29-M17-G1-X1] [𓋴𓇋𓄿𓏏], pyr [Gardiner: Q3-Z4-D21] [𓊪𓏭𓂋] — wound to fix them; and resinous coatings worked in between layers as adhesive, sealant, and preservative at once. Herodotus's "bandages smeared with gum" (Section V) is the same system seen from outside and in one breath.

Between the layers, at prescribed stations of the body, the embalmers laid the amulets. The full typology belongs to Amulets and Talismans; the wrapping stations that matter here are the heart scarab of green stone over the heart (Section VIII); the wḏꜣt [Gardiner: D10] [𓂀] eye, wholeness restored, laid with the dead; the ḏd [Gardiner: R11] [𓊽] pillar at the throat or spine, stability itself; scarabs, plaques, and the small figures of the Four Sons; and, in the Ritual's hands, bandages painted with gods — Hapi, Isis, Nephthys, Ra, Min — folded a stated number of times and bound into palm and fist, so that the finished hands of the sꜥḥ [Gardiner: S29-D36-V28] [𓋴𓂝𓎛] literally grasp a folded pantheon. Amulets recovered from unwrapped and scanned mummies stand in the museum record exactly where the texts put them — the Met's scarabs found strung between a mummy's knees and hand, and bound on a mummy's ring finger, are the type specimens of the station system.

The face received the period's signature treatment. The Louvre-Carlsberg manual — a text older than the Ritual by well over a millennium — preserves a procedure its modern editor, Sofie Schiødt of the University of Copenhagen, identifies as one of its most exciting new details, and her summary is quoted here from the University's published statement: plant-based aromatic substances and binders are cooked into a liquid, a piece of red linen is coated with it, and — in Schiødt's words — "the red linen is then applied to the dead person's face in order to encase it in a protective cocoon of fragrant and anti-bacterial matter." The application was renewed at every four-day interval of the wrapping period (Section XII): the face, the seat of recognition on which the whole theology of Section II depends, was cocooned again and again while the rest of the body waited its turns. The Ritual's three chapters for the head are the same priority, liturgically elaborated.

Over the finished wrapping came the face's public form: a mask — of cartonnage, plaster, or, for royalty, gold — restoring the features in idealised, imperishable material, and in the Roman period, for some, a painted portrait panel. Then the coffin: Herodotus's hollow wooden human-shaped case, the nested coffins and sarcophagi of greater burials, the canopic chest beside them (Section IX), and the equipment of the tomb. The wrapped body that left the wꜥbt [Gardiner: G43-D36-D58-X1] [𓅱𓂝𓃀𓏏] was no longer, in the language of everyone present, a corpse. It was a sꜥḥ [Gardiner: S29-D36-V28] going to its Opening of the Mouth and its tomb.

XVI. The Saqqara Workshop — a 26th-Dynasty Facility and Its Labelled Vessels

In 2016–2018 the Saqqara Saite Tombs Project, directed by Ramadan B. Hussein, excavated the first substantially intact embalming facility of the Late Period yet found: a 26th-Dynasty (Saite, c. 664–525 BCE) complex a few metres south of the Pyramid of Unas at Saqqara. Its plan is the textual geography of Sections I and IV made stone: a subterranean embalming room — the wꜥbt [Gardiner: G43-D36-D58-X1] [𓅱𓂝𓃀𓏏] — with an aboveground structure identified as probably the ibw [Gardiner: M17-D58-G43] [𓇋𓃀𓅱] purification installation, and burial shafts beyond, one descending some 30 metres to communal burial chambers. Workshop above and below, tombs attached: a vertically integrated funerary business, of exactly the date at which Herodotus, a century later, describes the craft's three retail grades.

The facility's archive is pottery. One hundred and twenty-one inscribed vessels — 59 marl-clay beakers and 62 red bowls — were recovered, many labelled in hieratic and demotic with the two kinds of information a workshop needs on a pot: what is in it, and what it is for. The use-labels read like fragments of the Ritual itself: "to put on his head," "bandage or embalm with it," "to wash," "to make his odour pleasant." Rageot and colleagues selected 31 of the vessels, with four further samples from the burial chambers, for gas chromatography–mass spectrometry, and published the programme in Nature 614 (2023) as "Biomolecular analyses enable new insights into ancient Egyptian embalming." Because each analysed vessel carried its Egyptian label, chemistry could for the first time be read directly against vocabulary — the experiment the whole subject had been waiting for since the labels were first copied.

The mixtures, not the ingredients, are the finding. Head mixtures (eight vessels) combine elemi, Pistacia, conifer oils and tars, fat, and beeswax. Bandage mixtures (eight vessels) are dominated by juniper/cypress, cedar, fat or oil, and elemi — with, contrary to expectations formed at other sites, no bitumen and no beeswax in the bandage vessels at all. In three vessels, triterpenic palmitates prove that resins were deliberately mixed into fat or oil and heated together: formulation, performed at temperature, by design. And the two famous names resolved as recipes (Section XIV): antiu, in all five labelled vessels, is cedar plus juniper/cypress oil or tar in animal fat — a manufactured product, myrrh excluded; sefet is a fat-based scented unguent with plant additives. The authors' larger conclusion is economic as much as chemical: a workshop drawing Levantine conifers, Dead Sea bitumen, and rainforest elemi was plugged into a supply network they describe as almost global, and its embalmers were selecting, with a formulator's intent, for properties modern analysis confirms — antibacterial and antifungal action, hydrophobicity, adhesion. The 26th-Dynasty bench was an applied-chemistry laboratory that also, and without contradiction, recited glorifications.

XVII. Animal Mummification at Industrial Scale

Everything in this article so far describes the treatment of one body at a time. Alongside it, from the Late Period into the Roman era, Egypt ran animal mummification at a scale that has no parallel in the ancient world. Salima Ikram's standard fourfold classification divides animal mummies by purpose: pets, buried to accompany their owners; victual mummies, joints and prepared foods for the dead person's table; sacred animals, individual creatures kept as a god's embodiment and buried with full honours at death; and votive mummies — the overwhelming majority — bred or captured, killed, mummified, and sold to pilgrims to be offered to a god and buried in that god's catacombs as a permanent, physical prayer.

The numbers, stated with the caution they require, are these. Ikram has estimated that the known animal necropolises — more than thirty sites — once held at least twenty million mummies; higher popular estimates run far beyond that and are not counts. At North Saqqara, the Catacombs of Anubis, sampled and projected by Nicholson, Ikram, and Mills (2015), yield an estimate of some 7.7 million animals, overwhelmingly domestic dogs, in galleries whose main corridor runs about 173 metres. At Saqqara's ibis catacombs of Thoth, an ancient text speaks of 60,000 living ibises maintained for the rite, and archaeologists estimate some four million ibis mummies were buried there. Cats for Bastet, falcons for Horus, baboons, crocodiles, shrews, fish, snakes: as Ikram puts the breadth of the practice, it is easier to list the creatures Egypt did not mummify than those it did.

The craft scaled accordingly. Votive production was a workshop industry with breeding programmes, rapid bench protocols, and quality grades of its own: fine specimens fully eviscerated, dried, oiled, and wrapped with modelled detail and applied decoration — and, as CT surveys by the Manchester animal-mummy programme and others have repeatedly shown, a substantial minority of bundles (around a third in the Manchester scans) containing partial remains, a few bones, feathers, or substitute material in a full-sized, beautifully wrapped package. The archive reports that finding without embroidery and without the modern sneer it sometimes attracts: the bundle's efficacy, in the votive logic, rode on its dedication and form, and the workshops supplying a mass pilgrimage market plainly graded their output to their prices — as, Section VI shows, the human workshops openly did. Experimental work by Ikram on animal material confirms the shared technology from the other side: the same core sequence as the human process, at about forty days, scaled to the species.

XVIII. Modern Replication — Brier and Wade (1994) and the Mummification of Alan Billis (2011)

Twice, the full process has been run on a human body under modern documentation. The two experiments were designed a generation apart around the debate of Section XI, and together they constitute the craft's modern evidence base. Both ran inside the only frameworks that make such work lawful and ethical: documented donor consent for this specific use, institutional anatomical or human-tissue approval, and a licensed setting. The procedures of this article are history; the replications are cited as experiments, and nothing in this section is an invitation to imitate them outside those frameworks.

Brier and Wade, 1994 — the Dry Method, First Modern Human Mummification

In May 1994 the Egyptologist Bob Brier (Long Island University) and the anatomist Ronn Wade (University of Maryland), working with a 76-year-old man who had died of a heart attack and donated his body through the Maryland State Anatomy Board, performed the first modern human mummification following Egyptian methods. Their tools were commissioned replicas in copper, bronze, and stone; the copper blades proved ineffective in practice and were abandoned, while obsidian cut as the texts imply it should — itself a small experimental confirmation of the Ethiopian-stone tradition (Section V). Brain-removal trials on donated heads produced the whisking-and-draining finding of Section VII. The evisceration was made through a left-side incision of about three to three and a half inches; the heart was left in place; the cavity was cleansed with palm wine and myrrh. The natron was Egyptian, collected by Brier himself, and its quantities are the experiment's most quoted hard numbers: 29 linen bags of natron packed into the cavity; the body laid on 211 lb (96 kg) of natron and covered with 583 lb (264 kg) more — roughly 360 kg in all — in a room held at 104 °F (40 °C) with dehumidifiers, for 35 days.

The outcome is the dry method's proof. The body's weight fell from 188 lb to 79 lb across the natron term (31 lb of the loss being the removed organs), and to 51 lb after three further months of drying. The skin came out leathery, the features drawn into the classic mummy's aspect — Brier's verdict, oft-quoted since, was that after five weeks the result looked "just like Ramses the Great": the famous appearance of the ancient royal mummies is, the experiment shows, something the process itself produces within weeks, not something three thousand years add. Partial unwrappings across the following decades found no deterioration. Their published conclusion stands as Section X's epigraph in all but type: simple dehydration by natron is the key to mummification.

Buckley and Fletcher, 2011 — "Mummy Stu": the Bath Method and Alan Billis

The second experiment honoured a different donor's wish. Alan Billis, a 61-year-old Torquay taxi driver — "Mummy Stu" to the production that filmed him, from the stew the process was expected to make and triumphantly did not — volunteered before his death from terminal lung cancer in January 2011, and his body was mummified at Sheffield's Medico-Legal Centre by the chemist Stephen Buckley (University of York) with the archaeologist Joann Fletcher, under Human Tissue Authority approval, after years of preparatory trials — some two hundred pigs' legs' worth, in the team's oft-told count. The documentary Mummifying Alan: Egypt's Last Secret (Channel 4, 2011) carried the work to a wide public and won the BAFTA for Specialist Factual.

The protocol deliberately diverged from Brier's at the two points Section XI identifies. The brain was left in place — the 18th-Dynasty work Buckley aimed to reproduce retains it in about half of royal cases — while the lungs and intestines were removed through the side incision and the cavity padded with linen. The body was then coated in a protective layer of sesame oil, beeswax, and pine resin — Buckley's reconstruction of the skin's defence in elite practice — and immersed for 35 days in a natron solution bath, followed by drying in a low-humidity, high-temperature chamber and wrapping in linen treated with oils, resins, and spices; the whole, death to completion, took about three months. On tissue imaging, the result matched 18th-Dynasty mummies closely, and Buckley was particularly pleased with the preservation of the retained brain. Billis's mummy was subsequently housed at the Gordon Museum of Pathology in London.

Read together, the experiments do not crown a winner; they map a possibility space. Dry packing works, in 35 days, with about 360 kg of natron and Egyptian tools. The bath works, in 35 days, behind an oil-and-wax skin defence. A third modern datum fixes the craft's floor in time rather than technique: Buckley and colleagues' 2014 analysis of Predynastic burial linens from Mostagedda (held at Bolton) found a complex embalming recipe — fats, oils, and conifer resin — already in use around 4300 BCE, a millennium and a half before the date once assumed for deliberate embalming treatment. The desert taught the craft; the craft, it turns out, was already old when the pyramids were young.

XX. Esoteric Analysis — Preservation as Transformation

This section is interpretation, presented as the archive presents all esoteric framing: as a reading of the material, not as a finding from it. The findings are Sections I–XIX.

Read with the eyes of the later tradition — the tradition this archive's alchemy article traces from the temple workshops into the Hermetic laboratory — embalming is the most fully documented transformation process of the ancient world, and its structure is the structure the alchemists would inherit. A composite, perishable body is first separated: its volatile and putrescible parts drawn out and treated apart, each under its own guardian. The remainder is subjected to the black work of the natron bed, in which the body's own water — its life-medium — is surrendered to a divine salt, and what emerges is dark, shrunken, and leathery: a prima materia that no longer decays because decay has finished with it. Then comes the washing and the whitening of clean linen, layer upon layer, the body rebuilt in textile to a fullness it no longer has in flesh; the reddening touches — red linen at the fingers, the red face cocoon renewed at the four-day stations; and the gilding, gold nails on the digits, the imperishable metal set precisely where the body's own keratin had been. Separation, nigredo, albedo, rubedo's colours, and the gold: the sequence is all here, in a workshop manual, centuries before it is anywhere else — stated not as philosophy but as procedure, which is how Egypt always preferred to state philosophy.

The identification of craftsman and god carries the same reading one level deeper. When the ḥry-sštꜣ [Gardiner: V28-D21-Z4-S29-N37-X1-G1] works masked as Anubis, the workshop asserts that the transformation of the dead is not performed for the gods but by one, through human hands — the operator inside the operation, as the later alchemical visions of Zosimos would dream it. And the product of the work is named, in plain Egyptian, with the word this article began from: sꜥḥ [Gardiner: S29-D36-V28] — the mummy and "the noble one" in a single term. The craft's own vocabulary refuses to distinguish the preserved body from the dignified person. Whatever else the esoteric reading adds, it should not be allowed to add more than that; the Egyptians had already said it, in one word, on the first day.
