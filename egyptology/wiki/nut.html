I. The Name — Nwt Means "Sky"

Nut's Egyptian name is nwt [Gardiner: N35-W24-X1-N1] [𓈖𓏌𓏏𓇯], and it means, simply and completely, "Sky." That is the whole of its meaning, and this article begins by insisting on the point, because a god's name in Egypt is a definition, and a false definition propagates through everything built on it. Nwt [Gardiner: N35-W24-X1-N1] is the ordinary Egyptian word for the sky as a goddess — the feminine counterpart, in usage, of the common noun pt [Gardiner: N1] [𓇯], "sky, heaven," with which her name could be used interchangeably in texts. She is not named for an activity, a craft, or a quality. She is named for the thing she is.

The writing of the name repays close reading, sign by sign. The first element is the water-pot sign nw [Gardiner: W24] [𓏌]. In this name the pot is a phonogram: it contributes the sounds n-w, and nothing else. It does not contribute the idea of a pot, of water, of carrying, or of holding. It is followed by the feminine ending t [Gardiner: X1] [𓏏], the small loaf-sign that marks the word as feminine, and by the flat sky-sign pt [Gardiner: N1] [𓇯] set at the end as a determinative — the silent classifying sign that tells the reader which semantic field the word belongs to. In fuller writings an initial n [Gardiner: N35] [𓈖] is written before the pot as a phonetic complement, and a seated-goddess determinative may close the word instead of, or beside, the sky-sign. The scholar Or Graur, in his study of Nut and the Milky Way discussed in Section VI, analyses the name in exactly these terms: the phonetic combination nw [Gardiner: W24] plus t [Gardiner: X1], together with the hieroglyph for sky, pt [Gardiner: N1], and a female determinative.

Because the pot sign is so visible in her name — and is even worn on her head as an identifying emblem in some images (Section IV) — it has attracted folk etymologies. This archive states the correction plainly, once, and then drops it: there is no weave, binding, knotting, or textile etymology in the name nwt [Gardiner: N35-W24-X1-N1]. No Egyptian root connects her name to weaving or tying; the claim does not appear in the standard lexica, in James P. Allen's grammars, or in the specialist literature on the goddess, and any interpretation of Nut as a "weaver of the sky" built on her name is built on nothing. The Egyptians themselves played with the sounds of her name in other, attested directions: Graur notes that the names of Nut and of the primeval waters, nwn [Gardiner: N35-G43-N35] [𓈖𓅱𓈖] — Nun, the abyss that surrounds the world — share the watery n-writing and the sky-sign in their spellings, a graphic kinship between the sky above and the waters beyond it that Egyptian scribes could see on the page. That is the kind of wordplay the sources support. The loom is not.

Her name also explains her grammar. Because nwt [Gardiner: N35-W24-X1-N1] is a feminine noun, the sky in Egyptian is grammatically and theologically she — the reverse of the pattern more familiar from Greek and later European tradition, where the sky is male and the earth female. In Egypt the earth, gb [Gardiner: G38-D58-A40], Geb, is the god, and the sky is the goddess who arches over him. The archive's survey of the cosmological deities sets out this inversion and its consequences; Sections II and III below follow them into the creation account itself.

The full sign list is given in the hieroglyph reference.

Pronunciation can only be approximated for a language whose vowels were not written. Egyptologists vocalise her name conventionally as "Nut" — the consonants n-w-t with a conventional vowel supplied — and older literature also prints Nuit, Newet, and Neuth, all attempts at the same three consonants. Greek writers, meeting her through the Osiris cycle, identified her with Rhea, the mother of the Olympian gods — an identification that matters for Section IX, where the fullest surviving narrative of her children's births turns out to be a Greek text about "Rhea," not an Egyptian text about Nut at all.

II. Nut in the Ennead — Daughter of Shu and Tefnut

Nut belongs to the third generation of the world. In the Heliopolitan creation account — the theology of Heliopolis, organised as the Ennead, psḏt [Gardiner: N9-X1] [𓇷𓏏], "the Nine" — the self-created god Atum, tm [Gardiner: U15] [𓍃], emerged from the inert primeval waters, nwn [Gardiner: N35-G43-N35] [𓈖𓅱𓈖], and produced from himself the first differentiated pair: Shu, the air, and Tefnut, moisture. From that pair came the second: Geb, the earth, and Nut, the sky. From Geb and Nut came the gods of the moral and historical world — Osiris, Isis, Seth, and Nephthys, with Horus the Elder counted among them in the tradition Plutarch preserves (Sections VIII–IX). Nine gods in all, and Nut stands at the hinge of the system: everything above her in the genealogy is a principle — self-creation, air, moisture, earth — and everything below her is a person with a story.

Her father is šw [Gardiner: H6-G43-A40], Shu, whose sign is the feather šwt [Gardiner: H6] [𓆄]: the god of the air and of the empty, sunlit space between earth and sky. Her mother is tfnt [Gardiner: X1-I9-N35-X1-B1], Tefnut, moisture and cohesion, Shu's sister and consort. The archive treats the pair at length in its survey of the god Shu and the goddess Tefnut, and in the Ennead and Ogdoad; what matters for Nut is the inheritance. From Shu she receives her element: she is what the air holds up. From the pair together she receives her position: she is a product of differentiation, a boundary that exists only because the undifferentiated has been divided. Before Shu and Tefnut there is no "above." Nut is the first above there is.

Her partner is her brother. Gb [Gardiner: G38-D58-A40], Geb, is the earth itself — the ground that bears the temples, the soil of the Black Land, the body on which all earthly life takes place. Egyptian images show him reclining beneath her, sometimes with vegetation growing from his body, his laughter said in later tradition to shake the earth. Nut and Geb are siblings, spouses, and the two largest facts of the visible world, and the creation account does not treat their union as a scandal to be explained: sibling marriage among the first gods is simply how a closed divine family, with no outside to marry into, continues itself. The human royal family of Egypt, which also practised sibling marriage in some periods, understood its own custom against this template.

GRANDFATHER — ATUM

Tm [Gardiner: U15] [𓍃], the self-created, who emerged from nwn [Gardiner: N35-G43-N35] and began the genealogy. Nut is his great-granddaughter in the Heliopolitan count, and his descendant through two acts of differentiation.

FATHER — SHU

Šw [Gardiner: H6-G43-A40], air and light-filled space. He will stand between his children forever, arms raised, holding his daughter up (Section III). Nut's height is his posture.

MOTHER — TEFNUT

Tfnt [Gardiner: X1-I9-N35-X1-B1], moisture and cohesion. With Shu she forms the first pair, the first duality inside the unity of Atum.

BROTHER AND CONSORT — GEB

Gb [Gardiner: G38-D58-A40], the earth. See the archive's article on Geb. Separated from Nut by their father's arms, he remains the fixed term of her arch: she is measured by the distance from him.

One structural feature of the Ennead is easy to miss and essential to Nut. The Nine are not a pantheon in the sense of a court of colleagues; they are a sequence, a genealogy that is also a physics. Each generation is the previous generation divided. Atum divides into air and moisture; air and moisture produce earth and sky; earth and sky produce the gods who contend, murder, mourn, and rule. Nut is therefore not merely "a sky goddess" among others. She is the moment in the sequence at which the world acquires a ceiling — an inside. Everything Egyptians meant by the ordered cosmos, the world held against the waters of nwn [Gardiner: N35-G43-N35], happens underneath her.

III. The Separation — Shu Between Earth and Sky

The most reproduced image in Egyptian cosmology shows a separation, not a union. Geb lies along the ground, Nut arches above him fingertip to fingertip and toe to toe, and between them stands their father Shu, arms raised to her belly, holding her up and holding them apart. The Global Egyptian Museum's entry on the goddess summarises the scene in one sentence: in the Ennead of Heliopolis Nut is the wife of the earth god Geb, her brother, and she is separated from him by their father Shu, the god of the air. The vignette is not an illustration of a story so much as a diagram of a permanent condition. The separation is not something that happened once; it is something that is happening, continuously, and would stop — with catastrophic results — if Shu ever lowered his arms.

Read physically, the diagram is exact. What lies between the ground and the sky, for any observer standing in Egypt, is air: transparent, weightless-seeming, and the medium of light and breath. Egyptian cosmology personified that interval as a god with a job. Shu is the space in which birds fly, clouds form, and the sun travels; Nut is its upper boundary; Geb is its floor. The archive's cosmological survey states the resulting structure as a set of nested containers — the waters of nwn [Gardiner: N35-G43-N35] outside, Nut as the limit of the manifest world, and everything that lives living under her. Beyond her back there is no weather, no day, and no time as Egypt counted it. She is the inside surface of the world.

The supports multiply in the fuller versions. In the image that accompanies the Book of the Heavenly Cow (Section X), Nut in cow form is held up not by Shu alone: gods of the ḥḥ [Gardiner: C11] [𓁨] type — Heh, "millions," eternity personified as a kneeling figure with palm-ribs on his head — stand at her legs, two to a leg in Budge's description of the Seti I painting, steadying the four legs that are the four cardinal points. In the arched-woman versions, her own hands and feet do the work: four points of contact with the earth, at the four quarters of the horizon, ꜣḫt [Gardiner: N27] [𓈌]. Either way the theology is the same. The sky does not float. It is supported, by named powers, at named places — and a thing that is supported can, in principle, be let down, which is why Egyptian eschatology knows the terror of the sky falling and why the daily maintenance of the cosmos, ma'at, mꜣꜥt [Gardiner: C10] [𓁦], against disorder, is a real task and not a metaphor.

The separation also creates the erotic tension at the centre of the system, and the sources do not hide it. Nut and Geb reach for each other across the body of their own father, forever. The archive's Ennead article reads this, in its interpretive register, as the engine of manifestation: differentiation held open by force, so that a world can exist in the gap. Stated without interpretation, the factual core is enough. Egyptian creation is not a making of things out of nothing; it is a prising apart of things that were one — and Nut is the half that went up.

IV. Iconography — the Arched, Star-Covered Body

Nut's default form is a woman — young, usually nude, her body elongated far beyond natural proportion and bent into an arch. Her head is in the west and her groin in the east: the orientation is fixed by the primary texts and is the key to her astronomy, because the sun enters her at the west, at her mouth, and is born from her at the east (Section V). Or Graur's study, working from the Fundamentals and the funerary texts, puts the point as a rule of the evidence: her head and her groin are firmly associated by primary texts with the western and eastern horizons respectively, and any reading of her image that turns her around has the myth backwards. Her fingertips and toes touch the horizon at the four supports. Along her body the stars are painted — in the great ceiling versions (Section XIII) as ordered rows of decans and constellations, in smaller versions as a field of five-pointed stars, sbꜣ [Gardiner: S29-D58-G1] [𓋴𓃀𓄿], "star," scattered across a blue or black ground that is her skin become night.

Not every image arches her. Standing or kneeling, in human form and at human scale, she is identified by the sign of her name worn on her head: the water-pot nw [Gardiner: W24] [𓏌], sometimes set within or beneath a sun disk. The Global Egyptian Museum notes both conventions — usually depicted as a woman, sometimes with the hieroglyphs for her name on her head, often bending over the earth with stars on her body — and adds the winged form: occasionally she has wings, as a sign of the coolness and protection she gives. The winged, kneeling Nut is the form that spreads across coffin lids over the breast of the deceased (Section XII), where the wings do the same work as the arch at a smaller scale: to cover, to shade, and to hold off harm. For the wider grammar of such images, see colour symbolism — her blue is the blue of the daytime sky and of the celestial water, her star-field the black-blue of night, and the same body must be both.

THE ARCHED WOMAN

Head west, groin east, limbs descending to the four quarters of ꜣḫt [Gardiner: N27], body covered with sbꜣw [Gardiner: S29-D58-G1-Z2] [𓋴𓃀𓄿𓏥], "stars." The form of ceilings, coffin vignettes, and the Fundamentals picture (Sections VII, XIII).

THE STANDING GODDESS

At human scale, crowned with the nw [Gardiner: W24] pot of her name. The form in which she acts in scenes — offering, mourning, receiving — rather than the form in which she is the sky.

THE WINGED PROTECTRESS

Kneeling, wings outspread over the deceased. The British Museum's description of the anthropoid coffin of Nestawedjat is representative: the goddess Nut spreads her wings over the breast, while the compartments below carry the protecting gods (Section XII).

THE CELESTIAL COW

A cow whose belly carries stars and beneath which the boats of Ra sail; her four legs are the four cardinal points and Shu stands under her belly. Section X is devoted to this form and its book.

THE SYCAMORE

A woman emerging from, or indicated by the arms within, a sycamore tree, nḥt [Gardiner: N35-V28-X1] [𓈖𓎛𓏏], offering water and food to the dead. Section XIV.

THE SOW

A sow suckling her piglets — the stars at her body. A rarer, textually grounded form (Section XV), and the source of a rebuke in the Fundamentals tradition: even her children, the stars, are eaten and born again.

Identification, as throughout this archive, rests on context and inscription, not on a single attribute. A starry arched woman over Geb and Shu is Nut by composition. A cow in the sky needs her caption or her company — the boats of Ra, the Heh supports, Shu beneath — because Hathor too is a cow and the two goddesses' bovine forms overlap, a genuine and acknowledged convergence treated in the archive's article on Hathor. Where the name-sign nw [Gardiner: W24] appears on the head, or the name nwt [Gardiner: N35-W24-X1-N1] is written beside the figure, the question is settled the Egyptian way: by the word.

V. Swallowing the Sun — Evening, the Duat, and Dawn

Every evening, Nut eats the sun. Every morning, she gives birth to it. The two verbs are the Egyptian ones, and the archive keeps them literal because the texts do: to swallow is ꜥm [Gardiner: D36-G17] [𓂝𓅓], and to give birth is ms [Gardiner: F31] [𓄟]. As the sun sets in the west it reaches her mouth; she swallows it; through the night it travels inside her body, through the dwꜣt [Gardiner: N15] [𓇽], the Duat, the hidden region where the dead and the unborn wait; and at dawn she bears it again in the east, renewed. The Global Egyptian Museum states the cycle in the same terms: the sun travelled along her body every day towards the west, was swallowed by Nut, travelled through her body during the night, and she gave birth to it again in the east the next morning. Graur's study adds the mechanism as the Fundamentals tradition presents it: the swallowed sun passes into the Duat inside her, encounters Osiris there, is made effective again, and is reborn at sunrise.

Hold the two journeys apart, because Egyptian sources run both at once and confusion between them is the commonest error in popular accounts. By day, the sun god Ra, rꜥ [Gardiner: N5] [𓇳], travels openly across the sky in his barque — the voyage treated in the archive's article on the solar barque — visible, in the air, under Nut's arch and along her body. By night, in the swallowing account, the same sun is inside her, hidden, gestating. The day journey is a sailing; the night journey is a pregnancy. Egyptian theology was untroubled by offering both, on the same ceiling, in the same tomb (Section XIII): the Books of Day and Night, which map the two voyages, were painted side by side precisely because each says something the other cannot — one about kingship and procession, the other about death and regeneration.

What is true of the sun is true, in the same texts, of the stars. The decanal stars (Section VI) also disappear into her and also emerge from her, each in its season — the Fundamentals describes their cycle of vanishing and return in the language of death and rebirth inside her body, and applies the same pattern to the moon, iꜥḥ [Gardiner: N11] [𓇹], and the planets. Nut is therefore not a metaphor for the sky as scenery. She is the sky as a process: the one body in which every light in heaven is periodically extinguished and periodically restored. Grḥ [Gardiner: W11-D21-V28] [𓎼𓂋𓎛], "night," and hrw [Gardiner: N7] [𓇵], "day," are not two places in her cosmology. They are two states of her body.

For the dead, the conclusion was drawn explicitly and early, and it is the foundation of everything in Sections XI and XII. If the sun can enter Nut and come out renewed, so can a person. The Egyptians, in the Global Egyptian Museum's summary, hoped to be born again every day after death just like the sun, and to live among the stars; thus Nut became a funerary goddess, stretching protectively over the deceased, through whom the deceased could rise again. The sarcophagus and the tomb were identified with Nut herself — the dead person laid, quite literally, inside the goddess who knows how to bring what she swallows back into the light. Her swallowing is not a threat. It is the promise on which the whole funerary system is built: what Nut takes in, Nut gives back.

// THE DAILY CYCLE IN ONE GLANCE //

Evening: the sun reaches the western ꜣḫt [Gardiner: N27] and her mouth; she performs ꜥm [Gardiner: D36-G17], the swallowing. Night: the sun is inside her, in the dwꜣt [Gardiner: N15], made effective again in the presence of wsir [Gardiner: Q1-D4-A40] [𓊨𓁹𓀭], Osiris. Dawn: she performs ms [Gardiner: F31], the birth, at the eastern ꜣḫt [Gardiner: N27]; the sun performs prt [Gardiner: Q3-D21-X1] [𓊪𓂋𓏏], the going-forth, into the day. The stars run the same cycle on longer clocks (Section VI).

VI. The Stars on Her Body — Decans, Sopdet, and the Milky Way Question

The stars painted on Nut's body are not decoration. In the developed astronomical tradition they are a working instrument: the decans, bꜣktw [Gardiner: G29-V31-X1-B1-G43] [𓅡𓎡𓏏𓁐𓅱] — "those connected with work," the thirty-six star-groups whose successive risings divided the night into hours and the year into thirty-six ten-day periods. Thirty-six decans at ten days give the 360 days of the schematic year, to which the five additional days of Section IX were added. Their risings just before dawn marked the decades; their march across the night sky marked the hours; and because each decan in turn vanished into the sun's glare for a season and then returned, their cycle was written on Nut's body as a cycle of disappearances into, and births out of, the goddess — the solar pattern of Section V repeated thirty-six times at thirty-six speeds. The archive treats the system as a whole under mathematics and astronomy; here what matters is the address. Egyptian astronomy's home is a goddess's skin.

First among the decans stands spdt [Gardiner: M44-X1] [𓇮𓏏], Sopdet — the star Sirius — written with the "sharp, pointed" sign spd [Gardiner: M44], whose post-audit value this archive uses. Her dawn rising after her period of invisibility heralded the inundation and the New Year, and she was personified as a goddess in her own right, closely identified with Isis. Beside her in the early astronomical diagrams stands sꜣḥ [Gardiner: G39-V28] [𓅭𓎛], Sah, the constellation Egyptologists identify with Orion, the celestial form of Osiris. A scholarly study of the constellations in the Egyptian astronomical diagrams (Priskin, in the Belmonte tradition of research) notes that when figured constellations first appear, at the beginning of the second millennium BCE, as inserts into the decan tables on coffin lids, there are four of them: the goddess Nut (nwt) [Gardiner: N35-W24-X1-N1] holding up the sky hieroglyph, the Foreleg in the northern sky, Sah in the south, and Sopdet. It is worth pausing on that list. In the earliest surviving attempt to draw the constellations, one of the four figures drawn is Nut herself — the map contains its own maker, the sky pictured inside the sky.

The northern stars behave differently, and the difference is theological. The circumpolar stars never set: they never enter Nut's mouth, never die, and are therefore called imperishable. The funerary texts' promise that the deceased will join the imperishable stars (Section XI) is an observational statement before it is a hope — those stars, uniquely, are exempt from the swallowing. Everything else in heaven submits to her cycle. They alone are already what the dead wish to become.

Is Nut the Milky Way?

A modern question, asked in modern terms, and this archive reports the current state of the argument rather than a verdict. The Milky Way is the most conspicuous structure in a dark Egyptian sky, and it has long been tempting to say that Nut is the Milky Way. Or Graur's interdisciplinary study (2024) tested the identification against simulations of the ancient sky and the primary texts, and concluded more precisely: with her head fixed in the west and her groin in the east, the summer orientation of the Milky Way could be read as a figurative marker of her torso or backbone, and its winter orientation as a marker of her arms — the Galaxy figuring parts of her, in season, rather than being her whole body. His companion study of Nut on coffins (2025) examined a catalogue of 555 coffin elements, including 118 cosmological vignettes of the Twenty-first and Twenty-second Dynasties, and reported a unique feature on the outer coffin of Nesitaudjatakhet: a thick, undulating black curve bisecting Nut's star-studded body, which recalls the Great Rift that cleaves the Milky Way in two. Graur's own summary of the position, in that study, is the one this archive adopts: though Nut and the Milky Way are linked, they are not synonymous. The goddess is the whole sky. The galaxy, on the evidence, is one of the ways her body was drawn.

VII. The Fundamentals of the Course of the Stars — the "Nut Book"

One ancient book belongs to Nut outright, and it is among the most important scientific texts to survive from Egypt. Modern scholarship long called it the Book of Nut. Its real title was recovered by Alexandra von Lieven from the papyrus fragments she published in her edition of 2007, Grundriss des Laufes der Sterne — The Fundamentals of the Course of the Stars. Von Lieven's edition, as its publisher's description records, is a complete re-edition and study of the work: since the standard edition in Neugebauer and Parker's Egyptian Astronomical Texts, five new sources had been identified, some containing considerably more text than those previously available, four of them papyri from the temple library of Tebtunis, published there for the first time. The work is, in the publisher's phrase, a handbook on religious astronomy — and its presiding image is Nut's body, with the explanations of the stars' behaviour written out along and inside the figure of the goddess.

The monumental copies fix the work in place and time. Graur's survey lists them: monumental versions of the Fundamentals survive in the Cenotaph of Seti I at Abydos — the Osireion — and in the tombs of Ramesses IV and of the noblewoman Mutirdis (TT 410, Twenty-sixth Dynasty), with related Nut imagery on the ceilings of Ramesses VI and Ramesses IX, and further witnesses on funerary papyri and coffins. The text itself is far older than its oldest surviving monumental copy: the decan list beneath Nut's body in the Seti I version points back to data of the Twelfth Dynasty, and von Lieven argued — against the possibility that the Middle Kingdom material is itself the original — that the earlier list goes back to the Old Kingdom. The Fundamentals is therefore best understood the way this archive understands most Egyptian sacred science: as a composition that evolved over a very long period, copied, commented, and re-edited, whose core observations are among the oldest in the record. Later copies carry an ancient commentary, glossing the base text passage by passage — Egyptian scholars annotating Egyptian astronomy, in a tradition von Lieven's later work on commentaries and discursive texts sets out in detail.

What does the book actually teach? Its subject is the behaviour of the decans, bꜣktw [Gardiner: G29-V31-X1-B1-G43], across the year: their heliacal risings and settings, the period each spends invisible — swallowed, in the book's mythological register, and gestating inside the goddess — and its return to visibility, its birth. Around that core the work gathers the movements of the sun, the moon, and the planets, and the scheme of the hours. Its most distinctive feature is the one its modern name preserves: all of this is mapped on Nut. The text refers the reader to the picture — where on her body a given passage belongs, which stars stand at her head, her arms, her torso, her groin — so that the goddess's figure functions as the book's diagram, its table, and its memory system at once. To learn the Fundamentals was to learn to read a body.

This is also where the archive's two registers meet without strain. The Fundamentals is observational astronomy: decan lists are data, their periods checkable, their drift over centuries the very thing that lets modern scholars date the lists. It is simultaneously theology, in which a star's invisibility is a sojourn inside a goddess and its return a birth. Egyptian religious astronomy did not experience these as two descriptions competing for the same facts. The goddess is how the data were stored — in a culture whose star clocks were painted inside coffin lids (Section XII) so that the dead could tell the time, the sky's order and the sky's person were a single technology. Compare the archive's treatment of the decans' later career, when the same thirty-six powers pass into Hermetic and astrological tradition, in Mathematics and Astronomy and Alchemy.

VIII. Mother of the Gods — Osiris, Isis, Seth, and Nephthys

Nut's children are the gods on whom Egyptian narrative religion turns. By Geb she is the mother of wsir [Gardiner: Q1-D4-A40] [𓊨𓁹𓀭], Osiris; of ꜣst [Gardiner: G1-S29-X1] [𓄿𓋴𓏏], Isis; of stš [Gardiner: E20] [𓃩], Seth; and of nb.t-ḥwt [Gardiner: V30-X1-O10] [𓎟𓏏𓉡], Nephthys — "Mistress of the House," written with nb [Gardiner: V30] [𓎟], "lord, mistress," the feminine t [Gardiner: X1], and ḥwt [Gardiner: O10] [𓉡], "mansion, temple." The Global Egyptian Museum gives the family in the same four names, and adds the qualification this archive also makes: sometimes Horus is also called her son. That fifth child is Ḥr-wr [Gardiner: G5-G36] [𓅃𓅨], Horus the Elder, Ḥr [Gardiner: G5] [𓅃] with wr [Gardiner: G36] [𓅨], "great, elder" — not the posthumous son of Isis treated in the archive's Horus article, but an elder brother within Nut's own generation of children, a sky god in his own right. The distinction is ancient and load-bearing, and Section IX shows where the fivefold list, Horus included, is spelled out.

Consider what it means to be the mother of this family. Her eldest line produces the murdered god and his avenger, the mourning sisters, and the murderer: the entire Osiris myth — the story Egypt told itself about death, legitimacy, and resurrection — is an affair among Nut's children, conducted under her body. She does not intervene in it. That absence is itself a datum. Nut in the Egyptian sources is almost never a protagonist: she does not scheme like Isis, rage like Seth, or suffer like Osiris. Her role in the family drama is the role she has in the cosmos — she is the space it happens in, the ceiling over the courtroom of the gods, and the mother to whom every one of the contending parties can appeal as children appeal to a mother, which is exactly how the dead appeal to her in Section XI. Egyptian theology distributes its labours: some gods act, and one goddess contains.

The kinship cuts the other way as well. Because the king was the son of Geb's line — heir of the earth god's throne — and because Nut had borne the first royal family, the deceased king could address her, in the oldest funerary texts, as his mother outright, and be received as such. Nut's maternity is not a sentiment in these texts; it is a legal relationship, the ground of a claim. The child of Nut has a right to her protection and to a place in her sky, and the texts of Section XI are drafted like claims: they remind her of the relationship and ask her to act on it.

OSIRIS

Wsir [Gardiner: Q1-D4-A40] [𓊨𓁹𓀭]. Eldest in Plutarch's birth order (Section IX); murdered by his brother, revived, and installed as king of the dead — the god the sun meets inside Nut's body by night. See Osiris.

ISIS

Ꜣst [Gardiner: G1-S29-X1] [𓄿𓋴𓏏]. The great magician, mistress of ḥkꜣ [Gardiner: V28-D28-G1] [𓎛𓂓𓄿], heka; searcher, mourner, and restorer of her brother-husband. See Isis.

SETH

Stš [Gardiner: E20] [𓃩]. Storm, desert, and necessary violence; the family's internal catastrophe and, bound at the prow of the solar barque, its defender against Apep.

NEPHTHYS

Nb.t-ḥwt [Gardiner: V30-X1-O10] [𓎟𓏏𓉡]. The faithful mourner who, with Isis, guards the dead god and the dead generally; Nut's daughter stands at the foot of the coffin as Nut arches over it.

HORUS THE ELDER

Ḥr-wr [Gardiner: G5-G36] [𓅃𓅨]. Counted as the fifth child in the tradition Plutarch preserves, and in some Egyptian traditions: the elder sky-falcon, brother — not son — in the Osirian generation. See Horus.

IX. The Five Days — Plutarch and the Epagomenal Births

The Egyptian civil year had 365 days: twelve months of thirty days, and five days left over, standing outside the months at the year's end. Greek and Latin writers called the five the epagomenal days — the days "brought in additionally." Egyptian tradition regarded those days as the birthdays of Nut's children. But the famous story that explains them — why the children could be born on no ordinary day, and how the five days were won — survives in full only in a Greek text: Plutarch's treatise On Isis and Osiris ( De Iside et Osiride), written around 100 CE, more than a thousand years after the Pyramid Texts and in a philosophical language that is not Egypt's. This archive uses the story, because it is beautiful, famous, and probably preserves genuine Egyptian material — and labels it, every time, as what it is: Plutarch's telling, a late Greek retelling, not an Egyptian scripture. The factual Egyptian core is the calendar itself and the association of the five days with the five births; the narrative machinery is Plutarch's.

The telling, in paraphrase, runs as follows. Rhea — Plutarch's Greek name for Nut, identified with the mother of the Olympians — had lain secretly with Cronus, his Geb. The Sun, discovering it, cursed her: she should give birth in no month and in no year — on no day, that is, that belonged to the calendar at all. Hermes — Thoth, ḏḥwty [Gardiner: G26] [𓅝] — who loved her, played draughts with the Moon and won from her light the small fractions from which he assembled five whole days, and set those days outside the 360 days of the year, where the curse could not reach. On them, in order, Rhea bore her children: on the first, Osiris; on the second, Arueris, whom Plutarch identifies with the Elder Horus and with Apollo; on the third, Typhon, his Seth; on the fourth, Isis; on the fifth, Nephthys. The Global Egyptian Museum's entry preserves the skeleton of the same account in reference form: Plutarch relates that the sun god Helios — the Greek equivalent of Re, rꜥ [Gardiner: N5] — put a curse on Rhea (Nut) forbidding her to give birth on any day of the calendar year, and that thanks to the intervention of Hermes (Thoth) five epagomenal days were added to the year, on which Nut gave birth to her five children, including Apollo (Horus).

Two cautions belong with the story, and this archive attaches them permanently. First, the gambling scene, the curse, and the birth order are attested as a connected narrative only in Plutarch; Egyptian sources know the five days, know them as dangerous and liminal — days outside the year's protection, requiring their own apotropaic lists and calendars of lucky and unlucky days — and know the children as Nut's, but no surviving Egyptian text tells the draughts game. Second, Plutarch writes as a Greek philosopher with his own programme, allegorising as he goes; his Seth is already "Typhon," a Greek monster's name. Used with those labels on, the passage is precious evidence for how the myth was told in Roman-era Egypt and for the calendar theology beneath it. Used without them — quoted, as it too often is, as "the Egyptian myth of the creation of the epagomenal days" — it misleads. The archive's rule for all such material applies: the label travels with the story.

What the Egyptians themselves did with the five days is, in its way, as striking as Plutarch's tale. Days that belonged to no month were days on which the ordinary order, mꜣꜥt [Gardiner: C10], stood thinner than usual — fitting birthdays for a generation that includes both the murdered god and his murderer. The year itself thus ends, every year, with a small enacted reminder: order has an edge, the gods were born on the far side of it, and the calendar, like Nut's body, contains things that its own rules cannot quite govern.

X. The Celestial Cow — the Book of the Heavenly Cow

Nut's second great book is a narrative, and its central image is her second great form. The Book of the Heavenly Cow — also translated Book of the Cow of Heaven, and in its first part the Destruction of Mankind — tells of the rebellion of humankind against the aged Ra, of the slaughter wrought by his Eye, of his mercy in stopping it, and finally of his withdrawal from the earth. The text is recorded in the New Kingdom, in Middle Egyptian, and may have been composed in the Middle Kingdom; the earliest known copy was found on the outermost gilded shrine of Tutankhamun, and complete versions stood in the tombs of Seti I, Ramesses II, and Ramesses III, with a portion in a niche of the tomb of Ramesses VI. The work is divided, on the wall, by its great central picture: the Cow of Heaven herself.

In paraphrase of the narrative's second half: Ra, weary of rule over rebellious humankind, resolves to ascend beyond their reach, and Nut is the means of his ascent. At the direction of Nun, the primeval waters, Nut becomes a cow, and Ra is set upon her back. As she rises, her legs tremble at the height. Ra therefore ordains her supports: Shu is placed beneath her, under her belly, arms upraised — the separation scene of Section III retold as an emergency measure that becomes eternal — and the Heh gods, ḥḥ [Gardiner: C11], stand at her legs to steady them. E. A. Wallis Budge's description of the Seti I painting, in his edition of the legend, gives the picture's inventory: the lower part of the Cow's belly is decorated with a series of thirteen stars; beneath it are the two boats of Ra, the Semketet and the Mantchet; each of her four legs is held in position by two gods; and Shu, with outstretched uplifted arms, supports her body. Budge's summary of the image's meaning is equally compact: the Cow represents the sky in which the boats of Ra sail, and her four legs are the four cardinal points which cannot be changed.

The cow form is not a whimsy. The cow is Egypt's great image of patient, nourishing maternity: the animal whose milk feeds calves and humans, whose body is large enough to carry, and whose stance — four legs at four quarters — maps naturally onto a sky held up at four points. As Graur's study notes in passing, in the Book of the Divine Cow (as he titles it) it is Nut who, turned into a cow, supports Ra on her back. The same bovine maternity belongs to Hathor, and the two goddesses converge here so thoroughly that the Heavenly Cow has often been assigned to Hathor outright; the text's own naming of Nut, and Shu's position beneath her, keep this archive's attribution where the sources put it, while the overlap itself is real and is treated in Hathor. Above the cow's back, in the story's new order, Ra creates the fields of the blessed dead and turns the flowers there into stars — the sky, having been lifted beyond rebellion, is furnished. Below, rule of the earth passes through the succession the myth establishes, with Osiris governing and Thoth deputising for the night. The present world, in this book, is what exists after the sky was raised: distant from its god, held up by appointed supports, and lit by lights that are, in part, an afterthought of mercy.

XI. Nut in the Pyramid Texts — "Nut Has Spread Herself Over You"

Nut is present at the very beginning of Egyptian literature. The Pyramid Texts — the oldest large corpus of religious writing in the world, carved in the pyramids of the late Old Kingdom — open, in Samuel A. B. Mercer's arrangement, with a section he titled "Nut and the Deceased King," Utterances 1–11, and return to her in two whole series of praise: Utterances 427–435, "In Praise of Nut," and Utterances 443–452, a second series in praise of Nut. Between those poles she runs through the corpus as the king's mother, his protector, and his destination. James P. Allen's modern translation of the Texts — the standard the archive follows for the corpus alongside R. O. Faulkner's — organises part of the Teti corpus under a heading that states her function in a phrase: "Spells for Entering the Womb of Nut." The dead king does not merely go to the sky. He goes back into his mother, to be born again out of her as the sun is born — the swallowing and birth of Section V, claimed by an individual, in the first person, as a right of birth.

The characteristic formula is the one this section's title quotes. A British Museum guide, describing a small terra-cotta coffin in its collection, printed the words it bears and identified them for what they are: on the cover of the coffin are the words, in the guide's older rendering, "Thy mother Nut spreadeth herself over thee in her name of Nut" — and the guide adds, simply, that this is an extract from the Pyramid Texts. The formula's logic deserves the scrutiny the Egyptian scribes gave it. She spreads herself over the dead in her name of Nut — that is, the act of covering is performed by, and guaranteed by, her name and nature as sky: because she is nwt [Gardiner: N35-W24-X1-N1], "Sky," spreading over what lies beneath her is not something she chooses to do for the deceased. It is what she is, invoked in the specific case. The archive's study of the power of names treats this pattern — protection by identity, activated by speaking the name — across the corpus; the Nut formula is among its oldest and most durable examples, repeated from Old Kingdom royal pyramids onto the coffins of ordinary people for the next two and a half thousand years (Section XII).

What the king asks of her, in the Utterances, is total. In Mercer's rendering of the opening utterances, Nut speaks in her own voice over the dead king: she presents him to his family — giving him his sister Isis, that she may take hold of him and restore his heart to his body, and his sister Nephthys likewise — and she declares him her son, her beloved, her eldest, set upon the throne of Geb. Elsewhere the king comes to her, in the utterance the Faulkner-based online translation renders as a doubled cry of approach: this king comes to you, O Nut, this king comes to you, O Nut — and begs her to open his place in heaven among the stars of heaven. The two movements are the two halves of the relationship: she descends to cover the coffin; he ascends to enter her sky. They meet, in the theology of the tomb, exactly at the coffin lid — which is why the lid becomes her image.

Through Nut the king joins the stars that do not die. The Pyramid Texts' stellar destiny — the deceased as a star among the imperishable, circumpolar stars of Section VI, a companion of the gods of the northern sky — is a destiny inside her body, and the texts move between the two phrasings without distinction, because there is none: to be a star in her, unwearied and undying, and to be her child, enfolded and protected from all things evil, are the same hope stated in astronomy and in kinship respectively. The later funerary corpora — the Coffin Texts and the Book of the Dead — inherit both, and democratise both: what the Old Kingdom carved for kings alone, the Middle Kingdom painted for officials and the New Kingdom wrote for anyone who could commission a papyrus. The texts are surveyed in Funerary Texts and Book of the Dead Passages.

XII. Nut on Coffins and Sarcophagi — the Lid as Sky

If one object in the Egyptian record belongs to Nut, it is the coffin lid. The reasoning is built into the furniture itself. A coffin — qrs [Gardiner: Q6] [𓊭] in one of its Egyptian names — is a box with a lid over a body; the sky is a surface over a world. To lay a body in a coffin and set Nut on the inside of its lid is to place that body under a private sky, with a mother spread over it, for the whole of its afterlife. The Global Egyptian Museum states the tradition's span: from an early period the sarcophagus and tomb were identified with Nut; even in the Old Kingdom the ceiling of the burial chamber was decorated with stars, or the sarcophagus called "mother"; and many coffins from later periods have a depiction of Nut on the inside of their lids — she is literally stretching herself out above the deceased.

The British Museum's Middle Kingdom coffins of Seni, from Deir el-Bersha (c. 1850 BCE), show the tradition fully formed for a non-royal official. The Museum's record for the inner coffin (EA30842, belonging to the outer coffin EA30841) notes that its exterior is carefully painted, with an eye-panel and standard texts, with references to the protection given to the dead man by the goddess Nut, while the inside is richly decorated: at the top, an elongated hieroglyph for "sky" — a blue vault studded with stars — and below it a large inscription promising that Seni will receive offerings and enjoy a state of blessedness. The coffin's inside is thus a sky with a text under it: Nut's body as the ceiling of the dead man's world, and the promise of his maintenance written across her. Middle Kingdom coffin lids also carried the diagonal star tables of Section VI — the decan clock painted where the deceased's face would see it — so that the lid was at once the goddess, the sky she is, and the instrument that reads her.

In the New Kingdom and after, the image sharpens into portraiture. On anthropoid coffins Nut kneels on the lid itself, wings spread over the breast of the deceased: the British Museum's description of the wooden anthropoid inner coffin of Nestawedjat records that the entire surface, inside and out, was plastered and covered with religious images and texts, that the goddess Nut spreads her wings over the breast, and that the lower compartments of the lid are occupied by deities who offer protection in speeches inscribed along their figures — the Four Sons of Horus, Horus himself, Geb, and two forms of Anubis — with two large wedjat eyes, wḏꜣt [Gardiner: D10] [𓂀], and a figure of Isis completing the lid's design. Read the composition as the deceased experienced it, from inside: mother above, family around, the healed Eye watching. In the Twenty-first and Twenty-second Dynasties the figure grows cosmological again — the "cosmological vignettes" of Graur's 2025 catalogue, in which the full-length arched Nut, with Geb and Shu, is painted inside the coffin as a complete diagram of the world the deceased now inhabits — and full-length depictions of Nut continued to appear inside coffins of the Twenty-fifth and Twenty-sixth Dynasties and beyond. Royal sarcophagi carry her at monumental scale in the same position: the underside of the lid, the first thing, in the fiction of the tomb, that the risen dead would see.

The protection had a specific verbal form, the formula of Section XI — Nut spreading herself over the deceased in her name of Nut — inscribed on coffins from the Middle Kingdom onward as her speech to the coffin-owner, sometimes expanded into a long address in which she enfolds, covers, and guards the dead against every evil thing, and promises that she will not be parted from them. Egyptologists call such texts the Nut formula, or Nut's speech on the coffin, and its persistence is itself the evidence: for two thousand years, across every change of coffin shape, artistic style, and political order, the one fixture nobody redesigned was the mother on the underside of the lid. Coffin, sarcophagus, and tomb chamber are successive enlargements of a single idea — a body-sized piece of sky, made personal — and the archive's articles on shabtis and the broader funerary assemblage treat the company her image kept.

XIII. Astronomical Ceilings — Nut in Tombs and Temples

Scale the coffin lid up to architecture and you get the astronomical ceiling: a tomb or temple room whose vault is Nut's body at building size, with the order of heaven mapped across her. The tradition begins early — star-covered burial-chamber ceilings in the Old Kingdom are the direct ancestors — and reaches its most complete expression in the New Kingdom royal tombs of the Valley of the Kings, where, as the Global Egyptian Museum notes, the sun's cycle through Nut is depicted on the ceilings of the tombs in the Valley of the Kings in particular, but also appears in temples. In the tomb of Ramesses VI the vault carries the Books of Day and Night together: Nut stretched along the ceiling twice over, the sun's barque journeying by day across one body and the sun's gestation by night inside the other, so that the buried king lay under a double diagram of the very cycle he hoped to join.

The Cenotaph of Seti I at Abydos — the Osireion — preserves the Fundamentals version at monumental scale: the goddess arched across the ceiling with the decan lists and explanatory texts disposed along her body, the version from which the reconstructions reproduced in modern scholarship (Frankfort's plates, used in Graur's study) were drawn. The tombs of Ramesses IV and of Mutirdis carry the same book (Section VII). Temple ceilings take the scheme public in the late periods: at Dendera, Nut arches in the astronomical compositions of the Hathor temple, in company the Alexandrian astronomical scholarship of the temple of Esna — the subject of von Lieven's Der Himmel über Esna, "The Sky over Esna," a case study in religious astronomy from that temple's cosmological ceiling and architrave inscriptions — extends to a whole provincial temple's vault. The pattern is consistent for a millennium and a half: wherever Egyptians built a ceiling that was meant to mean the sky, they did not paint an abstraction. They painted her.

The ceilings also settle, visually, the question of what an Egyptian temple or tomb was. A room whose ceiling is a goddess's body, whose floor is the earth, and whose walls carry the gods in procession is a cosmos in cross-section — the visitor stands inside the diagram of Section III, in the air-space of Shu, under Nut, on Geb. The archive's temple architecture article develops the building-as-cosmos reading in full; Nut's ceilings are its most literal proof, since here the building does not symbolise the cosmos by proportion or orientation but depicts it, one body per element, at full scale, overhead.

XIV. Nut and the Sycamore — the Tree Goddess Who Gives Water and Air

Nut has a second habitat, smaller and stranger than the sky: a tree at the edge of the desert, where the dead come to drink. The tree is the sycamore fig, nḥt [Gardiner: N35-V28-X1] [𓈖𓎛𓏏] — one of the few native Egyptian trees of useful size, growing where cultivation meets the necropolis, and therefore standing, in image after image, exactly on the border between the world of the living and the world of the dead. In the tree-goddess scenes a woman emerges from the foliage — upper body human, lower body lost in the trunk — extending a tray of food and a jar of water to the deceased and their bꜣ [Gardiner: G29] [𓅡], the ba, who comes to her as a bird comes to a tree. The Global Egyptian Museum notes the form among Nut's aspects: in addition to the notions of rebirth and protection, Nut is depicted giving the deceased food and water and cool air, and like Hathor she could be portrayed as a tree goddess giving the deceased water.

The textual anchor is the Book of the Dead, chapter 59, the chapter of snuffing air and having power over water in the realm of the dead. In E. A. Wallis Budge's public-domain translation of the Papyrus of Ani, the deceased speaks: "Hail, thou Sycamore tree of the goddess Nut! Give me of the [water and of the] air which is in thee." The request is precisely aimed. What the dead fear in the Egyptian afterlife is not fire but privation — thirst, suffocation, the second death of being forgotten and unfed — and what the sycamore of Nut holds is the two things her element contains: water, and air, the breath of her father Shu's realm, stored in the shade of a tree. Marie-Louise Buhl's classic study of the goddesses of the Egyptian tree cult ( Journal of Near Eastern Studies, 1947) traced the type in detail: the goddess in the tree, arms extended to the kneeling deceased with food and the water jar, appears with chapter 59 and chapter 62 texts, on tomb walls such as those of Sennedjem — where Nut stands in a sycamore laden with fruit, her lower body hidden in the trunk, offering the jar and the tray to the dead man and his wife — and on offering tables of the Ptolemaic period, the scene worn smooth by use.

The sycamore was shared. Hathor is "Lady of the Sycamore" par excellence, with an ancient cult tree south of Memphis; Isis, too, gives water from the tree; and Nut's claim, in Buhl's analysis and in the scenes themselves, is one strand of a threefold tree-goddess tradition in which the great mother goddesses meet at the desert's edge in a single merciful function. This archive does not force a choice among them where the images do not: the tree scenes are sometimes captioned, sometimes not, and the function — a goddess who is a tree, feeding the dead at the border — mattered more than the personnel. But Nut's presence in the type is not incidental, and it is not a borrowing that needs apology. The sky that swallows the sun and bears it again is, at ground level, the shade that keeps water cool at noon in a land where both are life. Her two forms state one doctrine at two scales: nothing that enters her is lost; everything she holds, she gives back — as light, as breath, or as water from a jar in a tree.

XV. Other Forms — Sow, Vulture, and the Winged Protectress

A goddess who is a whole sky accumulates animal forms the way the sky accumulates weather, and the sources are candid about the range. Graur's survey of her representations, following Billing's monograph on the goddess (2002), notes that though Nut's earliest and most common representation is anthropomorphic, she is also depicted as a cow, a vulture, a sow, and a hippopotamus. Each form is a partial reading of her, and each repays a moment's attention, because none of them is arbitrary.

The sow is the most theologically pointed of them. A sow with her litter at her teats is an image of the stars at the body of their mother — and of the fate the Fundamentals tradition assigns them: the mother who bears the stars also takes them back, as a sow was proverbially said to eat her own piglets. The image carries the swallowing doctrine of Section V into the star-world with deliberate earthiness, and the tradition even stages the objection: Geb, in the dramatic material associated with the Fundamentals, reproaches Nut for devouring her offspring, and Shu must mediate between the parents. The archive reports this as the tradition presents it — a mythic aetiology of why stars disappear — and notes its function: it gives the daily and annual vanishings of heaven a family explanation, in which disappearance is not annihilation but return to the mother, pending rebirth.

The vulture reads her from the side of protection. The vulture's great wings, held over her young, made the bird a hieroglyph and an emblem of motherhood itself in Egyptian writing, and the vulture headdress is the crown of goddesses and queens as mothers. Nut in vulture form, or winged in the kneeling form of Section IV, is the same emphasis in feathers: cover, shade, and the coolness the Global Egyptian Museum names as the meaning of her wings. The hippopotamus, by contrast, belongs to her watery margin — the female hippopotamus as a fierce defender of her young in the river shallows, at the edge of the waters of nwn [Gardiner: N35-G43-N35] over which her body is stretched. None of these forms displaces the arched woman; they annotate her. The archive lists them, as it lists the forms of Horus, so that a reader meeting Nut as a pig in a star text or a cow on a shrine wall will recognise the same goddess under the annotation.

XVI. Cult, Epithets, and Presence — a Goddess Everywhere and Her Own Few Houses

Nut presents a paradox of presence. She is overhead in every temple, on the ceiling of every decorated tomb, and on the lid of every inscribed coffin in Egypt — and she was served by comparatively few dedicated sanctuaries and priesthoods of her own. The reference literature's summary, reflected in the standard modern accounts, is that Nut's cult was minor in institutional terms, though not absent: dedicated sanctuaries and priests of Nut are attested, and food offerings were dedicated to her in a funerary context. The disproportion is theologically intelligible. Gods with local houses — Amun at Thebes, Ptah at Memphis, Hathor at Dendera — are gods you go to. Nut is the god you are always already under. Her temple is the ceiling of everyone else's, and her daily liturgy is the sunrise.

Her epithets map her functions more honestly than a list of shrines could. She is "the Great," the title the Pyramid Texts put at the head of her speeches — in Mercer's rendering she is "Nut, the great, who is within the lower mansion," the great one speaking from her house at Heliopolis, and "Nut, the great" again as she gives the king his sisters and his throne. She is "Mistress of the Sky," mistress of the stars, mother of the gods — the maternity of Section VIII stated as a title — and, in the tradition the Global Egyptian Museum records, "Lady of Heliopolis," believed to dwell in the lower mansion there: the Ennead's city keeps her as a resident, not a visitor. In funerary use the epithets turn tender and specific: she is the one who enfolds, who covers, who gives cool water and air, the mother who does not part from her child. Egyptian epithet-systems are functional documents; read in sequence, Nut's say: great in extent, local in address, maternal in action.

Her appearances in cultic time match her appearances in cultic space: ubiquitous at the joints of things. The five epagomenal days (Section IX) are her children's birthdays and the year's most dangerous seam, marked with their own protective traditions. The dawn is her daily festival, which no priesthood needed to schedule. And in the temple astronomy of the late periods — Dendera's ceilings, Esna's "sky" inscriptions — her body is the medium in which the calendar itself is published, the decans and later the zodiac disposed along her as the year's order made visible. A goddess whose cult is the ceiling, the dawn, and the calendar has not a minor cult. She has a distributed one — and the distinction, like so much in her dossier, is a matter of reading the evidence at the scale she works at.

XVII. Esoteric Analysis — the Container That Is Also a Mother

This section is the archive's interpretive reading of the Nut material, offered as interpretation. It makes no claim that any Egyptian text states it, and it should be weighed against the primary material of Sections I–XVI.

Most gods in most pantheons are agents: they do things, in stories, for reasons. Nut's strangeness — and, in this archive's reading, her power as a figure for practice — is that she is a god whose primary act is to contain. Her verbs in the sources are a small, exact set: she spreads herself over, she enfolds, she swallows, she bears, she gives back. She is the only member of the Ennead whose body is the stage for all the others, and the only one whose central miracle is performed on a schedule so regular that Egyptians set their clocks by it. The alchemical tradition this archive also documents has a name for the vessel in which a work of transformation is enclosed so that what dies in it can be reborn from it; the parallel with Nut is the archive's own, drawn openly. She is the vessel of the world: a sealed container with an inside in which the sun is dissolved nightly and regenerated, whose walls are a mother's body, and whose operation is not a technique anyone performs on her but a rhythm everything inside her undergoes. Read through the archive's article on Alchemy, the daily cycle of Section V has the shape of the opus in miniature — a nightly dissolution, a sojourn in darkness in the presence of the dead god, a dawn restoration — repeated not once, in one vessel, but every night, in the largest vessel there is. The reading is offered in that spirit: as a correspondence to be tested against the texts, in the manner of the archive's cosmological survey, not as an Egyptian doctrine.

A second strand of the reading concerns her relation to the dead, and here the archive can stay closer to its sources, because the Egyptians drew the conclusion themselves. The funerary system asks a terrifying question — what holds the dead person together when the body fails? — and Nut is one of its oldest answers: a mother does. Not a judge, not a ferryman, not a spell, though Egypt has all of those; underneath them all, a mother who covers the body with her own, whose name spoken over a coffin performs the covering, and inside whom the dead are gestated for a second birth as stars. The composite soul — the kꜣ [Gardiner: D28] [𓂓], the bꜣ [Gardiner: G29] [𓅡], the ꜣḫ [Gardiner: G25] [𓅜] — disperses at death into her custody: the ba drinks at her sycamore, the akh shines in her body, and the coffin texts ask her, in the formula of Section XI, simply not to be parted from her child. If the Ennead is a physics, Nut is the point where the physics becomes personal: the boundary of the world turns out, on inspection, to have a face, a name, and a mother's obligations — and to keep them.

// THE NUT PATTERN, AS THE ARCHIVE STATES IT //

Containment before action — the vessel is prepared before the work begins, and the sky is raised before anything can happen under it. Take in without destroying — what she swallows is gestated, not annihilated; disappearance, in her pattern, is a phase, not an end. Keep the schedule — renewal in her world is rhythmic and reliable, a dawn and not a rescue. Cover what you love in your own name — the coffin formula protects by identity: she spreads herself over the dead as Nut, the sky doing what the sky does. Hold the boundary — beyond her back are the waters; a life, like a cosmos, is partly the art of knowing what stays outside. This is the archive's interpretive summary of the Nut material, offered as a reading to be tested against Sections I–XVI.

XVIII. Sources and Primary Texts

The two verbatim ancient quotations in this article are identified at the point of use and kept short: the Pyramid Texts formula "Thy mother Nut spreadeth herself over thee in her name of Nut," quoted from the British Museum's guide to its Egyptian rooms, which prints the words from a coffin cover in its collection and identifies them as an extract from the Pyramid Texts; and the address to the sycamore in Book of the Dead chapter 59, quoted from E. A. Wallis Budge's public-domain translation of the Papyrus of Ani. Everything else ancient is labelled paraphrase, and Plutarch is labelled as Plutarch wherever he is used.

Primary Texts

Pyramid Texts — Nut and the deceased king (Utterances 1–11 in Mercer's arrangement); the two series in praise of Nut (Utterances 427–435 and 443–452). Translations: Samuel A. B. Mercer (1952); R. O. Faulkner (1969); James P. Allen, The Ancient Egyptian Pyramid Texts (2005; updated edition 2015) — for the Teti corpus's "Spells for Entering the Womb of Nut" and the Allen renderings of the stellar destiny.

The Fundamentals of the Course of the Stars (the "Nut Book") — edition: Alexandra von Lieven, Grundriss des Laufes der Sterne: Das sogenannte Nutbuch (Copenhagen, 2007), recovering the work's ancient title from the Tebtunis papyri; earlier edition in Neugebauer and Parker, Egyptian Astronomical Texts. Monumental copies: Cenotaph of Seti I (Osireion), Abydos; tomb of Ramesses IV; tomb of Mutirdis (TT 410). See also von Lieven, Der Himmel über Esna (2000), on the Esna ceiling.

The Book of the Heavenly Cow (including the Destruction of Mankind) — copies on the outermost shrine of Tutankhamun and in the tombs of Seti I, Ramesses II, Ramesses III, and Ramesses VI. Translation used for the picture's inventory: E. A. Wallis Budge, Legends of the Gods / The Destruction of Mankind (Books on Egypt and Chaldaea), describing the Seti I painting; modern translation by Erik Hornung in William Kelly Simpson (ed.), The Literature of Ancient Egypt.

Book of the Dead, chapter 59 — the sycamore of Nut; Budge's translation of the Papyrus of Ani (British Museum EA10470), quoted in Section XIV.

Plutarch, De Iside et Osiride (c. 100 CE), chapter 12 — the curse on Rhea, Thoth's draughts game with the Moon, and the births on the five epagomenal days; used in Section IX only, as a Greek retelling.
