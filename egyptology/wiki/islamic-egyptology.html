I. The Ontology of Arabic Interest — Kemet as Source

Medieval Arabic scholars (8th–14th centuries CE) maintained a profound and systematic interest in Ancient Egypt. Unlike the later Western "rationalist" historiography, they did not view the Pharaonic past as "pagan" error, but as the Original Fountainhead of alchemical, philosophical, and mathematical wisdom.

In this research framework, the Islamic Period represents a Preservation Cycle within the Unbroken Chain. Arabic scholars served as the primary "Data-Nodes" that protected the Kemetic-Hermetic corpus during the centuries when Europe had lost access to its ancestral frequencies. They viewed the monuments of Egypt as "Silent Teachers"—functional resonators that encoded the secrets of the universe in stone and script (Medu Netjer).

The interest had a theological door as well as a scholarly one. The Qurʾān names Idrīs as a prophet, and Islamic tradition identified Idrīs with both Enoch and Hermes — so that Egypt's ancient sage entered Islam not as a pagan relic but as a prophet of the reader's own religion, to whose Egyptian career the pyramids and temples were the standing monuments (§XII). A civilisation that believed a prophet had founded the sciences in Egypt had a standing reason to describe, copy, and argue about Egyptian remains. The identification is a tradition, reported here as such; its consequences for the preservation cycle of §II are documentary history.

The chronological frame matters to everything that follows. The scholars of this article work between the 8th and the 15th centuries — after the last hieroglyphic inscription (Philae, 394 CE) and the last Demotic graffito (452 CE), after the temples' closure, and centuries before Champollion. They could not read the inscriptions. What they could do — and did, at a scale Europe would not match for Egypt until the Description de l'Égypte — was describe the monuments, copy the signs, collect the local traditions about them, maintain the Coptic language that would one day crack them (§XVI), and transmit the Greek sciences of Egypt through Syriac and Arabic into the Latin West (§§VIII, XVIII). This article's subject is that millennium of work, assessed at the strength the evidence bears in each section.

II. The Preservation Cycle — The Arabic Data-Nodes

Okasha El Daly's Egyptology: The Missing Millennium (UCL Press, 2005) names the period this article covers: from the Muslim annexation of Egypt in the 7th century CE to the Ottoman conquest in the 16th, a full millennium in which Arabic writers — not Europeans — were the ones describing Egypt's monuments, copying its inscriptions and arguing about its scripts. El Daly's threefold aim, as his reviewers summarise it, was to show that medieval Arabs were interested in ancient Egypt, that their material bridges the gap between the classical writers and the Renaissance, and that it remains useful to Egyptology now.

The preservation was distributed, which is why it worked. Geographers and travellers described temples in situ (§§X, XIII); alchemists copied sign lists and technical treatises (§§III, VIII, IX); Coptic scholars produced the grammars and glossaries that kept the Egyptian language legible (§XVI); and a large, still largely unedited genre of treasure-hunting manuals recorded monuments, inscriptions and local traditions because the information had practical value. No single institution held the archive, so no single collapse destroyed it.

El Daly's stronger evaluations are contested — reviewers, notably in the Egyptologists' Electronic Forum, accept the recovered corpus while disputing how much of it amounts to successful decipherment (§III). The corpus itself is not in dispute: the manuscripts exist, many published for the first time in his book.

The treasure manuals repay a closer look, because they are the period's most purely Egyptian genre. Their premise — that the pharaohs buried treasure under talismanic protection — sent their readers to real monuments with real notebooks: the manuals describe temple sites, copy inscription-like sign sequences, name local landmarks, and preserve Coptic and local Arabic lore about specific ruins. Their sceptical contemporary survives too: al-Jawbarī's Book of Charlatans (13th century) exposes the treasure-hunters' frauds in detail, and in doing so documents the same world from the other side — the hunters, their manuals, their fumigations, and their forged inscriptions are all in his exposé. Between the manuals and their critic, the modern reader gets the period's monument-culture whole: belief, practice, and fraud, each attesting the others.

III. Ibn Wahshiyah — The Alchemical Decipherment (fl. 9th/10th c.)

The most discussed attempt at linguistic recovery before Champollion is that of the Nabataean scholar Ibn Wahshiyah (fl. 9th/10th century; the date 904 attaches traditionally to the Nabataean Agriculture rather than securely to this work) in his Shauq al-Mustaham ("The Desired Knowledge of Ancient Alphabets").

THE PHONETIC BREAKTHROUGH — EL DALY'S THESIS

Okasha El Daly argues that Ibn Wahshiyah correctly identified that hieroglyphs were not merely decorative icons but possessed Phonetic Values, and correctly identified a number of individual signs by linking them to their Coptic and Arabic equivalents. The Shauq's sign lists are largely symbolic or pseudo-hieroglyphic, though one appendix assigns Arabic-letter equivalents to 34 signs.

SYSTEMATIC DETERMINATIVES — A CONTESTED CLAIM

El Daly further credits Ibn Wahshiyah with realising that some signs functioned as Determinatives—silent markers indicating the semantic category of a word. This reading is contested: Tara Stephan comments that El Daly "vastly overemphasizes Ibn Wahshiyya's accuracy." The archive presents the decipherment claim as El Daly's thesis, not as settled fact. See Decipherment History Wiki.

The man behind the Shauq needs his own caution. "Ibn Waḥshiyya" is the named translator-author of the Nabataean Agriculture, a vast compilation presented as translated from ancient Babylonian sources — a framing modern scholarship treats as itself partly pseudepigraphic — and the Shauq's full title announces its programme: The Longing of the Aspired-to for the Knowledge of the Writing of the Ancient Nations. It is a comparative catalogue of scripts — Egyptian temple writing among them — written by a scholar of Iraq working in Arabic, drawing on sources and informants now largely lost. El Daly's case rests on taking that catalogue seriously as fieldwork; his critics' case rests on how much of it dissolves, on inspection, into the symbolic sign-lore of §VI. Both cases are about the same manuscripts, which is why §IV states the defensible core rather than either verdict.

IV. Phonetic and Symbolic Synthesis — Identifying Determinatives

What the Arabic decipherment attempts actually achieved sits between two caricatures — total failure and a decipherment eight centuries before Champollion. El Daly's UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology survey (2025) states the defensible core: medieval scholars worked, as the Europeans later would, through a third script, using Coptic, Greek and Demotic material still available in the early medieval period, and in his assessment several of them correctly identified at least part of the small set of alphabetic signs.

For Ibn Wahshiyah (§III) the concrete evidence is the appendix to the Shauq al-Mustaham assigning Arabic-letter equivalents to 34 signs: some values are partly correct, many are not, and the lists around them are largely symbolic or pseudo-hieroglyphic, as the Yale Peabody's Echoes of Egypt survey notes. A later attempt, by Abu al-Qasim al-Iraqi (13th/14th century), copied an ancient Egyptian text and assigned phonetic values to several of its signs.

The determinative claim — that Ibn Wahshiyah understood some signs as silent semantic markers — is El Daly's reading, and the archive presents it as such: Tara Stephan's judgment, quoted in §III, is that El Daly vastly overemphasises Ibn Wahshiyah's accuracy. The phonetic insight itself, that hieroglyphs could carry sound as well as symbol, is the point the sources support; a working decipherment is not.

Abū al-Qāsim al-ʿIrāqī's attempt, two to four centuries later, shows the method at its most concrete. In his Kitāb al-Aqālīm al-Sabʿa ("Book of the Seven Climes") he copied out an ancient Egyptian inscription — a genuine temple text, on El Daly's analysis — and worked through its signs one by one, assigning sound-values drawn from Coptic. Some assignments are right; the system as a whole does not resolve into a reading. The episode is the medieval decipherment effort in miniature: real texts, a real method (the Coptic bridge of §XVI), partial phonetic success, and no general solution — because the signary's full logic, with its mixture of phonograms, logograms, and determinatives, could not be recovered from values alone.

V. Dhu al-Nun al-Misri — The Sufi Connection and Gnosis

The Egyptian Sufi master Dhu al-Nun al-Misri (d. 859 CE) is a pivotal link in the initiatory chain. Born in Akhmim (Panopolis), later tradition — and El Daly's reading of the Ḥall al-Rumūz attributed to him — credits him with knowledge of the hieroglyphs; there is no contemporary evidence that he could read them, and the claim should be read as tradition, not documented fact.

Dhu al-Nun viewed the temples as Theaters of Wisdom. He integrated Kemetic concepts of the Heart (Ib) and Vital Breath (Hu) into the Sufi mystical vocabulary. For Dhu al-Nun, the alchemical "Transmutation of Lead into Gold" was the structural equivalent of the Sufi path of the soul's return to the divine source. He effectively re-coded Pharaonic initiatory technology for an Islamic audience, ensuring the survival of the Kemetic "Internal Path."

What is documented for Dhū al-Nūn is a Sufi career, and it is substantial enough without the hieroglyphs. Born at Akhmim in Upper Egypt — the same Panopolis that produced Zosimos (§VIII) and the Greek alchemical tradition — he became one of the formative voices of early Sufism: his sayings are preserved in the classical Sufi manuals, al-Qushayrī's Risāla and Ḥujwīrī's Kashf al-Maḥjūb among them, and the tradition associates him with the early articulation of maʿrifa — direct experiential knowledge of God, as distinct from learned ʿilm. The association is the tradition's, and this article states it at that strength: he is associated with maʿrifa's early formulation, not documented as its inventor. The hieroglyph-reading attribution belongs to the same later tradition that made him a sage of the temples; both attributions tell us how Islamic Egypt remembered him — an Egyptian holy man whose authority was felt to reach back past Islam into the monuments among which he lived.

VI. Hieroglyphic Mysticism — The Perception of the Hidden

Most medieval readers, Arabic and European alike, inherited the late-antique way of reading hieroglyphs: as symbols, each sign a compressed picture of a hidden truth about nature. The channel for that reading was the Hieroglyphica attributed to Horapollo (4th/5th century CE), which explains signs allegorically and almost never phonetically — and which set the terms in which the script was discussed for a thousand years.

In Arabic writing the symbolic reading fused with alchemy. Ibn Wahshiyah's lists present the temple script — the writing of the barabi, the ancient temples — as one among many secret alphabets of the sages, and the same milieu that copied sign lists also wrote alchemy in cover-names and ciphers. A script believed to hide the laws of nature in pictures was, for an alchemist, not a puzzle but a model: his own art did the same thing in words.

This is the perception §IV has to be read against. The mysticism was not the opposite of the decipherment attempts; it was their motive. Scholars looked for phonetic values precisely because they believed the signs concealed real knowledge worth recovering.

Horapollo's Hieroglyphica itself has a transmission worth noting, because it is the one "Egyptian" book every later tradition shared. Composed in Greek (its own claim to translate an Egyptian original is part of its fiction), it circulated in the Byzantine and Arabic worlds, was carried to Italy in the early 15th century, and was printed in Greek in 1505 and in Latin in 1515 — becoming the Renaissance's handbook of the symbolic reading at exactly the moment Ficino's generation was forming its Egypt (§XVIII's other shore). The Arabic sign-lists of §§III–IV and the Florentine emblem-books are cousins: both descend from the late-antique decision that a hieroglyph is a picture of a truth rather than the writing of a word.

VII. Al-Kīmiyā — The Etymological Link to Kemet

Arabic scholarship was the primary vehicle for the survival of Hermeticism. The word Alchemy itself derives from the Arabic al-kīmiyā. A derivation from the name Kemet (the Black Land) has often been proposed, but the etymology is disputed — another view derives the Greek khēmeia from khyma, "pouring" — and no single derivation is settled.

They saw the Nile Valley as the cradle of the "Sacred Art," preserving and expanding the works of Hermes Trismegistus and Zosimos of Panopolis. The corpus attributed to Jabir ibn Hayyan provided a "Systemic Hardening" of alchemical theory. Modern scholarship dates that Jabirian corpus to c. 850–950, treats it as the work of an anonymous school writing under a pseudonym — the historical existence of a single Jabir is doubted — and distinguishes it from the later Latin "Geber" corpus (the pseudo-Geber works, including the Summa perfectionis, c. before 1310), a separate body of writing falsely attributed to him. The Arabic alchemical tradition provided the "Operating Infrastructure" that would eventually be exported to Europe, triggering the Renaissance.

VIII. Hermetic Migration — Preservation of the Zosimos Corpus

Zosimos of Panopolis (modern Akhmim, c. 300 CE) is the hinge figure of Greek-Egyptian alchemy: the Suda credits him with 28 books of Cheirokmeta, and his surviving Authentic Memoirs — On Apparatus and Furnaces in Michèle Mertens' edition (1995) — mix exact apparatus description with the allegorical Visions. In Greek he survives fragmentarily, in Byzantine compendia copied seven centuries after his death.

The fuller survival ran east. Twelve treatises attributed to Zosimos survive in Syriac (Cambridge Mm. 6.29, 15th century), and the same codex carries the tradition's workshop literature with them — treatises of Democritus on the preparation of gold and of silver, an extract of "various technical receipts," and chemical fragments: recipe material of the same Greek workshop kind as the Leyden and Stockholm papyri, though not a copy of either papyrus itself. Greek alchemy then reached the Islamic world in the 8th–9th century translation movement, largely via Syriac: the Jabirian corpus (§IX) is the great Arabic systematisation of that inheritance, citing Democritus and the older authorities, though often through later intermediaries rather than directly.

The transmission left fossils in language itself: alembic (from the Greek ambix, the still-head, via Arabic al-anbiq), alkali, elixir, aludel — and alchemy. Without the Syriac and Arabic copies, Zosimos would be a name and a handful of Greek excerpts; through them, his apparatus, his recipes and his dream-allegories all remained readable.

IX. Jabir ibn Hayyan (Geber) — The Systematic Alchemist

The works attributed to Jabir ibn Hayyan are Arabic alchemy's foundation corpus — and, as §VII already records, modern scholarship dates them to c. 850–950 and treats "Jabir" as the name of an anonymous school rather than one 8th-century author. About 215 treatises survive. Their traditional master, the Imam Ja'far al-Sadiq (d. 765), belongs to the corpus's own account of itself.

The systematisation is real, whoever wrote it. The corpus gives an early form of the sulfur–mercury theory of metals, which remained dominant into the 18th century; the oldest known systematic classification of chemical substances; instructions for deriving sal ammoniac from organic materials; and detailed, testable procedures for alloying, purifying and testing metals, with fractional distillation used to separate a substance's "natures."

Its most original framework is the science of the balance ('ilm al-mizan): an attempt to reduce every substance to quantitative proportions of the four natures — hot, cold, wet and dry — computed, in the corpus's arithmology, from the numerical values of the letters of its Arabic name, the hidden natures falling in the proportion 1:3:5:8. A Latin reader met Jabir as Geber: Gerard of Cremona translated the Book of Seventy as the Liber de septuaginta (§XVIII), while the famous Latin Summa perfectionis is a separate, later pseudo-Geber work, not a translation. The Arabic golden age that this section compresses now has a division of the archive to itself: the Arabic hub treats the Jābir corpus, al-Rāzī, and their world in full.

X. Al-Mas'udi — Sacred Geography and the Pyramids

Al-Mas'udi (c. 896–956), born in Baghdad and dead at Fustat in Egypt, combined history and scientific geography in the Muruj al-Dhahab (The Meadows of Gold, completed 943, revised to 956) — a universal history built on a lifetime of travel through Persia, India, East Africa and Egypt. He is often called the Herodotus of the Arabs, and the comparison is structural: both men report what they saw, what they were told, and mark the difference unevenly.

On Egypt he is a witness before he is a theorist. In the published translation of the Meadows he describes temples at Arsinoe, Akhmim and Samannud, and the pyramids as very high, remarkable structures whose sides are covered with inscriptions in the scripts of vanished nations — which, he states plainly, no one can read. His reported dimensions, estimated at 400 cubits or more, are a traveller's figures, offered as estimates.

Alongside his own observation he transmits the Surid tradition (§XII) as a Coptic account: the antediluvian king who built the pyramids to store the sciences against the Flood. Recording a local tradition as a local tradition — attributed, not endorsed — is itself a scholarly act, and it is how that tradition survived to be argued about.

Al-Masʿūdī's Egyptian chapters sit inside a larger, partly lost corpus that shows the period's historiography at full stretch. His Akhbār al-zamān ("History of Time"), surviving in part, is the great reservoir of the Surid and antediluvian traditions of §XII; his Kitāb al-Tanbīh wa-l-Ishrāf ("Book of Notification and Verification," 956) revises and corrects the Meadows at the end of his life. The layering is the method: observation in the Meadows, tradition in the Akhbār, correction in the Tanbīh — a 10th-century historian maintaining, across three books, exactly the distinction between what he saw and what he was told that this article has been at pains to keep.

XI. Monuments as Talismans — The Theater of Wisdom

A large body of medieval Arabic writing viewed the Egyptian monuments not as ruins but as talismans — objects charged with protective power by their makers. The belief is documented across the genres this article has surveyed: the Akhbār al-zamān tradition peoples the pyramids and temples with guardian figures set over their treasures (§XII); the treasure-hunting manuals of §II assume, as their working premise, that buried hoards are protected by talismanic guardians that must be identified and neutralised; and al-Maqrīzī's great compilation (§XX) reports the Sphinx itself — under its local name Balḥīb — as a talisman raised against the encroaching sands of Giza. These are reports of what medieval writers believed and practised, and this section presents them at exactly that strength.

The talisman reading had a learned wing as well as a popular one. In the astral-magic tradition that produced the Picatrix (§XIX), a talisman is a made object in which a celestial power is deliberately lodged by correct material, correct image, and correct timing — and a temple or pyramid built by the sages of the first age could be read, within that theory, as a talisman at landscape scale: the sacred architecture of Kemet interpreted as functional theurgical technology. In this archive's comparative vocabulary, that reading treats the alignment and endurance of the monuments as a working maintenance of cosmic order (Ma'at) — offered as the archive's comparison, in the open. What the sources themselves document is the belief and its literature; claims sometimes attached to this material in modern retellings — measured "resonant acoustics" of the pyramid chambers, recorded observations of sound-effects — have no medieval source that this archive could find, and are not made here.

XII. The Theology of the Pyramids in Arabic Thought

Arabic writing gave the pyramids a theology, not a builder's name. In the dominant tradition — told in the Akhbar al-zaman, by al-Mas'udi (§X), and at length by al-Maqrizi — the Giza pyramids were raised before the Flood by King Surid, or by Hermes, identified with the prophet Idris (Enoch). Warned by dreams and astrological calculation of the coming deluge, the builder stored the sciences inside them: inscribed accounts of astronomy, medicine and the arts, treasures, and the bodies of ancestors, guarded by talismanic figures (§XI).

Abu Ma'shar's Book of Thousands (c. 850) preserves the variant in which the first Hermes, fearing the destruction of knowledge in the Flood, built the great temples (barabi) of Upper Egypt and carved the arts and their explanations into them. The pyramid and temple versions share one premise: Egypt's monuments are arks of knowledge in stone, built because their builders expected civilisation to be interrupted.

Read as history of the Old Kingdom, the tradition fails — modern scholarship treats it as a medieval construction with late-antique ingredients. Read as theology, it explains the Arabic millennium's behaviour: a monument believed to be a divinely warned storehouse of science is something you copy, guard and study, not quarry without a second thought.

The tradition behind the theology is the Akhbār al-zamān ("Tidings of Time") complex — the pseudo-al-Masʿūdī compilation of antediluvian lore that al-Maqrīzī and the later antiquarians (§XX) quarry for the pyramids' inner history. Its Sūrīd narrative is the fullest statement of the storehouse idea: the king dreams of the Flood centuries before it, consults his priests, and builds against it — the pyramids as arks of knowledge in stone, their interiors stocked with the sciences, star-lore, and talismans of the antediluvian world. The text's own transmission is characteristically layered: attributed to al-Masʿūdī (d. 956), compiled in something like its surviving form centuries later, and known to the Latin world in modern times through partial translations. The archive's rule applies to it without strain: the Akhbār is first-class evidence for what medieval readers believed the pyramids were — and the belief itself is a historical force, since it is why generations of copyists, translators, and would-be decipherers (§III; §IV) kept returning to monuments a merely pragmatic culture might have treated as quarries. The storehouse was a library in the only sense that ever operated: men behaved toward the pyramids as toward books because their books told them the pyramids were books.

XIII. Al-Idrisi — Mapping the Temples of Upper Egypt

Muhammad al-Idrisi (1100–1165), working at Palermo for Roger II of Sicily, produced in 1154 the Nuzhat al-Mushtaq and the world map known as the Tabula Rogeriana — the most advanced geography of its century, built from travellers' reports and earlier books as much as from his own journeys. El Daly devoted an appendix of The Missing Millennium to the books on ancient Egypt that al-Idrisi used: his Egypt is a compilation from named sources, and can be source-criticised like one.

His Upper Egyptian notices are among the fullest medieval descriptions of standing temples. In the published translation of his Egypt section, temples of the same berba type stand at Esna and Dendera, but the one at Akhmim is, in his account, the most solidly built and the most remarkable for its sculpture — depicting, he reports, the celestial bodies and various arts, with a great number of inscriptions. Akhmim's temple he attributes, following his sources, to Hermes before the Deluge (§XII).

The value of the passage is descriptive, not theological: a 12th-century geographer, writing for a Norman king, records which temples stood, where, and what their walls showed. That is the preservation cycle (§II) working at map scale.

The Palermo context sharpens the point. Al-Idrīsī worked for a Norman Christian king who kept an Arabic-speaking court; the Tabula Rogeriana was engraved on a great silver planisphere (lost in a revolt within the decade) and accompanied by the Nuzha's seventy sectional maps. Egypt's temples thus entered the most ambitious geographical project of the 12th century through a Muslim scholar compiling Arabic sources for a Christian patron — the preservation cycle's indifference to confessional boundaries (§II) operating at the level of royal cartography.

XIV. The Sabians of Harran — The Hermetic Outpost

Harran, in upper Mesopotamia, kept a Hellenised Semitic astral religion alive into the Islamic centuries. From the 9th century its adherents claimed the Qur'anic name Sabians — a people of the Book — and with it legal protection as a recognised community; medieval sources report that they acknowledged Hermes Trismegistus as their prophet, Hermes being identified in Islamic tradition with Idris/Enoch. Modern scholarship (notably Kevin van Bladel, The Arabic Hermes, 2009) treats the Harranians as a real channel by which Hermetic and Greek scientific material entered Arabic — while noting that the evidence for their Hermetic doctrine beyond the Hermes-as-prophet claim is thin.

The channel had names. Thabit ibn Qurra (826–901), a Harranian Sabian brought to Baghdad under the Banu Musa, translated and revised Euclid, Apollonius, Archimedes and Ptolemy from Greek into Arabic; the astronomer al-Battani (d. 929) came from the same community. Harran's contribution to transmission was thus not a library of Hermetic secrets but working scholars, bilingual in Syriac, Greek and Arabic, inside the Abbasid translation movement (§VIII).

The community's own foundation story survives in the Fihrist (§XV), and it is told there as a story: when the caliph al-Maʾmūn passed through Harran (the encounter is conventionally dated c. 830, an inference from the reign rather than a date the account itself gives), he demanded to know by what scripture the Harranians claimed protected status, and the community — after a interval of mortal anxiety, in the telling — adopted the Qurʾānic name Ṣābians and named Hermes as their prophet. Modern scholarship, van Bladel prominently, treats the account as a later construction that nonetheless preserves the transaction's shape: a pagan astral community securing legal existence inside Islam by presenting Hermes in the one role Islam had a form for — prophet. The archive reports the story as the Fihrist tells it and van Bladel reads it, not as a transcript of 830.

XV. The Fihrist and the Bibliographers — Cataloguing Hermes

The Arabic transmission had librarians, and their catalogues are primary evidence for what the Hermetic Egypt of this article actually contained. The great witness is the Fihrist ("The Catalogue") of Ibn al-Nadīm, a Baghdad bookseller's son, completed in 987 CE: a universal bibliography of books known to its author, organised by subject, with authors' biographies and chains of transmission. Its seventh discourse covers philosophy and the ancient sciences, and its pages on the Hermetic and alchemical literature list works under Hermes' name, name the translators who carried Greek and Syriac material into Arabic, and record the book-trade's own knowledge of what existed, what was rare, and what was attributed. For the modern historian the Fihrist is the period's card catalogue: claims about an Arabic Hermetic literature (§XIV of the Hermeticism article) can be checked against what a 10th-century Baghdad bibliographer could actually shelve.

Two Andalusian bibliographers extend the record west. Ibn Juljul of Córdoba (c. 944–c. 994), in his Generations of Physicians and Wise Men, preserves the fullest quotation of Abū Maʿshar's three-Hermeses history (§XII's theology in its astrological form): the antediluvian Hermes-Idrīs who built in Egypt, the Babylonian, and the Egyptian teacher of Asclepius. Ṣāʿid al-Andalusī (1029–1070), in the Ṭabaqāt al-Umam ("Generations of the Nations," 1068), surveys the sciences nation by nation and gives Egypt its Hermetic primacy in the same scheme. Between Baghdad and Córdoba, the bibliographers show the Arabic Hermes as a settled item of learned knowledge by the year 1000 — catalogued, biographised, and argued over, which is to say, transmitted in the only way that finally matters: as books that other books cite.

The Fihrist's architecture deserves a paragraph of its own, because it is this section's discipline in built form. Its ten discourses proceed from scripts and the Qur'an's sciences through grammar, history, poetry, theology, and law to philosophy and the "ancient sciences," concluding — the placement is itself a judgement — with storytellers, fables, magic, and alchemy. The Hermes material sits in the seventh discourse, among the philosophers and the ancient scientists, which is where a Baghdadi bookseller of 987 expected his customers to look for it. The first discourse's catalogue of scripts is §III's home: the scripts of the nations described by a man who sold books in all of them and pretended to no decipherment he did not have. Bibliography, in al-Nadīm's hands, is a map of the knowable with the unknowns left visibly blank — the exact discipline the legendary links of the Unbroken Chain fail, and its own sections observe.

XVI. The Coptic Bridge — Linguistic Continuity in the Caliphate

Coptic is the last stage of the ancient Egyptian language, written in a Greek-derived alphabet. After the Arab conquest (642 CE) it remained the spoken language of most Egyptians for centuries, losing ground to Arabic only gradually, and it was the bridge every later decipherer — medieval or modern — had to cross: Champollion's own key was his knowledge of Coptic.

The bridge was maintained deliberately. From the early second millennium CE, Coptic scholars produced grammars of Coptic written in Arabic and Coptic–Arabic glossaries, the scalae (Arabic sullam, "ladder"): Anba Yuhanna al-Samannudi's ecclesiastical scala in the 13th century, the rhymed scala of Abu Ishaq ibn al-'Assal, and Abu Shakir ibn al-Rahib's of 1263–64, are the landmarks catalogued in the Coptic Encyclopedia. Their purpose was practical — to let Arabic-speaking Christians keep reading their liturgy — but their effect was to keep Egyptian vocabulary glossed and ordered in the language of the ruling culture.

El Daly stresses the same bridge from the other side (§IV): the Arabic decipherment attempts were made "often invoking the help of local Copts," and the scripts were approached through Coptic and Demotic intermediaries still readable in the early medieval period. When Athanasius Kircher published the scalae in his Lingua Aegyptiaca Restituta (1643), the Coptic bridge carried Egyptian into European scholarship on Arabic-made piers.

XVII. The Arabic Magical Tradition — Continuity of Heka

This archive uses Heka for the Egyptian understanding of magic as a working force in the world. The Arabic tradition that inherited Egypt's landscape did not treat that force as extinct: it treated the temples and pyramids as charged objects — talismans (§XI) with guardians — and produced a practical literature for dealing with them. The treasure-hunting manuals (§II) are full of it: directions to buried hoards, the inscriptions and talismanic figures protecting them, and the fumigations and formulas said to neutralise the guardians.

At the learned end stands the Ghayat al-Hakim — the Picatrix (§XVIII) — an 11th-century Arabic compendium of astral and talismanic magic drawing on Hellenistic astrology, Neoplatonic material and Hermetic treatises circulating in Arabic. Its fourth book preserves Sabian and Hermetic lore, including Hermes as builder of talismanic images and of the fabulous talisman-guarded city later Latin readers called Adocentyn. Abu al-Qasim al-Iraqi (§IV) likewise combined copied temple inscriptions with alchemical and talismanic interpretation in one work.

The continuity claimed here is of stance and place, and should not be overstated: the Arabic magicians worked the same monuments, in the same conviction that carved stone and correct timing made power usable, but through Greek, Syriac and Arabic texts — not through an unbroken pharaonic priesthood, for which there is no evidence.

The Picatrix's working instructions repay paraphrase in their own terms, because they show what "talismanic magic" concretely meant in the tradition §XIX traces. A typical operation runs: determine the desired effect's planetary ruler; wait for — or elect — the hour when that planet is dignified, ascending, and free of affliction; prepare the image in the metal, stone, or clay proper to the planet, engraving the figures and characters the book prescribes; fumigate with the planet's suffumigation; and address the planet's spirit by its names in prayer, presenting the image as the spirit's dwelling. The sage, in the book's own prologue, is the man who knows the heavens' influences and can bind them into matter at the elected moment — knowledge first, operation second, and the operation void without the astronomy. Whether such operations were performed as written, in al-Andalus or anywhere, the sources do not let us count; what is documented is the book's own claim about itself, its enormous manuscript diffusion in Arabic and Latin, and its demonstrable readership among the Renaissance magi of §XVIII. Practice is the chain-link the archive cannot certify; the text, the timing doctrine, and the readership are certified completely.

XVIII. The Latin Translation Movement — Bridge to Europe

The Arabic corpus reached Latin Europe mainly through Toledo in the 12th century. Gerard of Cremona (1114–1187) went to Toledo for Ptolemy's Almagest, learned Arabic, and stayed translating — some seventy works are credited to him and his circle, across astronomy, medicine, philosophy and alchemy, including Jabir's Book of Seventy (§IX). What Europe received this way was Greek science as the Arabic tradition had preserved, corrected and extended it.

The Hermetic-magical material travelled the same road by a documented detour: the Arabic Ghayat al-Hakim was put into Castilian for Alfonso X in 1256 and from Castilian into Latin as the Picatrix — the astral-magic handbook that fed Ficino's astral medicine and Agrippa's occult philosophy.

One famous text did not come this way, and the archive keeps the routes separate: the Corpus Hermeticum itself reached Florence in c. 1460 as a Greek manuscript (CH I–XIV), brought from Macedonia by Leonardo da Pistoia for Cosimo de' Medici; Ficino set aside his Plato to translate it, completing the translation in 1463, and it was printed in 1471. The philosophical Hermetica came to the Renaissance from Byzantium in Greek; the alchemical and astrological Hermetica came largely through Arabic. Conflating the two routes erases the Arabic bridge this article exists to record.

XIX. The Picatrix Route in Depth — Authorship, Contents, and Influence

§XVIII recorded the route; this section opens the book. The Ghāyat al-Ḥakīm ("The Aim of the Sage") is a four-book manual of astral and talismanic magic: the first book treats the heavens, the planets, and the theory of celestial influence; the second, the making of talismans and the election of times; the third, the sympathies and antipathies of things and the spirits of the planets; the fourth, the properties of plants, stones, and figures, prayers to the planets, and the lore of the Sabians and other ancient nations (§XIV). Its author boasts, in the prologue tradition, of having pillaged some two hundred and twenty-four books — David Pingree's source-study (1980) traced the amalgam: Hermetic material produced in the Near East in the 9th and 10th centuries, the Cyranides tradition, Nabataean lore, the epistles of the Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ, and Sabian material among them. The Ghāya is a compilation and knows it; its value to this article is that its footnotes, so to speak, are the Arabic Hermetic shelf itself.

Book of the Ghāya Contents in Outline
I The heavens and the planets; the theory of celestial influence on the sublunar world; the wisdom of the ancients introduced.
II Talismans and elections: choosing the hour, the material, and the image by the Moon's and planets' configurations.
III Sympathies and antipathies; the spirits of the planets and their invocation; the virtues of things under each planet.
IV Properties of stones, plants, and figures; planetary prayers; Sabian and ancient-nation lore, including the talisman-guarded city of Adocentyn.

The authorship is a genuine scholarly problem, and it is stated here as one. Ibn Khaldūn attributed the work to the Cordoban astronomer Maslama al-Majrīṭī (d. 1005–1008) — but the book's own introduction dates its completion to 348 AH (c. 959 CE), after the author's Rutbat al-Ḥakīm of 343 AH, decades before al-Majrīṭī could have written it. Pingree accordingly dated the composition to the middle of the 11th century and its author to an anonymous "Pseudo-Majrīṭī"; Maribel Fierro has argued, and Godefroid de Callataÿ and Sébastien Moureau have developed the case, that the author is a different Cordoban — Maslama ibn Qāsim al-Qurṭubī (d. 964) — which would make the book's own 950s dates correct and place it in the world of Andalusian Sufism. The archive takes no side beyond the evidence: the attribution to al-Majrīṭī is chronologically untenable as it stands; the al-Qurṭubī attribution is the leading current alternative; the text is mid-10th to mid-11th century, Andalusian, and composite.

Its European influence ran through the §XVIII translation — Castilian for Alfonso X, completed 1256–1258 on the Latin prologue's dating, then Latin — into the mainstream of Renaissance magic: the Picatrix stands behind Ficino's astral medicine in De vita coelitus comparanda (used with the caution its sources demanded) and behind Agrippa's De occulta philosophia. The Arabic original was unknown to Western scholars until an Arabic manuscript was identified around 1920; the modern editions are Ritter and Plessner's German translation from the Arabic (1962) and Pingree's edition of the Latin version (1986). The route, in sum, is the best-documented single channel by which Arabic Hermetic material — Egyptian in its own self-presentation, Near Eastern and Andalusian in its making — entered Latin Europe.

XX. Egyptian Antiquarianism — ʿAbd al-Laṭīf and al-Maqrīzī

The millennium's antiquarian peak is a physician from Baghdad. ʿAbd al-Laṭīf al-Baghdādī (1162–1231) lived in Egypt through the famine of 1200–1202 and wrote the Kitāb al-Ifāda wa-l-Iʿtibār ("Book of Instruction and Admonition," c. 1204), an account of Egypt — its plants, animals, buildings, food, and famine — built on personal examination: his title itself promises only "things witnessed and events observed in the land of Egypt." His monument chapters are the period's finest. He measured the Giza pyramids; he reasoned that they were tombs, while recording the Hermetic alternative (burial of Agathodaimon or Hermes) as a view in circulation; he described the Sphinx — Abū al-Hawl, "Father of Terror" — with its face still bearing red pigment, "as bright as if freshly painted," and its nose present to his description; and he condemned, in terms a modern conservationist would recognise, the quarrying and destruction of ancient buildings by his contemporaries, recording the dismantling of the smaller Giza pyramids under Saladin's officer Karakūsh for the walls and bridges of Cairo.

Two centuries later the tradition found its compiler. Taqī al-Dīn al-Maqrīzī (1364–1442), in the Khiṭaṭ — his vast topographical history of Egypt and Cairo — devoted a long chapter to the pyramids that is best read as an archive of the whole Arabic tradition this article has surveyed: Saʿīd al-Andalusī's Hermes who foresaw the Flood (§XV), the Surid narrative (§XII), the catalogues of wonders, the talismanic Sphinx (§XI), the Karakūsh demolitions, and a poet's verses canvassing every theory of the pyramids' purpose — tomb, treasure-house, observatory, cosmic diagram — that the tradition had produced. Al-Maqrīzī endorses none of it wholesale; he files it, with attributions. That is Egyptian antiquarianism under Islam at its maturity: not decipherment, which remained out of reach, but a maintained, sourced, self-critical record of the monuments and of everything that had been said about them — the record without which El Daly's millennium (§II) could not have been recovered at all.

The afterlife of these texts closes the article's circle. ʿAbd al-Laṭīf's manuscript was brought to scholarly attention in 1665 by the Oxford orientalist Edward Pococke, and its translations (Latin 1800, French by Silvestre de Sacy 1810, English by Zand and the Videans, 1965; a new English version, A Physician on the Nile, 2021) made it a founding document of Egyptology in Europe. A third Egyptian history of the period, the 13th-century compilation of Murtaḍā ibn al-ʿAfīf, reached English readers in John Davies's translation of 1666 — among the first histories of ancient Egypt available in English, and itself a carrier of the Hermes-Idrīs and Surid traditions into the language of the Royal Society's generation.

XXI. Hermes in Later Islamic Philosophy — Suhrawardī and Ibn ʿArabī

The Arabic Hermes did not remain a figure of the antiquarians. Shihāb al-Dīn Suhrawardī (d. 1191), founder of the Illuminationist (Ishrāqī) school, placed Hermes at the head of one of the two great lines of ancient sages in his Ḥikmat al-Ishrāq ("Philosophy of Illumination"): a chain of wisdom running from Hermes through the Greek and Persian sages to Plato, and a parallel Persian line — both converging, in Suhrawardī's construction, on his own philosophy of light. The construction is Suhrawardī's philosophy of history, presented here as his: what it documents is that by the 12th century "Hermes" named, for a major Islamic philosopher, the fountainhead of an entire tradition of wisdom that included Egypt as its source-country.

Ibn ʿArabī (1165–1240) received the figure through the Idrīs identification of §I: the Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam ("The Bezels of Wisdom") devotes a chapter to Idrīs, the prophet raised to a high place, whose wisdom the chapter expounds. Between Suhrawardī's sage-chain and Ibn ʿArabī's prophet-chapter, the Egyptian Hermes completes his Islamic career: bibliographers' catalogue entry (§XV), astrologers' antediluvian founder (§§XII, XV), alchemists' eponym (§§VII–IX), and finally a station in the metaphysics of the two most influential mystical philosophers of the later Islamic world. None of these figures read a hieroglyph; all of them worked with an Egypt they had received through the transmission this article documents — through ḥkꜣ [Gardiner: V28-D28-G1] [𓎛𓂓𓄿]'s Arabic heirs, the translators, compilers, and travellers of §§II–XX.

XXIII. Ethics of Stewardship — The Responsibility of the Bridge

The study of Islamic Egyptology is an Ethical Duty. It reveals that the "Unbroken Chain" was maintained by scholars who looked across religious boundaries to protect the universal truth.

The responsibility of the researcher is to recognize the Arabic Contribution to the Code. By honoring these medieval bridge-builders, we are protecting the integrity of the initiatory data-stream from modern chauvinism. The ultimate lesson of the Arabic transmission is that the wisdom of Kemet is a Global Heritage, and its "Voice" (Hu) can be heard in every language that seeks the truth. We are the inheritors of this "Arabic Bridge," and our task is to maintain the clarity of the signal for the future.
