I. About This Selection — Reading the Passages Themselves

The companion page on the Funerary Corpus surveys the whole landscape of Egyptian mortuary literature — Pyramid Texts, Coffin Texts, Books of Breathing, and the collection modern scholarship calls the Book of the Dead. This page does the opposite job. It slows down over a small number of individual compositions from that collection and asks, passage by passage: what does this spell actually say, what was it for, who spoke it, at what moment, and what was physically done with it — recited over a corpse, painted on a coffin, carved into a stone scarab, or carried into the tomb on a roll of papyrus?

The collection's own title is the place to start. The Egyptians called these compositions rw nw prt m hrw [𓂋𓅱 𓈖𓅱 𓊪𓂋𓏏 𓅓 𓉔𓂋𓅱] [Gardiner: D21-G43 / N35-G43 / Q3-D21-X1 / G17 / O4-D21-G43] — "utterances of going forth by day." The first word is itself instructive: a single utterance or spell is an rꜣ [𓂋𓄿] [Gardiner: D21-G1], literally "mouth" extended to mean a spoken formula, and rw is its plural. The title is therefore not a book title in our sense. It is a description of a repertoire: spoken formulas whose whole purpose is movement — the dead person's freedom to leave the tomb, travel, and return, by day, in whatever form they choose.

That repertoire was never a fixed book. University College London's Digital Egypt project, whose chapter-by-chapter survey of the corpus is a principal source for this page, puts the scale plainly: the Book of the Dead is "the modern term for about two hundred compositions assigned numbers in modern times for reference purposes." No ancient manuscript contains all of them. Each surviving papyrus is a selection, made for one named owner, in one workshop, at one moment in a tradition that ran from the early New Kingdom (c. 1550 BCE) into the Roman period. The Papyrus of Ani — the Theban scribe Ani, c. 1250 BCE, now British Museum EA10470 — is the most famous selection, not a standard edition.

Two manuscripts anchor this page's quotations, and their differences are a lesson in themselves. The Papyrus of Ani (British Museum EA10470) was made in Thebes in the Nineteenth Dynasty for Ani, a true scribe of the king, reckoner of the divine offerings of all the gods, and overseer of the double granary of the Lord of Tawer (the British Museum's record of the papyrus gives his titles in that form), and for his wife Tutu; published descriptions give its length at roughly twenty-four metres of papyrus, and the British Museum acquired it in 1888, through E. A. Wallis Budge, from the tomb at Thebes where it had been found. It is a deluxe commission — cursive hieroglyphs, full-colour vignettes, its owner portrayed again and again inside his own book. The Papyrus of Nu (British Museum EA10477) is its elder by roughly a century and a half: a mid-Eighteenth-Dynasty roll (c. 1400 BCE) whose owner is known to scholarship simply by his name, Nu. UCL's chapter translations used on this page are taken from Nu's manuscript, while the Faulkner quotations follow the Ani tradition — so that a reader comparing a UCL passage with a Faulkner passage here is comparing two real, distinct, individually owned books, separated by some six generations of Theban scribes. That is the normal condition of this literature, and the citations on this page are written to keep it visible rather than to blend the witnesses into a single synthetic "text."

A Note on Spell Numbering

The numbers used throughout this page ("Spell 125," "Spell 30B") are modern filing numbers, not ancient chapter divisions. In 1842 Richard Lepsius published a Ptolemaic-period manuscript, the Papyrus of Iuefankh (Iufankh) in Turin, and numbered its compositions 1 to 165 in the order they happened to stand in that one roll. Édouard Naville and then E. A. Wallis Budge added further numbers from New Kingdom and Third Intermediate Period manuscripts; Willem Pleyte added still more from short amuletic compositions. The extended sequence now runs to about Spell 190, with lettered subdivisions (30A, 30B, 125A–125D, and so on) where scholars distinguished close variants. UCL's survey is candid that the principles by which ancient compilers sequenced a roll for an individual owner remain only partly understood — for the New Kingdom, Günther Lapp identified dominant groupings in the Papyrus of Nu, and Paul Barguet proposed a four-part reading of the more regular Late Period sequence: proceeding to the burial place (Spells 1–16), regeneration (17–63), transfiguration and judgment (64–129), and the underworld (130–162), with additional formulae appended (163–165). The numbers are a map drawn by modern scholars over ancient material. They are indispensable for finding a passage, and meaningless as a claim about how any Egyptian ever read the roll.

The selection on this page was made on three criteria. First, consequence: these are the spells that did the heaviest work in the funerary system — opening the mouth, staffing the afterlife, defending the heart, conducting the judgment. Second, survival across media: the passages chosen are the ones Egyptians took off the papyrus and put onto scarabs, coffins, tomb walls, and shabti figures, which is the strongest ancient testimony to what mattered most. Third, verifiability: every passage quoted here is quoted from a published translation whose wording was checked against a fetched source during the writing of this page, and every paraphrase is labelled as a paraphrase. The honesty rules behind that third criterion are set out in the next section, because in this corpus they are not a formality.

// HOW THE EGYPTIAN IS WRITTEN ON THIS PAGE //

Egyptian words are given in standard transliteration, followed by the hieroglyphs sign by sign and their Gardiner codes, in the form: ib [𓄣] [Gardiner: F34] — the heart, written with a single sign. Spellings are written phonetically — each sign contributes the sound shown in the decode — and silent determinative signs (classifiers that were written but not pronounced) are generally omitted so that every sign shown can be sounded out. Two conventions need flagging. The reed-leaf sign M17 carries the value i in the sign-list phonetic table; Egyptological convention writes that same consonant j in some words, so the final sign of wšbtj and the first sign of jꜣrw (in sḫt-jꜣrw, the Field of Reeds) are that reed-leaf, read j. And two signs used on this page carry values settled by direct comparison of the archive's V2 sign map against the official Unicode annotations: the door-leaf O31, valued ꜥꜣ, and the balance U38, valued mḫꜣt. Where a spelling in older publications differs, the transliteration here follows Allen's Middle Egyptian conventions.

One more orientation before the passages themselves. Almost every spell in the corpus is written as direct speech in the first person, and the speaker is the deceased — identified, in the standard scribal fiction, as "the Osiris [Name]," the dead person already bearing the name of Osiris, the god whose death and vindication every justified person re-enacts. Where a spell is spoken by a god — Thoth pleading in Spell 1, for instance — the deceased speaks in that god's person. The texts are scripts for a voice that death was supposed to have taken away. Everything in their use follows from that.

II. The Translators — Faulkner, Allen, and the Problem of Translation

No reader meets the Book of the Dead unmediated. Between a modern reader and Ani's papyrus stand the copyists of three thousand years, the decipherment of the scripts in the nineteenth century, and a small number of scholarly translations on which all popular knowledge of the corpus rests. This archive's overhaul of its Egyptology pages was ordered because an earlier generation of pages presented invented "translations" — fluent, plausible, and fabricated. The passages on this page are therefore handled under a strict rule, stated here so the reader can hold the page to it: a passage is placed in quotation marks only when its wording has been seen, in a fetched and identified published source, in the translation named in its citation line. Everything else is presented as paraphrase or summary, and is called that.

Raymond O. Faulkner

Raymond Oliver Faulkner (1894–1982) produced the translation through which the English-speaking world chiefly reads the corpus: The Ancient Egyptian Book of the Dead, published by the British Museum in 1972 and revised under the editorship of Carol Andrews (British Museum Publications; University of Texas Press edition 1985, reissued with Andrews's revisions in 2000). Faulkner was a philologist of the first rank — his Concise Dictionary of Middle Egyptian remains a working tool — and his Book of the Dead is a readable rendering of the Theban tradition, heavily weighted toward the Papyrus of Ani, with hieroglyphic text printed for the major spells. His English is dignified and slightly archaic, a deliberate register choice for liturgical material. Where this page quotes "Faulkner," it quotes that edition, and the citation line says where the wording was verified.

Thomas George Allen

T. G. Allen's The Book of the Dead or Going Forth by Day (Oriental Institute of the University of Chicago, 1974) is the other pillar. Allen's aim was different from Faulkner's: not a beautiful book but a complete scholarly instrument — ideas, texts, and translations assembled from the widest manuscript base then available, including spells Faulkner's Ani-centred volume omits. The UCL Digital Egypt survey used throughout this page takes its titles for Spells 166–190 from Allen 1974, and its section divisions for long compositions such as Spells 17 and 125 likewise follow Allen's edition. Several compositions in the Reader's Table below (Section XVI) are known to most readers, if at all, through Allen. Where a title or division on this page rests on Allen, the page says so; this page makes no direct quotation from Allen's translation, and claims none.

The Modern Correctives: Quirke and the UCL Texts

Stephen Quirke's Going Out in Daylight — prt m hrw: The Ancient Egyptian Book of the Dead: Translation, Sources, Meaning (Golden House Publications, 2013) is the most important recent English treatment, notable precisely for its refusal to smooth the corpus into a single "book": Quirke translates from named manuscripts and keeps their differences visible. The Glencairn Museum's published study of a Book of the Dead mummy bandage quotes Quirke's Spell 125 renderings, and two short quotations on this page are taken from Quirke by that route, cited to his page numbers as given there. Separately, UCL's Digital Egypt site publishes transliteration-and-translation pages for individual chapters (among them Spells 1, 15, 17, 30A, 99, and 125A–C), mostly from the mid-Eighteenth-Dynasty Papyrus of Nu (British Museum EA10477). Those UCL translations are quoted on this page under the label "UCL Digital Egypt translation," with the manuscript named. A passage in the Papyrus of Nu and the "same" passage in the Papyrus of Ani are two witnesses, not one text, and the citations keep them distinct.

// WHY THE WORDING VARIES — A WORKED EXAMPLE //

The heart spell exists in two close forms, 30A and 30B. In the UCL translation of 30A (from an Eighteenth-Dynasty papyrus in Hannover), the dead person calls the heart "my heart of my earthly being." In Faulkner's rendering of the Ani tradition of 30B, the corresponding phrase runs "O my heart of my different ages!" Both are honest translations of real manuscripts; the Egyptian phrase itself — the heart "of my forms/ages of being" — is compressed poetry that no two translators unpack identically, and the manuscripts themselves are not letter-identical. A page that silently blends such variants into one smooth "the text says" is already, in a small way, fabricating. This page quotes each form under its own spell number and its own translator, and lets the seam show.

Two further cautions belong in this section. First, the great Victorian translator E. A. Wallis Budge — whose Ani facsimiles made the papyrus famous and whose name is attached to the British Museum's acquisition of it in 1888 — rendered the corpus in an English so loose, and at points so inventive, that his versions are treated on this page as history of scholarship, not as a source for wording. Readers who meet a Book of the Dead quotation online will very often be meeting Budge, unattributed. Second, spell titles ("Spell for not allowing the heart to be taken away," and so on) are themselves translations of the ancient rubrics — the headings Egyptian scribes wrote, often in red ink, above each composition. The titles quoted in the Reader's Table follow the UCL survey's renderings of the Papyrus of Iuefankh titles for Spells 1–165 and Allen 1974 for the additions. They are the closest thing the corpus has to an ancient table of contents, and they are relentlessly practical: not one of them names a doctrine; every one names a job to be done.

III. Spell 1 — The Funeral Procession and the First Words

Spell 1 is where most Books of the Dead begin, and its position is a stage direction. From the Ramesside Period onward, UCL's chapter survey notes, Spell 1 is regularly written beneath an extended illustration of the funeral itself: the procession to the tomb, the coffin on its sledge, the mourners, and the rites performed before the tomb-chapel. In Late Period and Ptolemaic manuscripts that procession frieze becomes the standard illustration standing over Spells 1–15. Text and picture together form the opening of the roll, and the opening is a funeral.

The ancient title, as UCL renders it from the Papyrus of Iuefankh, begins "Start of the formulae for going out by day…" — the corpus announcing itself. Within the spell, the rubrical frame in the Papyrus of Nu version names the occasion directly: the day of burial, qrs [𓈎𓂋𓋴] [Gardiner: N29-D21-S29]. The composition is older than the Book of the Dead itself: UCL notes that much of it already exists in the late Middle Kingdom, written on coffin walls as Coffin Text 314. What the New Kingdom did was move it to the head of the papyrus roll and put the funeral procession over it.

Who Speaks, and What the Speech Does

Unusually, the main speaker of Spell 1 is not the deceased but Thoth — ḏḥwty [𓆓𓎛𓅱𓏏𓏭] [Gardiner: I10-V28-G43-X1-Z4] — speaking for the deceased, as he spoke for Osiris. The argument of the spell is a precedent: Thoth rehearses everything he did at the original justification of Osiris — fighting for him, presenting him before the divine tribunal, standing with Horus on the days of wrapping, mourning, and festival — and the recitation transfers that advocacy to this dead person, here, at this burial. The deceased is folded into the Osiris story by having its lawyer repeat the case.

// SPELL 1 — THE OPENING ADDRESS (QUOTATION) //
"Day of burial of Osiris; going in after going out by N. O Bull of the West, says Thoth, King of Eternity."

— Book of the Dead, Spell 1, opening lines (N = the name of the deceased, here Nu). Translation: UCL Digital Egypt chapter 1 page, from the Papyrus of Nu.

// SPELL 1 — THE ADVOCATE'S CLAIM (QUOTATION) //
"I am Thoth who justifies Horus against his enemies, on that day of judgement in the Great Temple of the Official which is in Iunu."

— Book of the Dead, Spell 1. Translation: UCL Digital Egypt chapter 1 page, from the Papyrus of Nu (British Museum EA10477, mid-Eighteenth Dynasty, c. 1400 BCE). Iunu is Heliopolis.

The spell then turns from Thoth's credentials to direct petition. The gods who admit souls into the house of Osiris are asked to admit this one on identical terms — a passage whose fourfold parallelism is quoted here because it shows the corpus's characteristic method: the dead person's faculties are restored by being equated, one by one, with the faculties of the transfigured dead already inside.

// SPELL 1 — ADMIT MY SOUL WITH YOU (QUOTATION) //
"Bring my soul close with you to the house of Osiris, so that it may hear as you hear, so that it may see as you see, so that it may stand as you stand, so that it may sit as you sit."

— Book of the Dead, Spell 1. Translation: UCL Digital Egypt chapter 1 page, from the Papyrus of Nu.

Summary of the remainder (paraphrase, after the UCL translation): the speaker asks the doorkeepers of Osiris's house to open the ways so the soul may "go in in fury, and emerge in peace," neither blocked nor turned back; asks that bread and beer be given to the soul at every occasion; and closes with a verdict already pronounced — the speaker has proceeded and no fault has been found, the scales standing empty of any charge. The spell ends, in other words, by reciting the result of the judgment before the judgment scene has been reached. That is not narrative confusion. It is the corpus's operating principle that a correctly spoken formula does not describe an outcome but performs it, a principle examined directly in Section XV.

When and By Whom It Was Spoken

Procedurally, Spell 1 belongs to the funeral day itself. Its natural speaker in the living world was the lector priest, the ḫry-ḥbt [𓐍𓂋𓏭 𓎛𓃀𓏏] [Gardiner: Aa1-D21-Z4 / V28-D58-X1] — literally "he who is over the festival-book," the ritualist who carried the papyrus roll and read the service from it while the procession moved. The spell's own self-description supports the assignment: in the UCL translation the speaker lists cultic offices among Thoth's identifications — "I am the pure-priest in Djedu… I am the sm [𓋴𓅓] [Gardiner: S29-G17] priest in his task" — the same grades of priest who conducted actual funerals. The roll buried with the dead then preserved the spoken service in permanent form: what the lector said once, at the procession, the papyrus went on saying in the tomb. On the division of labour between the spoken funeral and the buried text, see Ritual Practice and Opening of the Mouth, the rite toward which the procession was travelling.

IV. Spell 6 — The Shabti Spell: "Here Am I"

Spell 6 is the shortest passage on this page and, by count of surviving copies, very likely the most frequently inscribed text in all of Egyptian funerary literature — because it was not confined to papyri. Its ancient title in the UCL survey is "Formula for making ushabti figures do work in the underworld," and its destination was the body of a shabti figure itself: the mummiform statuette, equipped with hoes and seed-baskets, that was placed in the tomb to perform agricultural labour in the next world in its owner's place. The spell is the figure's activation script and its letter of appointment in one.

The word itself explains the mechanism. The figure's name, wšbtj [𓅱𓈙𓃀𓏏𓇋] [Gardiner: G43-N37-D58-X1-M17], is built on the verb wšb [𓅱𓈙𓃀] [Gardiner: G43-N37-D58], "to answer." The shabti is "the Answerer": the being who, when the dead person is summoned to the corvée labour of the afterlife — ploughing, irrigating, and the notorious task of carrying sand from east to west — answers the summons in the owner's stead. Spell 6 is a rewording, as the World History Encyclopedia's survey notes, of Coffin Text Spell 472; the Book of the Dead version standardised it for the statuettes.

// SPELL 6 — THE SHABTI FORMULA (QUOTATION) //
"…you shall detail yourself for me on every occasion of making arable the fields, of flooding the banks or of conveying sand from east to west; 'Here am I,' you shall say."

— Book of the Dead, Spell 6. Translation: Raymond O. Faulkner, in Carol Andrews and Raymond O. Faulkner, The Ancient Egyptian Book of the Dead (University of Texas Press, 2000), p. 36, as quoted in a published study of the shabtis of Meretites; the same Faulkner wording is reproduced by the World History Encyclopedia, "Shabti Dolls: The Workforce in the Afterlife."

The opening of the spell (paraphrase): the figure is addressed directly — "O shabti, allotted to me" — and the arrangement is set out as a standing instruction: if the owner is summoned or detailed for any work that must be done in the realm of the dead, and obstacles are put in the figure's way as they would be for a man at his duties, the shabti is to put itself forward for the owner on every occasion. The spell is drafted like a legal instrument. It names the parties, anticipates obstruction, specifies the tasks, and scripts the exact words of acceptance.

How the Spell Was Deployed

The inscription was the deployment. On better figures the full Spell 6 runs down the front of the body in horizontal registers — a published analysis of the Late Period faience shabtis of a woman named Meretites counts nine to ten registers of the spell on each of her fourteen large figures, while her remaining 291 smaller shabtis carry only a short ownership text. That two-tier pattern is typical of large sets: a fully spelled cadre whose text does the legal work, and a workforce identified by name alone. Sets grew to industrial scale — ideally 365 workers, one for each day, plus overseers — and every figure in the tomb was, in effect, a printed copy of the owner's standing orders. The dead did not need to recite Spell 6 in the afterlife for it to operate; they had already published it, hundreds of times, on the workers themselves. For the figures' materials, overseers, and boxes, see the archive's Shabtis page.

Note what the spell assumes about the afterlife it serves. The realm of the dead in Spell 6 is not a paradise of rest but an administered agricultural estate — the Field of Reeds, sḫt-jꜣrw [𓇏 𓇋𓄿𓂋𓅱] [Gardiner: M20 / M17-G1-D21-G43] — with fields to be made arable, banks to be flooded, and quotas of sand to be shifted, under the same corvée logic as Pharaonic Egypt. Spell 148 (Section XI) arranges the estate's catering; Spell 6 arranges its labour supply. The spells are bureaucratic because the world they build is bureaucratic, and their magic is the magic of correct paperwork, filed in advance, in triplicate, in stone and faience.

V. Spell 15 — Hymns to Ra at Rising and Setting

Spell 15 is not one composition but a shelf of them: the sun-hymns. In the Late Period sequence, UCL's survey explains, the fifteenth "chapter" is a group of hymns to the sun-god followed by a full-height illustration of the worship of the sun — Lepsius numbered the hymns 15 and the vignette 16 — and the term Spell 15 has since been applied to sun-hymns in earlier manuscripts as well. Ramesside and Third Intermediate Period papyri contain numerous different hymns under this number, and the hymn frequently stands at one end of the scroll, where a roll is opened or closed with the sunrise.

These hymns had a life outside the tomb that the other spells on this page largely did not. UCL's chapter 15 page traces the same hymns into the solar cult of the tomb-chapel: hymns to the sun-god placed at the doorway on the east side of Theban, Memphite, and Amarna chapels, with the tomb-owner carved beside them, arms raised in adoration facing the dawn; stelophorous statues — the owner kneeling behind a stela inscribed with a sun-hymn, hands raised over it — set above chapel doors; and pyramidia crowning chapel roofs, inscribed toward the rising and setting sun. Jan Assmann's studies of the New Kingdom sun-hymn repertoire, cited by UCL, established the standard versions of the genre. The hymn was architecture, furniture, and papyrus text at once, and its daily performance was built into the chapel's orientation: the building itself faced the moment the words described.

// SPELL 15 — HYMN AT THE RISING (QUOTATION) //
"Hail Ra in your rising, Atum Horakhty, I worship you, your beauty in my eyes, your light unfurling on my breast. You go, you rest in the evening boat, your heart is elated in the morning boat, you travel the sky in peace."

— Book of the Dead, Spell 15 (sun-hymn, Assmann's Standard Hymn C). Translation: UCL Digital Egypt chapter 15 page. Ra is rꜥ [𓇳] [Gardiner: N5].

The two boats named in the hymn are the sun's daily equipment: the morning boat, mꜥnḏt [𓅓𓂝𓈖𓆓𓏏] [Gardiner: G17-D36-N35-I10-X1], in which the god travels elated after his rebirth, and the evening boat, msktt [𓅓𓋴𓎡𓏏𓏏] [Gardiner: G17-S29-V31-X1-X1], in which he descends toward the underworld — the vessel whose nightly voyage the Netherworld Books map hour by hour, and whose journey is traced on this archive's Solar Barque page. The hymn's verb for what the worshipper does is dwꜣ [𓂧𓅱𓄿] [Gardiner: D46-G43-G1], "to adore, to worship at dawn" — a word whose own writing can use the star sign (N14, itself valued dwꜣ), because dawn adoration and the morning star belong to the same act.

A further procedural note belongs to this spell, because it governed the living as much as the dead. The sun-hymn was the one part of the funerary repertoire with a daily, public performance slot: the tomb-chapel's east doorway faced the sunrise the hymn salutes, the stelophorous statue held the text up to the dawn permanently, and the festival calendar returned to the hymns at the solar stations of the year. A Book of the Dead owner's family, visiting the chapel with offerings, re-entered the book at its most familiar page — the spell that everyone present had, in some form, already heard at sunrise. The boundary between the corpus for the dead and the liturgy of the living runs thinnest exactly here, at Spell 15.

Rising and Setting: The Diptych

As UCL summarises the quoted hymn's continuation (paraphrase of the translation published there): all the god's enemies are felled; the untiring stars rejoice for him and the undying stars worship him; he rests in the western horizon beautiful as Ra every day, "alive and stable as my lord." The hymn is a diptych in a single text — it salutes the rising and already contains the setting, because the deceased who recites it claims both ends of the solar circuit as their own itinerary. To go forth by day is to rise with Ra; to survive the night is to travel in his boat and rise again. Spell 15 is therefore not decorative praise attached to the front of the roll. It is the timetable of the entire enterprise, stated as worship. The sun-hymns also frame judgment: in the UCL translation of Spell 125C quoted in Section IX, the assessor gods are described as those "who live on truth, who consume truth before Horus who is in his sun-disk" — the tribunal sits, quite literally, in the light of the hymn's addressee.

VI. Spell 17 — The Great Theological Catechism

Spell 17 is the intellectual summit of the corpus, and the strangest. UCL's chapter page describes it without exaggeration as one of the longest individual compositions in the Book of the Dead and "one of the most remarkable examples of writing from ancient Egypt, with its unparalleled structure of internal commentary." The spell identifies the deceased with the creator-god in a rolling first-person litany — and then, every few lines, it interrupts itself to ask what its own words mean, and answers. The text carries its own ancient commentary inside itself, gloss upon gloss, frequently offering more than one explanation for a single phrase. It is a scripture that behaves like a school text: the recitation and the lesson are the same event.

The composition is old. UCL notes that it already exists in the Middle Kingdom as Coffin Text 335, and that in both corpora it is one of the most frequently found formulae of all. From the Ramesside Period to the Ptolemaic, it was written beneath a series of vignettes illustrating its themes — in the Papyrus of Ani the Spell 17 vignettes (the two lions of the horizon, the deceased at the senet board, the gods of the judgment's entourage) are among the most reproduced images in Egyptology.

// SPELL 17 — THE OPENING IDENTIFICATION (QUOTATION) //
"I am Ra in his rising, first in ruling what he made."

— Book of the Dead, Spell 17, opening section (sectioning after T. G. Allen, 1974). Translation: UCL Digital Egypt chapter 17 page.

// SPELL 17 — YESTERDAY AND TOMORROW, WITH ITS ANCIENT GLOSS (QUOTATION) //
"Mine is yesterday, I know tomorrow. What does it mean? Yesterday is Osiris, Tomorrow is Ra."

— Book of the Dead, Spell 17, section 3 in the section numbering of T. G. Allen (1974). Translation: UCL Digital Egypt chapter 17 page (version compiled from Naville's edition of the New Kingdom and Third Intermediate Period papyri).

Read the mechanics of that short exchange carefully, because they are the spell in miniature. A claim is made in the first person — the deceased owns yesterday and knows tomorrow, a creator's prerogative over time. The text then cross-examines itself: what does it mean? And the gloss does not explain so much as re-describe, in UCL's phrase, in religious terms: yesterday is Osiris, tomorrow is Ra. Past time is the dead-and-risen god; future time is the sun in his circuit. The deceased who has merged with both is no longer located at a point in time but straddles its whole span — which is precisely the state the whole corpus exists to manufacture. The gloss continues, in the same UCL rendering (paraphrase): tomorrow is further identified with the day on which the rebels against the Lord of All were slaughtered and Horus was appointed ruler; an alternative gloss offered by the text itself makes the day the day of Osiris's burial, fixed by his father Ra. The alternatives are not scribal indecision. Egyptian theological writing treats multiple true identifications as cumulative, and Spell 17's glosses are the earliest large-scale Egyptian example of the method.

What the Catechism Was For

The ancient title given in the UCL survey runs "Formulae for elevation and transfiguration, for going out from the necropolis, for being in the following of Osiris, and being content with the food of Wennefer." Transfiguration — becoming an ꜣḫ [𓄿𓐍] [Gardiner: G1-Aa1], an effective, luminous spirit — is the spell's stated product, and its method is knowledge performed aloud. The long litany that the glosses punctuate is a sequence of identifications: the speaker is the creator in his rising, is the great god self-begotten, is equipped with the two plumes that the gloss identifies with Isis and Nephthys, knows the secret of Osiris, and cannot be overcome. To recite Spell 17 correctly was to demonstrate, proposition by proposition and gloss by gloss, that the speaker possessed the theological map of the cosmos and their own place in it. In a corpus where knowing a name or a fact is repeatedly the exact difference between passage and destruction (Section XIV), Spell 17 is the comprehensive examination — set, marked, and annotated within the text itself.

Its antiquity mattered to its users. A spell already venerable when the New Kingdom scribes copied it from Middle Kingdom coffins carried authority the way a citation carries authority: the glosses present themselves as received interpretation, older than any living priest. Modern scholarship reads the same structure historically — a core litany accreting commentary across centuries of school transmission, probably in the scribal environment of the House of Life. Both readings can be held at once, and the spell seems designed for exactly that double reading: it is at once a performative identification with the creator and a curriculum for learning what that identification means.

𓄿 𓐍 𓂋 𓅱

VII. Spells 21–26 — Restoring Mouth, Memory, and Heart

Spells 21 to 26 form the corpus's repair kit: a tight cluster of short formulae whose shared work is to give back to the dead person, one by one, the faculties that death and embalmment had suspended. Their titles in the UCL survey of the Papyrus of Iuefankh sequence are worth reading as a list, because the list is a diagnosis — it states exactly what a newly dead person was understood to lack:

// THE RESTORATION CLUSTER — TITLES AFTER THE UCL SURVEY //

Spell 21 — Formula for giving the mouth of a man to him in the underworld.

Spell 22 — Another formula for giving the mouth of a man to him in the underworld.

Spell 23 — Formula for opening the mouth of a man in the underworld.

Spell 24 — Formula for fetching the words of power of a man for him in the underworld.

Spell 25 — For giving the memory of a man to him in the underworld.

Spell 26 — Formula for giving the heart of a man to him in the underworld.

// THE SEQUEL — SPELLS 27–30, TITLES AFTER THE UCL SURVEY //

Spell 27 — Formula for preventing the heart of a man being taken away from him in the underworld.

Spell 28 — Formula for preventing the chest of a man being taken away from him in the underworld.

Spell 29 — Formula for preventing the heart of a man being removed from him in the underworld. (29B is separately a formula for a heart-amulet of seheret-stone.)

Spell 30 — Formula for preventing the heart of a man being kept away from him in the underworld — the base form of which 30A and 30B, the heart-scarab texts of Section VIII, are the standing variants.

The sequence is a clinical order of operations. First the mouth (21–23): without it the dead cannot speak, and a corpus made of spoken formulae is useless to a mute owner. Spells 21–23 are the textual counterpart of the physical Opening of the Mouth rite performed on the mummy and on statues with the adze and the psš-kf blade; the rite restored the senses in the embalming tent, the spells restore them in the Duat, and the two were understood as one operation conducted in two places. Then words of power (24): the spell "fetches" the owner's heka — their operative magical capacity — which death was liable to scatter, and note the direction of travel: the power is not created but retrieved, fetched back to its owner like property from storage. Then memory (25): the dead must remember who they are, their name, their life, and the contents of the very book they are carrying; a related formula, Spell 90 in the UCL list, exists "for granting memory always to a man." Then the heart (26): the ib [𓄣] [Gardiner: F34], seat of thought, memory, and moral record, is given back — and the cluster is immediately followed by its anxious sequel, Spells 27–30, whose titles are all variations on preventing that same heart from being taken away, kept away, or removed. The corpus restores the heart with one hand and chains it down with the other; Section VIII explains why.

Procedure: Small Spells, Specific Moments

These are working spells, sized for use. Their brevity distinguishes them from the great set-pieces (17, 125, 175): each does one thing, addresses one deficiency, and could be recited over the mummy at the relevant stage of preparation, inscribed on the equipment that embodied the faculty — mouth spells in the orbit of the burial chamber and the coffin, the heart spell in the orbit of the chest — and carried in the papyrus as the owner's own copy to speak in the underworld. The cluster also teaches the corpus's anatomy of the person. A human being, in this system, is an assembly of separable components — the composite soul of kꜣ [𓎡𓄿] [Gardiner: V31-G1], bꜣ [𓃀𓄿] [Gardiner: D58-G1], name (rn [𓂋𓈖] [Gardiner: D21-N35]), shadow, heart, and body — each of which can be lost, stolen, damaged, or restored independently. Spells 21–26 are the maintenance manual for that assembly at its most vulnerable moment.

Spell 30A's opening, quoted in the next section, belongs to this same restoration sequence in spirit even though it stands a few numbers later: the heart addressed there is called by both of its Egyptian names in a single breath — ib [𓄣] [Gardiner: F34], the inner heart of thought and feeling, and ḥꜣty [𓎛𓄿𓏏𓏭] [Gardiner: V28-G1-X1-Z4], the physical fore-heart, the organ in the chest. Egyptian psychology did not treat these as a metaphor and its referent. They are two aspects of one witness — and it is the witness, precisely, that the next spell exists to manage.

VIII. Spell 30B — The Heart Scarab Inscription

Spell 30B is the one passage in the corpus whose primary home was not a papyrus at all. Its ancient title, in the UCL survey, is "Formula for preventing the heart of a man from opposing him in the underworld," and its standard carrier was an object: the heart scarab, a large scarab carved from hard green or black stone, inscribed with the spell on its flat underside and laid over the heart of the mummy. The Metropolitan Museum of Art's record for the heart scarab of the foreign queen Manhata (c. 1479–1425 BCE) states the arrangement in one sentence: among the spells of the Book of the Dead, Spell 30B "calls upon the heart not to bear witness against the deceased during judgment in the afterlife," and the spell "was usually inscribed on a large scarab made of green stone that was placed on the mummy's chest." UCL's page on the sister-text 30A adds the chronology: the earliest heart scarabs inscribed with this chapter date to the mid-Thirteenth Dynasty — centuries before the New Kingdom papyri — and the practice of laying a great scarab over the heart continued at least into the third century BCE.

// SPELL 30B — THE HEART SCARAB TEXT (QUOTATION) //
"O my heart which I had from my mother! O my heart which I had from my mother! O my heart of my different ages! Do not stand up as a witness against me, do not be opposed to me in the tribunal, do not be hostile to me in the presence of the keeper of the balance, for you are my ka which was in my body, the protector who made my members hale."

— Book of the Dead, Spell 30B. Translation: Raymond O. Faulkner, The Ancient Egyptian Book of the Dead, as published for the Papyrus of Ani (British Museum EA10470); wording verified in the British Museum Papyrus of Ani reproduction text. The kꜣ named in the spell is written [𓎡𓄿] [Gardiner: V31-G1].

The logic of the spell is juridical, and slightly desperate. At the judgment (Section IX), the heart will be weighed — but the heart is also the archive of the life, the one witness who was present for everything and cannot be cross-examined away. Spell 30B is an address to that witness before the hearing: part plea, part instruction, part reminder of shared interest. You are mine, it says; you were in my body; you made my members sound; the happy place is where we are going. The Faulkner text of the Ani version continues (paraphrase): the heart is begged not to make its owner's name stink before the tribunal that fashions men, and not to tell lies about the deceased in the presence of the god. The spell does not ask the heart to lie for the deceased. It asks it not to volunteer.

// SPELL 30A — THE CLOSE VARIANT (QUOTATION) //
"My heart of my mother, my heart of my mother, my heart of my earthly being. Do not stand against me as witness beside the lords of the ritual. Do not say against me, he did do it, about my actions."

— Book of the Dead, Spell 30A. Translation: UCL Digital Egypt chapter 30A page, from an Eighteenth-Dynasty papyrus in Hannover (Kestner Museum 1970.37). UCL notes 30A is the less common form; 30B is the heart-scarab standard.

The Object as Procedure

Everything about the heart scarab is specified like a prescription. The stone is green — the colour of wꜣḏ [𓅱𓄿𓆓] [Gardiner: G43-G1-I10], freshness and regeneration — and hard, so the text cannot wear away. The Metropolitan Museum's second heart-scarab record, for a woman named Gautsoshen (Third Intermediate Period, c. 1080–945 BCE), reports the find-spot exactly: the scarab was found "in the center of Gautsoshen's chest, within her mummy wrappings," its underside inscribed with Spell 30B — and, a detail worth dwelling on, her name had been scratched crudely over an erasure at the head of the inscription. The scarab, like her Book of the Dead papyrus, was reused: a stock object, manufactured in advance with the spell already carved, personalised at need. The workshops of the dead ran on the same economy as the workshops of the living. Even the most intimate address in the corpus — a person pleading with their own heart — could be bought ready-made, with a blank left for the name.

The scarab beetle itself is the spell's second text. The creature is ḫprr [𓐍𓊪𓂋𓂋] [Gardiner: Aa1-Q3-D21-D21], from ḫpr [𓐍𓊪𓂋] [Gardiner: Aa1-Q3-D21], "to come into being" — the root of the dawn-god Khepri, who rolls the sun into existence each morning. To lay the heart spell on a scarab was to set the plea for silence inside an emblem of self-creation: the organ that might condemn the dead is placed under the sign of the being who makes himself anew. The object argues the case before a word is read.

IX. Spell 125 — The Judgment and the Negative Confession

Spell 125 is the corpus's courtroom, and its longest sustained performance. The UCL survey gives its ancient heading as "The book of entering the broad hall of the Two Goddesses Right" — the hall of the two mꜣꜥt [𓅓𓄿𓂝𓏏] [Gardiner: G17-G1-D36-X1] goddesses, Maat doubled, truth facing truth — and divides the spell into the working parts modern scholarship uses: 125A, the address and confession before Osiris; 125B, the confession before the forty-two assessor gods; 125C, a further declaration in the hall; and 125D, the full-height vignette of the judgment itself. The deceased enters, announces that they know the gods present and their names, declares a life lived on Ma'at, recites the great list of things not done, and submits to the weighing described in Section X.

The address to the forty-two assessors is the structural heart of 125B. Each assessor is greeted by name and by the place they come from, and to each the deceased denies one specific offence. The form matters as much as the content: the speaker must know who each judge is — name and origin — before the denial is even heard, which makes 125B the judgment's own password-examination (Section XIV) folded inside its confession. Faulkner's translation of the Ani tradition opens the sequence like this:

// SPELL 125B — THE FIRST ASSESSORS (QUOTATION) //
"O, Far-strider who came forth from Heliopolis, I have done no falsehood. O, Fire-embracer who came forth from Kheraha, I have not robbed. O, Nosey who came forth from Hermopolis, I have not been rapacious."

— Book of the Dead, Spell 125. Translation: Raymond O. Faulkner (1972), p. 31, as quoted in a published scholarly discussion of the Weighing of the Heart rite.

The denials that follow range across the whole of Egyptian moral life — violence, theft, fraud in measure and in cult, offences against gods, neighbours, the dead, and the king's order. In Stephen Quirke's translation, a run of them keeps the characteristic drum-beat of short, absolute negatives:

// SPELL 125 — FROM THE NEGATIVE CONFESSION (QUOTATION) //
"I have not caused ailment; I have not caused hunger; I have not caused tears; I have not killed."

— Book of the Dead, Spell 125. Translation: Stephen Quirke, Going out in daylight — prt m hrw (2013), p. 271, as quoted in the Glencairn Museum's study of a Book of the Dead mummy bandage.

And the confession is not only negative. In 125A/C the deceased states the positive record — the acts of feeding, clothing, and ferrying that Egyptian ethics counted as Ma'at in action — in a passage quoted by the same Glencairn study from Quirke's translation (p. 273), and given in near-identical words in the UCL translation of 125C from the Papyrus of Nu:

// SPELL 125 — THE POSITIVE DECLARATION (QUOTATION) //
"I have given bread to the hungry, beer to the thirsty, clothes to naked, a ferry to the boatless."

— Book of the Dead, Spell 125. Translation: Stephen Quirke (2013), p. 273, as quoted by the Glencairn Museum. The preceding lines in Quirke's rendering run: "See I am come before you, there being no evil of mine, no crime of mine, no wrong of mine… I live on what is right, I consume what is Right."

// SPELL 125C — THE PLEA AGAINST THE DEVOURER IN THE HALL (QUOTATION) //
"Rescue me from Bebon who lives on the entrails of the great, this day of the great count."

— Book of the Dead, Spell 125C, section 2 (sections after T. G. Allen, 1974). Translation: UCL Digital Egypt chapter 125C page, from the Papyrus of Nu. The address that precedes it hails the assessors as gods "in whose bodies there is no falsehood, who live on truth, who consume truth before Horus who is in his sun-disk."

What Kind of Speech Act Is a Negative Confession?

It is tempting to read the forty-two denials as a conscience exam — a list the deceased checks against their memory. The Egyptians who wrote the rubrics seem to have intended something more operative. The confession is recited in the hall, before the named assessors, as the formal plea that opens the weighing; its truth is not asserted to be self-evident but is tested by the scales in the same scene. The UCL translation of 125C has the deceased open with the claim that does all the work: "Hail to you, those gods who are in this broad hall of the Two Goddesses of Right. I know you, I know your names" — knowledge first, innocence second, verdict third. And the declaration insists on its own ground: the assessors are gods "in whose bodies there is no grg [𓎼𓂋𓎼] [Gardiner: W11-D21-W11]" — no falsehood — "who live on truth, who consume truth." A defendant who has, in fact, lived against Ma'at gains nothing by the recitation that the scales will not immediately expose. The confession is a key cut for one lock, and the lock is real.

For the text's ethics — how the forty-two denials map the moral economy of a New Kingdom life, from market fraud to cultic neglect — the weighing has its own dedicated treatment on the archive's Weighing of the Heart page, and the two-truths theology on Ma'at and Isfet. What belongs here is the performance note: the confession was spoken, by the deceased in the first person, name by name, in a hall whose personnel the speech itself has just proved the speaker can identify. It is the longest password in the book, and it happens to be a life.

X. Spell 125 Continued — The Weighing Scene and Its Dramatis Personae

Spell 125D — the element Lepsius's numbering treats as part of the spell and every museum treats as the icon of the entire corpus — is the full-height vignette of the judgment: the balance, the heart, the feather, and the court in attendance. In the Papyrus of Ani the scene spreads across what is now British Museum frame EA10470,3, and its composition is so stable across manuscripts that the figures can be read like a cast list. Each person present has a procedural function; nobody in the scene is decoration.

ANUBIS — THE CONDUCTING OFFICER

Anubis — inpw [𓇋𓈖𓊪𓅱] [Gardiner: M17-N35-Q3-G43] — leads the deceased into the hall and kneels at the balance, adjusting its plummet. He is the embalmer-god who prepared the body now presenting its heart for inspection: the technician of the corpse becomes the technician of the verdict.

THE BALANCE AND THE FEATHER

The instrument is the mḫꜣt [𓍝] [Gardiner: U38], the balance itself. Against the heart is set not a weight but the feather of Ma'at — šwt [𓆄] [Gardiner: H6] — the lightest standard in the Egyptian world, so that the test is not whether the heart is good enough but whether it is exactly, unburdenedly true.

THOTH — THE RECORDER

ḏḥwty [𓆓𓎛𓅱𓏏𓏭] [Gardiner: I10-V28-G43-X1-Z4] stands with palette and pen and announces the result to the Ennead. The god who spoke as advocate in Spell 1 here records the outcome — the same office, at the other end of the case.

AMMIT — THE EXECUTIONER IN WAITING

The composite Devourer — crocodile, lion, hippopotamus — crouches by the balance for the heart that fails. She has no cult and no other scene: she exists only as the consequence, the second death given a body and set where the defendant can see her throughout the proceedings.

THE FORTY-TWO ASSESSORS

The bench addressed in 125B, each named, each from a named place — traditionally correlated with the forty-two nomes of Egypt, so that the whole land, district by district, sits in judgment on a life lived inside it.

OSIRIS — THE PRESIDING JUDGE

Enthroned at the scene's end, Osiris receives the justified. The verdict word is mꜣꜥ-ḫrw [𓅓𓄿𓂝 𓐍𓂋𓅱] [Gardiner: G17-G1-D36 / Aa1-D21-G43] — "true of voice," justified — the epithet written after the deceased's name in every text on this page, including the rubrics of the spells themselves.

The weighing's outcome is announced, in the vignettes, by Thoth, and its social consequence is admission: Horus — ḥr [𓎛𓂋] [Gardiner: V28-D21] — takes the justified by the hand and presents them to Osiris, as in the Papyrus of Hunefer's celebrated version of the scene. What has been decided is less a sentence than a status. The justified dead are mꜣꜥ-ḫrw, and the status is retroactive over the whole book: the papyrus the deceased carried has addressed them as "the Osiris [Name], true of voice" from its first spell. The book speaks the verdict in advance everywhere, and Spell 125 is where the cosmos is depicted agreeing. For the full iconographic and theological analysis — the Hall of Two Truths, the assessors' register, the Field of Reeds that lies beyond — see The Weighing of the Heart.

XI. Spell 148 — Provision for the Justified

Judgment passed, the justified dead still have to eat — forever. Spell 148 is the corpus's catering contract, and it is built, like Spell 125B, entirely out of names. Faulkner's chapter heading, as the spell lists give it, is "for making provision for a spirit in the realm of the dead," and the UCL survey's rendering of its ancient title is more ceremonial: a book "for making the transfigured spirit excellent on the heart of Ra, causing him to have power before Atum, magnifying him before the foremost of the west [Osiris], enabling him to go out before the Ennead," appointed for performance on named festival days — the day of the full moon, the sixth day, the fifteenth day. Its vignette is a full-height fixture of the great papyri: the deceased in adoration before a falcon-headed mummiform Osiris, and behind the god the spell's personnel — seven cows, their bull, and four steering-oars, each captioned with a name.

The deceased's claim, as the Totenbuch-based spell summaries paraphrase Faulkner's text, is justified by knowledge: the spell in its full form grounds the right to provisions in knowing the names of the seven cows and their bull and of the four steering-oars of the sky. The names of the cattle, as translated in Faulkner's edition and reproduced in the published spell lists, run:

// SPELL 148 — THE SEVEN COWS AND THEIR BULL (NAMES AS TRANSLATED) //

Mansion of Kas, Mistress of All · Silent One who dwells in her place · She of Chemmis whom the god ennobled · The Much Beloved, red of hair · She who protects in life, the particoloured · She whose name has power in her craft · Storm in the sky which wafts the god aloft · and the Bull, husband of the cows.

Names as given in the translation of Spell 148 in Faulkner's edition (British Museum Press, 1985, p. 137, per the Totenbuch-based spell list); the four steering-oars are addressed in the spell as the good steering-oars of the northern, eastern, southern, and western sky. Presented here as a translated name-list, not as a continuous quotation.

Summary of the spell's action (paraphrase, after Faulkner as summarised in the Totenbuch-based lists): the deceased hails each cow, each oar, and the fathers and mothers of the gods by name, and asks them for bread and beer, offerings and provisions, life, prosperity, health, and long duration — and for protection from every harmful thing that might be said or done against the spirit by men, gods, spirits, or the dead, by day or by night, in the monthly festival, the half-monthly festival, or the year. The cows are often associated with the Seven Hathors, the fate-goddesses of birth, though their names and associations vary between manuscripts — see Hathor for the goddess whose herd this is.

The Rubric: A Painted Board and an Offering

Spell 148's rubric is one of the most fully procedural in the corpus, and it is summarised here (paraphrase, after the rubric as translated in Faulkner's Ani edition) because it shows the corpus operating as a ritual script rather than a reading text. The spell is to be spoken by a man when Ra shows himself, over these gods depicted in paint on a writing-board; offerings of bread, beer, meat, poultry, and incense are to be set before the painted figures; and the invocation-offering for the spirit is made in the presence of Ra. The benefit is then stated in the rubric's contractual style: the soul will have provision in the God's Domain, Ra will be his helmsman and protection, and none of his enemies will know him in the God's Domain, in the sky, on earth, or in any place where he walks. The Totenbuch-based summary adds the rubric's framing conditions: the composition is presented as secret knowledge, and its recitation prescribes ritual purity. A picture, an offering, a sunrise, a pure speaker, and a memorised herd of names: that is the complete machine, and not one part of it is metaphorical.

The spell's afterlife on stone confirms its standing. Spell 148's scene — deceased adoring, cows and bull in registers, the four falcon-headed rudders — was carved in temples and on tomb equipment (it appears, for instance, on the false-door stela of Senenmut), one of a small group of Book of the Dead compositions promoted out of the papyrus into permanent public media. A provision spell that works by being seen with its offerings in front of it travels naturally to walls: the wall, like the writing-board of the rubric, holds the picture still so the offering can be made forever.

XII. Spell 175 — A Dialogue with Atum on the End of the World

Spell 175 is the corpus's most quietly astonishing text, and the one that least fits the "guidebook for the dead" stereotype. Its modern title in the UCL additions list is "Formula for not repeating death" — for not dying a second time — and UCL notes it survives in only four sources, the main one being the papyrus of a man named Ra, from Memphis, now in Leiden (RMO Papyrus T5). What the formula contains is not an instruction but a conversation: Osiris — and with him every deceased who speaks the spell — questions tm [𓏏𓅓] [Gardiner: X1-G17], Atum, the self-created first god, about the place he has come to and the span of life remaining to him. Atum's answers escalate from consolation to cosmology.

THE MANUSCRIPT

Spell 175 is rare: four sources in total, per the UCL additions list, against the dozens or hundreds of copies of Spells 17, 30B, or 125. Its main witness belonged to a man actually named Ra, and came from Memphis — a Memphite copy of a dialogue in which the Heliopolitan creator promises a Memphite dead man the lifespan of the universe.

THE FAMILY OF THE SPELL

"Not repeating death" is a small genre of its own: Spell 175 is followed in the additions by Spell 176, "Formula for not repeating death in the underworld," from more than one New Kingdom source, and the dread it answers is the corpus's deepest one — not the first death, which the whole book presupposes, but the second: annihilation at the judgment, treated on the Weighing of the Heart page.

The opening (paraphrase, after the published translations): Osiris laments the land of the dead he has entered — it has no water, it has no air; it is depth unfathomable, black as the blackest night, and men wander helplessly in it; a man cannot live there in peace of heart. Atum answers by decree: the justified shall live for millions of years, Horus shall rule in his father's place, and Osiris has been given the realm of the dead while his son holds his throne "in the Boat of Millions of Years." Then Osiris asks the direct question — how long shall I live? — and Atum's reply becomes the most famous eschatological passage in Egyptian literature:

// SPELL 175 — ATUM ON THE END OF ALL THINGS (QUOTATION) //
"You are to have millions of millions, a lifespan of millions… I shall indeed destroy all I made, and this land shall turn into Nun, as a floodwater, as its original condition. I alone am to remain, with Osiris, when I have transformed myself into other snakes, which men do not know, which gods do not see."

— Book of the Dead, Spell 175. Translation: Stephen Quirke, Going out in daylight (2013), p. 438, as quoted in R. Borges Pires, "Before Time, after Time," Res Antiquitatis (2019). Nun, the primeval water, is nwn [𓈖𓅱𓈖] [Gardiner: N35-G43-N35].

Sit with what the spell is doing. A funerary formula "for not dying again" secures its promise by extending the deceased's lifespan to the lifespan of the cosmos itself — and then, having reached the end of the cosmos, refuses to stop. When Atum unmakes everything he made and the ordered world returns to the flood it rose from, two beings remain: Atum, and Osiris — which is to say, Atum and this dead person, who has been speaking as Osiris throughout. The creator's final form is a serpent "which men do not know, which gods do not see": an unseen, unknowable survival-form outside the created order altogether. Egyptian religion is often described as having no eschatology; Spell 175 is the standing counterexample, and characteristically it is filed not as a doctrine but as a spell — something to be used, by a named individual, against a named fear.

The spell also carries one of the corpus's sharpest theodical exchanges (paraphrase): Osiris objects that every other god has his place in the Boat of Millions of Years, while he is assigned the silent land; and he raises the case of stḫ [𓋴𓏏𓐍] [Gardiner: S29-X1-Aa1] — Set, his own murderer — asking whether Set's soul too will be sent west, a fate apart from all other gods. The question is left hanging in the dialogue's theology of exceptional fates: Osiris's portion, like Set's, is singular. For the creator-god theology that stands behind Atum's speech, see Cosmological Deities and Memphite Theology; for a companion creator, Ptah.

XIII. Spells 182–185 — Hymns to Osiris and the Advocacy of Thoth

The numbers above 165 are the corpus's annexes — compositions gathered into the sequence by Naville, Pleyte, and Allen from manuscripts where the neat Late Period order no longer held — and their last great cluster, Spells 181–185, is a hymnal to Osiris. UCL's additions list, following Allen 1974, gives the contents with a bibliographer's precision that itself tells the story of survival: Spell 181, a formula for entering the tribunal of Osiris whose second part is a hymn to Osiris frequently found already on Middle Kingdom stelae; Spell 182, the "Book for causing Osiris to endure," attested in more than one New Kingdom source; Spell 183, a hymn to Osiris in one Nineteenth-Dynasty and one Third Intermediate Period source; Spell 184, a formula for being beside Osiris, in a single fragmentary New Kingdom papyrus (that of Wia); Spell 185, a hymn to Osiris perhaps in a single source only, the Third Intermediate Period papyrus of Sutymes in Paris. Allen further registered a tail of additional Osiris hymns as 185A–M, on the ground that Spell 15 likewise covers a range of hymns rather than one text.

Two characterisations of this cluster are safe on the published titles, and both matter. First, these are hymns, not procedures: where Spells 1–162 mostly equip, threaten, restore, and negotiate, the 181–185 group praises — Osiris enduring, Osiris hymned, the deceased stationed beside Osiris. They represent the corpus in its devotional register, the same register as Spell 15's solar hymns, and their presence at the end of expanded manuscripts reads like a coda: after the journey, the judgment, and the provisioning, the justified settle into liturgy. Second, the advocacy that opens the whole book does not disappear from the coda. Spell 182's work of "causing Osiris to endure" belongs, in the tradition as Faulkner's edition presents the cluster, to the same divine counsel who pleaded Spell 1 and recorded Spell 125: Thoth, the god whose office is to make the Osiris-case hold — before the tribunal, before the Ennead, and finally before time itself. The book that begins with a lawyer ends with a liturgy, and the lawyer is still on the record. (This paragraph is summary and interpretation of the cluster's published titles and standard presentation, not a quotation.)

SPELL 182 — CAUSING OSIRIS TO ENDURE

UCL's title for it is a "Book" in its own right — "Book for causing Osiris to endure" — attested in more than one New Kingdom source. Its work is maintenance of the god himself: the justified dead join the party that keeps Osiris established, the reversal of a book in which the gods have done all the maintaining.

SPELL 183 — HYMN TO OSIRIS

A hymn to Osiris surviving in one Nineteenth-Dynasty source and one from the Third Intermediate Period — the devotional voice carried across the same centuries that carried the judgment scene, in far fewer copies.

SPELL 184 — BEING BESIDE OSIRIS

A formula "for being beside Osiris," preserved in a single fragmentary New Kingdom manuscript, that of a man named Wia, in the Czartoryski collection. One damaged roll is the whole of its transmission — a reminder of how much of this literature is lost.

SPELL 185 — HYMN TO OSIRIS (SUTYMES)

A hymn perhaps unique to one Third Intermediate Period papyrus, that of Sutymes, now in the Bibliothèque nationale, Paris. Allen registered still further Osiris hymns as 185A–M, treating the number the way Spell 15 is treated: as a shelf, not a single text.

Why Hymns Close the Book

There is a structural intelligence in ending with praise. The transformation spells of the corpus's middle ranges (Spells 76–88 in the UCL list: taking the form of a golden falcon, a divine falcon, a lotus, the bennu-heron, a swallow, a snake, a crocodile — even, in Spell 82's startling title, "taking the form of Ptah, eating bread, drinking beer") give the justified a repertoire of forms; the judgment gives them a status; Spell 148 gives them rations. What remains for a being who is fed, justified, and form-free is exactly what the gods themselves do in the hymns: abide, attend, and praise. The deceased ends the book as a member of the choir rather than a petitioner at the door. It is also worth noting, against any picture of the corpus as a closed canon, that these hymns survived by the thinnest of threads — one papyrus here, a stela tradition there — and were numbered into the "Book" only by nineteenth- and twentieth-century scholarship. The archive of the dead was assembled twice: once by the workshops, and once by the editors.

The hymns to Osiris gathered here are the late flowering of a devotion whose central narrative — the murder, mourning, and vindication of the god — is treated in full on The Osiris Myth and Osiris. Readers following the voice of the deceased through this page will notice that the hymns reverse the book's usual direction of speech: everywhere else the dead speak as Osiris; in the hymns they finally speak to him, from alongside, as one justified person addressing the first of the justified.

XIV. The Passwords — Names of Gates, Ferrymen, and Door Parts

This is the section the rest of the archive points to. The Pantheon page observes that the Book of the Dead "provided passwords and invocations to speak the names of these gods correctly, because knowing the name gave power over the threshold they guarded" — and the claim is literal. A large, coherent stratum of the corpus consists of nothing but examinations in sacred nomenclature: the deceased is stopped — by a ferryman, a gate, a boat, a door, a hall — and is required to say what things are called. The name is the password; the password is the name. The theology behind it is the doctrine of the rn [𓂋𓈖] [Gardiner: D21-N35], the name as a constituent part of a being (see The Power of Names): to know a thing's true name is to know its nature, its allegiance, and its mythic identity, and a being compelled to hear its own true name spoken by a stranger must treat that stranger as an insider.

The Ferry Examination: Spell 99

The fullest surviving examination is Spell 99, "Formula for fetching the ferry in the underworld," which UCL traces back to Middle Kingdom coffins (Coffin Texts 404–405). The dead person must cross a difficult waterway to reach Osiris — "I have come to see my father Osiris," in the UCL translation of the Papyrus of Nu version — but the ferry will not move for a passenger who cannot pass the catechism. The boat's own parts interrogate him, one after another, in a tabulated question-and-answer that runs to twenty-two addresses in the Nu manuscript. Quoted here are the summons and two of the exchanges, exactly as UCL translates them:

// SPELL 99 — SUMMONING THE FERRY (QUOTATION) //
"Bring me the ferry, tie for me the cords, in peace, in peace. Come, come, hasten, hasten, I have come to see my father Osiris."

— Book of the Dead, Spell 99. Translation: UCL Digital Egypt chapter 99 page, from the Book of the Dead of Nu (British Museum EA10477). The boat is a dpt [𓊛] [Gardiner: P1].

// SPELL 99 — THE BOAT ASKS ITS NAMES (QUOTATIONS) //
"Tell me my name, says the rudder: Your name is Aker."
"Tell me my name, says the sail: Your name is Nut."

— Book of the Dead, Spell 99. Translation: UCL Digital Egypt chapter 99 page. Aker is ꜣkr [𓄿𓎡𓂋] [Gardiner: G1-V31-D21], the earth-horizon god; Nut, the sail, is nwt [𓈖𓅱𓏏] [Gardiner: N35-G43-X1], the sky — the sail is named for the sky it holds.

The remaining identifications (paraphrase of the UCL translation's table): the mooring-post is Lord of the Two Lands in the shrine; the mallet is the haunch of the Apis; the prow-rope is a tress of the mooring of Anubis from the embalmers' labour; the mast is the bringer of the great goddess after her distance; the oars are the fingers of Horus the Elder; the scoop is the hand of Isis staunching the blood from the eye of Horus; the steering-oar is "Accurate, shining in water at the border of your wings"; the wind is the north wind that comes from Atum to the nose of Khentyimentiu; and the boat itself bears the most violent name of all — the hand of Isis, cut by Ra to bring blood to the Evening Boat. Not one answer is arbitrary. Every part of the ferry is a god, a body-part of a god, or an episode of the myths; the examination certifies that the passenger knows the cosmos is built this way, all the way down to the bailer.

Gates, Approaches, and the Door of the Hall

The same examination guards the land routes. Spells 144–147 in the UCL sequence are a gate-cycle: Spell 144, "Knowing the names of the keepers of the seven approaches"; Spells 145 and 146, the gateways of the Field of Reeds and of the domain of Osiris, gate by gate, each with its keeper, its watcher, and its herald to be named; Spell 147, formulae for the approaches of Osiris's domain and the gods in their caverns. A gate's leaf — ꜥꜣ [𓉿] [Gardiner: O31] — and its posts and bolts are persons with names, and the pattern holds to the very threshold of judgment. Spell 125 itself ends, in the 125C continuation, with the deceased interrogated at the gate of the hall — "Who are you? What is your name?… What have you passed?… What did you see there?" — answering each question with the ritual knowledge the sequence demands, until the gate itself delivers the verdict on the examination:

// SPELL 125C — THE GATE ADMITS THE ONE WHO KNOWS (QUOTATION) //
"Come then, enter this gate of this broad hall of the Two Goddesses of Right. You know us."

— Book of the Dead, Spell 125C, closing exchange (sections after T. G. Allen, 1974). Translation: UCL Digital Egypt chapter 125C page, from the Papyrus of Nu.

"You know us" is the whole doctrine in three words. Across the corpus, salvation is repeatedly transacted as recognition: the deceased knows the ferryman's boat, the gate's keepers, the assessors' names, the cows of provision, the steering-oars of the sky — and is known, and passed, in return. Ignorance, not sin, is the most common cause of destruction in the Duat's geography; the sins are handled by the scales, but the road is handled by the names. This is why a book — a portable memory, consultable at every checkpoint — was the core funerary technology of the New Kingdom, and why its vignettes (Section XV) show the deceased reading from the roll at the moment of challenge. The passwords are not a theme of the Book of the Dead. In large part, they are its contents.

XV. How the Passages Were Used — Recitation, Inscription, Amulets, Vignettes

A spell in this corpus is not a text that happens to have uses; the use is the spell. Every passage on this page existed simultaneously in up to four modes — spoken, written, worn, and pictured — and the modes were understood to reinforce one another. This section gathers the procedural evidence passage by passage, because the "how" is where the Book of the Dead stops resembling literature and starts resembling equipment.

1. Recitation: The Spoken Funeral and the Rubrics

The rubrics — the red-ink headings and footnotes the scribes wrote with the spells — are performance directions. They name the speaker ("Words spoken by the Osiris [Name]"), the occasion (Spell 1 at burial; Spell 130, in its UCL title, "to be performed on the day of the birth of Osiris"; Spell 136, a ritual "on the sixth day"; Spell 140, a ritual for the filling of the wḏꜣt [𓂀] [Gardiner: D10] eye on the last day of the second month of winter), the material accompaniment (Spell 148 over a painted board with offerings set out; Spell 137A, in its longer New Kingdom form, the ritual of the four torches with performance instructions), and the conditions (Spell 148's rubric, per the Totenbuch summary, presents the composition as secret and prescribes purity for its reciter). The benefit-clauses that close so many rubrics are written in the same contractual voice as Spell 99's, whose UCL-translated rubric promises the knower of the formula a loaf, a jug, and a field reaped for him by the Followers of Horus in the Field of Reeds, and seals the promise with the corpus's characteristic guarantee-phrase:

// SPELL 99 — THE RUBRIC'S SEAL (QUOTATION) //
"…a true matter a million times."

— Book of the Dead, Spell 99, closing rubric. Translation: UCL Digital Egypt chapter 99 page (versions of the Papyrus of Hepres and the Papyrus of Satiah).

In the living world, the voice at the funeral was professional: the lector priest (ḫry-ḥbt, Section III) read the service from a roll while the wꜣb [𓅱𓄿𓃀] [Gardiner: G43-G1-D58] priests and the sm priest performed the physical rites. But the spells are scripted in the deceased's own first person, and the fiction is systematic: the officiant's voice is lent to the dead for the day, and the buried papyrus then preserves the loan in perpetuity. The workshops that produced the rolls worked within temple scriptoria — on which see Scribal Arts and the House of Life — and production ranged from bespoke commissions like Ani's, its owner a senior temple scribe portrayed throughout his own book, to stock rolls written with a blank where the name would go. The Gautsoshen scarab (Section VIII), its owner's name scratched over an erasure, shows the same economy at the level of the single amulet.

2. Inscription: One Text, Many Carriers

Follow a single spell across its carriers and the system's priorities become obvious. Spell 30B lives on papyrus, but its canonical carrier is the green-stone heart scarab laid in the wrappings (Metropolitan Museum records, Section VIII). Spell 6 lives on the bodies of shabti figures by the hundred. Spells 155–160 in the UCL list are not really "texts" at all in the reading sense: each is the charge for a specific amulet placed at the neck of the transfigured dead — a ḏd [𓊽] [Gardiner: R11] pillar of gold (155), a tyet-knot of red stone (156), a vulture of gold (157), a broad collar of gold (158), a papyrus-sceptre of green stone (159) — the spell being the words that make the object operative and the object being the spell made durable; see Amulets and Talismans. Spell 162, "for creating heat under the head of a transfigured spirit," charges the inscribed disc placed under the mummy's head. Spell 148 moves between papyrus, a painted writing-board, and temple walls. Coffins carried the ancestors of these spells as Coffin Texts, and the earliest carriers of all were pyramid walls. The direction of travel across three thousand years is constant: a formula migrates toward whatever surface will keep it closest to the body, the name, or the route of the dead.

3. Vignettes: Pictures That Work

The illustrations are not illustrations. UCL's survey notes the small group of full-height vignettes that structure the great rolls — the adoration of Osiris at the roll's opening, the sunrise scene (Spell 16), the Field of Offerings (Spell 110), the judgment (125D), the boats of the gods (Spell 143), the provision scene of Spell 148, the mounds of Spell 150, Anubis embalming (Spell 151) — and these monumental images do in paint what the spells do in words. Spell 148's rubric makes the mechanism explicit: the gods are to be depicted in paint on a writing-board and given offerings before the depictions. The picture is a presence-device; the offering made to it is made to the god. Likewise the funeral frieze standing over Spells 1–15 does not depict a funeral that is over — it holds the funeral permanently in progress above the words the funeral required. To read the vignettes as decoration is to repeat, in art history, the error of reading the spells as poetry: both are machinery, drawn and written respectively.

MODE ONE — SPOKEN

At the funeral by the lector priest; in the Duat by the deceased in the first person. The rubrics are the stage directions, and the rolls were also studied and commissioned in life — Ani was himself a temple scribe, buying the voice he would need.

MODE TWO — WRITTEN

On the papyrus roll in the coffin; on coffin interiors in the older Coffin Text tradition; on tomb walls, stelae, and temple walls for the promoted compositions (1, 148, 151); in black ink with the rubrics — the instructions — picked out in red.

MODE THREE — WORN

On the body, inside the wrappings: the heart scarab over the heart (30B), the amulet charges at the neck (155–160), the heat disc under the head (162), and Spell 6 on the shabti workforce standing in the tomb's boxes — text as equipment, placed where it was to act.

MODE FOUR — PICTURED

The full-height vignettes hold the rites permanently in progress: the funeral over Spells 1–15, the judgment at 125, the provision scene at 148. Spell 148's rubric — paint the gods on a board, set offerings before the painting — states the principle outright: the image is an address at which the god receives.

// THE FOUR MODES, ONE SPELL — SPELL 30B AS A WORKED EXAMPLE //

Spoken: the plea to the heart belongs to the judgment's script; the deceased addresses the heart before the tribunal, in the hall, as 30A's papyrus form shows.

Written: the same words stand in the papyrus roll as the owner's copy, among the heart-protection sequence (Spells 26–30) that the roll assembles.

Worn: inscribed on the underside of a green-stone scarab, set over the heart inside the wrappings, where it speaks continuously and cannot be mislaid — mid-Thirteenth Dynasty to the third century BCE.

Pictured: the scarab is itself an image — the ḫprr beetle of coming-into-being — and the weighing vignette (125D) shows the very scene the spell is managing, heart on the scales, at the centre of the great papyri.

XVII. An Esoteric Reading — The Passages as a Technology of Transformation

The reading offered in this section is interpretation — a modern esoteric framing of the passages, set out as such. It is not presented as established Egyptological fact, and where it differs from the scholarly accounts given above, the scholarly accounts govern.

Read with the eyes of the later mystery traditions — the traditions that received Egyptian material through the Corpus Hermeticum, the Greek Magical Papyri, and the temple lore of the Ptolemaic Period — the passages on this page compose a single instrument with a recognisable design. Esoteric readers from the Hermetists onward have treated the corpus not as a mortuary manual but as a map of initiatory transformation usable by the living: death as the figure for any radical passage of consciousness, the Duat as the figure for the interior territory crossed in it, and the spells as rehearsed responses to the states encountered on the way. That reading is an overlay on the texts, not their original funerary sense — but it is an old overlay, arguably as old as the Books of Breathing, and it explains the corpus's long second life in Western esotericism better than any other single idea.

Within that framed reading, the passages divide by function into a curriculum. The restoration cluster (21–26) reads as a protocol for recovering faculties one at a time — voice, power, memory, heart — which practitioners have mapped onto the deliberate reassembly of attention after dissolution experiences of every kind. The password examinations (99, 144–147, 125C) read as training in recognition: the traveller passes not by force but by knowing what each threshold-figure truly is, a discipline the esoteric traditions generalised into the doctrine that every guardian is a faculty of the traveller's own soul, named and therefore integrable — the rudder is Aker, the sail is Nut, and the boat that carries the soul is built of gods because the soul is. Spell 17's glossing method becomes, in this reading, a contemplative technique in its own right: assert an identity, interrogate it ("What does it mean?"), and let the answer re-describe the self at a deeper level, exactly as the text does. And the judgment (125) becomes a technology of self-audit: a fixed list of denials, addressed one by one to named internal witnesses, with the heart — the one faculty that cannot be deceived because it was present for everything — placed on the scales rather than argued with. The heart scarab, on this reading, is the curriculum's most honest emblem: the practitioner does not silence the heart by force but by prior arrangement, having spent a life making the heart into an ally that will not need to testify.

The esoteric reading also inherits the corpus's central caution, and it is worth stating in the traditions' own spirit: in the texts themselves, the formulae are never sufficient alone. The confession works because the scales stand behind it; the passwords work because the traveller has become the kind of being the names describe; Spell 148's rubric demands purity of its speaker as a material condition, not a courtesy. Later esoteric use that treats the passages as verbal machinery — recite and receive — is using the book against its own rubric. What the passages model, on any reading, is preparation: a life arranged so that, at the examination, the answers are simply true. That is the point at which the esoteric interpretation and the scholarly one converge, and this section ends there deliberately — the convergence is a reading, offered as one, and the passages remain available to stricter eyes than these.

Spell 175, finally, is the esoteric reading's favourite text, and the framing discipline matters most there. In the interpretation offered by several modern schools, Atum's serpent — the form that survives the unmaking of the world, unknown to men and unseen by gods — is read as an image of the innermost self persisting beyond every form it has inhabited, and the dialogue as a whole as a meditation assigned against the fear of extinction. That is a reading of the spell, layered over a funerary formula whose ancient rubric promises something plainer and stranger: that the one who knows it will not die a second time. The interpretation is recorded here because the archive's readers will meet it elsewhere, usually without its framing. With the framing kept — interpretation labelled as interpretation, the passage itself quoted only in a named scholar's translation — the two can coexist on one page, as they have coexisted, carefully or carelessly, in every century since the temples closed.
