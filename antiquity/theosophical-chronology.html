I. What Tier C Means Here — A Chronology with Authors

This article is classified Tier C — Modern Construction. That classification is stated here in words, as this hub requires, and it governs everything that follows. Tier C means that the subject is a documented product of the modern period, whose makers, publications, and revisions can be named and dated — not an ancient record recovered intact, and not a finding of geology or archaeology. The Theosophical chronology is, in that respect, an exemplary Tier C subject: almost everything in it can be followed, edition by edition and letter by letter, from the early 1880s to the present.

The system was built principally by Helena Petrovna Blavatsky (1831–1891), co-founder of the Theosophical Society in 1875. Its first public form was given not by Blavatsky herself but by the Anglo-Indian newspaper editor A. P. Sinnett, in The Occult World (1881) and Esoteric Buddhism (1883), working from a correspondence he believed — and presented — as coming from two hidden teachers, the Mahatmas Koot Hoomi (signed “K.H.”) and Morya (signed “M.”). Its canonical form is Blavatsky’s The Secret Doctrine: The Synthesis of Science, Religion, and Philosophy, published in two volumes in London in 1888 — Volume I, Cosmogenesis, and Volume II, Anthropogenesis — presented by her as a commentary on an archaic text, the Stanzas of Dzyan. Its popular cartography was supplied by W. Scott-Elliot in The Story of Atlantis (1896) and The Lost Lemuria (1904). And its most extensive revision was supplied, a generation later, by Annie Besant and C. W. Leadbeater, whose Man: Whence, How and Whither (1913) announced itself, in its subtitle, as A Record of Clairvoyant Investigation.

Every claim in this article is therefore attributed to its author. Where the text says “the Third Root Race began” without qualification, it is reporting — in compressed form — what Blavatsky, Sinnett, Scott-Elliot, or Besant and Leadbeater taught, and the surrounding paragraphs name which of them taught it. Nothing in the chronology is narrated here as established fact about the human past. Where the chronology conflicts with the geological and palaeoanthropological record — and on dates, bodies, and continents it conflicts comprehensively — that is said plainly, in §VII and elsewhere, with the evidence. Respect, in this hub, means accurate description of what a system teaches and why its makers believed it. It does not mean blurring the line between a teaching and a finding.

A word on vocabulary is needed at the outset, because the system’s central term misleads modern readers. In Blavatsky’s usage, a Root Race is not a “race” in the modern biological or census sense. It is a successive, world-wide stage of humanity — a whole humanity, occupying its own continent over millions of years, with its own kind of body, its own dominant sense, and its own spiritual task — through which, in her teaching, all human souls pass in turn by reincarnation. Present humanity, of every modern people, belongs in her scheme to a single Root Race, the Fifth. That is what she taught, and it matters for §XVI. It is also true — and §XVI records it just as plainly — that her writings use the racial language and hierarchies of nineteenth-century anthropology, that some passages rank contemporary peoples as older or younger “stocks” within the scheme, and that later writers tore the vocabulary out of the system and put it to uses she did not direct. Both halves of that paragraph belong to the record, and this article keeps both.

THE CLAIM, AS ITS AUTHORS MADE IT

An archaic wisdom, preserved by hidden adepts (the Mahatmas), transmitted in letters and in the Stanzas of Dzyan, recording humanity’s passage through seven Root Races on a chain of seven globes, over immense cycles of time.

A synthesis constructed in 1880–1888 from the comparative religion, Orientalist scholarship, evolutionary popular science, and occult literature of Blavatsky’s own century. No independent manuscript of the Book of Dzyan has ever been produced. See §III.

II. The Architecture at a Glance — Chains, Globes, Rounds, Races

The Theosophical chronology is not a list of dates. It is a piece of architecture — a nested set of cycles, each turning inside the next — and its dates only make sense once the architecture is understood. This section describes that architecture as its authors presented it, in the order a reader meets it: from the largest cycle down to the human one. Everything here is attributed teaching, stated in the present tense of the system itself for clarity, and §VI then gives the numbers the system assigns to it.

Chains and Globes

In the system as Sinnett first expounded it and Blavatsky then elaborated it, evolution does not happen on one world. A planetary chain consists of seven globes, lettered A through G, of which only one — Globe D, our Earth — is, in the teaching, on the physical plane; the others are held to exist on subtler planes and to be invisible to ordinary sight. A stream of evolving life, the “life-wave,” passes in order through all seven globes. Humanity’s present chain is, in the teaching, the Earth Chain, and it is itself a re-embodiment of an earlier Moon Chain, whose beings and failures supply much of the plot of Volume II of The Secret Doctrine. The standard Theosophical reference summary of Anthropogenesis puts the point compactly, in paraphrase: human evolution occurs on the seven globes of the Earth Chain, and the kingdoms of nature — elemental, mineral, plant, animal, human, and beyond — progress by moving from globe to globe across successive rounds.

Rounds

One complete passage of the life-wave around all seven globes is a Round. Seven Rounds complete a chain’s active life, after which, in the teaching, the chain dissolves and its fruits pass to a successor chain. Present humanity is taught to be in the Fourth Round of the Earth Chain — the round of fullest materiality, the bottom of a descending arc that the later rounds are held to re-ascend. The first three Rounds, in Blavatsky’s account, recapitulate the earlier kingdoms in ethereal, “filmy” forms before humanity proper appears on Globe D in the Fourth.

Root Races, Sub-Races, Family Races

Within one globe in one Round, humanity unfolds in seven successive Root Races. Each Root Race divides into seven sub-races, and each sub-race into smaller branches — called, in different presentations of the literature, branch races or family races. (Sinnett’s 1883 exposition uses a slightly different nesting of the same terms — great races, branch races, and root races within them — a reminder that the terminology was still settling in the first public form; Blavatsky’s 1888 usage, Root Race / sub-race / family race, became the standard one.) Races overlap: the seed of the next is, in the teaching, selected and set apart while its predecessor still flourishes, and each transition is accompanied by geological upheaval that remakes the continents for the incoming humanity. Humanity is presently taught to be in the Fifth Root Race of the Fourth Round on Globe D — in Blavatsky’s term, the “Aryan” Root Race, using “Aryan” in the philological sense current in her century for the Indo-European language family and its speakers (see §XVI on what later writers did with the word).

Three features of this architecture deserve emphasis, because they explain almost everything else in the system.

First, it is septenary throughout. Sevens nest inside sevens at every level — globes, rounds, races, sub-races, the seven principles of the human constitution in Sinnett’s exposition, the seven senses developed one per Root Race in the standard summaries (hearing, touch, sight, taste, and smell assigned in that order across the races). The recurrence is deliberate: Blavatsky presented the sevenfold pattern as a law of nature, drawn in her account from the archaic source, and the chronology’s elegance for its adherents lies largely in that self-similarity.

Second, it is an arc, not a ladder. The first half of the cycle is a descent — spirit involving itself ever more deeply in matter, bodies condensing from ethereal to fully physical — reaching its densest point in the Fourth Root Race and the Fourth Round. The second half is a re-ascent, in which the embodied mind is held to spiritualise itself again. “Evolution,” in this system, therefore means something different from its Darwinian sense: not descent with modification by natural selection, but a pilgrimage of monads — individual spiritual units — through forms, gathering experience across hundreds of incarnations. Sinnett’s exposition reckoned the total in the hundreds per globe (about eight hundred incarnations for each monad on each planet of the chain, in his presentation of the letters’ teaching), separated by long intervals in a post-mortem state the literature calls Devachan.

Third, the chronology is inseparable from the geography. Each Root Race has its continent, and the continents rise, break, and sink as the races succeed one another. That is why a chronology article and a lost-continents article in this hub keep citing each other: in the Theosophical system, when a humanity lived and where it lived are the same statement made twice. The companion article on Lemuria treats the Third Race’s continent in full; the Fourth Race’s continent, Atlantis, and the later reworkings of both are treated in Mu and Atlantis Reborn.

III. The Claimed Source — The Stanzas and the “Book of Dzyan”

The Secret Doctrine does not present itself as Blavatsky’s invention. It presents itself as a commentary — volume after volume of it — on a short, cryptic, archaic text: the Stanzas of Dzyan, which Blavatsky said were translated by her from the Book of Dzyan, written in a sacred language she called Senzar and preserved, in her account, by initiates in Asia, inaccessible to European scholarship. Volume I gives the Stanzas of Cosmogenesis; Volume II gives twelve Stanzas of Anthropogenesis, broken into forty-nine slokas (verses), on which the whole Root Race chronology is hung as commentary. The opening pages of Volume II, checked for this article in the Theosophical University Press online edition of the text, head the section exactly so: “Stanzas Translated with Commentaries from the Secret Book of Dzyan,” with a footnote in Blavatsky’s voice explaining that only forty-nine slokas out of several hundred are given, and that a periphrasis is sometimes used where a literal translation would be unintelligible.

One sloka, verified in that opened text (Vol. II, Stanza II, sloka 5), will show the reader the register of the claimed source — and how much interpretive work the commentary is doing on it:

“The wheel whirled for thirty crores more. It constructed rupas: soft stones that hardened; hard plants that softened. Visible from invisible, insects and small lives. She shook them off her back whenever they overran the mother.” — Stanzas of Dzyan, Anthropogenesis, Stanza II, sloka 5, as translated and printed in H. P. Blavatsky, The Secret Doctrine, Vol. II (Theosophical University Press online edition, checked for this article)

“Thirty crores” — a crore being ten million in the Indian numbering Blavatsky adopts — is three hundred million, of years in her commentary’s reading. A single compressed verse of that kind becomes, under commentary, an epoch of pre-human evolution. The movement from verse to chronology is the movement of the whole book, and a reader who sees it once, clearly, can read the rest of The Secret Doctrine accurately: short, oracular stanza; immense, erudite, comparative commentary; a date or a race assigned where the stanza itself gives neither in so many words.

// THE DOCUMENTARY POSITION, STATED PLAINLY //

No independent manuscript of the “Book of Dzyan” has ever been produced — not in Senzar, not in any other language, not in any public or scholarly collection. No reference to such a book, independent of Blavatsky and of the Theosophical literature that depends on her, has been established in the Tibetan, Sanskrit, or other corpora in which scholars have looked for it. The scholarly consensus — represented in this article by Wouter J. Hanegraaff and Joscelyn Godwin, among others (see §XVIII) — accordingly treats the Stanzas as a nineteenth-century composition: a synthesis, in archaic dress, of the Hindu, Buddhist, Kabbalistic, Hermetic, and contemporary scientific materials that Blavatsky’s commentary everywhere displays and frequently cites. Theosophists continue to receive the Stanzas as Blavatsky presented them. Both positions are recorded here. The documentary position is the one just stated, and a reader of this hub is entitled to it without varnish: the chronology’s claimed antiquity rests on a source that only its claimant has ever reported seeing.

That finding does not, by itself, settle what kind of text The Secret Doctrine is, and scholars of esotericism are careful on the point in a way §XVIII develops. A text can be a modern synthesis and still be a major religious document — indeed, the synthesis is the historical fact about it, and tracing its ingredients (Fabre d’Olivet’s earlier scheme of successive prehistoric races, noted by the historian James Webb; the Hindu cycle literature; contemporary geology and evolutionary writing) is the normal work of the field, not an exposure exercise. What the documentary position does settle is the tier. A chronology whose source cannot be examined cannot be filed as an ancient tradition (Tier B), however old it claims to be. It is filed, here, as what its publication history shows: a modern construction (Tier C) that presents itself as an ancient record.

IV. Blavatsky Builds the System — The Secret Doctrine (1888)

Helena Petrovna Blavatsky (1831–1891) was born in the Russian Empire, travelled — on her own account, very widely; on the documented record, widely enough — and in 1875 co-founded the Theosophical Society in New York with Henry Steel Olcott, William Quan Judge, and others. The Society’s stated objects centred on universal brotherhood, the comparative study of religion, philosophy, and science, and the investigation of unexplained laws of nature and latent human powers. Her first large book, Isis Unveiled (1877), had already ranged across ancient religion and modern science in defence of an occult reading of both. After the Society’s move to India (headquarters at Adyar, Madras, from 1882), and after the Sinnett correspondence described in §VIII, Blavatsky withdrew to Europe and wrote, in Ostend, Würzburg, and London, the work on which the chronology stands or falls: The Secret Doctrine, published in October–December 1888.

The Society behind the book has its own dated place in the record, because the chronology’s warrant (§VIII) presupposes it. The Theosophical Society was formally founded in New York City on 17 November 1875, by Blavatsky, Henry Steel Olcott, William Quan Judge, and a small company of others — sixteen founders in the usual count. Olcott — lawyer, journalist, and a former colonel of the American Civil War — became its first president and held the office until his death in 1907, supplying the organising force that held the Society together while Blavatsky supplied its teaching. Its objects, as they settled, centred on forming a nucleus of the universal brotherhood of humanity, encouraging the comparative study of religion, philosophy, and science, and investigating unexplained laws of nature and the latent powers in man. Blavatsky and Olcott left New York for India in late 1878, reaching Bombay in 1879, and in 1882 the Society established its international headquarters at Adyar, on the outskirts of Madras (now Chennai), where the Adyar Society is headquartered still. Judge remained in America to carry the work there — the arrangement whose later strains produced the American secession of 1895 (§XVIII).

The book is organised as commentary on the Stanzas (§III), framed by what Blavatsky calls three fundamental propositions: in paraphrase, (1) an omnipresent, eternal, boundless, and immutable Principle, beyond speculation; (2) the eternity of the universe as a boundless plane, periodically the playground of innumerable universes incessantly manifesting and disappearing — the law of periodicity, of ebb and flow; and (3) the fundamental identity of each soul with the universal Over-Soul, and its obligatory pilgrimage through the cycle of incarnation in accordance with cyclic and karmic law. The chronology is proposition (2) and proposition (3) worked out as history: periodicity supplies the rounds and cycles; the pilgrimage of souls supplies the monads who must inhabit every race in turn. Nothing in the system is decorative. The dates are long because the pilgrimage is long, and the pilgrimage is long because, in the doctrine, experience in every form of humanity is required.

How the Book Argues

Volume II’s method, page by page, is comparative accumulation. A stanza is printed; then Hindu Puranic literature, the Kabbalah, Egyptian and Greek materials, the Finnish Kalevala (whose first Rune stands as epigraph to the Anthropogenesis Stanzas in the text checked for this article), and the geology, palaeontology, and anthropology of the 1870s–1880s are marshalled around it as corroboration, correction, or foil. Contemporary scientists — Darwin, Haeckel, Huxley, and many lesser names — are quoted extensively, praised where their findings can be read as approaching the doctrine, and reproved where the doctrine is held to go beyond them. Godwin’s The Theosophical Enlightenment (1994) remains the fullest map of that reading: Blavatsky appears there not as an isolated visionary but as the synthesist of a century of English-language esoteric and mythographic literature, brought together with Orientalist scholarship in a single system (§XVIII).

For the chronology specifically, two argumentative habits matter most. First, Blavatsky adopts the scientists’ deep time and then outbids it: where geology offered tens of millions of years, the Stanzas, in her commentary, offer hundreds of millions, and geology is treated as having glimpsed the foothills of a range it cannot yet survey (§VII). Second, she reverses the direction of explanation for the fossil and anthropological record: forms that science read as human ancestors are, in her account, often read as degenerations, side-branches, or the discarded experiments of earlier races — most strikingly in her teaching that the anthropoid apes descend, in part, from a Lemurian transgression rather than standing in humanity’s ancestry, a claim the companion Lemuria article records and flags, and which modern biology does not support in any form. A reader who grasps those two habits can predict, on almost any topic, how Volume II will handle new evidence: absorb its timescale, invert its genealogy.

Three narrative hinges carry the whole sequence, and each is a piece of doctrine before it is a piece of story.

1. The Separation of the Sexes (Third Race)

Blavatsky taught that humanity was not originally sexual. The Stanzas’ sequence — self-born, sweat-born, egg-born — is glossed by her as a graded passage from asexual projection, through budding and exudation, through an egg-laying hermaphroditic phase, to distinct males and females by the later sub-races of the Third Race. The slokas checked for this article state the outline in the Stanzas’ own idiom (Stanza VI, sloka 22, in paraphrase of its compressed wording): the second evolved the egg-born, the third; first male-female, then man and woman. Only with separated sexes does “humanity” in the full modern sense — sexual, physical, mortal — begin in her system, and that beginning is what her most famous date measures (§VI).

The commentary on Stanza VI (Secret Doctrine II:131–132, in the text checked for this article) insists the change was gradual rather than a single act: the man-bearing eggs gave birth, gradually and almost imperceptibly, first to beings in which one sex predominated over the other, and only finally to distinct men and women, and Blavatsky states that order — sexless, then a solitary hermaphrodite, then separated — as an axiom of the system. Later Theosophical summaries accordingly read the separation as the tradition’s true Fall: the point at which humanity becomes fully physical, sexual, and mortal.

2. The Awakening of Mind (Late Third Race)

Bodies, in the teaching, are not yet human until mind enters them. Blavatsky taught that the Sons of Wisdom — higher beings, the Manasaputras — incarnated in, projected a spark into, or deferred entering the ripe Third Race forms (the Stanzas’ threefold division, slokas 24–27 as printed in the text checked for this article), thereby awakening Manas: self-conscious intellect, and with it moral responsibility. Those who received no spark are, in her account, the “mindless” or “narrow-headed,” whose fate drives the Lemurian fall narrative. The doctrine does heavy work: it explains, inside the system, both human intelligence and human inequality of capacity without recourse to a single creation or a single fall. Outside the system, it has no evidential standing, and its mapping onto contemporary peoples in some passages is among the parts of Volume II that modern readers — including many Theosophists — find hardest (§XVI).

3. Catastrophe as Punctuation (Every Transition)

Each Root Race ends, in the teaching, in geological catastrophe that destroys its continent and most of its population: Lemuria by fire (volcanism and convulsion), Atlantis by water (subsidence and flood, in stages over a long period in the fuller literature), the pattern alternating by a stated law. Survivors — a selected “seed” humanity under instructors — carry the thread into the next race. Catastrophe in this system is therefore not an interruption of the chronology; it is its punctuation, the full stops between its sentences. That is one reason the scheme proved so portable (§XV): any flood story, any sunken-land tradition, any ruined continent in any literature could be filed, by a sympathetic reader, as a garbled memory of a race-transition — and was, extensively, by the literature that followed.

VI. The Scheme’s Own Numbers — Dates and Durations

The Theosophical chronology is famous for the size of its numbers and notorious, even among its friends, for their instability. Blavatsky herself withholds some figures — the duration of the earliest races is, in her text, a matter for initiates — gives others as round immensities, and gives a few with startling, to-the-year precision. Later systematists then harmonised, recomputed, and in places flatly revised her figures, so that “the Theosophical date” for a given event often depends on which decade’s literature is being quoted. This section presents the scheme’s own numbers as the scheme’s, with their sources in the literature distinguished, and without smoothing the disagreements between them. None of the figures below is endorsed as a date in Earth history; the geological position is given in §VII.

The Anchor Figure — Eighteen Million Years

The most cited number in the whole chronology is Blavatsky’s answer, in Volume II, to her own rhetorical question whether human beings could have existed eighteen million years ago. She answered, in the affirmative, that the figures of eighteen million years embrace, in her phrase as discussed in the secondary literature checked for this article, the duration of sexual, physical humanity — counted from the separation of the sexes in the later Third Root Race (Secret Doctrine II:157, in the pagination of the standard editions). In a related computation (II:69 and II:715, as traced by the commentators), she reckoned the era of Vaivasvata Manu — the progenitor figure of the present humanity in her reading of the Hindu sources — at a figure in the neighbourhood of 18.6 million years before her own time (the precise string of digits varies in transcription between commentators, and this article deliberately gives the rounded form rather than pretend to a precision the secondary record does not sustain). The point for a Tier C history is not the digits. It is the gesture: a date for the origin of sexual humanity that is precise in form, immense in scale, and derived — in her presentation — from the Stanzas and from Hindu cycle arithmetic, not from any fossil or stratum.

The Larger Frame

Around that anchor, the literature arranges still larger figures. Blavatsky placed the First and Second Root Races in a past she associated, in her geological correlations (II:710 and following, as summarised by her commentators), with the Primary era of nineteenth-century geology, and wrote that for the last 120 million years — “even before that time,” in the passage her systematists quote from II:312 — the Earth was ready to receive her human stock, the actual duration of the first two and a half races being withheld. The Third Race she assigned to the Secondary (Mesozoic) era; the Fourth to the Tertiary; the Fifth to the Quaternary and the present. Later “traditional Theosophy” summaries, systematising her scattered figures, commonly state that the Lemurian (Third) Root Race began about 34½ million years ago and that the change to modern sexual reproduction began about 16½ million years ago — figures that come from the systematising literature (and, for the second, from Man: Whence, How and Whither, p. 96 of the first edition, as its commentators note) rather than from a single dated sentence of Blavatsky’s, and that sit in visible tension with her own eighteen-million-year anchor. The Atlantean (Fourth) Root Race is commonly placed by the same traditional summaries as arising about 4½ million years ago. A widely read modern Theosophical magazine summary (Quest, Fall 2019, published by the Theosophical Society in America and consulted for this article) goes further in the other direction, stating that the First Root Race began to develop over 1.5 billion years ago — a figure that reflects later attempts to stretch the scheme across the modern, radiometric timescale. The spread between 18 million, 120 million, and 1.5 billion, all offered inside the same tradition for overlapping events, is itself the finding of this section: the chronology’s numbers were never a fixed table. They were a scale — a commitment to immensity — with figures supplied, revised, and re-supplied as each generation’s geology moved the horizon.

Three causes recur in the literature itself. First, Blavatsky distinguishes exoteric from esoteric computation and sometimes gives both, without always marking which is on the page. Second, her Hindu source arithmetic — yugas, manvantaras, and kalpas, treated in this hub’s Traditional Chronologies article — yields different totals depending on whether divine or human years are used, a conversion her commentators argue over at length. Third, each successor generation re-anchored the scheme to the geology of its own day (§VII, §XIII). A chronology that claims to be timeless but must be re-dated every fifty years is telling its historians something important about what it is: a framework in search of a timescale, not a timescale recovered intact.

VII. Vast Timescales Against the Geology of Her Day

It is tempting, from the far side of radiometric dating, to treat Blavatsky’s millions as pure fantasy set against a sober scientific consensus. The history is more interesting than that, and fairer to everyone involved. In the 1880s, science itself had no settled timescale — and the timescale its most eminent physicist defended was shorter than the one its geologists wanted, and shorter, in places, than Blavatsky’s.

The uniformitarian geology of Hutton and Lyell had accustomed geologists to thinking in effectively unlimited time: strata kilometres thick, laid down grain by grain, implied durations that Lyell’s followers were content to leave unnumbered and immense. Against that stood William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, whose cooling-Earth calculations — first published in 1862, giving an age between 20 and 400 million years, and narrowed over the following decades to some 20–40 million years, with a final 1897 figure of about 24 million years in the standard histories — set a physicist’s ceiling that many geologists found impossibly cramped for the sedimentary record they had mapped. Radioactivity, the heat source whose absence flawed Kelvin’s premises, was not discovered until 1896 (Becquerel) and not grasped as a source of terrestrial heat until the early 1900s; radiometric dating belongs to the twentieth century, and the modern figure for the Earth’s age — about 4.54 billion years, settled by Clair Patterson’s meteorite lead-isotope work in 1953–1956 — was unguessable in 1888. In short: when Blavatsky wrote, informed opinion placed the Earth’s age somewhere between roughly twenty million years and an unnumbered abyss, and argued about it in public.

Read against that background, her chronology’s strategy becomes legible. Her eighteen million years for sexual humanity fits — just — inside even Kelvin’s ceiling, and her geological correlations (Primary for the first races, Secondary for the Third, Tertiary for the Fourth) use the era-names and relative sequence of the geology she read. Where she exceeds the science, she exceeds it in the direction Lyell’s party already favoured — more time, not less — and she says, in substance, that the initiates’ records confirm the geologists against the physicists. The Theosophical chronology was therefore not, in 1888, the chronological outlier it later became. It became an outlier afterwards, for a specific reason: science revised its numbers by measurement, and the chronology revised its numbers by re-anchoring (§VI), and the two procedures diverged. Radiometric dating fixed the Earth at 4.54 billion years, the dinosaurs’ extinction at 66 million years ago, the genus Homo at under three million years, and anatomically modern humans at roughly three hundred thousand years — a framework in which there is no room, at any point, for gigantic physical humanities tens of millions of years old, for continents sinking under human civilisations in the Indian or Atlantic Oceans, or for a separation of human sexes eighteen million years ago (sexual reproduction itself is over a billion years old; the human lineage is not). Those sentences are stated here plainly, as this hub requires, and kindly: Blavatsky built with the timescale her century gave her, and the century after hers took the gift back.

VIII. The Mahatma Letters — Authority Before the Book

The chronology did not begin as a book. It began as a correspondence — and the correspondence remains the foundation of its authority for adherents, because The Secret Doctrine itself repeatedly defers to the teachers behind it. Understanding the letters, and exactly what is and is not known about them, is therefore not a detour from the chronology. It is the chronology’s provenance.

Between 1880 and 1884, A. P. Sinnett — editor of The Pioneer, a leading Anglo-Indian newspaper at Allahabad — and, in the earlier phase, his fellow civil servant and Theosophist A. O. Hume, received a long series of letters signed by two personages who presented themselves as adepts of an esoteric brotherhood centred in Tibet: Koot Hoomi (signing “K.H.”) and Morya (signing “M.”). Blavatsky, then in India, acted — in the account Sinnett accepted and published — as intermediary and amanuensis-at-a-distance: questions were put through her, and answers arrived by means the correspondence describes as occult, including “precipitation” (the direct materialisation of writing), as well as by ordinary-seeming delivery in unusual circumstances. The letters range across cosmology, the constitution of man, reincarnation and its intervals, and — centrally for this article — the planetary chain, the rounds, and the races: the whole architecture of §II exists in the letters before it exists anywhere in print. Sinnett preserved the originals; they passed eventually to the British Library, where they remain available for inspection, and they were published in full only in 1923, edited by A. Trevor Barker as The Mahatma Letters to A. P. Sinnett.

The role the letters play in the system can be stated in one sentence: they are its warrant. Blavatsky’s personal authority was always contested — in her lifetime, ferociously (§X) — but the doctrine, as Sinnett and then Blavatsky presented it, did not rest on her word alone. It rested on the word of teachers held to be members of a brotherhood preserving the archaic wisdom across millennia, of whom she was the messenger and, in her own presentation, sometimes the imperfect transmitter (errors in transmission are acknowledged, and debated, inside the correspondence itself). The Stanzas of Dzyan (§III) stand behind The Secret Doctrine in exactly the same relation as the Mahatmas stand behind the letters: an unexaminable source, vouched for by the chain of transmission, carrying a chronology no archive can check. A Tier C history notes the pattern without needing to psychologise it: twice over, the system’s deepest claims rest on sources its adherents receive on trust and its historians cannot inspect.

// WHO WERE THE MAHATMAS? — THE POSITIONS, FAIRLY //

Theosophical position: Koot Hoomi and Morya are (or were) living adepts, members of a Great White Brotherhood watching over human evolution, who communicated genuinely with Sinnett through Blavatsky. Critical position (Hodgson, 1885; many scholars since): the letters were written by Blavatsky herself, in disguised hands, possibly with confederates (§X). Middle scholarly positions: K. Paul Johnson’s The Masters Revealed (1994) and related work argue, in paraphrase, that the Mahatma personae were modelled on real men Blavatsky had known, transformed in the telling — a thesis Theosophists reject and other scholars treat as suggestive rather than proven. This article adopts no position beyond the documentary one: the letters exist, in the British Library; their content is the chronology’s first form; their authorship has been disputed since 1884 and remains disputed.

IX. Sinnett Gives the Chronology Its First Public Form (1881–1883)

Alfred Percy Sinnett (1840–1921) was not an occultist by trade. He was a Fleet Street journalist turned editor of the most influential English-language newspaper in India, and his two books did for the chronology what a good editor does for any difficult material: they made it readable, orderly, and apparently reasonable, years before its canonical text existed. Any account that begins the Theosophical chronology in 1888 has missed its first, and in some ways its most successful, publication by half a decade.

The Occult World (1881)

The Occult World (London, 1881) introduced the Mahatmas to the reading public: who Sinnett believed them to be, how the correspondence had begun (with a letter Sinnett addressed to “the Unknown Brother” and placed, on Blavatsky’s advice, in a cabinet), and what phenomena had, in his account, attended it. The book is a narrative of initiation into a correspondence, not yet a system; but it established, in print, the two premises on which everything after it depends — that the teachers exist, and that Sinnett was in genuine communication with them. It went through multiple editions and made the Mahatma letters a topic of drawing-room and periodical discussion across the English-speaking world.

The book’s making is part of its evidence. It was compiled from the Simla visit of September–October 1880, when Blavatsky and Olcott stayed with the Sinnetts and Blavatsky put Sinnett into contact with the two adepts, and it incorporated the witness reports of Blavatsky’s phenomena that The Pioneer had published in the second half of 1880, including Sinnett’s own report of 7 November 1880. It is dedicated to the Mahatma Koot Hoomi. Nicholas Goodrick-Clarke’s assessment, in the standard reference summary, is that the book gave sensational publicity to Blavatsky’s phenomena and to the Mahatma letters, and drew the attention of the London Society for Psychical Research — the attention that issued, four years later, in the investigation of §X.

Esoteric Buddhism (1883)

Esoteric Buddhism (London, 1883) is the chronology’s first systematic public form, and this article consulted its text directly (in the widely reproduced transcription of the sixth edition) for the summary that follows. Sinnett — who apologised in his preface for the title, explaining that “Buddhism” was used for the Buddha’s esoteric doctrine as his teachers presented it, not for the exoteric religion — expounds, in order: the esoteric teachers; the sevenfold constitution of man; the planetary chain of seven worlds, around which the human life-wave circles; the world-periods, with their rounds and races under a universal septenary law; the post-mortem state (Devachan); and the progress of humanity through its successive great races. In his exposition, in paraphrase of the text consulted: each entity must pass seven times round the chain of seven planets, accomplishing seven rounds, of which we are in the fourth; on each planet seven great races successively rise, flourish, and decline, each dividing into branch races in turn; the fourth great race inhabited the continent Plato called Atlantis; the third inhabited a now-submerged continent in the Indian Ocean “which we may call Lemuria” — the name arriving in the Theosophical system, in print, through Sinnett’s page before Blavatsky’s; and the majority of present humanity belongs, in his presentation, to the fifth great race. The architecture of §II is all here, in 1883, in clear expository prose, five years before the Stanzas were offered as its source.

That sequence — letters, Sinnett’s system, Blavatsky’s commentary — matters for the historian, and it is recorded here without insinuation. Inside the movement, the sequence is unproblematic: the teachers taught Sinnett first in outline, then taught through Blavatsky in fullness, and the Stanzas are the archaic record behind both. Outside the movement, the sequence is the evidence: a system that appears first in an 1880s correspondence, is systematised by a journalist in 1883 from that correspondence, and is then presented in 1888 as the commentary on an immemorial text, has the publication history of a modern construction whatever its adherents believe about its ultimate origin. Tier C files it by its publication history.

// SINNETT’S BOOKS AS DOCUMENTS //

Esoteric Buddhism remains the easiest entry point to the chronology as a system — clearer than The Secret Doctrine by an order of magnitude — and historians value it for exactly that clarity: it shows what an intelligent, sympathetic, non-occult reader of the letters in 1883 understood them to teach, before later controversy and later elaboration complicated the record. Read it as the chronology’s first edition.

X. The Coulomb Affair and the Hodgson Report (1884–1885)

No honest history of the chronology can omit the scandal that struck its warrant (§VIII) while the system was still being built, because the scandal shaped everything after it — including The Secret Doctrine itself, which Blavatsky wrote in Europe after leaving India under its cloud. The facts below are the agreed skeleton; the interpretations remain, as §XI shows, in dispute.

Alexis and Emma Coulomb were a married couple employed at the Theosophical Society’s headquarters at Adyar — Emma as housekeeper, Alexis as a handyman — who fell out with Blavatsky and with the Society’s leadership and were dismissed in 1884. In September 1884 a Madras missionary magazine, the Christian College Magazine, published letters purporting to be from Blavatsky to Emma Coulomb, instructing her in the fraudulent production of phenomena: sliding panels in the “shrine” cabinet at Adyar where Mahatma letters were held to appear, a dummy head for apparitions of Koot Hoomi, and related stagecraft. The Coulombs supported the letters with their own testimony about the headquarters’ arrangements. Blavatsky denied the letters and the charges, wished to sue, and was overruled by the Society’s committee, which judged litigation ruinous; she resigned her office and left India in March 1885, never to return.

The Society for Psychical Research (SPR) in London, which had been investigating Theosophical phenomena with initial sympathy, sent a young Australian researcher, Richard Hodgson, to India to investigate on the spot. His report — the “Report of the Committee appointed to Investigate Phenomena connected with the Theosophical Society,” published in the SPR’s Proceedings in December 1885, with the bulk of it written by Hodgson and endorsed by the committee (which included Edmund Gurney, F. W. H. Myers, and Frank Podmore) — is universally known as the Hodgson Report. In paraphrase of its conclusions: the Blavatsky–Coulomb letters were genuine; the Adyar phenomena were fraudulent; the Mahatma letters were written by Blavatsky herself in disguised hands (a conclusion Hodgson supported with handwriting comparison, including the opinion of the London calligraphic expert F. G. Netherclift on specimens supplied by Sinnett), with one document attributed to her associate Damodar; and Blavatsky herself was summed up, in the report’s most quoted judgement, in terms that consigned her to permanent remembrance as an accomplished and ingenious impostor. The report devastated her reputation in Britain and has shadowed every subsequent discussion of the letters — and therefore of the chronology built on them.

// WHAT THE HODGSON REPORT DOES AND DOES NOT SETTLE //

The report, even taken at full strength, is a verdict on authorship and phenomena — on whether Blavatsky wrote the letters and staged the marvels — not a refutation of the chronology as a system of ideas, which stands or falls as a doctrine independently of who held the pen. Conversely, even a complete demolition of Hodgson (§XI) would not establish that the letters came from Mahatmas, still less that their chronology is true: it would return the authorship question to “unproven,” where, on the documentary evidence available to historians, it remains. This article keeps those two questions — who wrote the letters, and whether the chronology describes the past — rigorously apart, because conflating them is the commonest error on both sides of the controversy.

XI. The Reassessment — Vernon Harrison and After

A century after the Hodgson Report, the case was reopened from inside the institution that had published it. Vernon Harrison (d. 2001) was a research worker in questioned documents, a professional examiner of forgery, and a member of the SPR. His paper “J’Accuse: An Examination of the Hodgson Report of 1885” appeared in the Journal of the Society for Psychical Research, vol. 53, in April 1986 (pp. 286–310), and was followed by a book-length expansion, H. P. Blavatsky and the SPR (Theosophical University Press, 1997), incorporating a further study based on a line-by-line microscopical examination of the British Library’s set of 1,323 colour slides of the Mahatma Letters originals. Harrison’s work was consulted for this article in that 1997 edition, and his conclusions are given here in paraphrase.

Harrison concluded, in substance: that the Hodgson Report is not a scientific study but a flawed and untrustworthy document that should be read with great caution, if not disregarded; that Hodgson’s handwriting evidence was methodologically unsound — resting on fragments, mostly isolated characters torn from context and from documents that could for the most part be neither identified nor accurately dated, with no adequate illustration of the allegedly incriminating Blavatsky–Coulomb letters at all; that Hodgson had been prepared to use any evidence, however trivial or questionable, to implicate Blavatsky, while ignoring evidence that told the other way; and that the SPR committee of 1885 could not be exonerated from blame for publishing, in Harrison’s judgement, a thoroughly bad report that it had done little more than rubber-stamp. Harrison was careful — and this article follows him in the care — to state what his examination did not show: demolishing Hodgson’s proof of forgery is not proving the letters genuine, and Harrison presented his conclusion as a verdict on Hodgson, not as a certificate for the Mahatmas.

The present state of the question, stated fairly, is therefore threefold. First, Hodgson’s 1885 conclusions can no longer be quoted, after Harrison, as settled forensic fact; the most damaging single document in Theosophical history has been shown, by a qualified examiner publishing in the SPR’s own journal, to be evidentially weak in the ways listed above. Second, Harrison’s critique was not a formal retraction of the 1885 report by the SPR as a body, and scholars outside the movement have continued to defend parts of Hodgson’s case and to contest parts of Harrison’s; the exchange of replies and counter-replies is itself part of the record Harrison’s 1997 volume prints. Third, and decisively for this hub’s purposes, neither verdict — forgery proven, or forgery unproven — bears on the tier of the chronology. A letter genuinely received from a hidden adept in 1882 and a letter composed in Adyar in 1882 are, for the historian of the human past, the same kind of object: a nineteenth-century document asserting a chronology for which no independent evidence has been produced. The Hodgson controversy is a controversy about honesty. The Tier C classification is a judgement about evidence. Both are recorded here; neither is allowed to do the other’s work.

XII. Scott-Elliot — The System Gets Its Maps (1896–1904)

Blavatsky died in London on 8 May 1891. The chronology she left was immense, allusive, and — on its geography and its sub-race detail — radically incomplete. The man who completed it for the reading public was William Scott-Elliot (1849–1919), a Scottish merchant banker and Theosophist, whose two short books did for the Fourth and Third Root Races what Sinnett’s had done for the system as a whole: they made it visual, tabular, and concrete. The companion Lemuria article treats his Lost Lemuria (1904) in full; this section treats his work as what it is in the chronology’s biography — its cartographic consolidation.

The Story of Atlantis (London: Theosophical Publishing Society, 1896) presented, with four maps, a continuous history of the Fourth Root Race: the successive configurations of the Atlantean continent across four mapped periods, the sub-races of Atlantis (named, in his presentation, from the Rmoahal and Tlavatli through the Toltec, Turanian, Semitic, Akkadian, and Mongolian stocks), its civilisations, its technologies, its corruptions, and its staged destruction — with dated catastrophes assigned, in his chronology, across hundreds of thousands of years, the final sinking of the remnant island he called Poseidonis placed, following Plato’s figure as the literature received it, in the ninth millennium BCE. The Lost Lemuria (1904) performed the same service for the Third Race, with two maps, a geological evidence chapter compiled from Haeckel, Wallace, and Blandford, and a closing description of Lemurian man. The two books were combined after his death as The Story of Atlantis and the Lost Lemuria (1925), the form in which most twentieth-century readers met them.

Scott-Elliot’s stated method is the hinge between the Blavatsky phase and the clairvoyant phase (§XIII), and he states it himself, in paraphrase: the further evidence was obtained from the same source and in the same manner as the Atlantis material — that is, from clairvoyant investigation of archaic records, associated in his circle with C. W. Leadbeater, whose copies of ancient maps Scott-Elliot says he was privileged to obtain — and he grades his own maps, candidly, as approximate reproductions of damaged originals (for Lemuria, a broken terra-cotta model and a badly preserved, crumpled map), not to be taken as accurate to a single degree of latitude or longitude. The candour deserves the respect this hub pays it throughout: Scott-Elliot tells his reader exactly what kind of evidence he is offering. What he is offering is not survey data. It is testimony — second-hand testimony to clairvoyant copies of unproduced originals — and the maps’ enormous influence (§XV) flowed not from their evidential strength but from their form: a map looks like knowledge even when its legend, read carefully, says it is a vision.

// WHAT SCOTT-ELLIOT CHANGED //

After 1896–1904, the chronology had what Blavatsky never gave it: a continuous, dated, mapped narrative that a reader could follow from continent to continent without mastering the Stanzas. Almost every later popular account of Atlantis and Lemuria — including many that have never heard Scott-Elliot’s name — reproduces his sub-race names, his map shapes, or his catastrophe dates. In the chronology’s biography, he is its most consequential editor.

XIII. The Clairvoyant Revisions — Besant and Leadbeater

The third phase of the chronology’s construction belongs to the Adyar Theosophical Society under its second president, Annie Besant (1847–1933, president from 1907), and to Charles Webster Leadbeater (1854–1934), a former Anglican clergyman turned Theosophical lecturer who claimed, from the 1890s, a trained clairvoyance capable of direct investigation of past events, of the inner planes, and — in Occult Chemistry (1908, with Besant) — of the interior of the atom. Where Blavatsky’s warrant had been a text (the Stanzas) and Sinnett’s a correspondence (the letters), Besant and Leadbeater’s warrant was a faculty: clairvoyant investigation, exercised jointly, checked — in their presentation — by cross-observation between the two investigators, and offered to the reader as a method, repeatable in principle by anyone who developed the same faculty. The chronology that resulted is therefore not merely Blavatsky’s with details filled in. It is Blavatsky’s framework re-derived, re-dated, and in places quietly replaced, by a new kind of authority.

Man: Whence, How and Whither (1913)

Man: Whence, How and Whither: A Record of Clairvoyant Investigation (Adyar: Theosophical Publishing House, 1913; 524 pages in the edition catalogued by Google Books) is the phase’s central document. Compiled by Besant and Leadbeater — with Besant responsible, in the standard bibliographic analysis, for the introduction, the opening chapters, the foreword to the concluding part, and the epilogue, drawing in part on her earlier The Pedigree of Man (1903), and Leadbeater for the bulk of the narrative chapters — the book works through the whole architecture of §II as narrative history: the first and second chains; early times on the Moon Chain, including the individualisation of its animal humanity; the Earth Chain and the early stages of the Fourth Round; the Fourth Root Race and the civilisations of Atlantis, including extended accounts of Atlantean Peru and Chaldaea; the beginnings of the Fifth Root Race, the building of its great city, and its sub-races in turn (Arabian, Iranian, Keltic, Teutonic, in their enumeration); and a concluding part, “Man: Whither,” on the beginnings of the Sixth Root Race — published separately in 1931 as The Beginnings of the Sixth Root Race. Dates are given throughout with a specificity Blavatsky rarely attempted: individualisation events on the Moon Chain, migrations, foundings, and catastrophes are placed in particular millennia tens of thousands — and, for the deeper past, millions — of years before the present. The sexual changes of the Third Race, to take the figure already met in §VI, are placed by the book (p. 96 of the first edition, as its commentators cite it) as beginning some 16½ million years ago and occupying some 5½ to 6 million years — a clairvoyant figure, offered beside Blavatsky’s eighteen-million-year anchor without any sense, in the text, that the two authorities needed reconciling.

The method deserves a precise description, because later literature often softens it. In the investigators’ own presentation, the past was examined by directing clairvoyant attention to the “records” of events — the Akashic record, in the vocabulary the movement developed — and reporting what was seen, in the first person plural of joint observation, with scenes, speeches, and interior states rendered as narrative. No instrument, archive, or independent witness stands behind any particular scene. The Theosophical reference literature itself records, in paraphrase, that the venture — detailing with remarkable specificity the dates and lives of persons as far back as 23,650 BCE — tested the credulity even of persons prepared to accept Leadbeater’s visions of the astral plane and the atom. That sentence, from inside the tradition’s own encyclopaedic literature, is the fairest single measure of what the clairvoyant revisions asked of their readers.

BLAVATSKY’S WARRANT (1888)

A text: the Stanzas of Dzyan, translated by her from an unproduced original. Authority by transmission.

SINNETT’S WARRANT (1881–1883)

A correspondence: letters from unmet teachers, preserved as originals. Authority by attestation.

BESANT & LEADBEATER’S WARRANT (1913)

A faculty: joint clairvoyant investigation of the records of the past. Authority by claimed direct perception.

Each warrant, it will be noticed, is less checkable than the one before it by any means available to an outsider — and each produced a chronology more detailed than the one before it. That inverse relation between checkability and detail is, for the historian, the signature of the whole construction, and the reason this hub insists on naming the warrant alongside every date it reports.

XIV. The Alcyone Narrative — Past Lives as Chronology

The clairvoyant revisions had a human centre, and the chronology’s strangest chapter is organised around a child. In 1909 Leadbeater encountered, on the beach at Adyar, the fourteen-year-old Jiddu Krishnamurti (1895–1986), son of a Theosophical employee, and became convinced — on clairvoyant grounds, in his account — that the boy was the chosen vehicle for the coming World Teacher. Under the star-name Alcyone assigned to him in the investigators’ system of pseudonyms, Krishnamurti’s past lives became the thread on which Besant and Leadbeater strung a continuous narrative of the races.

The series began in print as “Rents in the Veil of Time,” in The Theosophist from April 1910, presenting thirty of Alcyone’s past lives, ranging — in the standard biographical summary consulted for this article — from 22,662 BCE to 624 CE, in eleven of which Alcyone was female. Around Alcyone the investigators assembled a cast of some two hundred and fifty named characters — a “band of Servers” — each identified by a star-name and each mapped, in the published tables, to a living or historical person: Alcyone himself (Krishnamurti); Herakles (Besant); Sirius (Leadbeater); Vajra (Blavatsky); Ulysses (Olcott); Fides (George Arundale); and scores of others, incarnating together, generation after generation, through the Moon Chain, Lemuria, Atlantis, and the Fifth Race civilisations. The material was absorbed into Man: Whence, How and Whither (1913) and then expanded into its own monument, The Lives of Alcyone (1924, two volumes, forty-eight lives), in which the chronology ceases to be a table of races and becomes a multi-millennial group biography, with marriages, betrayals, sacrifices, and recognitions carried across lives separated by thousands of years — including, in the Moon Chain chapters, the individualisation of the future Alcyone, Sirius, and Herakles as devoted animal servants of a lunar household, a scene Leadbeater narrates at novelistic length.

Three facts complete the record, and all three are stated here in the hub’s usual manner — plainly, and without relish. First, the Alcyone chronology was presented by its authors as investigated fact, not as edifying fiction, and it was received as fact by a large part of the Adyar membership, for whom the star-name tables functioned as a kind of spiritual peerage. Second, it was contested inside the movement from the start, and the custody litigation brought by Krishnamurti’s father in 1913 — together with the older scandals attaching to Leadbeater, which lie outside this article’s subject — made the Adyar chronology a public controversy, not merely an internal doctrine. Third, in 1929 Krishnamurti himself dissolved the Order of the Star, the organisation built to herald him, and repudiated the role the chronology had assigned him, thereafter teaching, for more than fifty years, in explicit rejection of gurus, systems, and prepared paths. The Alcyone narrative thus survives as a chronology whose central figure declined, in adulthood and in public, to inhabit it — a fact no later presentation of the material is entitled to omit, and which this article records as the narrative’s documented ending.

// WHY ALCYONE BELONGS IN A CHRONOLOGY ARTICLE //

Because it shows where the system’s logic leads when it is followed without reserve. A chronology of races, warranted by clairvoyance, becomes — by the same warrant, with no new evidence of any other kind — a chronology of named souls, with dates to the year in the twenty-third millennium BCE. The step from Man: Whence, How and Whither to The Lives of Alcyone is not a change of method. It is the same method, trusted further. Historians of the movement treat the episode accordingly: not as an embarrassment to be skipped, but as the clearest exhibit of what clairvoyant historiography, as a practice, actually produced.

XV. Afterlife in Later Movements

The Theosophical chronology is the single most influential piece of deep-time architecture in modern esotericism. Almost every later system that offers its adherents a vast, staged, catastrophic human past — with lost continents, precursor races, hidden masters, and a coming transformation — is either a direct descendant of the 1881–1913 construction described above, or a reaction against it that keeps its shape. This section traces the main lines of descent factually, without attempting a census of the hundreds of groups involved.

Anthroposophy

Rudolf Steiner (1861–1925), general secretary of the German section of the Theosophical Society, broke with Adyar in 1912–1913 — the immediate occasion being the Alcyone / World Teacher promotion described in §XIV — and founded the Anthroposophical Society. Steiner’s own accounts of Atlantis and Lemuria, drawn in his presentation from his reading of the Akashic record and published in Cosmic Memory (articles from 1904) and An Outline of Occult Science (1909), retain the Root Race architecture, the Lemurian and Atlantean epochs, and the clairvoyant warrant, while re-centring the whole on a Christian and Rosicrucian frame and on Steiner’s own authority. Anthroposophy is thus the chronology’s first great schism: the system, minus Adyar.

Steiner had led the Society’s German section as its general secretary from 1902, and his break was as much about orientation as about Krishnamurti: where Adyar looked east, to India and its teachers, Steiner was building a Western, Christian and Rosicrucian path, and he objected in particular to Besant’s presentation of Krishnamurti as a coming World Teacher. Most of the German membership followed him out. The Anthroposophical Society he founded made its centre at Dornach, Switzerland, where the Goetheanum was begun in 1913, and his version of the chronology reached English readers chiefly through Cosmic Memory, the book form of his 1904 Akasha-Chronik articles.

The Alice Bailey Works and the “New Group” Literature

Alice A. Bailey (1880–1949), a former Theosophical Society member who founded the Arcane School (1923), presented her books as dictated by a Tibetan teacher, “Djwal Khul” — a warrant in direct succession to the Mahatma letters (§VIII). Her system retains the hierarchy of Masters, the staged evolution of humanity, and the expectation of a coming transformation, recast in a more Christian and organisational vocabulary. Through Bailey’s books and their United Nations-adjacent readership, Theosophical deep time entered mid-twentieth-century spiritual politics in a form its originators would have recognised at once.

The institutional detail is Santucci’s, in his Dictionary of Gnosis & Western Esotericism entry on Bailey, which remembers her for three accomplishments: serving as amanuensis for the Tibetan teacher Djwhal Khul, the teachings appearing in some twenty-four books written from 1922 into the late 1940s, beginning with Initiation, Human and Solar (1922) and published through the Lucis Trust, founded with her husband Foster Bailey in 1922; establishing the Arcane School in April 1923, a correspondence school modelled on Blavatsky’s own Esoteric Section; and organising, with Foster Bailey, the service activities known as World Goodwill and Triangles. Bailey had joined the Theosophical Society in 1917 and dated her first contact with the Tibetan to 1919 — a claim the Adyar leadership questioned, which is part of why her work grew up outside the Society whose system it continues.

Ascended Master Movements and the New Age

The “I AM” Activity of Guy and Edna Ballard (from the 1930s) and its successors, including the Church Universal and Triumphant under Mark and Elizabeth Clare Prophet, carried the Masters, the lost continents, and the catastrophic race history into an American, Christianised idiom of decrees, dispensations, and survival. In the wider New Age literature that followed — the milieu in which “Lemuria” and “Mu” became bywords for a gentler, more psychic precursor humanity, relocated to the Pacific and to Mount Shasta in California — the chronology survives in dissolved form: its continents and its golden ages retained, its septenary architecture and its dates usually dropped. The companion article on Mu and Atlantis Reborn treats James Churchward’s Mu books (from 1926), the most successful of these dissolutions, in their own right.

The Traditional Timelines

Finally — and most relevantly for this hub — the Theosophical chronology became a template against which other deep-time schemes are still presented and compared. Twentieth- and twenty-first-century traditionalist timelines, including the Inner Garden Foundation timeline that this hub takes as a Tier C primary source in its own right, arrange antediluvian kings, divine dynasties, lost continents, and world ages in a single immense sequence in a manner that owes its modern form, in part, to the precedent Blavatsky set: that such a sequence could be printed, tabulated, and defended as a system. Section XVII takes up that comparison directly, and the hub’s Traditional Chronologies article sets the Theosophical scheme beside the yuga cycles, the king lists, and the other deep-time architectures on common axes.

XVI. A Necessary Plain Word — The Term Misused

This section is brief, factual, and unsparing in both directions, because the subject requires all three.

The word at the centre of the chronology — “race” — and its companion word “Aryan” were taken up, in the decades after Blavatsky’s death, by writers whose purposes were not hers. In Austria and Germany, the current historians call Ariosophy — the “wisdom of the Aryans” of Guido von List (who called his own doctrine Armanism) and Jörg Lanz von Liebenfels (whose earlier labels were Theozoology and Ario-Christianity) — combined völkisch nationalism, Social Darwinist race theory, runic and solar symbolism, and occult notions derived in part from Theosophy into an ideology of Aryan supremacy and antisemitic conspiracy. Nicholas Goodrick-Clarke’s The Occult Roots of Nazism (1985) remains the standard scholarly account, and its conclusions are more precise than either polemic on the subject usually allows: in paraphrase of Goodrick-Clarke’s argument as summarised in the scholarly literature, Ariosophist ideas and symbols filtered through to several antisemitic and nationalist groups in late Wilhelmian Germany from which the early Nazi party emerged in Munich after the First World War, and demonstrable links exist in particular cases (notably around Heinrich Himmler’s circle and the Ahnenerbe) — while the central importance of “Aryan” racism in Ariosophy itself is traced by Goodrick-Clarke (p. 14, as quoted in the Theosophical literature’s own engagement with him) to the racial concerns of Social Darwinism in Germany, compounded by occult notions deriving from Theosophy, rather than to Theosophical doctrine as such; and his comprehensive study finds, in the standard summary, little evidence of direct Ariosophist influence on Nazi policy beyond such particular cases. The Theosophical Societies themselves were, for the record, suppressed in Nazi Germany.

Two distinctions, both documented, close the matter as far as this hub is concerned. First, Blavatsky’s Fifth Root Race — her “Aryan” race — comprises, in her system, all present humanity, through which all souls pass; the term names a stage, not a master people, and Theosophical writers have always been able to say so truthfully. Second, and with equal truth: her writings employ the hierarchical racial anthropology of her century, rank contemporary peoples as remnants or younger stocks in ways that are indefensible as science and were already contested in her lifetime, and supplied — in the Root Race vocabulary itself — the very terms the Ariosophists abused. A Tier C history does not choose between those two sentences. It prints both, attributes the abuse to the abusers on Goodrick-Clarke’s evidence, attributes the vocabulary to its author, and moves on. Readers who meet “root race” language in modern supremacist material are meeting a theft, documented as such; readers who meet it in The Secret Doctrine are meeting a nineteenth-century system that must be described whole, including the parts its admirers would prefer to skip.

XVII. Echoes in the Traditional Timelines of This Hub

The Before Kemet hub exists to keep three kinds of deep past apart: the attested record (Tier A), the ancient traditions about the deep past (Tier B), and the modern constructions (Tier C). The Theosophical chronology earns its place in that design not only as a Tier C object in itself, but as the clearest available demonstration of how Tier B material is processed into a Tier C system — because Blavatsky, unusually among system-builders, prints her ingredients alongside her synthesis and invites the comparison herself.

Her deepest debts are to the Hindu cycle literature. The yuga and manvantara arithmetic of the Puranas — world ages in millions of years, a day of Brahma in billions, humanity reconstituted under a Manu after each dissolution — supplied both her scale (§VI–§VII) and several of her dramatis personae, Vaivasvata Manu above all. In their own tradition those cycles are Tier B material of great antiquity and sophistication, treated on their own terms in this hub’s Traditional Chronologies article; in Blavatsky’s hands they become something else — a chronology of globes and races that no Purana teaches — and the transformation, not either term of it, is the Tier C fact. The same processing is visible at smaller scale throughout the hub’s territory: the antediluvian king lists’ immense reigns (Tier B; see Antediluvian King Lists) reappear in Theosophical literature as garbled memories of Lemurian and Atlantean dynasties; the divine dynasties of Egyptian tradition (Tier B; see Divine Dynasties) are filed as records of the early Fifth Race’s instructors; and Plato’s Atlantis (Tier B in origin, in this hub’s filing; see Plato’s Atlantis) is promoted to the Fourth Root Race’s continent and given Scott-Elliot’s maps. In each case the ancient text says less than the system built on it, and says it differently. Keeping the two apart — while honouring the ancient text in its own right and describing the system accurately in its — is the whole method of this hub, and the Theosophical chronology is its most instructive test case precisely because its makers were so learned in the materials they transformed.

The Theosophical chronology is what a traditional timeline looks like after it has passed through a nineteenth-century study — printed, systematised, re-dated, and issued with an authority its sources never claimed in that form.

A reciprocal point, made in fairness, completes the section. Modern traditional timelines — including the Inner Garden Foundation’s, which this hub documents respectfully as a Tier C primary source — are sometimes presented as if they were independent confirmations of the Theosophical scheme, because they share its furniture: world ages, lost continents, long antediluvian reigns, a coming age. The documentary record supports a weaker and more interesting claim: the furniture is shared because the modern esoteric world has a common workshop, and Blavatsky’s was its largest bench. Agreement between two Tier C timelines is evidence of a shared tradition of telling, not of the events told — exactly as agreement between two Tier B king lists is evidence of a shared scribal tradition. This hub values both kinds of agreement, files both under their tiers, and mistakes neither for Tier A.

XVIII. The Modern Scholarly Frame — and How to Read the Chronology Now

The academic study of Theosophy is a mature field with its own journals, controversies, and classics, and a Tier C article should end by handing its reader that field’s map, rather than a verdict of its own invention. Four names organise most of it.

Joscelyn Godwin — The Intellectual History

Godwin’s The Theosophical Enlightenment (Albany: State University of New York Press, 1994; xiii + 448 pp., in the SUNY Series in Western Esoteric Traditions) is the standard history of the English-language esoteric currents — from the mythographers and solar-phallic theorists of the late eighteenth century, through Godfrey Higgins’s Anacalypsis (which a contemporary reviewer already called a precursor to Isis Unveiled), the esoteric sciences, and the rise of Spiritualism — that converged, for a moment, in the Theosophical Society. Godwin’s thesis, in the publisher’s and reviewers’ paraphrase consulted for this article, is double: Theosophy owed as much to the sceptical Enlightenment as to any doctrine of spiritual enlightenment, and Blavatsky stands in his account as a product of that nineteenth-century sceptical-esoteric world who joined its Western and Oriental threads in a union that did not survive the century. His method — respectful exhibition of his subjects’ world on its own terms, without concealing his judgement that many of them were, in his assessment, deluded and deluding — is the method this hub aspires to, and his book is dedicated, fittingly, to Leslie Price, founder of the journal Theosophical History, and to James A. Santucci, its editor.

Wouter J. Hanegraaff — The Framework of Western Esotericism

Hanegraaff, the field’s leading theorist, supplies the distinctions this article has used throughout: his separation of the modern, Blavatskian movement (“modern Theosophy”) from the older, Boehmian theosophy; his account of esotericism as “rejected knowledge” — bodies of material expelled from the academies and preserved, transformed, in counter-traditions; and his insistence that such material be studied historically, by its documents and its transmission, rather than believed or debunked as a bloc. On the Book of Dzyan (§III), Hanegraaff’s position represents the scholarly consensus this article reported: no independent manuscript, no independent attestation, a nineteenth-century synthesis to be studied as such.

The biographical anchor is brief and checkable: Hanegraaff (born Amsterdam, 10 April 1961) was appointed to the Amsterdam chair of History of Hermetic Philosophy and Related Currents in 1999, served as first president of the European Society for the Study of Western Esotericism (2005–2013), and was elected to the Royal Netherlands Academy of Arts and Sciences in 2006. His dissertation, New Age Religion and Western Culture (1996), was the first academic analysis of the New Age movement’s belief systems in their broader historical setting, and Esotericism and the Academy (2012) gave the “rejected knowledge” argument its full historical form.

James A. Santucci — The Movement from Inside the Academy

Santucci, editor of Theosophical History and a leading scholarly interpreter of the movement, supplies the careful terminological and doctrinal analysis on which any accurate summary depends — including the now-standard convention, shared with Godwin, of writing “Theosophy” with a capital T for Blavatsky’s movement and “theosophy” with a small t for the older tradition of religious illumination. His work models the position this hub takes toward living traditions generally: a scholar may study a movement’s claims without sharing them, and describe them without caricature, and the movement’s own scholars are colleagues in that work, not exhibits in it.

Santucci is Professor Emeritus of Religious Studies at California State University, Fullerton, with a doctorate from the Australian National University in Asian Civilizations. He has edited Theosophical History since 1990 — the journal Leslie Price founded in 1985, to which Godwin’s book above is dedicated — and its Occasional Papers since 1993, and he is the author of La società teosofica and An Outline of Vedic Literature and a contributor to the Dictionary of Gnosis & Western Esotericism. The combination — a Sanskritist and historian of Indian religions editing the movement’s own history journal to academic standards — is itself a small exhibit of the scholarly frame this section describes.

The Wider Shelf

Around those three stand Bruce F. Campbell’s history of the movement, Ancient Wisdom Revived (1980), which classified Theosophy plainly as an esoteric religious tradition; K. Paul Johnson’s controversial work on the Mahatmas’ possible human originals (§VIII); Nicholas Goodrick-Clarke’s studies of Theosophy’s German and Austrian afterlives (§XVI); and a continuing journal literature in Theosophical History, Aries, and Gnosis — including recent work tracing the Hermetic sources of the root-race structure itself — that keeps every section of this article, in principle, revisable by better evidence. That revisability is the difference between the scholarly frame and every warrant in §XIII: scholarship’s chronology of Theosophy can be corrected, and regularly is.

Campbell’s volume, to give the shelf’s senior item its due, runs the movement’s first century in 249 pages: Blavatsky’s sources and the plagiarism charges laid against them, the Coulomb affair and the SPR verdict, Besant’s conversion, and the succession struggle that ended in the American Section’s secession under Judge in 1895 and the rival presidencies of Katherine Tingley and Besant. His conclusion on the Masters — that their existence, and their inspiration of the Theosophical writings, are among the weakest of the movement’s claims — stands alongside his judgement that The Secret Doctrine was nonetheless perhaps the major work of occultism of its century. The pairing is the scholarly frame in miniature.

How to Read the Chronology Now

As a document of 1881–1913, read it whole. Sinnett for the system, Blavatsky for the synthesis, Scott-Elliot for the maps, Besant and Leadbeater for the clairvoyant method at full stretch. No later summary — including this article — is a substitute for the experience of the originals, all of which are freely available in the editions listed in the Sources.

As an account of the human past, do not use it. Its dates, bodies, and continents are contradicted, at every testable point, by the geological, palaeontological, genetic, and archaeological record summarised in §VII and in this hub’s Tier A articles. That sentence is not a sneer; it is a filing instruction, and it is the same instruction this hub applies to every Tier C system, including the ones it documents most sympathetically.

As an influence, reckon with it seriously. The chronology taught the modern West to imagine deep time as a staged, meaningful, catastrophic human story — and that imaginative template, detached from its septenary machinery, still structures popular prehistory, traditional timelines, and a good deal of fiction. To understand why a lost continent or a precursor race feels, to a modern reader, like an explanation, it is necessary to understand the system that first gave that feeling an architecture. That system is the one built, between a Simla editor’s desk in 1880 and an Adyar printing house in 1913, by the people named in this article. It has authors. It has dates. It has a tier. And — kept in its tier, read in its originals, and credited to its makers — it has a permanent place in the history of how human beings have imagined their own antiquity, which is the history this hub exists to tell.
