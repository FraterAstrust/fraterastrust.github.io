This is a Tier A article: everything in it rests on published archaeological, palaeoclimatic, and rock-art research, and where scholars disagree — as they do, fundamentally, about the chronology of Saharan rock art — the disagreement itself is reported with its positions attributed. No claim here depends on ancient legend or modern construction. One famous modern construction does appear, in §VI, because it attached itself to these paintings in the twentieth century and cannot be honestly omitted from their history; it is labelled there for what it is, given one paragraph and no more, and its home in this archive is the Tier C division.

The subject is the rock art of the central Sahara: the paintings in the sandstone shelters of the Tassili n'Ajjer in southeastern Algeria, the engravings cut into the black-varnished plateau of the Messak in southwestern Libya, the painted canyons of the Ennedi in northeastern Chad, and the shelters of the Gilf Kebir and Jebel Uweinat in Egypt's far southwest. Together these massifs hold tens of thousands of images — cattle by the thousand, elephants and giraffes and hippopotami, masked and floating human figures, hunters, herders, dancers, horsemen, camel riders, hand stencils, and beasts that match no living animal. They were made, in the main, while the Sahara was green: during the long wet interval climatologists call the African Humid Period, when monsoon rain reached hundreds of kilometres north of its modern limit, lakes filled basins that are now dust sources, and pastoral peoples spread across country that today supports almost no life at all.

Dating honesty is this article's spine, and it is stated here so that nothing later needs to be taken back. Almost no Saharan panel is directly dated. The pigments are mostly mineral — iron oxides and manganese — and carry no radiocarbon clock of their own; the engravings are cuts in rock, and a cut has no date stamped in it. What the chronology of this art actually rests on, panel by panel and scheme by scheme, is indirect: one image lying over another (superimposition), the darkening of a surface with age (patina and desert varnish), the animals depicted and the periods when those animals are independently known to have lived in the region, and — rarely, and therefore precious — excavated deposits physically associated with a decorated stone. Every date offered in this article should be read with that scaffolding in mind. Where a scheme's dates are contested, the contest is printed beside them.

This article is also the home, in this hub, of material that two companion articles invoke in passing. Nabta Playa and the Green Sahara treats the pastoral world of Egypt's Western Desert and gestures at the rock art of the wet Sahara as that world's picture of itself; The Nile Bridge weighs the Saharan contribution to predynastic Egypt. Both gestures land here. What follows is the image-world itself: its climate stage, its corpus, its great sites, its contested chronology, its methods, and — in §XII — an assessment of what it does and does not show, kept at exactly the strength the evidence has reached.

I. The Climate Stage — The African Humid Period

Every image in this article was made on a stage that no longer exists. The modern Sahara is the largest hot desert on Earth, some nine million square kilometres in which the centre receives, in the Eastern Sahara synthesis discussed below, under 2 mm of rain a year against potential evaporation measured in thousands of millimetres. For most of the last glacial period the desert was, if anything, larger and harsher, and the central massifs stood empty or nearly so. Then the rains came back — not as a local accident, but as the regional expression of a planetary mechanism — and for roughly nine thousand years northern Africa carried grass, trees, rivers, and lakes where it now carries sand. The rock art is the human record of that interval. The interval itself must be understood first, on the climate evidence, before a single painting is allowed to testify.

The Orbital Mechanism, Stated Plainly

The driver is precession: the slow wobble of Earth's axis that moves the seasons around the planet's elliptical orbit on a cycle of roughly twenty-one thousand years. In the early Holocene, that geometry placed the Northern Hemisphere's summer close to perihelion — Earth's nearest approach to the Sun — and northern summer insolation rose to peak values on the order of eight percent above today's, as Peter deMenocal and colleagues set out in the marine-core study that still anchors the period's dating (Quaternary Science Reviews 19, 2000). More summer heat over the North African landmass means a stronger contrast between hot land and cooler ocean, and a stronger contrast pulls the West African monsoon — the seasonal rain system that today waters the Sahel — far to the north of its modern limit. Rain that now falls on Mali and Niger fell, in the humid period's height, on the Hoggar, the Tassili, and the Fezzan. The mechanism is gradual; the climate's response, the records suggest, was not — vegetation and ocean feedbacks amplified the slow orbital push into comparatively abrupt transitions, a non-linearity that matters again at the period's end.

DeMenocal's core — Ocean Drilling Program Site 658C, off Cap Blanc, Mauritania — works as a dust gauge: wind-blown sediment from the Sahara settles offshore, so the flux of land-derived material in the core tracks how much bare, eroding ground the desert presented to the wind. That flux is low from about 14,800 to 5,500 years ago, and those two numbers bracket what is now called the African Humid Period. Both transitions, in the core, are abrupt — accomplished within decades to centuries — and both occurred, on the authors' calculation, as summer insolation crossed a threshold about 4.2 percent above its present value. A dry interruption corresponding to the Younger Dryas punctuates the humid interval in the same record. These are the dates this article uses for the period as a whole: c. 14,800–5,500 BP — while noting at once that a bracket drawn from a marine core off Mauritania is a continental frame, not a local timetable, and that individual basins filled and emptied on their own schedules within it.

A Watered Continent — Lakes, Rivers, Fauna

The scale of the wet Sahara is easiest to grasp at its largest single feature. In the Chad Basin, south of the central massifs, shorelines and lake deposits record a water body the literature calls Lake Mega-Chad. Simon Armitage and colleagues, dating its shorelines and sediments by luminescence methods (Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2015), reconstructed it at highstand at about 361,000 square kilometres in area and up to some 160 metres deep — very probably the largest lake on Earth at the time — against the few thousand square kilometres of the modern Lake Chad that is its shrunken remnant. Humid conditions appear in its basin by about 15,000 years ago; the lake reached its highstand by about 11,500 years ago and held it until about 5,000 years ago, when its level fell rapidly. Its northern basin, dry for roughly the last thousand years, is today the Bodélé Depression — one of the most productive dust sources on the planet, a neat emblem of the reversal.

Mega-Chad was the giant, not the exception. Across the modern desert, the humid period filled playa basins like the one at Nabta Playa, sustained groundwater-fed lakes such as Lake Yoa in northern Chad — whose annually layered sediments give the eastern Sahara its finest continuous climate archive — and set rivers running down wadis that are now dry valleys: the Wadi Irawan system draining the Messak, the watercourses of the Tassili whose plateau name itself, in the usual rendering, means a plateau of rivers. The fauna followed the water. The lake and river deposits, the faunal assemblages from excavated camps, and — with the cautions of §X — the rock art itself agree on the cast: elephant, rhinoceros, giraffe, hippopotamus, crocodile, buffalo including the great extinct Pelorovis (the Bubalus of the older literature), warthog, antelopes and gazelles in variety, and the large predators behind them. This was savanna country of varying wetness — lush along the lakes and rivers, dry grassland between, always seasonal and always capable of failing — and not, anywhere in the central Sahara, a jungle. The qualification matters, because the art is sometimes recruited to prove a paradise. It proves a savanna, which is remarkable enough.

People on the Stage — The Climate-Controlled Model

The human timetable for the eastern half of this world was set by Rudolph Kuper and Stefan Kröpelin, synthesising radiocarbon dates from some 150 excavated sites across the Eastern Sahara of Egypt, Sudan, Libya, and Chad (Science 313, 2006: 803–807). Their finding is stated in their title: occupation was climate-controlled. Settlement appears across the region with the sudden onset of humid conditions at about 8500 BCE; it flourishes through the early and middle Holocene; and from about 5300 BCE a gradual desiccation drives a staged retreat — populations shifting southward toward the Sahel and eastward toward the Nile as the desert margin moves over their former ground. The authors drew the large conclusion plainly: that southward-shifting desert margin helped trigger the emergence of pharaonic civilisation along the Nile and influenced the spread of pastoralism across the continent — the argument this hub's Nile Bridge article examines from the Nile side.

Two cautions keep the model at its real strength. The first is geographical: Kuper and Kröpelin's synthesis is an Eastern Sahara synthesis, built on excavated and dated occupation sites; the central massifs of this article lie at and beyond its western edge, and their occupation history — read mostly from the art and from sparser excavation — need not march in step with the eastern transect in every detail. The second is about the ending. Whether desiccation was abrupt, as the Mauritanian dust core and the Mega-Chad shorelines suggest, or gradual, as the Lake Yoa pollen succession suggests — humid ecosystems there giving way progressively between roughly 5,000 and 3,000 years ago (Kröpelin et al., Science, 2008) — remains a live question in the climate literature, and the honest answer is that both patterns may be true at different places: a system can cross a threshold quickly in one basin and unwind slowly in a groundwater-buffered lake a few hundred kilometres away. For the art, what matters is the direction, which no record disputes: after about 5,500 BP the wet world contracted, century by century, toward the desert of today, and the later imagery — horse, then camel — was made inside that contraction.

II. The Corpus — What Survives and What Does Not

No complete inventory of Saharan rock art exists, and none is close. The counts that can be responsibly given are local: more than 15,000 drawings and engravings identified on the Tassili n'Ajjer alone, in UNESCO's figure; thousands of figures in the single shelter of Wadi Sura II; more than five hundred sites recorded by Gérard Bailloud in one-sixth of the Ennedi in a single pioneering survey. Summed across the central massifs — Tassili, Tadrart Acacus, Messak, Ennedi, Tibesti, Gilf Kebir, Uweinat, the Hoggar, the Aïr — the corpus runs to the tens of thousands of images the brief for this article describes, and every surveyor who has worked a new wadi has added to it. Digital enhancement is still adding to it without moving a stone: when photographs from one famous Tassili site were processed with the DStretch software that accentuates faint pigment, an inventory of 51 subjects recorded by Lhote's team expanded to 165, with figures appearing on walls previously thought blank (reported in Le Quellec's synthesis of the field). The corpus is not a closed canon. It is a sample, still growing, of an original production whose total size is unknowable.

Two Media, Two Survivals

The corpus divides by technique, and the division is a preservation filter before it is an artistic one. Engravings — images pecked, incised, or abraded into exposed rock surfaces: boulders, plateau pavements, the walls of wadis — are cut into the stone itself and survive as long as the surface does. The great engraved bestiaries of the Messak and of the Oued Djerat belong to this medium, their lines darkened or lightened against the surrounding varnish according to age and exposure. Paintings — pigment laid on the rock, in red and yellow ochres, white clays, black manganese and charcoal — survive only where the rock shelters them: under overhangs, in shallow caves, on walls protected from direct sun, rain-wash, and wind-blown sand. The Tassili's painted shelters, the Ennedi's painted canyons, and the Wadi Sura shelters are all, in this sense, accidents of geology — sandstone that happened to weather into roofs.

The consequence is a double bias that every interpretation of this art must carry. First, the painted record is a shelter record: whatever was painted on open faces — and there is no reason to think nothing was — is gone, so the balance between painting and engraving in the surviving corpus says little about their balance in the living culture. Second, the surviving record is a stone record: images on wood, hide, bark, basketry, or skin — the portable art that ethnographic pastoralists produce in quantity — have perished entirely, along with any painting on mud, plaster, or tent leather. What the massifs preserve is the fraction of a visual culture that happened to be executed on durable rock in protected places. It is an enormous fraction, and it is still a fraction. Claims that begin "the Saharans depicted…" should silently continue "…on the rock that survives, in the places that sheltered it."

ENGRAVINGS

Pecked and incised images on exposed rock — wadi walls, boulders, plateau stone. Survival is a function of the surface: where desert varnish reforms over a cut line, the line can be read; where the surface spalls, the image goes with it.

PAINTINGS

Mineral pigments on sheltered walls — red and yellow ochre, white clay, black manganese and charcoal. Survival requires a roof. The painted corpus is therefore a map of sandstone shelters as much as of painters.

STENCILS

Hands — and, at Wadi Sura II, apparently not only hands (§VIII) — outlined by blowing pigment around them. A stencil preserves a body's outline at life size: the most indexical image in the corpus, and among the hardest to date.

THE MISSING MEDIA

Wood, hide, fibre, skin, and plaster leave no Saharan record. The rock corpus, however vast, is the durable residue of a visual culture whose portable majority is lost — a bias stated here and assumed throughout.

Who Made It — The Standing Answer

The makers were the hunter-gatherer and then pastoral populations of the humid Sahara: communities known independently from their camps, their burials, their pottery, their stone tools, and their herds, whose sequence the companion articles in this hub trace for the eastern desert. Nothing in the corpus requires any other authorship, and §VI will deal, in its place, with the one modern attempt to supply some. Within that plain answer, almost everything specific is inference. A painted cattle scene shows that its maker knew cattle intimately; it does not name the maker's language, ethnicity, or polity, and the older literature's habit of assigning each style to a distinct incoming "people" — a Bubalus people, a Round Head people — is one of the habits the critics of §V dismantled. Styles are evidence of communities of practice. They are not passports.

The massifs treated in this article are a selection, made on the strength of their documentation. The Tassili (§III–VI) is the type-site of the whole field and the source of its classic chronology. The Ennedi (§VII) is its eastern-central giant, least excavated and most continuous into living tradition. The Gilf Kebir and Uweinat (§VIII) anchor the east and hold the corpus's most-discussed shelters. The Messak (§IX) holds its most important dated monuments. The neighbouring Tadrart Acacus in Libya — Fabrizio Mori's ground, and the Tassili's twin across an unnatural modern border — is treated here as part of the Tassili-Acacus world rather than given a section of its own; its evidence enters wherever the chronology debate requires it, which is often.

III. Tassili n'Ajjer — The Plateau and Its Imagery

The Tassili n'Ajjer is a sandstone plateau in southeastern Algeria, at the borders of Libya, Niger, and Mali, covering — in the boundaries of its World Heritage inscription — some 72,000 square kilometres. Tassili n Ajjer is a Tamahaq name, conventionally rendered "plateau of the Ajjer," for the Kel Ajjer Tuareg whose country this is. Erosion has cut the plateau into a landscape early visitors described in architectural metaphor and never improved on: "forests of stone," "rock cities" — freestanding towers and blocks of sandstone, deep gorges, natural arches by the hundred, and, in the sheltered hollows between the towers, the shallow rock shelters whose walls carry the paintings. Relict life survives in the gorges on a scale that itself testifies to the wetter past: permanent pools, a flora of hundreds of species, and the Saharan cypress, the tarout, among the rarest trees on Earth, growing nowhere else. The plateau was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1982 and as a Biosphere Reserve in 1986.

UNESCO's summary remains the cleanest statement of the corpus: more than 15,000 drawings and engravings identified, recording — in the inscription's own framing — climatic changes, animal migrations, and the evolution of human life on the edge of the Sahara from about 6000 BCE to the first centuries of the present era. Two features of that framing deserve the reader's care. The start date is the conservative one the inscription adopted; claims for earlier beginnings are part of the chronology debate of §V, not a settled extension of it. And the phrase "drawings and engravings" conceals the plateau's true range: naturalistic animal studies of the first quality, vast multi-figure compositions, tiny schematic signs, masked figures, domestic scenes of camps and herds, chariots, inscriptions in Libyc and Tifinagh scripts running into the recent past — ten thousand years of image-making, by the longest schemes, stacked in the same shelters, often on the same walls.

The Imagery, Walked in Order of the Walls

A reader meeting the Tassili corpus for the first time should begin where its makers' world is most legible: the animals. The engraved gorge of the Oued Djerat, in the plateau's north, carries along some thirty kilometres of wadi wall a bestiary in cut stone — elephants, rhinoceroses, giraffes, hippopotami where the wadi once held water, and the great buffalo Pelorovis antiquus with its sweeping horns, one famous panel showing the buffalo ringed by small dog-headed human figures, a composition to which §IX returns in its Messak context. The paintings add the herds: cattle in their hundreds in the pastoral scenes, shown horned and lyre-horned, piebald and solid, in camps, on the move, being milked, tethered at night lines — the most complete visual ethnography of early African pastoralism that exists anywhere, painted by the pastoralists themselves.

The human imagery divides, even to an untrained eye, into two worlds. The later world is narrative and grounded: hunters with bows, herders with staffs, women and children in camps, warriors with shields, horsemen, camel riders — people with feet on an implied earth, doing recognisable things. The earlier world — the paintings classed, in the classic scheme, as Round Head — is stranger, and §V and §VI are devoted to it: large figures with smooth, featureless, globular heads, bodies that float or swim in empty space, arms raised, figures wearing what read as masks or elaborate coverings, beings of more than human scale attended by tiny humans, animals that match no species. Compositions in this manner lack the ground lines, landscapes, and backgrounds of the later scenes; figures hang in the rock's own field. It is the most distinctive painted manner in the whole Saharan corpus, and the least understood — a combination that has made it, as §VI records, a screen for other people's theories ever since it was first published.

Between and around these worlds lie the plateau's quieter records: the Running Horned Woman of Aouanrhet — a graceful running female figure with horn-like projections, dots streaming from her body, found by Lhote's team in a secluded massif they took for a sanctuary — geometric signs, hand stencils, and the long tail of the tradition running through horse and camel imagery to Tifinagh inscriptions of the historic Tuareg world. The Tassili's claim on the world's attention is not any single panel. It is density sustained over millennia in one walkable landscape: no other place on Earth lets a visitor read so much of a prehistoric image-world in so few days' travel — which is precisely why the fight over how to order that world in time, taken up in §V, has been so long and so consequential.

IV. Lhote and the Copying Enterprise — Historiography

The Tassili's art was never lost. The Kel Ajjer knew the shelters; what happened in the twentieth century was not discovery but publication — the transfer of the paintings from local knowledge into the European archive — and the manner of that transfer shaped everything the world has since thought about them. This section tells it as historiography: who copied what, by what methods, with what consequences, because in this field the copies are themselves primary documents, and some of the most famous "Tassili paintings" in circulation are, in part, documents of 1956 rather than of the Holocene.

From Brenans to the 1956–57 Expedition

The first systematic European recording was military. Between 1933 and 1940, Lieutenant Charles Brenans, a French camel-corps officer, sketched and noted dozens of sites in the plateau's canyons and deposited his records with the Bardo Museum in Algiers, whence they reached the great prehistorian Henri Breuil in Paris. Brenans also guided the young Henri Lhote — explorer, ethnographer, and Breuil's protégé — to the plateau, travelling with Kel Ajjer guides whose knowledge located the art. Lhote returned repeatedly — in 1956–57, 1959, 1962, and 1970 — but it is the first great expedition that fixed the Tassili in the world's imagination: sixteen months in 1956–57, a team of artists and copyists working shelter by shelter, producing hundreds of full-size copies of painted walls. Exhibited in Paris at the Musée des Arts Décoratifs in 1957–58, the copies caused a sensation among artists and scientists alike; Lhote's book, À la découverte des fresques du Tassili (1958; in English, The Search for the Tassili Frescoes, 1959), became the field's founding popular text and remains the source from which most general readers, and most later writers, have taken their Tassili.

The achievement was real, and this article records it at full strength before the audit: the expeditions inventoried a corpus of world importance that might otherwise have faded unrecorded, established the plateau's protection trajectory — national park, World Heritage, biosphere reserve — and gave scholarship a first corpus to argue about. Without the copies, there is no debate in §V, because there is no shared body of images to debate. Every later worker in the central Sahara, including Lhote's sharpest critics, has worked downstream of the enterprise of 1956–57.

The Copies as Documents — and the Damage Debate

The audit, stated factually and without relish, has three heads. First, the recording method damaged originals. To make faint paintings legible for tracing and photography, the team routinely wetted the walls — Lhote's own narrative describes setting out with a can of water and a sponge — and in places traced or heightened lines directly. Wetting raises pigment colour dramatically for a few minutes and accelerates its flaking and fading thereafter; repeated wetting by later visitors, following the pioneers' example, compounded the loss. Modern assessments, including Jeremy Keenan's severe retrospective in Antiquity (2002), describe the practice in the vocabulary of vandalism; the factual core beneath the vocabulary — walls washed, colours brightened for the copy at the original's expense, archaeological material collected and removed in quantity from the shelters — is not seriously disputed, and its worst cost is scientific: surface material that might one day have carried datable organic traces was, in places, washed or handled away before the questions that needed it had been formulated (§XI).

Second, the copies are interpretations. A full-size hand copy is a reading: the copyist decides where a faded line runs, what counts as figure and what as stain, and — in documented instances in this corpus — completes or sharpens what the wall only suggests. Some of the most widely reproduced Tassili images owe part of their crispness and drama to the copy rather than the original, and at least a few figures published from the expeditions have been alleged, by named critics from the 1960s onward, to be modern additions or jokes worked into the record. The allegations attach to specific figures, not to the corpus; they have never been shown to touch its great majority; and the correct scholarly response — the one the field has in fact adopted — is neither to discard the copies nor to trust them blindly, but to treat each as a document requiring verification against the wall, exactly as §XI describes modern re-recording doing, with photography and DStretch where the pioneers used sponge and tracing paper.

Third, the naming did interpretive work. Lhote was a romantic writer of genuine gifts, and his names — the "Great God," the "Horned Goddess," the joking "Martians" for the Round Head figures — taught the public how to see the paintings before scholars had agreed on what they were. §VI follows one of those names into its long afterlife. The point here is archival: in the Tassili literature, the layer of 1950s mediation — copies, names, and narrative — lies between every modern reader and the walls, and honest work in the field consists largely of keeping that layer visible. That is the spirit in which Lhote is cited in this article's Sources: as historiography — the indispensable, compromised record of how this corpus entered the world's knowledge — and not as a dating authority.

One further count in the modern audit belongs in the text, not the margin. The expeditions' published narratives centred the European "discoverer" and treated the Kel Ajjer guides — without whom the shelters would not have been found, reached, or survived in — as auxiliaries, in language Keenan's critique quotes to the record's discredit; the Swiss ethnologist Yolande Tschudi, whose Tassili work preceded Lhote's, went unacknowledged in his discovery claims. The paintings' first custodians were the plateau's own people. This article's phrasing throughout — the art was published, not discovered — is deliberate.

V. The Classic Periodisation and Its Critics

Every account of Saharan rock art, this one included, must organise tens of thousands of undated images into some order, and the order the world inherited is the classic periodisation: a succession of five great manners, each named for its signature subject, marching from wild fauna to the modern desert's animal. The scheme grew from the work of the pioneers — Breuil, Lhote, and Fabrizio Mori in the Acacus — and was consolidated in the grand synthesis of Paul Huard and Jean Leclant in the 1970s. It is presented here in full, because it remains the field's shared vocabulary; and it is presented, as the brief for this article requires, as a scheme — a scholarly instrument with named critics and a revised successor — and never as settled chronology.

What the Scheme Got Right

The periodisation endures because its relative order is anchored in observation. Where images overlap on a wall, the pastoral manner does overlie the wilder manners, and horse and camel imagery does sit latest in the superimposition sequences; the faunal succession the scheme encodes — wild megafauna, then cattle, then horse, then camel — matches the independently known ecological succession of the drying Sahara (§X). As a first map of a vast corpus, drawn by scholars who had walked the massifs when no map existed, it was a formidable achievement, and its terms remain the only ones in which specialists from different schools can reliably understand one another. What failed, under audit, was not the map's outline but its legend: the absolute dates hung on each period, the assumption that each manner was one period, and the peoples marched in behind each style.

Muzzolini's Revision — The Short Chronology

The audit began in earnest with Alfred Muzzolini, whose L'art rupestre préhistorique des massifs centraux sahariens (1986) subjected the classic scheme to the archaeological record it had largely floated free of. His principal findings, as Le Quellec's later synthesis fairly summarises them: there was no consistent archaeological basis for the single vast hunting culture the Huard–Leclant narrative had imagined behind the early art; the Bubaline was more a style than a period, one that could still have been in use when pastoralists were already working in other manners — which dissolves the neat succession of the table above into overlapping regional practices; and the Bovidian was not one manner but several stylistic and cultural nuclei, whose separate delineation offered the only credible route to a finer chronology. The systematic result was a short (or recent) chronology: central Saharan rock art, on Muzzolini's argument, emerges around 5000 BCE — not in the terminal Pleistocene, not twelve thousand years ago — and the long chronology's early millennia are an artefact of stylistic assumption. After initial resistance, in Le Quellec's assessment, the scientific community has largely accepted Muzzolini's view. The British Museum's chronology page states the residual disagreement exactly: the largest discrepancy among Lhote, Mori, and Muzzolini concerns the Bubalus and Round Head material, where Mori's early/long chronology begins the art some 12,000 years ago and Muzzolini's recent/short chronology begins it some 6,000 years ago — a six-thousand-year gap, on the same walls.

Le Quellec and the New Chronology

Jean-Loïc Le Quellec — Muzzolini's ally in the short chronology and, in the decades since, the central Saharan field's most prolific reviser — has carried the revision from polemic into programme. Three of his lines of work matter here. The first is cartographic: mapping motif distributions across the massifs (pioneered with Yves and Christine Gauthier) to test whether supposed periods are in fact regions — manners that differ in space masquerading as manners that differ in time. The second is contextual dating: the circular stone monuments of the Messak called corbeilles, some carrying stelae engraved in the so-called Messak manner, have yielded radiocarbon brackets of 4,550–4,370 and 4,220–3,960 BCE — which date that manner, long assigned on style to the remote Bubaline, to the third horizon of the Neolithic (§IX). The third is computational: phylogenetic algorithms, borrowed from evolutionary biology, applied to coded corpora of related images — therianthropes of the Messak and Tassili among them — to generate testable hypotheses about which manners precede which, hypotheses that gain force where they coincide with the cartographic and contextual results.

The new chronology Le Quellec assembles from this work is, in his own description, still imprecise in places but coherent: the earliest central Saharan art is the Round Head manner, originating in the Acacus and Tassili and beginning around 5500 BCE; the Messak manner follows from around 4250 BCE; cattle imagery flourishes across the pastoral manners through the long interval between the arid events of c. 5200 BCE and c. 2200 BCE, with sheep and goats growing prominent in later pastoral styles such as the Iheren paintings from the middle of the fourth millennium BCE onward; the Caballine horizon follows the post-Neolithic arid event; and the Cameline horizon, in this revision, begins only around the fifth century CE, when the camel — present earlier at the desert's edges — becomes a motif of the central massifs' art. Readers should hold this summary as its author holds it: a working synthesis, explicitly open to correction by the next dated monument, and already at variance with the classic scheme at both ends — six millennia younger at its beginning, half a millennium later at its end.

The "Round Head" Problem

The Round Head manner concentrates every difficulty of the field into a single label, and it deserves its problem stated separately. The label names a head shape: smooth, round, usually featureless heads on painted human figures. Around that one trait, the classic scheme assembled a period, a people, and a span of millennia. Each element has been questioned. The trait itself varies — heads with indications of features, with horns or projections, with attached coverings, on bodies of very different rendering — so that the "manner" may be several manners sharing a convention for heads. The figures' contexts vary: some float in dense multi-figure compositions, some stand alone at monumental scale (§VI). The distribution is wide — Acacus, Tassili, and related material beyond — so that a single originating culture is an assumption, not a finding. And the date is the short-versus-long dispute in miniature: assigned by the classic scheme to deep antiquity on grounds of style and strangeness, redated by the revisionists to a beginning around 5500 BCE on grounds of context and association, and directly dated — like almost everything in this article — nowhere. What the Round Head paintings certainly are is early within the superimposition sequences of their shelters, stylistically distinct from the pastoral world that follows them, and the work of communities about whose beliefs the images tempt every viewer to speculate and entitle no viewer to conclude. Interpretations offered in the literature — masked dancers, deities, ancestors, figures in altered states — are reported in this archive as interpretations. The paintings keep their counsel, and §VI shows what happened when the twentieth century refused to let them.

// HOW TO READ DATES IN THIS ARTICLE //

From this section onward, a date attached to a style ("Round Head, from c. 5500 BCE") is a citation of the revised synthesis, not a measurement; a date attached to a monument or deposit ("the corbeille stelae, 4550–3960 BCE") is a radiocarbon bracket on associated material; and a date attached to an animal's presence is a faunal datum. The three kinds of date look alike in print and are not alike in strength. The article flags the kind wherever the distinction carries weight.

VI. The Famous Panels — Sefar, Jabbaren, and the Career of an Image

Two localities in the Tassili's southeast hold the paintings by which the whole corpus is known to the world, and they repay the discipline this article has been building: describe what is painted, at the size it is painted, before saying what anyone has made of it. The localities are Sefar and Jabbaren, clusters of shelters among the stone forests east of Djanet, and their great figures belong to the Round Head manner as §V has defined — and problematised — that term.

The "Great God" of Sefar, Described as Painted

The figure called the "Great God" of Sefar is a single monumental anthropomorph, more than three metres tall — among the largest painted figures in the Sahara — rendered in outline and pale pigment on a shelter wall. Its head is a large, smooth, rounded form without depicted facial features, carrying horn-like or antler-like projections; its arms are raised; its body is covered by a continuous surface of markings that have been read, by different viewers, as a garment, a hide, a mask's extension over the whole figure, or the figure's own patterned skin. Smaller human figures in the same shelter stand in a different scale-world entirely — the great figure dwarfs them as a being of another order dwarfs attendants, supplicants, or simply later, smaller additions to the wall; the superimposition relations of the Sefar shelters are complex, and not every figure on a famous wall was painted in the same episode, or the same millennium. That is the description. Everything beyond it — god, masked celebrant, ancestor, spirit — is interpretation layered on a figure whose makers left no caption, and the name "Great God" itself is one of Lhote's (§IV): a brilliant label that decided, in advance, the category to which the figure would be assigned by everyone who met it first in his book.

Jabbaren and the So-Called "Martian God"

At Jabbaren, a comparable great Round Head figure — round helmet-like head, featureless face-field, heavy body — received from Lhote, half in jest, the label that would outrun all his serious work: the "Great Martian God." Lhote himself presented the name as an evocation — the figure put him in mind of the Martians of contemporary science fiction — and Le Quellec's retelling preserves the joke's register exactly: the explorer called them "Martians" jokingly, and some hurried readers took the name seriously. The panels themselves, described as painted, show what Sefar shows: a monumental round-headed anthropomorph of the same manner, executed in the same pigments, in a shelter full of the same floating, masked, and ceremonial company. Nothing in the Jabbaren wall — no instrument, no vehicle, no star, no object that resists a Holocene pastoral or hunting context — accompanies the figure. Its fame is not in its iconography. It is in its nickname.

The career that nickname launched belongs, in this archive's discipline, to Tier C, and it is given here in the one paragraph the brief allows it — plainly, and without mockery, because the readers it reached were not fools but people handed a joke as a label and a copy as a photograph. In the ancient-astronaut literature that followed Erich von Däniken's Chariots of the Gods? (1968), the Jabbaren figure and its Round Head kin were reproduced — usually from the Lhote-era copies rather than from the walls — as helmeted visitors: the round head a space helmet, the continuous body-marking a pressure suit, the floating pose a figure in weightlessness. The reading requires every element of the description above to be replaced by a twentieth-century object, supplies the one accompaniment the wall does not contain (a technology), and dates the encounter by the very chronology the specialists dispute, always at its longest. As a chapter in the modern reception of these paintings it is fully documented and, in its way, instructive: it demonstrates how completely a mediated image — copied, renamed, and reproduced without its shelter, its superimpositions, or its corpus — can be made to mean whatever its mediator requires. The tradition that built a cosmology on such readings has its own home in this hub, at Mu and Atlantis Reborn, where it is treated, as here, as a modern construction with a documented history — and the paintings themselves remain where they always were: Tier A images, of contested date and unrecovered meaning, on walls in the Sefar and Jabbaren shelters.

A quieter afterlife deserves the last word in this section, because it is the one the evidence supports. The great Round Head figures have become, in the serious literature, the type case for a methodological virtue: restraint. Mori, Muzzolini, Le Quellec, and the DStretch re-recorders disagree about their date, their unity, and their context — and agree, in practice, on describing them fully, publishing them accurately, and leaving the space where a meaning would go respectfully empty until evidence fills it. A figure more than three metres tall, painted by hunter-herders of the green Sahara on a wall their descendants repainted for millennia, does not need to be a god, a Martian, or a shaman to be one of the great images of the human past. It needs to be looked at as painted. This article has tried to model the looking.

VII. Ennedi — Arches, Pastoral Scenes, and a Living Landscape

The Ennedi is a sandstone massif in northeastern Chad, at the Sahara's southern-central edge — terraced plateaus, ragged cliffs, and wadis eroded into one of the most sculptural landscapes in Africa, inscribed with the surrounding cultural landscape as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2016. It differs from the Tassili in a way that structures everything about its art: the Ennedi still catches summer rain, sparse but regular, and its deep gorges hold permanent and semi-permanent water — the gueltas — so that the massif never became the absolute desert the northern plateaus became. It is a refuge: for a relict fauna that includes the Sahara's last crocodiles, survivors in the gorge pools of the humid period's river world, and for human communities whose presence in the massif runs, without a clean break, from the painters into the present.

The Art of the Ennedi

The British Museum's African Rock Art survey gives the Ennedi corpus its standard outline: thousands of engravings and paintings, the oldest depictions reaching back to the fifth millennium BCE in the survey's assessment, in a profusion of local styles unmatched elsewhere in Chad. Bailloud's periodisation of the massif, still the working frame, runs in three broad movements. An Archaic movement carries the wild fauna — elephant, giraffe, rhinoceros, and the hunters who followed them. A long Pastoral movement, the corpus's centre of gravity, fills the shelters with cattle — herds, camps, huts, people in groups around their animals, and, in its later centuries, armed men with shields in hunting and fighting scenes. A final movement, conventionally the Camel period, brings the mounted art for which the Ennedi is especially celebrated: riders on camels and on horses rendered in exceptional detail — saddles, bridles, weapons, dress — including the manner specialists call the "Flying Gallop" style, its mounts stretched at full stride. Notably, and in contrast to the tidy Tassili succession, horse and camel imagery in the Ennedi are not cleanly separated into successive periods; the two mounts overlap, sometimes on the same walls — a local fact that §V's critics would recognise as the rule rather than the exception, and a standing warning against exporting any one massif's sequence across the desert.

Sites such as Gaora Halagana, with its dense palimpsests of superimposed periods, and Manda Guele, noted for the quality of its late mounted paintings, stand in the literature for hundreds of localities less published and less visited. The Ennedi's documentation lags the Tassili's by generations — its isolation far from the main routes kept its art unknown outside the region until the 1930s, and systematic work began only with Bailloud in 1956–57 — and the honest summary of its chronology is the one this article has given the whole corpus: a relative order, anchored in superimposition and subject, with absolute dates sparser here than anywhere else in this article. What the Ennedi lacks in dated precision it returns in continuity, and continuity is its real distinction.

A Living Tradition, Noted Respectfully

The Ennedi is not an abandoned archive. The massif is the country of the Toubou — the Teda and Daza peoples of the central Sahara — pastoralists whose seasonal movement between the gorge waters with herds of camels and goats continues the adaptive pattern the paintings record: water found in stone, herds moved to grass, camps made and struck in the same sheltered places. The painted tradition itself did not stop at some prehistoric deadline; imagery in the massif runs, in the World Heritage framing, from about 7000 BCE to the present, and recent and modern marking stands in the same shelters as the ancient herds. This article records that continuity without romanticising or annexing it: the Toubou are not a survival, a remnant, or an illustration of the Neolithic — they are the living community of a living landscape, in whose custodianship, day to day, the art actually rests. The crocodiles in the Guelta d'Archei and the herds that crowd down to drink beside them make the same point in another register: the Ennedi is the one great rock-art landscape of this article where the humid period never entirely ended, and its art should be read accordingly — not as a tomb's inscription, but as the oldest stratum of a record still being written.

VIII. Gilf Kebir and Uweinat — Wadi Sura I and II

In Egypt's far southwest, some 700 kilometres from the Nile, the sandstone plateau of the Gilf Kebir — roughly 7,500 square kilometres of tabular massif, incised by wadis whose flanks hold natural shelters — carries the eastern Sahara's most famous painted localities. A day's journey southeast, at the tripoint of Egypt, Libya, and Sudan, the granite-and-sandstone massif of Jebel Uweinat carries another dense concentration, surveyed in the same heroic age of desert exploration. Together they form the eastern anchor of this article's map, and the link in the chain between the central massifs and the Nile world of Nabta Playa — close enough to the valley that their imagery has always tempted scholars to read it as the Nile's prehistory, a temptation this section reports and, in the archive's manner, declines to indulge beyond the evidence.

1933 — Almásy and the Historiography of an Expedition

The Gilf's painted shelters entered the record in 1933, in the course of an expedition whose ostensible quarry was not art at all: the lost oasis of Zerzura, the "white city" of desert legend, sought by the Hungarian explorer László Almásy and the loose fraternity of desert travellers around the Zerzura Club. Almásy — aviator, motorist, and the most capable desert navigator of his generation — failed to find Zerzura and found, instead, in a wadi on the Gilf's western flank, a series of painted shelters so striking that he named the valley for them: Wadi Sura, the Valley of Pictures. In October 1933 he documented its principal shelter; in the same expedition season he led Leo Frobenius and Hans Rhotert to the region's sites, and the documentation published from that work — Rhotert's full publication followed in 1952 — fixed the Gilf in the scholarly record. Almásy's own account (1936) belongs, like Lhote's, to this article's historiography shelf: a primary document of the discovery era, written by a man who read the paintings' implication instantly and correctly — figures in water, in a waterless land, meant the land had changed — and whose later career, fictionalised beyond recognition in a famous novel and film, has nothing to do with the shelters and will receive nothing here.

Wadi Sura I — The "Cave of Swimmers"

The shelter Almásy documented — Wadi Sura I, improperly but immortally called the "Cave of Swimmers" — is a shallow overhang, a few metres deep, not a cave, its walls carrying small painted figures in red and dark pigment. The famous motifs are human figures disposed horizontally, arms swept and legs trailing, in postures that struck their first recorder, and every visitor since, as figures floating or swimming; cattle, giraffe, and other figures accompany them, and a partly destroyed headless beast — the motif that Wadi Sura II would multiply into a bestiary — appears here without parallel or explanation. Almásy's literal reading — swimmers imply water; water implies a wetter Sahara — was, as climate history, vindicated completely: the shelter overlooks a playa basin, and the Gilf's occupation belongs to the humid world of §I. As iconography, the literal reading has not held the field. András Zboray, the shelter's most devoted modern documenter, argues the figures are symbolic rather than observational — their meaning unrecovered, their horizontality a convention rather than a snapshot — and other readers have taken them for the floating dead, for dreamers, for spirits. The paintings, as at Sefar, keep their counsel; what is certain is the use to which the literal reading was first put, as the public's earliest and most vivid proof that the desert had once been green. On that point, if on no other, Almásy was simply right.

Wadi Sura II — The "Cave of Beasts"

In 2002, ten kilometres from the Swimmers, Massimo and Jacopo Foggini with Ahmed Mestekawi found the shelter that specialists now regard as the Gilf's greater treasure: Wadi Sura II, called the "Cave of Beasts" — a shelter some twenty metres wide overlooking a former lake basin, its rear wall carrying thousands of painted figures in red, yellow, white, and black. Published counts vary with the counting method — some five thousand figures is the commonly cited order, with fuller counts running higher — and the variety exceeds the number: files and dances of human figures, rows of cattle, giraffe and other fauna, the swimmers' motif again, hundreds of hand and foot stencils, and, presiding over the wall, the creatures that named the cave: large headless beasts, shown swallowing or disgorging human figures, without counterpart in the living fauna of any period. The University of Cologne's Wadi Sura Project, directed from the same research world as Kuper and Kröpelin's climate work, documented the shelter exhaustively; Förster, Riemer, and Kuper's assessment (2012) attributes the Wadi Sura imagery, on landscape and contextual grounds, to a hunter-gatherer society of c. 6500–4400 cal BCE — the "Gilf B" phase of the regional sequence — making the Cave of Beasts, unusually for this corpus, a shelter with a argued and published chronological home rather than a stylistic guess.

One finding from Wadi Sura II has travelled far beyond the field, and it earns its place here as a model of what close looking can still do to a famous corpus. Among the shelter's hundreds of hand stencils are small ones, long assumed to be the hands of human infants — baby hands pressed to the wall by their parents, in the sentimental reading the site's literature had settled into. A morphometric study published in 2016 compared those stencils, measurement by measurement, against the hands of babies born at term and preterm in a modern neonatal unit, and found that the small stencils differ significantly in size, proportions, and morphology from any human hand: the evidence points, the authors concluded, to a non-human stencil — most probably the forelimb of a reptile, with the desert monitor lizard, still living in the region, the closest match. If the identification holds — it is a single study, and this article reports it as one — Wadi Sura II preserves the only known non-human hand stencils in world rock art: the Gilf's hunter-gatherers stencilled a lizard's foot beside their own hands, for reasons as unrecovered as the headless beasts', and the sentimentality of the baby reading stands as this corpus's neatest lesson in the difference between a description and a story.

// UWEINAT, IN BRIEF //

Jebel Uweinat, southeast of the Gilf at the three-state tripoint, holds paintings and engravings in the same manners — cattle, giraffe, human figures, and, in its wadis, some of the eastern Sahara's densest concentrations — documented from the Almásy–Frobenius surveys of 1933–34 onward and still incompletely published. Its art is cited in this article as part of the Gilf–Uweinat eastern province; a full treatment awaits the survey literature its remoteness has so far denied it.

IX. The Messak and the Fezzan — Engraved Fauna and Dated Monuments

Southwest Libya's Messak Settafet is a plateau unlike any other in this article: a hamada of black stone, its surface filmed over kilometres with the dark coating called desert varnish, set in the Fezzan between the Tadrart Acacus to the west and the dune seas of the Murzuq to the east and south. The black ground is the art's ground. Engravers cut through the varnish into the paler stone beneath, and their lines stand out light against the dark plateau — or darken again as varnish slowly reforms over the cut, in the process on which §XI's patina arguments depend. The Messak is also a quarry landscape: its quartzite outcrops were worked from deep prehistory into the pastoral Neolithic, its tools circulating widely, so that the plateau was never merely a gallery. It was a workplace that became a ceremonial landscape, and the two histories are written in the same stone.

The Engraved Fauna — From Barth to the Wadi Mathendusc

The Messak holds this article's earliest European record. Heinrich Barth, crossing the plateau's southern fringe in 1850 on the journey published in 1857, found engravings in the Wadi Mathendusc (also rendered Mathendous) — among them the great naturalistic animals that made the wadi famous: elephants, rhinoceroses, giraffes, and buffalo of the first quality, cut at large scale into the wadi's walls. Paolo Graziosi's surveys of the 1930s–42 put the Fezzan's engravings into the scholarly literature, and the Mathendusc bestiary has stood since as the type assemblage of the engraved manner the classic scheme called Bubaline — the attribution §V has already flagged: a manner, on the revisionists' showing, is not yet a date. The Messak's engraved repertoire runs far beyond the great fauna. Its most distinctive inhabitants are the therianthropes: figures with the heads of wild dogs, in Le Quellec's analysis, standing in a corpus parallel to the jackal-headed figures of the Tassili — two neighbouring massifs elaborating, in his phylogenetic reconstruction, a shared therianthropic motif in local dialects, the Messak versions the earlier of the two. Whatever these beings meant, they were not doodles: they recur, they are composed, and they belong to the same thought-world as the dog-headed attendants ringing the Oued Djerat buffalo (§III) — a central Saharan symbolic vocabulary whose grammar we can increasingly describe and whose semantics remain, honestly, closed.

The Dated Monuments — Di Lernia's Breakthrough

The Messak is where the corpus's dating problem received its most important partial solution, and the solution's shape is the methodological heart of this article. The plateau is dense with stone monuments — tumuli, rings, and circular platforms — among them the structures called corbeilles, circular platforms into which upright stones were set, some of those stones engraved with cattle in the Pastoral manner. Savino di Lernia and Marina Gallinaro, of the Italian-Libyan Archaeological Mission in the Acacus and Messak, excavated such monuments and did what rock-art scholarship had rarely been able to do: they dated the context in which engraved stones stood. The result, published in Antiquity 84 (2010), is quoted here at its published strength: the Pastoral-style engravings on upright stones incorporated into the platforms belong to the period 5430–5150 BP — and probably earlier — the platforms themselves being, on the excavators' reading, places of animal sacrifice within a dense monumental landscape that served the ritual needs of a pastoral population in a harsh environment with little settlement of its own.

The companion excavation programme filled in the world those platforms served. Di Lernia and colleagues' study of the Messak's animal burials (PLoS ONE, 2013) documented a formalised ritual of the Middle Pastoral, dated 6080–5120 BP (c. 5200–3800 BCE): disarticulated animal remains — mostly cattle — deposited repeatedly in stone monuments across the plateau, analysed by radiocarbon, zooarchaeology, and strontium, carbon, and oxygen isotopes, the isotopes showing herds moved across the landscape to the monuments. Read together, the two studies give the central Sahara something it has nowhere else at this density: a pastoral ritual landscape — monuments, burials, and engraved art in excavated association — with radiocarbon brackets on its floruit. The parallel with Nabta Playa's cattle tumuli, a thousand kilometres east, is structural and striking — pastoral peoples at both ends of the green Sahara monumentalising cattle in stone — and this article states it as a parallel of practice, not a claim of contact, because no evidence of contact is in hand.

Two closing notes belong to the Messak's record. Its varnish has been a laboratory in its own right: Mauro Cremaschi's study of the Messak varnish (Geoarchaeology, 1996) established its nature, formation, and palaeoenvironmental implications, grounding — and bounding — every patina argument made from this plateau (§XI). And its modern history carries the corpus's sharpest conservation warning: the discovery of a major oil field beneath the plateau brought seismic survey and development pressure onto the monuments and engravings themselves, documented by the mission as damage already done before protection could follow. The Messak's art survived seven thousand years of desert. Its most dangerous century is the present one.

X. The Art as Climate Archive — Reading the Faunal Sequence

It is time to use the art for the purpose to which it is most often put — as testimony about the environment — and to do so under the discipline the last nine sections have built. The famous sequence is real, and it is usually drawn as a frieze: elephant, giraffe, and hippopotamus in the early manners; cattle dominating the long pastoral middle; the horse appearing as the land dries; the camel closing the sequence as the desert reaches its modern severity. UNESCO's Tassili inscription encodes the same reading — the art as testimony to climate change and animal migration — and at the scale of the whole corpus and the whole Holocene, the reading is sound. The depicted succession matches, in order and in broad timing, the succession the independent records establish: a humid savanna fauna in the early Holocene; domestic cattle present in the Sahara from the seventh–sixth millennia BCE onward on the faunal evidence; the horse a late, datable introduction of ultimately Eurasian origin; the dromedary later still, spreading with the caravan world of the deep desert. A picture archive and a sediment archive, made by different processes and studied by different sciences, tell the same large story. That convergence is the strongest thing that can be said for the art as environmental testimony, and this article says it without reservation.

What cannot be said — and what the dating honesty of the introduction requires be refused at the point of use — is the finer claim often built on the frieze: that a given panel dates the presence of its animal in its valley, or that the sequence can be read panel by panel as a rain gauge. Three caveats, each already earned in earlier sections, apply exactly here. First, depiction is not census. Artists paint what signifies, not what surrounds: cattle dominate pastoral art because cattle signified wealth and identity, sheep and goats — the everyday herd — are under-painted for millennia, and the hippopotamus at a wadi may record a resident population, a remembered one, or a being of story. Second, the styles are not dated independently of the fauna. To date a manner by its animals and then cite the manner as evidence for the animals' dates is the circularity Muzzolini's revision exposed; the frieze's broad order survives the audit because sediments and bones confirm it, not because the walls date themselves. Third, presence is not contemporaneity across distance. The Sahara dried unevenly — the Ennedi kept its gueltas and its crocodiles while the northern plateaus emptied — so a "late" animal in one massif and an "early" animal in another may be neighbours in time, their difference a map of water, not a clock.

Used at its proper strength, then, the art contributes three things no sediment core can. It shows the humid world inhabited — not fauna lists but relationships: herds tended and milked, game hunted with dogs and bows, animals buried in monuments, beings compounded of human and animal that map a symbolic ecology beside the physical one. It shows attention: the species the painters watched most closely, rendered with an accuracy that still lets zoologists name them, are a record of what mattered to the watchers. And it shows duration from inside: the same shelters repainted as the world dried, cattle walls overlaid by horsemen, horsemen by camel riders, until the last Tifinagh names stand over the oldest buffalo — a single place remembering its own transformation. Compare, in this hub, the very different testimony of Göbekli Tepe, where a hunter-gatherer world carved its animals in stone at the threshold of farming: the Sahara's image-world is that testimony's pastoral counterpart, spread across a subcontinent and ten millennia, and legible — at the strength stated here — as the green Sahara's own account of itself.

// THE SEQUENCE, AT ITS EXACT STRENGTH //

Established by independent evidence: the humid savanna and its megafauna (sediments, lake records, faunal assemblages); domestic cattle in the Sahara by the early pastoral Neolithic; horse and then camel as successive late introductions. Shown by the art in convergence with that evidence: the same succession, depicted in the same broad order, across thousands of walls. Not shown by the art alone: the date of any undated panel; the local extinction date of any depicted species; a year-by-year or valley-by-valley climate record. The frieze is a corroborating witness, not the court record — and a corroborating witness of the first importance is not a small thing.

XI. Method — Recording and (Rarely) Dating Rock Art

This section gathers, in one place, the methods on which every claim in the preceding ten sections rests — how the images are recorded, and how, on the rare occasions when it can be done at all, they are dated. It is placed near the end deliberately: a reader who has watched the methods work — and fail, and be revised — through the Tassili chronology fight and the Messak excavations is better placed to weigh them than a reader handed them as a preface.

Recording — From Tracing Paper to DStretch

The recording tradition runs in three generations. The first is the pioneers' — sketch, tracing, and full-size hand copy, with the costs §IV audited: the copy as interpretation, the sponge as solvent. The second is photographic and cartographic: systematic photography in raking and diffuse light, measured drawing from photographs rather than from the wall, and the mapping of motif distributions across whole massifs — the Gauthiers' cartographic method — which turned isolated famous panels into populations of images that could be counted, compared, and regionally sorted. The third, current generation is digital: high-resolution photogrammetry and reflectance imaging that model a wall's surface as well as its colours, and pigment-enhancement processing — DStretch foremost — which stretches the colour space of a photograph until pigment invisible to the naked eye separates from the rock. The DStretch result cited in §II — one Tassili site's inventory rising from 51 recorded subjects to 165 — is the emblem of the generation: the walls still hold more than the pioneers, working at the limit of the unaided eye, could see, and every "complete" inventory in the older literature is now understood to be a first pass. Modern recording is also, by deliberate contrast with 1956, non-contact: nothing touches the wall, nothing wets it, and the archive is made of light.

Relative Dating — Superimposition, Patina, Style, Fauna

Four relative methods do the corpus's daily chronological work, and each has a named weakness that the literature states as plainly as the method. Superimposition — where one image overlies another, the lower is earlier — is the soundest of the four and the narrowest: it orders the images on one wall, in one shelter, and says nothing directly about any other wall; the great regional sequences are built by chaining hundreds of local superimposition orders through shared manners, a chain whose every link is an act of stylistic judgement. Patina and varnish — the darkening of an engraved line as varnish reforms, the weathering of a painted surface — give a genuine relative signal within a single rock face under uniform exposure, and a treacherous one across faces, aspects, and microclimates; Cremaschi's Messak varnish work (§IX) is the model of how much local science a patina argument needs beneath it, and the history of the method includes quantitative varnish-dating techniques, once widely promoted, whose results the field no longer sustains. Style — attribution to a manner with an argued place in a sequence — is indispensable and, as §V showed, capable of generating six-thousand-year disagreements when it floats free of context. Depicted fauna and material culture — the extinct buffalo cannot be painted from life after its extinction; the chariot, the horse, the camel, the firearm each carry a terminus post quem from their known introductions — give real brackets, and real circularity when the brackets are then used to date the fauna's own history (§X). No responsible worker uses any one of the four alone; the chronologies that command assent are convergences of all four, stated with their seams showing.

Absolute Dating — The Rare Successes and the Usual Silence

Direct dating of a painting requires datable material in the paint: an organic binder, a charcoal pigment, a beeswax or plant-gum medium carrying radiocarbon. Saharan pigments are overwhelmingly mineral — ochres and manganese carry no carbon clock — and the attempts made on Tassili material have, in the published record, failed to return reliable direct dates; a 2012 Tassili study's experience, in which radiocarbon attempts on the paintings failed and the team fell back on dating associated materials, is the field's normal experience, not its embarrassment. What succeeds, instead, is dating by association, excavated: the di Lernia method of §IX, in which an engraved stone stands in a monument whose deposits yield AMS radiocarbon brackets; sediments banked against a decorated wall, dated by luminescence (OSL) to give a terminus for the art they bury; occupation deposits in a painted shelter, dating the human presence that the paintings accompany without dating the paintings themselves — Förster, Riemer, and Kuper's landscape attribution of Wadi Sura (§VIII) is this method at its most developed. Pigment analysis contributes character rather than chronology: microscopy and spectroscopy can identify an ochre's source, distinguish a painting's episodes by their recipes, and expose a modern addition by its chemistry — the test to which any suspected Lhote-era "completion" (§IV) would properly be put. The summary is the one the introduction promised: a handful of honourable brackets, honestly won, around a corpus that is otherwise dated by convergence of relative methods — and a field whose maturity is measurable by how plainly it now says so.

// A NOTE ON CONFIDENCE, FOR THE READER CITING THIS ARTICLE //

Dates in this article sort into three grades, and citations should preserve the grade. Grade one — radiocarbon or luminescence brackets on excavated contexts: the Messak platforms and burials, the Lake Yoa and Mega-Chad records, the Kuper–Kröpelin occupation synthesis. Grade two — argued scholarly syntheses from converging relative methods: Le Quellec's new chronology, the Wadi Sura attribution, Bailloud's Ennedi frame. Grade three — the classic periodisation's absolute spans, retained as vocabulary and reported as contested wherever they appear. An argument that needs a grade-three date to stand does not stand, and this archive does not build on one.

XII. What the Image-World Does and Does Not Show

The Green Sahara's image-world shows, first and beyond serious dispute, that the now-hyperarid central Sahara was inhabited, across the humid millennia, by communities with a rich, sustained, and regionally varied visual culture — hunter-herders and then pastoralists who painted and engraved on a scale that makes their massifs one of the world's great prehistoric art provinces. It shows those communities in their own rendering: their herds and hunts, their camps and ceremonies, their masked and monumental beings, their hands pressed to the wall beside — at Wadi Sura — a lizard's. It shows, in convergence with the sediment and bone records, the great ecological succession of the drying continent, wild megafauna giving way to cattle, cattle country to horse, horse to camel. And in the Messak's excavated monuments and Nabta's tumuli together, it shows pastoral peoples at both ends of the green Sahara investing their surplus and their grief in stone for their cattle — the deepest stratum of the African cattle complex, attested in the ground.

What it does not show is equally definite, and this article has tried to keep the negatives as visible as the positives. It does not show its own dates: with a handful of excavated exceptions, the corpus floats in relative order, and the six-thousand-year spread between the long and short chronologies is the honest measure of that float. It does not show named peoples, languages, kings, or gods; every such name in the older literature — Bubalus people, Round Head people, "Great God," "Horned Goddess" — is a modern label doing work the evidence did not assign it. It does not show visitors from anywhere but the Sahara: no image in the corpus requires, or survives the attempt to supply, an Egyptian, Mediterranean, or extraterrestrial author, and the diffusionist and astronaut readings alike dissolve on contact with the walls as painted. It does not show a single Saharan culture: the massifs differ, the manners overlap, the Ennedi refuses the Tassili's tidy succession — the image-world is plural, and its plurality is a finding, not a defect. And it does not, by itself, show the road to Egypt: that the drying Sahara fed peoples and practices toward the Nile is the argued thesis of the climate-controlled model and of this hub's Nile Bridge, resting on the settlement and faunal records — the art corroborates the world that thesis describes, and corroboration, here as everywhere in this archive, is not proof.

Perhaps that is the image-world's deepest instruction for this hub, and it is a methodological one. Here is a corpus of the first importance — tens of thousands of images, a whole continent's early Holocene painted by its inhabitants — whose honest study requires holding two postures at once: full seriousness about what the images are, and full candour about how little of what we say about them is measured rather than argued. The pioneers who copied the walls with sponges got the first posture gloriously right and the second badly wrong; the revisionists who halved the chronology and dissolved the peoples into styles have spent forty years repairing the second without surrendering the first. The paintings remain — fading a little with every decade of sun and wind, guarded now by parks, conventions, and the plateau peoples themselves — an elephant giving way to a cow, a cow to a horse, a horse to a camel, on walls that watched a green world dry. Attested, argued, and still, in the best sense, unfinished: that is the state in which this archive is content to leave them.
