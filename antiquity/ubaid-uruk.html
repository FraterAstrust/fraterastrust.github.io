This is a Tier A article: everything in it rests on excavated, surveyed, and published archaeological evidence, and where scholars disagree — as they do, sharply, about the size of Uruk, the purpose of its commonest pot, and the nature of its northern outposts — the disagreement itself is reported with its positions attributed. Two cautions govern the whole article and are stated here, once, at the front. First, the phrase "first city" is a superlative this archive uses only under protest and always with its definition attached: Uruk is the earliest settlement known to combine, at one place and time, a resident population in the tens of thousands, monumental public building, full-time craft and administrative specialists, and a dependent hinterland — and even that claim is a claim about the surviving and surveyed record, not about every city that may have stood on ground now buried under metres of silt. Second, every population figure in this article is an estimate built from area and an assumed density, and is printed with its method and its error bars, never as a count.

The story runs in two acts. In the first, the Ubaid period (c. 6500–3800 BCE), farming communities of the southern Mesopotamian alluvium — the flat, river-built plain of what is now southern Iraq — developed the repertoire on which everything later stands: irrigation agriculture below the rainfall line, the tripartite house and temple plan, painted pottery made in growing quantities, and, at Eridu, a small shrine rebuilt on the same footprint, generation after generation, until the accumulated rebuildings had become a temple platform. In the second act, the Uruk period (c. 4000–3100 BCE), one settlement of that world — Uruk itself, modern Warka — grew past every precedent, raised two monumental temple precincts, organised labour and rations on a scale no village could have managed, mass-produced a single coarse bowl by the hundred thousand, rolled cylinder seals across the clay of its storeroom doors, and planted settlements in its own image as far away as the Syrian Euphrates and the Iranian highlands. Between the two acts lies a genuine scholarly problem — continuity or rupture? — and this article treats it as one (§VIII), rather than smoothing it into a staircase.

This article is the foundation companion of the hub's Mesopotamia wing. The writing that Uruk's administrators invented is treated in its own article, Origins of Writing; here, script appears only where the administrative practice that carried it — sealing, counting, rationing — is the subject. The city's own later memory of itself, in the Gilgamesh tradition, is read in Gilgamesh and touched on in §XVII below; the king lists that made Eridu the first city of all are read as the Tier B texts they are in Antediluvian King Lists. And the parallel road — another farming world, on another river margin, building toward cities of its own — is Mehrgarh and the Road to the Indus, whose granary villages are the Ubaid world's exact contemporaries and whose people never met them.

I. Two Names, One Transformation

"Ubaid" and "Uruk" are archaeologists' names, not the names of peoples who ever called themselves by them. Both are site names: Tell al-'Ubaid, a small mound near Ur where the painted pottery that defines the earlier period was first recognised as a class, and Uruk — biblical Erech, modern Warka — the great city whose deep excavation levels gave the later period its sequence. The two terms were fixed as period names at a conference in Baghdad in 1930, and they have carried an unavoidable ambiguity ever since: each names at once a stretch of time, a repertoire of material culture, and — more contentiously — a kind of society. This article keeps the three senses separate wherever the argument turns on them. A pot in the Ubaid style found in Kuwait proves contact or imitation; it does not, by itself, prove that Ubaid people stood on that shore, and the distinction matters at every site discussed in §VI and §XV.

The chronology used here is the hub's conventional one. The Ubaid period of the south runs c. 6500–3800 BCE and is conventionally subdivided into phases Ubaid 0 to Ubaid 5, defined on the pottery sequences of Tell el-'Oueili and Eridu: Ubaid 0 (the 'Oueili phase) and Ubaid 1 (the Eridu phase) are known almost only in the far south; Ubaid 2 (Hajji Muhammad) widens the distribution; Ubaid 3–4 carry the repertoire across Mesopotamia and beyond; Ubaid 5 is a terminal phase shading into what follows. The Uruk period runs c. 4000–3100 BCE — the overlap with the Ubaid dates is real and deliberate, since the phases are regional as well as temporal — and is divided into Early, Middle, and Late, with the city's great expansion and its monumental precincts belonging to the Middle and above all the Late Uruk, c. 3600–3100 BCE. Absolute dates in this range rest on radiocarbon determinations correlated with stratigraphy, and individual authorities move phase boundaries by a century or two; the rounded figures here follow the syntheses cited in the Sources, principally Nissen (1988), Pollock (1999), and Liverani (2006).

One more preliminary, because it disciplines everything that follows. The southern alluvium is among the worst archaeological landscapes on earth for the survival of early evidence and among the best for late evidence. The rivers have buried the earliest settlements under metres of silt; the water table drowns the lowest levels of many mounds; and the sites that can be dug are the ones that grew tall — which is to say, disproportionately, the ones that succeeded. When this article says that Eridu's temple sequence is the longest known, or that Uruk was the largest settlement of its age, the unspoken rider is always known: the claim is about the record as excavated and surveyed to date, and §IX takes up, in the population context, just how much of the map the surveys actually cover. Tier A does not mean the evidence is complete. It means the claims are fitted to the evidence there is.

II. The Southern Alluvium — Land, Water, and Work

The stage is a plain built by two rivers. The Tigris and Euphrates, descending from the Anatolian and Iranian highlands, drop their silt loads as they cross the flat country of southern Iraq and have raised, over millennia, a deltaic alluvium of extraordinary fertility and extraordinary difficulty. The difficulties come first. Rainfall in the far south falls far below the roughly 200 mm a year that dry farming requires; the rivers flood in spring, driven by highland snowmelt, which is the wrong season for winter-sown cereals already ripening; the flatness that makes irrigation easy also makes drainage hard, and irrigation water left standing in a hot climate evaporates into salt. Whoever farmed the far south farmed by artificial watering, on a calendar set by the rivers, against a standing threat of salinisation — and did so, from the Ubaid period onward, as the basis of the densest populations the ancient world had yet supported.

The rewards matched the difficulties. Irrigated alluvial soil, renewed by silt and watered on demand, yields barley harvests that rain-fed farming cannot approach, and barley — salt-tolerant, storable, measurable — became the plain's staple and, in time, its unit of account. Around the fields ran a second economy of marsh and river: fish in quantities the Eridu temple offerings (§IV) make vivid, reeds for building, matting, and boats, waterfowl, and grazing on the marsh edges for sheep and goats whose wool would become the plain's great manufactured product. What the plain did not have is equally diagnostic: no stone for building or tools beyond river pebbles, no metal ores, no timber larger than palm and tamarisk. Every hard material of civilisation — copper, tin, gold, lapis, carnelian, cedar, basalt, even good grinding stone — had to come from the highlands that ring the plain, hundreds of kilometres away. That asymmetry, a breadbasket ringed by the sources of everything except food, is the geographical fact underneath both the Ubaid interaction sphere (§VI) and Algaze's model of the Uruk expansion (§XVI).

Irrigation is also a social fact, and its archaeology begins early. At Choga Mami, on the plain's northeastern edge, in levels of the Samarra-to-Ubaid transition (c. 6000 BCE and after), excavators traced small artificial water channels — among the earliest direct evidence for canal irrigation anywhere. By Ubaid 2–3, canal systems served settlements across the south. The scale of the earliest works should not be inflated: these were local channels, dug and maintained by communities, not state hydraulic empires, and the older theory that irrigation required — and therefore created — centralised despotism has not survived the evidence. What the canals did require was coordination: headgates shared, channels cleaned, water allocated in the dry weeks. The institutions that grew up around that coordination — and around the storage and redistribution of the harvest it produced — are the seed of the temple household of §XII. The granary at Mehrgarh and the canal at Choga Mami are the same kind of fact, on two different rivers: bulk food, held in common, by arrangement.

The head of the Gulf did not stand in the Ubaid period where it stands today. In the sixth millennium BCE the shoreline lay far inland of the modern coast — how far is debated — and Eridu, now stranded among dunes, stood near the marshy edge of the sea that its temple's fish offerings reflect. Sites of the interaction sphere now on dry land in Kuwait and Saudi Arabia were then coastal or near-coastal settlements, and a stretch of the Ubaid world — whatever stood on the since-drowned or silted shore — is simply missing from the record. Distances in this article are given between the places as they were, not as the modern map draws them.

Settlement on the plain began, so far as the record reaches, with the Ubaid itself. Earlier farming cultures — Hassuna, Samarra, Halaf — belong to the rain-fed north and the piedmont; the far south's first excavated villages are Ubaid 0–1, at Tell el-'Oueili (where occupation may begin before 6500 BCE) and at Eridu. Whether still earlier southern settlement lies buried beneath the alluvium is unknowable on present evidence; what can be said is that by c. 5500 BCE the south held a scatter of farming towns — Eridu, Ur, 'Oueili, and others known from survey — each centred, so far as excavation has reached, on the same institution: a temple on a growing platform, standing over a community of irrigating farmers. The type-site of that pattern, and the single most important stratigraphy of the Ubaid world, is Eridu.

III. Eridu — The First Temple Town

Eridu is the site now called Tell Abu Shahrain, in the desert of southern Iraq southwest of Ur — today a group of low mounds around a depression where water still gathers after rain, with the unfinished ziggurat of the Ur III period (late third millennium BCE) as its landmark. In the Ubaid period it was a town of the marsh edge: set in a basin beside the Euphrates' old courses, close to the Gulf's then shoreline, living by irrigation farming, marsh fishing, and reed and boat traffic. It was explored in the nineteenth century by J. G. Taylor, dug briefly by R. Campbell Thompson (1918) and H. R. Hall (1919), and then, in the seasons of 1946–1949, excavated by Fuad Safar for the Iraqi Directorate of Antiquities, with Muhammad Ali Mustafa and with Seton Lloyd as technical adviser. Their full report — Safar, Mustafa & Lloyd, Eridu (1981) — is the anchor source of this article's first half, and its most famous product is a single deep trench.

The trench — the excavators' Temple Sounding — was cut in the southwest corner of the main mound, below the later ziggurat, down to virgin soil. In it, the team found what no other Mesopotamian excavation has matched: a stack of superimposed temples, each built directly over the levelled remains of its predecessor on the same small plot of ground, numbered from Level XVIII at the bottom to Level I near the top. The pottery from the sounding became, with 'Oueili's, the yardstick by which the Ubaid phases are defined; the architecture became the type sequence for the Mesopotamian temple itself. A cemetery of the late Ubaid period, excavated beside the mound, held on the order of a thousand graves — mud-brick box burials, furnished modestly and remarkably evenly, a fact §XII returns to. Around the temple hill lay the town: houses, and the debris of a community whose size, at its Ubaid height, is estimated in the low thousands at most. Eridu was never, in this period, a city. It was something arguably more consequential: the place where the temple — as a building type, an institution, and a habit of rebuilding — was invented in durable form.

A note on the god. Later Mesopotamian tradition — Tier B material, treated in this hub as tradition — made Eridu the city of Enki (Akkadian Ea), god of the sweet waters beneath the earth, and called his temple the E-abzu, "House of the Abzu," the subterranean freshwater ocean. The tradition also made Eridu the first city of all: in the Sumerian King List, kingship descends from heaven at Eridu before anywhere else (see Antediluvian King Lists). This article, being Tier A, can certify neither the god nor the priority. What it can certify is the remarkable fit between the tradition and the trench: the oldest temple sequence in Mesopotamia does stand at Eridu, beside the marsh waters, and its earliest offering deposits are full of fish. The excavators themselves noted the continuity of the fish offerings with the later cult of the water god. Where tradition and stratigraphy point the same way, Tier A records the stratigraphy and labels the tradition — and lets the reader feel the weight of both.

IV. The Eridu Temple Sequence — Level by Level

The sequence is the single most instructive stratigraphy in this hub, and it deserves to be walked slowly. The procedure that produced it was the same at every rebuilding, and it is worth stating as a procedure, because it is the ancestry of the ziggurat (§XI). When a temple was to be renewed, its walls were levelled to a chosen height, the interior was filled with clean earth and mud-brick packing, and the levelled ruin was sealed under a new floor — so that each temple literally stands on the body of its predecessor, and the sacred plot rises, centimetre by centimetre and then metre by metre, above the town around it. Nothing was moved sideways. The footprint is the continuity: across some two thousand years, through eighteen rebuildings, the holy place at Eridu never changed its address. What follows is the sequence in outline, from the published report; dimensions are the excavators' figures as summarised in the standard syntheses, and individual levels vary slightly between summaries.

The First Shrines — Levels XVIII to XV

At the bottom, on virgin soil, Level XVIII left only "sleeper walls" — low foundation traces of the first structure on the spot, too ruined for a plan. Above it, Level XVII gave the sequence its first true room: a small square cella of about 2.8 × 2.8 m, a single chamber of mud-brick with, already, the two fittings that define the type — a niche in one wall, for the cult image, and a pedestal facing it, for offerings. Level XVI repeated the room slightly larger (about 3.5 × 3.5 m), its central pedestal heavily burnt and covered with ash — offerings had been burned there, in the middle of a room barely larger than a modern bathroom. Level XV enlarged the building again (about 7.3 × 8.4 m in the excavators' summary), still essentially a single-celled shrine with ancillary space. Read together, XVIII–XV are a village's holy room: hand-sized architecture, but already formal — niched, pedestalled, oriented, and rebuilt on the spot as a matter of course. The idea that a god's house has a fixed address, and that the address outranks the building, is present from the first legible level.

The Platform Appears — Levels XIV to XI

Levels XIV, XIII, and XII left no recoverable building — a salutary reminder, in the middle of the famous sequence, that even the best stratigraphy has lacunae, and that the "eighteen temples" of popular accounts include levels known only as fills and floors. With Level XI (Ubaid 3 horizon) the sequence changes gear: the temple, now an elongated building of about 4.5 × 12.6 m, was raised for the first time on a mud-brick platform — the levelled predecessors no longer sufficed, and the builders manufactured height deliberately. The platform's faces were articulated with the shallow buttresses and recesses that become the signature skin of Mesopotamian sacred architecture for the next three thousand years: a wall treatment with no structural need, pure articulation — the building announcing, in light and shadow, that it is not a house. From Level XI onward, the Eridu temple is a high temple in embryo, and the labour accounting changes with it: a platform is a public work, measured in basketloads, not a family's weekend.

The Tripartite Temple — Levels X to VI

Levels X and IX continue the elongated plan (about 5 × 13 m and 4 × 10 m respectively). With Level VIII (about 18 × 11 m) the building reaches its mature form, and Levels VII (about 17 × 12 m) and VI carry it to its Ubaid completion. The mature Eridu temple is tripartite: a long central hall, entered through an offset doorway, flanked on both long sides by rows of smaller rooms; at one short end of the hall, an altar against the wall; facing it, set about a third of the hall's length away, a free-standing offering table; in a corner, a stair — implying a roof in use, or an upper storey. The whole stands on its platform, buttressed and recessed outside, approached by a stair or ramp. In the Level VI temple and its predecessors the excavators found the offering deposits that give the sequence its sensory detail: quantities of fish, laid at the altar and the offering table — the marsh's tribute, in the house of the god the later texts would call lord of the waters. Temple VI, at roughly 280 m² of roofed plan in the standard comparison, is the largest Ubaid temple known; the figure is worth keeping, because §X will set beside it a single Uruk temple of some 4,600 m², and the ratio between the two numbers is one honest measure of what changed.

Terraces Without Temples — Levels V to I

Above Level VI the sounding changes character. Levels V to I, of the Early Uruk period, survive as platform and terrace remains without recoverable temple plans — the upper sequence was truncated by the Ur III builders who, two thousand years later, levelled the hilltop for the ziggurat of Amar-Sin (begun, on the inscriptional evidence, under that king and never finished). The loss is itself evidence of the site's continuing sanctity: the Ur III kings built here precisely because the hill was already ancient and holy. The sounding's lesson is therefore double. As architecture, it documents the whole growth of the temple type — cella, platform, tripartite plan, buttressed skin — inside one trench. As stratigraphy, it documents something rarer: an institution's memory of its own ground, maintained without writing, across a span of time longer than that which separates the Uruk period from ourselves by half again. When later Mesopotamians said that Eridu was first, they were wrong about kingship, so far as evidence can judge — and standing, unknowing, on a real antiquity that their own tradition had preserved.

The level numbers are the excavators' stratigraphic units, not a census of temples: three of the eighteen levels (XIV–XII) preserve no building at all, and five (V–I) preserve platforms without plans. The famous "eighteen superimposed temples" of popular writing is therefore a shorthand for something both less tidy and more impressive — eighteen successive building horizons on one footprint, of which the legible temples, from a 2.8-metre cella to a tripartite platform temple, form an unbroken developmental series. The continuity claim in this article is made at exactly that strength.

V. Ubaid Material Culture — Pots, Houses, Tools

The Ubaid is recognised, in the field and in the museum, by its pottery: a fine, buff to greenish ware, wheel-finished in its later phases on the slow wheel or tournette, painted in dark brown to black with a confident geometric vocabulary — bands, zigzags, cross-hatching, swirling fourfold motifs, stylised birds and goats at the repertoire's exuberant edge. The shapes are serving and storage vessels: open bowls, jars with painted shoulders, spouted vessels, and the tall "tortoise" jars of the cemeteries. Two features of the assemblage matter for the argument of this article. The first is standardisation within variety: the painted repertoire is shared, recognisably, from one end of the sphere to the other, yet every region — and in the north, every site — bends it locally. The second is trajectory: across the period, painting simplifies, production quickens, and plain wares multiply, until by Ubaid 5 and the Early Uruk the painted style that defines the period has effectively been retired in favour of speed. The bevelled-rim bowl of §XIII is unintelligible except as the end of that trajectory: the Ubaid already contained the pressure — more vessels, for more people, administered by more institutions — that the Uruk answered with the mould.

The houses tell the same story at architectural scale. The characteristic Ubaid house — excavated in plan at Tell Abada, Tell Madhhur, and elsewhere — is tripartite, like the temple: a long central hall or courtyard, flanked by rows of smaller rooms, in the developed form with the hall extended at one end into a T. Houses of ten to twenty rooms are known; some, at Abada, stand in planned clusters with shared walls and lanes. The identity of plan between house and temple is the period's deepest architectural fact, and it runs in both directions at once: the temple is a great house — the god's household, with hall, stores, and chambers — and the great house is a small temple, organised around its hall. When §XII describes the temple as an oikos, a household with estates and dependents, it is describing a metaphor the Ubaid built in mud-brick before anyone wrote it down. Smaller finds fill out the economy: clay sickles by the thousand (the alluvium has no flint; even the harvest was gathered with baked earth), hoes and mullers, baked-clay sling bullets, spindle whorls and loom weights for the wool economy, and model boats — Eridu produced a celebrated terracotta boat model with a mast socket — for the marsh traffic that tied the settlements together.

Two artefact classes deserve a sentence each, because both become load-bearing later. Stamp seals — small carved stones pressed into wet clay — appear in the later Ubaid, notably at Tepe Gawra in the north, marking jars, doors, and bales: the administrative gesture of §XIV in its first, flat form. And the figurines: Ubaid cemeteries and houses yield clay figures, female and male, some — famously, from Ur and Eridu — with elongated, reptilian-looking heads that have attracted a century of speculation. Tier A records what they are: hand-modelled clay figures, some with applied coffee-bean eyes and bitumen hair, buried with the dead and kept in houses, their meaning unrecoverable and their heads most plausibly a stylistic convention — cranial shaping is attested in the region's populations. They are evidence for a symbolic life, not for its content; the archive leaves the content blank rather than fill it.

PAINTED, THEN PLAIN

Ubaid pottery begins as the finest painted ware of its world and ends, by Ubaid 5, simplifying toward speed and volume. The Uruk mass-production of §XIII is the continuation of a trend, not a break in taste.

ONE PLAN, TWO SCALES

Tripartite planning — central hall, flanking rooms — organises both the Ubaid great house (Tell Abada, Tell Madhhur) and the Eridu temple. The temple household of §XII is this plan, institutionalised.

CLAY IN PLACE OF STONE

On a plain without stone, the Ubaid baked its sickles, sling bullets, and tools from clay. Material poverty at home and material reach abroad are the two faces of the same alluvium.

THE FIRST SEALS

Stamp seals in the later Ubaid (Tepe Gawra and elsewhere) mark containers and doors a millennium before the cylinder seal. Administration precedes writing by the whole length of the sealing tradition.

VI. The Ubaid Interaction Sphere — Gulf to North Syria

By Ubaid 3–4 (c. 4500–3900 BCE), material in the Ubaid repertoire — above all the painted pottery, with tripartite architecture, clay sickles, and associated small finds — is found across a zone some two thousand kilometres wide: the whole of Mesopotamia, the Susiana plain of southwestern Iran, the middle Euphrates and the Balikh and Khabur valleys of north Syria, the piedmont of southeastern Anatolia, and down the western shore of the Gulf to Qatar. Scholars call such a zone an interaction sphere (or, in Stein's term, an oikumene): a region within which goods, styles, techniques, and ideas circulated intensively enough to leave a shared material signature, without implying a single polity, a single people, or a single direction of flow. The phrase is chosen precisely to avoid what the older literature assumed — that a pot style is a passport, and its distribution a migration map. What the sphere's evidence shows, region by region, is more interesting than migration: it shows selective adoption, everywhere different.

The Gulf Shore

The Gulf evidence is the sphere's most instructive edge. At H3 as-Sabiyah in Kuwait — a settlement of stone-built structures on the then shore — excavators found Ubaid-style pottery, some of it, on fabric analysis, imported from Mesopotamia and some locally made in imitation; a pottery boat model; and, most remarkably, fragments of bitumen-coated reed boats, with barnacles still attached — the earliest direct evidence for seagoing reed vessels in the region, the physical means by which the sphere's southern arm travelled. Comparable Ubaid material occurs at Bahra 1 in Kuwait and at Dosariyah and other sites down the Saudi coast, accompanied by Arabian bifacial stone tools of wholly local tradition. The pattern is unambiguous and important: the Gulf communities were not Ubaid colonies and not passive recipients. They were Arabian fishing and herding peoples in sustained contact with the Mesopotamian shore — exchanging, on the evidence of Mesopotamian sites, fish, shell, and pearls northward, and taking southward a pottery style they made their own. Obsidian from Anatolian sources, found at Gulf sites, had crossed the whole sphere to get there. Contact, at this distance, was a relationship, not an occupation.

The North and the West

Northward, the sphere meets a different dynamic, treated in the next section: the Halaf world of the rain-fed plains, with its own fine pottery and its own long traditions, across which Ubaid material spreads in the fifth millennium until, in the Halaf–Ubaid Transitional and after, northern sites from Tell Zeidan on the Euphrates to Tepe Gawra near the Tigris are building tripartite temples of southern type — Gawra's sequence of Ubaid temples, with their niched façades and, in the later levels, stamp-seal administration, is the north's own Eridu in miniature. Westward, Ubaid-related material reaches the Amuq and the Mediterranean fringe; eastward, Susiana shares the repertoire while keeping its own sequence and, in time, its own administrative genius (§XIV). Gil Stein and Rana Özbal, comparing the two great expansions of early Mesopotamia, characterise the Ubaid oikumene's dynamic as fundamentally ideological and horizontal: a prestigious repertoire — temple plan, painted ware, house form — adopted and reworked by local communities who remained themselves, spread by emulation, intermarriage, exchange, and the slow prestige of the temple towns, rather than by settlement or force. The contrast with the Uruk oikumene of §XV, with its planted towns and its sealed warehouses, is the sharpest structural contrast in this article, and it is theirs.

What held the sphere together, institutionally, is harder to see than what travelled through it. There were no Ubaid kings, so far as any evidence reaches; no Ubaid writing; no Ubaid army anyone has found. The best current models speak of ranked, temple-centred communities — "trans-egalitarian" societies, in the jargon, where leading households and temple establishments competed in generosity, feasting, and building, and where the temple platform rising over each town was both the community's bank and its flag. That the sphere endured, coherent, for the better part of two millennia without any of the coercive apparatus of the state is a fact the Uruk comparison should not be allowed to belittle: the Ubaid interaction sphere is among the largest durable cultural formations of the pre-literate world, and it ran on prestige and reciprocity alone.

VII. The Halaf Question — The Northern Neighbours

The north into which the Ubaid repertoire spread was not empty, and it was not primitive. The Halaf period (c. 6100–5100 BCE, with transitional forms after) of the rain-fed plains — from the Euphrates to the Zagros foothills — had produced the finest painted pottery of the early Neolithic world, a ware of such technical command that its best vessels bear comparison with anything made before the wheel; circular tholos houses of a plan wholly unlike the south's tripartite rectangle; stamp seals; and a settlement network of prosperous farming towns, of which Tell Sabi Abyad, Arpachiyah, and the type-site Tell Halaf are the best excavated. The Halaf and the early Ubaid were contemporaries and neighbours for a thousand years, the one rooted in the dry-farming north, the other in the irrigated south, and their frontier ran across what is now northern Iraq. The question — the Halaf question — is what happened at that frontier in the fifth millennium, when Ubaid material appears at Halaf sites, Halaf pottery fades, and the north emerges, by c. 4300 BCE, building and painting (and then not painting) in the southern manner.

The older literature answered with replacement: Ubaid colonists, or a Ubaid conquest, extinguishing the Halaf. The current literature, built on stratigraphies like Tell Sabi Abyad's and Tell Zeidan's that show the two repertoires interleaved across a long Halaf–Ubaid Transition (c. 5300–4500 BCE in the north), answers with process instead. Halaf communities adopted southern traits piecemeal — a house plan here, a pottery form there, the temple institution at Gawra and Zeidan — while continuing local practices in parallel; Halaf painted decoration simplified toward Ubaid geometries by internal development as much as by import; and no destruction horizon, no intrusive burial population, and no break in settlement marks a takeover. Positions still differ in emphasis: some scholars stress demic movement — southern families settling northward along the rivers — as one strand among several; others, following the Stein and Özbal model of §VI, treat the whole northern Ubaid as an indigenous transformation under southern ideological influence. What is no longer seriously defended is the event: there was no Ubaid conquest of the Halaf, because conquests leave different evidence, and the transition's evidence is the evidence of neighbours becoming, over twenty generations, harder to tell apart.

The episode matters beyond its region, because it sets the interpretive baseline for §XV. Mesopotamian material culture demonstrably can spread across a thousand kilometres without colonists, garrisons, or political control — the Ubaid did it. Any model of the Uruk expansion must therefore prove, site by site, that this time the pots came with planters; and the strongest sites of §XV are strong precisely because they meet that burden — planned streets on virgin soil are not an ideology — while the weakest dissolve, under the same test, into the older pattern of contact and emulation. The Halaf question is the control experiment the Uruk debate runs against.

// TWO POTTERIES, TWO LOGICS //

Halaf ware is a virtuoso product: thin, hard, brilliantly painted, made in small quantities by skilled hands. Ubaid ware, as the period proceeds, moves the opposite way — quicker, plainer, more standardised, made in quantity. The northern transition is therefore also a change in the social logic of the pot: from display object to administered staple. The bevelled-rim bowl (§XIII) completes a trajectory the Halaf–Ubaid frontier already shows in cross-section.

VIII. The Transition Problem — Continuity or Rupture?

Between the world of §VII and the city of §IX stands a boundary the period names make look like a door — "Ubaid" ends, "Uruk" begins — and the evidence makes look like anything but. The transition problem is this article's central scholarly debate, and it is stated here as the literature states it: unresolved, with the balance of evidence differing by region and by class of material.

The case for continuity is strongest in the south itself. Settlement surveys in the Uruk and Nippur hinterlands (the work of Robert McC. Adams and Hans Nissen, Heartland of Cities, 1981, and its successors) show the southern settlement pattern developing without break: Ubaid towns persist into Uruk times, growing along the same channels and canals. The Eanna precinct at Uruk itself has Ubaid levels at the bottom of its sounding, and the Anu terrace (§XI) accumulates from Ubaid temples upward — the city's two holy places are both older than the city. Ceramic seriation, worked sherd by sherd, shows the plain wares of the Uruk growing out of late Ubaid forms by gradual simplification rather than arriving as a foreign kit; the tripartite temple, the buttressed façade, the platform, the stamp seal, and the temple household are all Ubaid inheritances, enlarged. On this reading — Nissen's, in its classic form — the Uruk period is the Ubaid's own logic, accelerated: irrigation, storage, temple administration, and craft production intensifying in place until quantity changed into quality and the town became a city.

The case for rupture rests on a different set of facts, and they are also real. Painted pottery — the Ubaid's signature for two thousand years — disappears, and disappears fast by archaeological standards. The cemeteries disappear with it: the Ubaid buried its dead in furnished, communal grounds like Eridu's thousand graves, while the Uruk period, across its whole span, has left almost no formal burials at all — a silence in the record that Pollock and others have rightly refused to treat as an accident, since a society does not misplace its dead for nine centuries. New things arrive in a cluster and at speed: the cylinder seal, the bevelled-rim bowl in its millions, numerical tablets and then writing, monumental art of a new, narrative kind, and — in the glyptic imagery — bound prisoners and armed men, among the first unambiguous pictures of organised violence in the region's record. Settlement, too, re-concentrates: the survey data that show continuity of place also show a violent re-ranking of scale, population draining from villages into a few enormous centres. On this reading, something — state formation, with its apparatus of coercion and extraction — really did begin, and the Ubaid's temple-town world was not merely accelerated but superseded.

The working synthesis most specialists now operate with — and the position of this article — holds the two readings at their respective strengths. In institutions and population, the south shows continuity: same towns, same temples, same canals, same households, growing. In political form and material style, the Late Uruk shows a genuine threshold: a new administrative technology, a new scale of mobilisation, and a new relationship between centre and countryside that the word "state" exists to name. The north complicates any simple version, since northern communities (Tell Brak, §XV) were urbanising on their own trajectory in the same centuries — the "transition" is not one event with one address. What can be said without remainder is that the Ubaid built the platform, in every sense, and the Uruk built the city on it; whether the builders would have recognised each other is a question the evidence leaves, properly, open.

// WHY THE BURIALS MATTER //

The near-absence of Uruk-period burials is the transition debate's most under-discussed datum. It cannot be explained by preservation — Uruk levels are excavated in bulk — and the usual explanations (disposal of the dead in rivers or marshes; cemeteries in unexcavated locations; a change in rite that left no durable trace) are all, at present, conjectures fitted to a silence. This article registers the silence as evidence of some profound change in practice, and declines to choose among the conjectures.

IX. Uruk the City — Scale, Survey, and the Population Problem

Uruk — Warka — lies in the desert of southern Iraq, a vast field of mounds enclosed by the trace of its city wall, some 9.5 km in circuit. German expeditions excavated there from 1912, in campaign after campaign across the twentieth century, concentrating on the two monumental precincts at the city's heart, Eanna and the Anu terrace (§§X–XI), and it is from Uruk that the period takes both its name and its chronological skeleton: the deep sounding in Eanna, with its numbered levels (XVIII at the bottom, through IV–III at the writing horizon), is the master sequence of the fourth millennium. But the city's claim to be first does not rest on its temples. It rests on its size — and size, in archaeology, is a measurement with a method, which this section is obliged to show.

How the Numbers Are Made

No census of Uruk exists. Every figure in this section is built in two steps, each with its own uncertainty. The first step is area, and it comes from surface survey: teams walk the mound field and the surrounding plain, collecting and dating the pottery scattered on the surface, and map the extent over which sherds of a given period occur. For Late Uruk Uruk, the mapped settled area is about 250 hectares (2.5 km²) — a figure most syntheses accept within a band of roughly 230–250 ha, though looser readings of the surface scatter have run as high as 500 ha, and the walled area of the following centuries encloses some 400–600 ha, the city reaching c. 600 ha by about 2800 BCE. The second step is density: the area is multiplied by an assumed number of persons per hectare, borrowed from excavated housing quarters and from ethnographic comparison with pre-modern cities of the region. The coefficients in the literature run from about 100 to 200 persons per hectare. Multiply honestly and the famous figure dissolves into its true shape: 250 ha at 100–200 persons per hectare gives 25,000 to 50,000 inhabitants — a range, not a number, with the true value hostage to assumptions about household size, the fraction of the mound actually roofed at any moment, and the proportion of public and empty ground. Popular accounts that print "50,000" are printing the top of the range; accounts that print "40,000" are printing its middle. This article prints the range.

What survives every recalculation is the shape of the settlement system, and it is the shape, more than the headline figure, that justifies the word "city." The surveys of the Uruk hinterland (Adams, Nissen, and their successors) show a ranked hierarchy: one primate centre of c. 250 ha; a tier of towns in the tens of hectares; and a base of villages and hamlets — and, across the Late Uruk, a measurable concentration, the countryside's population shifting toward the centre until, on Adams's figures, a large majority of the region's people lived in settlements of urban scale. A big village does not bend its region around itself in this way; a city does. Uruk also fed itself from that region — grain, fish, reeds, and labour moving inward along the canals — and the organisational machinery of the movement is §§XII–XIV. Compare the parallel measure at Mehrgarh: two hundred hectares of mounds that are five thousand years of villages stacked in a landscape, against Uruk's 250 ha occupied at once. The difference between a palimpsest and a population is the difference between the Neolithic and the urban, and survey archaeology — unglamorous, statistical, revisable — is what lets this article draw it.

// "FIRST CITY" — THE TERMS OF THE CLAIM //

Uruk's priority is a claim about a combination, not about any single trait. Monumental building is older (see the hub's Göbekli Tepe article); dense settlement is older (Çatalhöyük housed thousands without, so far as anyone can find, temples, palaces, or administrators); northern Tell Brak was urbanising independently in the same millennium (§XV). What no earlier or contemporary site yet shows is Uruk's full conjunction at Uruk's scale: tens of thousands of residents, a ranked regional hierarchy, monumental temple precincts, full-time specialists, mass-produced ration vessels, sealing administration, and — at the very end — writing. The claim is also bounded by survival: the alluvium buries its earliest levels, and the superlative is awarded on the record as it stands, with the excavation of the next decade entitled to appeal.

X. The Eanna Precinct and the Stone-Cone Temple

At the city's eastern centre stood Eanna — "House of Heaven" — the precinct that later ages knew as the domain of Inanna, goddess of Uruk; the name is historical, and its projection back into the fourth millennium is a convenience this article flags rather than hides, since no Uruk-period text names the precinct's god for us in so many words. Across the Late Uruk building levels (Eanna VI–III), on a precinct ground of some nine hectares, the excavators uncovered the most ambitious architecture of the age anywhere in the world: a succession of monumental buildings in mud-brick, baked brick, and — a sensation in a stoneless land — limestone, hauled from outcrops some 60 km away. The buildings overlap, replace, and crowd one another in plan, because the precinct was rebuilt wholesale, more than once, within the Late Uruk centuries: this was not gradual accretion but programmed renewal, the levelling of whole temple complexes and their replacement on new plans — itself a datum about the labour and authority at the institution's command.

The named buildings repay a walk-through. The Limestone Temple (Eanna V), some 76 × 30 m on a foundation of dressed stone blocks, is the largest single building of its horizon. Temple D (Eanna IV), at roughly 4,600 m² of plan, is larger still in area — set the figure beside Eridu's final Ubaid temple (c. 280 m²) and the sixteen-fold ratio is the transition of §VIII measured in floor space. Around them stand the Pillared Hall, the Great Court with its sunken, plastered basin, the small, brick-paved Riemchengebäude — named for the small square "pencil" bricks (Riemchen) of the later levels, whose standardisation is itself a production fact — and the building whose name this section carries: the Stone-Cone Temple (Steinkegelgebäude, Eanna VI).

The Stone-Cone Mosaic — A Procedure

The Stone-Cone Temple takes its name from its skin. Its walls — and the walls of the precinct's courts and of the related Mosaic Court building — were decorated with a mosaic made as follows. Cones of baked clay, and in this building of stone — black, white, and red — were manufactured in quantity, each a few centimetres long, their flat heads ground or dipped to a uniform colour. While the wall's mud plaster was still wet, workmen pressed the cones into it point-first, in dense fields, head outward, arranging the colours into lozenges, triangles, and zigzag bands across whole façades. The result, reconstructed from the thousands of cones recovered where the walls had collapsed, was a patterned textile in mineral: a building wrapped in geometry that caught the raking light of the plain. As procedure, the mosaic is a small mass-production system in its own right — standardised units, made in bulk, assembled by pattern, at architectural scale — and it belongs in this article beside the bevelled-rim bowl as evidence of the same organisational turn: the Uruk precincts are decorated the way the Uruk economy is run, by the ten thousand identical pieces.

What were these buildings for? The plans are the Eridu plan grown monumental — central halls, flanking rooms, niched and buttressed skins — and the conventional label is "temples." The label is probably right and certainly incomplete. The precinct also contained the find-contexts of administration: it is from Eanna's later levels (Uruk IV–III) that the great mass of the earliest tablets comes, discarded into the fills and levelling layers of the rebuildings — the archive of the institution, thrown out with its rubble (see Origins of Writing). Storerooms, courts, workshops, and halls stand in the same enclosure. The Eanna of the Late Uruk is best pictured not as a church but as a great household's seat: cult rooms at its ceremonial core, and around them the stores, offices, and reception spaces of the estate that §XII describes. The architecture and the economy are one institution, and the precinct is its body.

// STONE ON A PLAIN WITHOUT STONE //

Every block of the Limestone Temple and every stone cone of the mosaic was imported — quarried at outcrops tens of kilometres away and moved by boat and sledge to a city built otherwise entirely of river mud. The expenditure is the message: the precinct's builders spent transport labour on permanence and colour that mud-brick could have faked more cheaply. Monumentality, at Uruk, is measured in logistics.

XI. The Anu Terrace and the White Temple — Ancestry of the Ziggurat

Five hundred metres from Eanna stood the city's second holy place, the precinct later ages assigned to Anu, the sky god. Its form is the opposite of Eanna's spread: a single, ever-rising terrace. The sequence beneath it is Eridu's procedure repeated within one city — temple upon temple on one footprint from the Ubaid period onward, each rebuilding levelled, filled, and sealed into the growing mound, until by the Late Uruk the accumulated stack stood, on the usual reconstruction, some 13 metres above the plain — a five-storey artificial mountain of sacred debris, its sides revetted and its summit reached by stair and ramp. In the foundation deposits of its courses the excavators found, memorably, the bodies of a leopard and a lion: foundation sacrifice at the city's most conspicuous address, and a reminder that the terrace's meaning was never merely engineering.

On the summit, in the final Late Uruk phase (c. 3500–3300 BCE), stood the White Temple: a tripartite building of about 17.5 × 22.3 m — modest in plan beside Eanna's giants, and deliberately Eridu-like, the old form preserved at the old place — its mud-brick walls plastered inside and out with white gypsum, so that the building shone. Its central hall carried the full apparatus of the type: niched walls, an altar platform at one end reached by steps, and a free-standing pedestal with a semi-circular step and traces of burning, the offering place of §IV translated to the top of the city. A visitor approaching Uruk across the flat, green-black irrigation country saw, first and from miles away, a white building floating above the roofs on a terraced hill that no natural process had made. The effect was the point, and it was achieved by the simplest of means: two thousand years of not moving the temple.

Here the ancestry this hub most needs from Uruk can be stated exactly. The ziggurat — the staged temple tower of the third millennium, of which Ur's is the famous survivor and Amar-Sin's unfinished work at Eridu the local instance — is not an invention of the dynastic age. It is the Anu terrace, formalised: a temple platform whose height had been produced, first, by the stratigraphy of devotion (each temple entombed in the next one's platform), and then, as at Eridu Level XI and on the Anu sequence, manufactured deliberately in a single building programme. The construction sequence runs: shrine on the ground; shrine on its own levelled ancestors; shrine on a purpose-built terrace; terraces stacked and staged; the tower. Every stage is excavated, at Eridu or at Uruk, and the sequence is the longest continuous architectural development this archive can show. The temple that Egyptian builders of the same millennia raised in stone on the Nile had its own ancestry; Mesopotamia's was mud, memory, and accumulation — and it reached as high.

XII. The Temple Household — Estates, Rations, Labour

What kind of institution ran Eanna? The answer the current literature gives — Pollock's Ancient Mesopotamia: The Eden That Never Was (1999) is its fullest statement — is the household, scaled up. Mesopotamian society was organised, at every level, in houses: kin groups holding land, herds, and stores in common under a head. The great institutions were houses of the same kind, with the god as their head and a population of dependents in place of kin: the Greek word oikos, "household," is the literature's term for them. A great oikos of the Uruk period held, on the model the later texts let us reconstruct: fields worked by its own labour force and by tenants; herds of sheep and goats numbered, in the archaic accounts, in the thousands; orchards and fisheries; workshops — weaving establishments staffed largely by women, breweries, bakeries, potteries, and the seal-cutters and scribes of §§XIV–XV; storerooms under seal; and a staff graded from managers and priests down to fully dependent workers who owned, so far as the record shows, nothing but their ration entitlement. The temple was not a ritual annex to an economy located elsewhere. It was, with the great secular houses beside it, the economy's largest single firm.

The ration is the institution's connective tissue, and its mechanics can be reconstructed in outline. Produce flowed inward — grain from the estate's fields and from the dues of its hinterland, wool, oil, fish, dates — into sealed stores. It flowed outward again as measured disbursements: grain (barley above all), oil, and cloth issued to the household's people at graded rates, by status, sex, and age, on a monthly cycle. The earliest tablets, from Eanna's own levels, are dominated by exactly these accounts — herds, fields, labour gangs, disbursements — and one of proto-cuneiform's earliest compound signs, a human head with a bowl, is read as the sign for "ration" itself: the institution's defining transaction, drawn. (The script's own story is told in Origins of Writing; here what matters is the practice the script served.) Labour was organised in gangs under overseers, accounted in work-days, and mobilised seasonally for the works no village could attempt: the canals, the platforms of §§X–XI, the city wall. Estimates of the Anu terrace's labour cost run to thousands of person-years; whoever commanded those years commanded the city's surplus, and the ration bowl of §XIII is the wage-slip of the arrangement.

Two scholarly cautions complete the picture, and both are Pollock's discipline as much as her model. First, the oikos reconstruction leans, for its detail, on the archaic tablets of the period's very end and on third-millennium archives projected backward with care; for the Ubaid and earlier Uruk, the institution is inferred from architecture, storage, and sealing — real evidence, but evidence of capacity rather than of accounts. Second, the great households never held the whole economy: independent kin households farmed, herded, and traded alongside the oikoi throughout, and the balance between the institutional and the household sectors — and how coercive the institutions' hold on their dependents truly was — remains debated. What is not debated is the direction of travel: between Eridu's first platform and Uruk's ration accounts, the temple grew from a community's shrine into an estate that fed, clothed, housed, and directed thousands of people who were not its kin. The city and the household grew up together, and each is the other's explanation.

// THE HOUSEHOLD THEME, ACROSS THE HUB //

The temple-household is this hub's Mesopotamian through-line: founded here, in the Ubaid–Uruk evidence, it becomes the organising institution of the Sumerian city-states treated in the wing this article founds. The contrast the hub sets beside it is Mehrgarh's: five thousand years of farming development without — so far as its record shows — any such institution, its surpluses held in household granaries to the end. Two roads from the farming village: one through the great house, one around it.

XIII. Mass Production — The Bevelled-Rim Bowl

The commonest artefact of the Uruk period is a bad pot. The bevelled-rim bowl (BRB) is a small conical vessel, about 10 cm high with a mouth some 18 cm across, made of clay heavily tempered with chaff, low-fired to a porous, crumbly fabric, undecorated, its rim finished with a single knife-cut bevel. It occurs, at Late Uruk sites, in quantities that break the normal scale of ceramic archaeology: at many sites it forms the large majority — up to roughly three quarters — of the entire pottery assemblage, and single deposits yield thousands. It is found from the Susiana plain to the Syrian Euphrates, identical in conception everywhere, and it vanishes with the period that made it. No artefact better deserves this article's procedural treatment, because its meaning is in its manufacture.

How It Was Made

The bowl was mould-formed, and the sequence, reconstructed from the bowls' own surface evidence and from replication experiments, runs as follows. The potter prepared a coarse clay, kneaded through with chopped straw or chaff — the temper that makes the fabric porous and light and lets a thick-walled vessel dry and fire without cracking. A lump of the clay was pressed by hand into a mould: the majority view, from the crinkled exteriors, smooth interiors, and fist-marks preserved inside the bowls, holds that the mould was a conical former — whether a portable mould of wood or fired clay, or a conical hole shaped in the ground, remains debated, and the two need not exclude each other across so wide a distribution. The clay was worked up the mould's sides with the fingers, the surplus spilling over the mould's edge in the characteristic "frill" still visible on many specimens. The rim was then trimmed level with a blade or taut string, the cut producing the bevel that names the type. The bowl was released, dried, and fired at low temperature in simple kilns or clamps — a firing so light that many bowls can be scratched with a fingernail. Replication studies (Goulder's experiments, Antiquity 2010, are the most cited) found the moulding sequence fast and learnable: a practised hand produces a bowl in minutes. Multiply by the deposits, and the arithmetic of the type becomes its own argument: the BRB is not a vessel tradition but a production system, the first in the record organised for sheer disposable volume.

What It Held — The Ration Hypothesis and Its Critics

The classic interpretation is Hans Nissen's (1970): the bowls are ration measures. Nissen argued, from the bowls' standardised form, their mass discard in heaps, and the analogy of the ration accounts that writing soon makes explicit, that dependent workers of the great households (§XII) received their grain — barley, the staple wage — measured out in bevelled-rim bowls, and that the bowl, too cheap to keep, was thrown away after use. On this reading the BRB is the material signature of the ration system itself, and its distribution map is the map of organised, institutional labour in the fourth millennium.

The critics have been at the hypothesis for fifty years, and their objections are specific. T. W. Beale (1978), measuring capacities across a large series, found the bowls' volumes varied too widely for a standard measure, held too little — heaped measures aside — for a man's daily grain, and were in any case impractical carriers of grain or liquid; he proposed instead votive bowls, made for an occasion, brought to temples, emptied, and discarded. A. R. Millard (1988) reviewed the case and likewise found the ration reading stronger than its evidence. Later proposals multiply the functions: Daniel Potts (2009), from Iranian evidence, argued the bowls served as bread moulds in institutional bakeries — dough pressed into the bowl, baked, and turned out as a standard loaf — and Goulder's experiments showed the form does indeed bake a risen loaf well, its porous walls wicking moisture as Egyptian bread pots do. Others have proposed salt production, dairy processing, and general-purpose serving. The honest summary is that the ration hypothesis remains the most influential single reading, is not proven, and may be wrong in its specifics while right in its scale: whatever filled them, the bowls were made by the hundred thousand, used briefly, and discarded in heaps, in every place the Uruk order reached.

And that scale is the conclusion that survives every adjudication. A society that moulds, fires, fills, and throws away vessels by the million has crossed a threshold no painted-pottery world approached: production detached from the object's value and attached to the transaction's — the bowl as packaging. Whether the transaction was a wage, an offering, or a loaf, the BRB deposits are the first great rubbish of the administered world, and their sheer numbers imply the rest of the system without further argument: clay-diggers, temper-cutters, moulders, kiln crews, carriers, issuers, and the counters who tallied them all. The next section takes up the counters' other tool.

// THE BOWL, AT ITS EXACT STRENGTH //

Form: conical, c. 10 cm high, c. 18 cm rim diameter, chaff-tempered, low-fired, knife-bevelled rim. Capacity: commonly around 0.8–0.9 litres, with wide variance between individual bowls — the variance is the core of Beale's critique of the standard-measure reading. Frequency: up to c. 75% of ceramic assemblages at Late Uruk sites; deposits of thousands. Manufacture: mould-formed (majority view; ground-mould vs portable mould debated), minutes per bowl in replication. XIV. Seals and Sealing — The Administrative Hand

The seal is the Uruk world's signature in the most literal sense: a carved object whose impression says this was closed by an authority, and has not been opened since. Sealing is older than the city — stamp seals are Neolithic, and the later Ubaid used them at Gawra (§V) — but the Uruk period transforms the practice in instrument, in scale, and in consequence. The instrument is the cylinder seal: a small cylinder of stone — limestone, serpentine, lapis, rock crystal — drilled through lengthwise for a cord, its surface carved in intaglio with a scene that reads, when the cylinder is rolled across wet clay, as a continuous frieze. Cylinder seals appear in the Middle Uruk (c. 3600–3500 BCE), in southern Mesopotamia and at Susa, cut first with the drill and the cutting wheel — the mechanical tools whose marks the early "baggy" style still shows — and within two centuries they carry the period's whole visual repertoire: files of animals, offering bearers approaching a temple façade, herds at the byre, boats, contests, and the figure the literature calls the "priest-king," a man in a brimmed cap and net skirt who feeds the flock, receives offerings, and, in the darker scenes, confronts bound prisoners. The seal was an official's instrument and an amulet at once, worn on the person, buried with its owner in later ages, and recognised at a glance by everyone who handled clay — which is to say, by the whole administration.

The Procedure of Sealing

The practices can be reconstructed, from thousands of excavated sealings — the discarded clay itself — as a set of distinct operations, each answering a distinct administrative question.

The jar sealing answers has this container been opened? A jar's mouth was covered with cloth, leather, or a clay cap, tied with cord, and a lump of wet clay was pressed over the knot and rolled with the seal. The dried lump — the sealing — could not be removed without breaking; a broken sealing at destination was the audit's first fact.

The door sealing answers who closed this room, and has it been entered? A storeroom door was fastened by a cord wound around a peg set in the door-frame; clay was packed over peg and cord and rolled with the responsible official's seal. To open the room was to smash the sealing — legitimately, in the course of issue, the fragments were discarded in the room's own doorway deposits, where excavators find them in clusters that map, room by room, which seals controlled which stores. The door sealing is the period's most eloquent administrative artefact: a lock that records its keeper and testifies against its breaker.

The bulla answers what was promised, and by whom? A bulla is a hollow ball of clay enclosing small tokens — the calculi, in shaped clay, that stood for measures and commodities in the counting system older than writing (see Origins of Writing). The procedure: the tokens representing a consignment or obligation were placed inside, the ball was closed, its surface was marked with impressions of the tokens within — and then rolled all over with the seals of the parties or officials. The sealed ball travelled or was stored as the record of the transaction; in case of dispute, it was broken open and the tokens counted against the claim. Bullae are accounting with an audit trail in baked earth, and they stand in the direct ancestry of the tablet: the step from marks on a ball recording tokens inside it, to marks on a flat tablet recording goods directly, is the step Origins of Writing traces. Here, in the administrative article, what matters is the practice: by the Late Uruk, goods did not move unsealed, rooms did not stand unsealed, and obligations did not exist unsealed. Trust had been given a technology, and the technology was a rolled stone and a lump of mud.

The system's reach is its final fact. Identical sealing practice — same seal styles, same bulla form, same jar and door procedures — is found at every node of the expansion of §XV, from Susa to Habuba Kabira to Godin Tepe, where the sealed numerical tablets of the Oval enclosure show the practice operating inside a foreign town. Administration, in the Uruk world, was portable: a kit of seals, tokens, and habits that could be set down in a new place and begin, the same week, to count and to lock. That portability, more than any single artefact type, is what the expansion carried.

XV. The Uruk Expansion — Colonies, Enclaves, Outposts

In the later fourth millennium, material of unmistakably southern Uruk character — bevelled-rim bowls, cylinder seals and their sealings, bullae and numerical tablets, tripartite Mittelsaal houses, niched-and-buttressed public buildings — appears across the highland rim of the plain: up the Euphrates into Syria and southeastern Anatolia, across the northern plains, and eastward into Susiana and the Zagros. The phenomenon is the Uruk expansion, and its sites are not all the same kind of thing. Algaze's typology, which the debate of §XVI inherits, sorts them by their relation to the local world: colonies, new settlements of southerners on unoccupied ground; enclaves, southern quarters implanted within local towns; and outposts or stations, small installations on the routes between. Four sites carry the argument, and each is given here at its excavated strength.

Habuba Kabira — The Planted Town

Habuba Kabira South, on the Syrian Euphrates (excavated 1969–1975 in the Tabqa dam salvage, under Ernst Heinrich and Eva Strommenger; the site is now beneath Lake Assad), is the expansion's type-site. A town of some 22 ha in all — about 10 ha within its wall at the final phase — was founded on virgin soil in the Late Uruk and occupied for only about 120 years. It was planned: a mud-brick town wall some 3 m thick, with regular towers and gates; a main north–south street, paved, with drainage; lanes, courtyards, and some twenty excavated houses of the southern tripartite Mittelsaal type, averaging around 400 m² and the largest over 1,000 m²; and, on the adjoining hill of Tell Qannas, a monumental complex of great niched buildings on a terrace — the town's temple quarter. Its material culture is southern without admixture: Uruk pottery, cylinder seals, bullae, calculi, numerical tablets. And then, around 3100 BCE, it was left — abandoned apparently without violence, its houses emptied in order, as the whole northern network contracted. A planned city, built whole, lived in for four generations, and walked away from: Habuba is the single hardest fact for any purely commercial or acculturative reading of the expansion, because trade does not build town walls on empty ground.

Jebel Aruda — The Hill Station

Eight kilometres north of Habuba, on a rocky ridge above the Euphrates, Jebel Aruda repeats the pattern at smaller scale and higher drama: a settlement of southern houses disposed around a central monumental complex — two great niched buildings, conventionally temples, within their own enclosure on the summit — founded in the same Late Uruk horizon, occupied briefly, and abandoned in the same contraction, its main temple burnt at the end, with its contents in place. Aruda reads as the network's administrative-cultic station: less a town than a seat, the southern establishment's flag planted on the river road between the plain and the Anatolian sources of copper and timber. Together, Habuba and Aruda — with Tell Sheikh Hassan, upstream, showing an earlier, Middle Uruk southern presence beneath its later levels — document a settled southern occupation of the middle Euphrates bend, positioned exactly where the river routes meet the roads north.

Tell Brak — The Northern City That Was Already There

Tell Brak, in the Khabur plains of northeastern Syria, is the expansion's necessary corrective. Brak was no colony. Excavation and survey (Mallowan in the 1930s; the long Cambridge project under Joan and David Oates and their successors) have shown a northern city growing on its own trajectory: already a substantial centre in the early fourth millennium, reaching on survey estimates some 110–160 ha at its Late Chalcolithic height — a population, by the same area-and-density method as §IX, in the range of 17,000 to 24,000 — with monumental building (a great niched structure with walls 1.85 m thick and a basalt doorsill, dated c. 4400–3900 BCE), craft workshops in obsidian, shell inlay, and flint, feasting halls with mass-produced bowls of northern type, and the famous Eye Temple, named for its thousands of votive "eye idols," whose final form belongs to the period of southern contact. Southern Uruk material does reach Brak — seals, bevelled-rim bowls, a southern-style presence in the later fourth millennium — but it arrives at a peer, not a vacuum: Brak's urbanism is earlier than the contact and independent of it (Oates and colleagues, Antiquity 2007: "Early Mesopotamian urbanism: a new view from the north"). Any model of the expansion that cannot accommodate an indigenous northern city of near-Uruk scale is thereby refuted, and §XVI's debate is largely the working-out of that accommodation.

Godin Tepe — The Enclave in the Oval

Eastward, the pattern changes again. At Godin Tepe in the Kangavar valley of the central Zagros — astride the route later famous as the High Road to Ecbatana and Baghdad — the Canadian excavations (T. Cuyler Young Jr. and successors) found, in Period V (Late Uruk horizon), a local Iranian town and, set into its citadel, a walled Oval enclosure of wholly southern character: rooms around a courtyard, Uruk pottery in quantity, and the apparatus of southern administration in working order — some two dozen numerical tablets and sealed tablet blanks, cylinder sealings, tokens. Weiss and Young proposed the Oval as a merchant colony: a trading post of lowland merchants — from Susa, on the tablets' closest affinities — resident by arrangement inside a highland town that remained, outside the Oval's wall, entirely itself. Godin is the enclave type at its clearest, and its modesty is the point: a few rooms, a few tablets, a gate in a wall — the expansion, at this node, is a counting-house, not a conquest. When the Oval was abandoned, around 3000 BCE, the town continued without it, in local forms.

Around these four, the map fills in: Nineveh on the Tigris, with its Late Uruk levels and southern material; Hacinebi in Anatolia, where Stein's excavations found a small southern enclave inside a local town (§XVI); Arslantepe, where a local palace elite adopted southern administrative tools — sealings by the thousand — into a system of its own; Samsat, Tepecik, and the stations of the Euphrates and the Zagros roads; and Susa in Susiana, the expansion's eastern hinge, a southernised city with an administrative tradition — the proto-Elamite world — about to become a civilisation in its own right. The network's lifespan was short: founded in the centuries after c. 3600 BCE, flourishing c. 3400–3200, and contracted everywhere by c. 3100–3000, when the outposts were abandoned, the enclaves withdrawn, and the south turned inward to its Jemdet Nasr and Early Dynastic future. What the network was — that is the remaining question, and it is a question about models.

XVI. World System or Distance Parity? — Algaze and His Critics

The expansion's explanation was given its enduring form by Guillermo Algaze: The Uruk World System (1993; 2nd edition 2005) and Ancient Mesopotamia at the Dawn of Civilization (2008). Algaze adapted Immanuel Wallerstein's world-systems model — built for the capitalist world-economy — to the fourth millennium, and the adaptation is explicit about its mechanism. The southern alluvium, he argued, held decisive advantages: irrigation agriculture of unmatched productivity, cheap river transport, and an institutional head start in organisation. It lacked, absolutely, the raw materials of civilisation — metal, timber, stone — which lay in the highland periphery. The expansion was the core's answer: a network of colonies, enclaves, and outposts planted on the routes to those resources, securing asymmetric exchange in which southern manufactures, organisation, and ideology flowed out and highland materials flowed back — the world's earliest colonial system, in his phrase's implication, and a world system in structure if not in scale. The model's strengths are the facts of §XV: it predicts planted towns like Habuba exactly where they are found, explains the network's sudden contraction as a core decision, and gives the bevelled-rim bowls and sealings of the outposts a function in a single political economy.

The critique, whose fullest statement is Gil J. Stein's Rethinking World-Systems (1999), attacks the model's physics before its archaeology. In the pre-modern world, Stein argues, the costs of transport and communication grow so steeply with distance — weeks of river and road separate Uruk from Habuba, let alone from Godin — that a core cannot project and sustain political dominance across them: beyond a certain radius, power attenuates to parity, whatever the core's advantages at home. His alternative, the distance-parity model, predicts that long-distance contact between early complex societies will characteristically take the form of negotiated exchange between autonomous parties — trade diasporas, resident merchant enclaves operating by the host's leave — rather than colonial control. Stein's test case is his own excavation at Hacinebi, in southeastern Anatolia: there, a small Mesopotamian enclave, identified by its southern pottery, sealings, and foodways, lived for centuries inside a flourishing local town whose economy, craft production, and development show, on his analysis, no measurable southern domination and no disruption — contact without control, exactly as distance-parity predicts. Add Tell Brak's indigenous urbanism (§XV), the chemical sourcing studies that find almost no movement of ceramic vessels between the network's nodes — the pots are local-made in southern style, not traded goods — and the regional trajectories of the north that proceed through the contact centuries on their own logic, and the critics' composite picture is of an expansion that was real, southern, and organised, but segmented: colonies on empty river reaches, enclaves by negotiation in foreign towns, emulation everywhere, and domination — where it existed at all — a local and temporary affair.

Algaze's replies, in the 2005 second edition and after, concede ground and hold ground in specific places: the model's later statements distinguish more carefully among the nodes' types, accept that northern urbanism had indigenous roots, and rest the core's advantage less on coercion than on organisational asymmetry — the south's institutions could mobilise, record, and sustain exchange at a scale its partners could not match, which is itself a form of power, exercised without garrisons. Pollock's and Rothman's readings stand at further angles: emphasis, respectively, on the internal political economy that made expansion an outlet for the oikos system, and on the north's own Late Chalcolithic complexity, within which "Uruk" traits are one influence among several. This article's adjudication is the one the evidence currently supports: the expansion happened; its nodes differ in kind; Habuba-type colonies are genuine foundations of southern settlers; Godin- and Hacinebi-type enclaves are negotiated presences; Brak-type contacts are meetings of peers; and no single model — world system or distance parity — covers all three without remainder. The debate is not a scandal in the field. It is the field, working as it should, on evidence that has grown faster than any one model built to hold it.

ALGAZE — THE WORLD SYSTEM

A resource-poor, organisationally rich core plants colonies, enclaves, and outposts to secure highland materials in asymmetric exchange. Predicts Habuba; explains the contraction as systemic. (Algaze 1993/2005, 2008.)

STEIN — DISTANCE PARITY

Transport and communication costs cap the radius of pre-modern dominance; at distance, contact defaults to parity, diaspora, and negotiation. Tested at Hacinebi. (Stein 1999.)

THE NORTHERN EVIDENCE

Tell Brak's independent urbanism, near-zero inter-site ceramic trade on sourcing analysis, and unbroken local sequences constrain every core-dominance reading. (Oates et al. 2007, and the sourcing literature.)

THE SETTLED REMAINDER

Southern settlers demonstrably founded towns on the Euphrates and withdrew from them within centuries. Whatever the network was, it was planted — and unplanted — by decision.

XVII. The City Remembered — Gilgamesh's Uruk

Uruk outlived its period by three thousand years of continuous occupation, and it remembered itself. The memory's fullest form is the Epic of Gilgamesh — treated as the Tier B text it is, in this hub's Gilgamesh article — whose hero is a king of Uruk and whose opening movement, in the Standard Babylonian version, is not a battle or a journey but an invitation to inspect the city. In Andrew George's published translation (Penguin, 1999), the narrator directs the reader: "Climb Uruk's wall and walk back and forth! Survey its foundations, examine the brickwork! Were its bricks not fired in an oven? Did the Seven Sages not lay its foundations?" The poem then measures the city in its own terms — a square league of city, one of gardens, one of clay-pits, and the precinct of Ishtar — and returns to the wall at the epic's end, when the travelled, exhausted hero shows the boatman the same walls as the sum of what endures. The wall the poem celebrates — some 9.5 km of it — is archaeologically real, though it belongs to the centuries after the Uruk period proper (c. 3000–2800 BCE), and the King List tradition assigns its building to Gilgamesh himself. Tier A certifies the wall, the circuit, and the city's scale; the king, and the sages who laid his foundations, are the tradition's own.

What the memory gets right, across its two thousand years of transmission from the Uruk period to the epic's surviving copies, is the identity of the city: Uruk is, in its own literature, the city of the wall and of Eanna — the two facts a surveyor would still report. The Sumerian poems of the Gilgamesh cycle add the assembly of Uruk, its elders and its young men, debating war with Kish in Gilgamesh and Aga — a political memory, however literary, of the city as a body that could be consulted. And Eridu's memory (§III) completes the pair: the King List opens at Eridu, where kingship first descended, and passes in time to Uruk, whose dynasty — Enmerkar, Lugalbanda, Gilgamesh — the tradition makes the first fully human line of kings. Read as tradition, the sequence is theology. Read against the stratigraphy of §§IV and XI, it is also a fair map: the temple town first, the walled city after, in that order, in those places. The hub's rule applies one last time: the map is Tier A where the ground confirms it, Tier B where only the texts assert it — and the reader is told, at each step, which ground they are standing on.

A final cross-reference disciplines the wonder. At Göbekli Tepe, two and a half millennia before Eridu's first platform, hunter-gatherers raised carved stone enclosures of a scale the Ubaid never attempted; at Mehrgarh, in the same centuries as Uruk's rise, a farming world of equal antiquity was building — granaries, workshops, cemeteries — toward a wholly different urban future on the Indus. Uruk was not the only road, and its priority is a fact about the surviving record of one plain. What that plain's record shows, uniquely complete, is the mechanism of the urban threshold: not a genius, not a conquest, but an accumulation — of silt and grain, of platforms and tablets, of ration bowls and sealed doors — until the village's institutions were running a city, and the city, in time, remembered itself into epic.

XVIII. What the First Cities Do and Do Not Show

The Ubaid and Uruk evidence, taken at its excavated strength, shows the following. A farming society of the southern alluvium, over some two and a half millennia, developed in place: irrigation agriculture below the rainfall line; the tripartite house and temple; a temple institution that grew from a 2.8-metre cella at Eridu, rebuilt on its footprint through eighteen horizons, into platform temples and then into great households holding estates, herds, workshops, and thousands of ration-fed dependents; a repertoire of material culture shared, by adoption and exchange, from the Gulf to Anatolia; and then, within a few centuries, a city of 250 hectares and somewhere between 25,000 and 50,000 people, raising precincts of nine hectares, feeding its labour from mould-made bowls produced in the millions, sealing its stores and its obligations with rolled cylinders of carved stone, and planting its own urban form — walls, temples, counting-houses and all — up the rivers into foreign highlands, whence it withdrew, again within centuries, leaving the planted towns empty. Every element of that summary is a trench, a survey map, a tablet, or a sealing. It is, on the present record, the first fully documented passage of a human society from villages to a city, and the documentation — not merely the passage — is what makes Mesopotamia's case the foundation case.

What the evidence does not show must be entered in the same hand. It does not show a "first city" in any sense stronger than §IX's stated terms: the superlative is definition-bound, record-bound, and already qualified by Brak's parallel urbanism in the north. It does not show why the painted pottery died, where the Uruk dead were buried, or what the bevelled-rim bowl held — three silences at the centre of the period's loudest evidence. It does not show a single mechanism for the expansion, and it refutes, in its Brak and Hacinebi evidence, the simplest version of the most famous model proposed for it. It does not show kings, by name, in the Uruk period at all: the "priest-king" of the sealings is an iconographic figure, and the period's rulers are anonymous to us — a city that invented writing left its first two centuries of accounts unsigned. And it does not show inevitability. The Ubaid ran for two millennia without producing a city; the expansion's cities were abandoned within four generations of their founding; Mehrgarh ran for five millennia on the household road and Göbekli's builders raised monuments and went home to hunt. The urban threshold was crossed, here, by these people, through these institutions — temple, ration, seal, and wall — and the record of the crossing, kept in mud and read back layer by layer, is the subject this article exists to report.

Perhaps that is the deepest instruction the Eridu sounding offers this hub. Civilisation, in the one case where its foundations can be watched being dug, did not begin with a monument or a text. It began with a room three metres square, a niche, a burnt pedestal, and a community that rebuilt the room on the same spot when it decayed — and kept the appointment, through fish offerings and platforms and terraces, for two thousand years, until the accumulated faithfulness of the rebuilding had itself become the monument, and the town around it had work for seal-cutters and a use for ten thousand identical bowls. The first city was an accretion before it was an achievement. Its strata say so, and the strata — tiered, dated, and debated in the open literature — are where this archive leaves the claim.
