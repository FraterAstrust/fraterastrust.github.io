I. How to Read This Article — Tier C, and the Two Columns

This article is classified Tier C — Modern Construction, and the classification is stated in words here, at the outset, because everything that follows depends on it. Tier C, in the scheme of this hub, means that the subject is a body of ideas built in modern times — in this case, in the first half of the twentieth century — whatever ancient materials those ideas claim as their sources. The subject here is the Atlantis and Egypt material in the trance readings of Edgar Cayce (1877–1945). The companion article Mu and Atlantis Reborn treats Cayce briefly, as one figure in the longer modern Atlantis tradition; this article is the full treatment of Cayce himself, and it does not repeat that article's wider story.

Cayce's material demands a discipline the other Tier C subjects do not, because it comes in two columns that are constantly confused. In the first column stands what is documented: a real man, real sessions, on real dates, in real rooms, taken down in shorthand by a named stenographer, typed, filed, indexed, and preserved to this day by an organisation he founded. That column is unusually solid — few bodies of claimed revelation in history are as well attested as documents. In the second column stands what the readings assert: that Atlantis existed and fell in stages ending around 10,500 BCE; that its refugees carried civilisation to Egypt; that the Giza monuments were built under a priest named Ra-Ta at that remote date; and that a Hall of Records lies sealed between the Sphinx and the Nile. Nothing in the second column is established by the solidity of the first. A transcript proves that words were spoken. It does not prove that what the words describe ever happened. This article will keep the two columns visibly separate in every section: documented frame first, assertion labelled as assertion, and — where an assertion has been tested against independent evidence — the result of the test stated plainly.

// A NOTE ON QUOTATIONS //

The readings are a copyrighted, archived corpus, and this article quotes them only once, briefly, from a published scholarly summary in which the wording was verified (Joscelyn Godwin, Atlantis and the Cycles of Time, as presented on the publisher's page). Everywhere else, the readings' content is paraphrased and attributed, with reading numbers given so that any reader with access to the A.R.E. archive can check the paraphrase against the transcript. That checkability is the corpus's genuine strength, and this article uses it rather than decorating itself with unverified quotation.

A last word of register. The readings are not a dead curiosity. The Association for Research and Enlightenment, founded by Cayce in 1931, is active today; its members study the readings as a living spiritual tradition, and the archive at Virginia Beach is their scripture's manuscript room. This article treats that tradition with the respect due to any living faith — and treats its historical assertions with the scrutiny due to any historical claim. Those two duties do not conflict. They are, in fact, the same duty performed twice: to say exactly what a thing is.

II. The Documented Man — Edgar Cayce, 1877–1945

The biographical frame is not in dispute, and it can be stated briefly. Edgar Cayce was born on 18 March 1877 near Hopkinsville, in Christian County, Kentucky, and raised in the Disciples of Christ tradition — a devout, Bible-reading churchgoer and Sunday-school teacher for most of his life. His formal schooling ended early, and he worked in bookshops and then as a photographer, the trade he followed for much of his adult life. In 1901, in the standard account preserved by his organisation and his biographers, he gave the first of his trance discourses: having lost his voice to a persistent laryngeal condition, he was hypnotised and, in that state, spoke a diagnosis and treatment for his own ailment — after which, the account holds, his voice returned. Whether the episode is read as the opening of a psychic career or as a striking case of suggestion and psychosomatic illness is a question this article leaves to the reader; that the episode was reported, and that Cayce thereafter gave readings for others, belongs to the documented column.

For two decades the readings were occasional and mostly unrecorded. The career became an institution in stages that are likewise documented: the move to Virginia Beach, Virginia, in the 1920s; a hospital venture there at the end of that decade; the founding in 1931 of the Association for Research and Enlightenment (A.R.E.), the membership organisation that promoted, published, and organised the work; and a final period of extraordinary demand during the Second World War, when the waiting list for readings stretched for years and Cayce, against the advice his own readings gave him, increased his daily sessions to a pace his health could not sustain. He died on 3 January 1945 in Virginia Beach. His sons — Hugh Lynn Cayce, who led the A.R.E. for decades, and Edgar Evans Cayce, who compiled the Atlantis readings — carried the organisation and the archive forward. The popular biography There Is a River by Thomas Sugrue, published in Cayce's lifetime, and Jess Stearn's later bestseller The Sleeping Prophet — "sleeping" because Cayce reclined and closed his eyes to give a reading — made him, posthumously, the most famous American psychic of the twentieth century.

III. The Reading as Performance — Couch, Conductor, Stenographer

A Cayce reading was not a manuscript, a vision written up afterwards, or a book dictated over months. It was a performance with a fixed liturgy, and the fixity matters to the historian, because it is what makes the corpus a corpus. Cayce would loosen his collar and belt, lie down on a couch, fold his hands, and enter — by his own account and that of every witness — a sleep-like trance. His wife, Gertrude Evans Cayce, ordinarily served as conductor: she read aloud a formulaic suggestion naming the subject — often a person far away, identified by name and location — and posed the questions the subject or the sitters had submitted. Cayce then spoke, in a normal voice, in discourse that ranged from medical prescription to cosmology. At the end, the conductor gave the formula for waking, and Cayce woke, ordinarily remembering nothing of what he had said.

The third figure in the room is the one to whom the archive owes its existence. From September 1923, the readings were taken down in shorthand by Gladys Davis (later Gladys Davis Turner), Cayce's stenographer, who recorded her first reading for him on 30 August 1923 and began full-time work on 10 September 1923. Davis transcribed her shorthand into typewritten copies — one for the subject, one for the file — and over the following decades she built the subject index and cross-referencing system by which the corpus can be searched, work she continued with the Edgar Cayce Foundation long after Cayce's death. Readings given before 1923 survive only patchily, which is why the systematic corpus is effectively a 1923–1945 corpus. The point cannot be overstated: the readings exist as evidence because a stenographer was in the room. What the historian possesses is not Cayce's inner experience, about which the record can say nothing, but a dated, witnessed, transcribed text of what was said in that room — and that is already, for a claimed revelation, a great deal.

// WHAT THE PERFORMANCE ESTABLISHES — AND WHAT IT DOES NOT //

The stenographic record establishes, to a standard few comparable corpora meet, that specific words were spoken by Edgar Cayce, in trance, on specific dates, in response to specific questions, for specific named or numbered subjects. It does not establish the source of those words — Cayce's own account invoked a universal record and a higher self; sceptical accounts invoke the subconscious, prior reading, and the expectations of sitters — and it does not establish the truth of any historical statement the words contain. Both halves of that sentence are the archive's real value.

IV. The Archive — A.R.E., the Foundation, and the Numbering of the Readings

The counts in this section are the A.R.E.'s own published figures, checked against the organisation's current statements. The A.R.E. puts the complete corpus at 14,306 readings — the figure given on its own site for the entire set, held in its library at Virginia Beach and made available to members in a searchable database. (Counts of 14,307 or 14,308 circulate in secondary literature, reflecting small differences in how fragments and duplicates are tallied; the figure used here is the organisation's own headline number.) Of these, the A.R.E.'s health materials state that nearly 70 percent concern the diagnosis of disease and the outlining of treatment — the medical readings were always the bulk of the work, and the Atlantis and Egypt material treated in this article is a minority strand within the whole. The readings in their entirety address, by the organisation's count, some 10,000 different topics.

Two institutions divide the custody. The A.R.E., founded 1931, is the membership and publishing body. The Edgar Cayce Foundation, chartered in 1948 after Cayce's death, is the legal custodian of the readings themselves — the transcripts, reports, and correspondence — preserving the originals and governing research access. The readings are cited by a numbering system devised under Gladys Davis: each recipient or subject was assigned a number, and that person's readings are numbered in sequence after it. Thus 294 is Edgar Cayce himself, and 294-8 is the eighth reading in his own file; 378-16 is the sixteenth reading for subject 378; 958-3 the third for subject 958. Names were replaced by numbers in the public set to protect recipients' privacy — approximately 5,750 people received readings — and the numbering, reorganised under an indexing plan in 1959, is the citation system every scholarly and A.R.E. publication uses. When this article gives a reading number, it is giving the reader an address: the claim can be found, dated, and read in full in the archive. That is how a trance corpus ought to be cited, and it is to the Foundation's credit that it can be.

One caution belongs with the numbers. A count of readings mentioning Atlantis is a count of a theme in a corpus, assembled over twenty years of individual sessions for individual clients — not the chapters of a single composed history. The composite Atlantis and the composite Egypt that later books present were assembled from these scattered statements, principally by Edgar Evans Cayce in Edgar Cayce on Atlantis (1968) and by the A.R.E.'s own research files. The assembly is legitimate work, and the A.R.E. does it openly. But the reader should know that the seamless narrative is an editorial product laid over a fragmentary record — as, to be fair, is much of ancient history. The difference is that ancient history's fragments are then tested against independent evidence. Sections XIV–XVII trace what happened when Cayce's were.

V. Life Readings — How Atlantis and Egypt Entered the Corpus

Reincarnation was not in Cayce's teaching from the start, and its entry is datable. Cayce began as a fundamentalist Christian who wrestled, by his own account, with doubts about what was speaking through him. In 1923 — the year the stenographic record begins — the readings took up the idea of past lives, and a new genre, the life reading, was born: instead of diagnosing the subject's body, the reading narrated the subject's previous incarnations and drew lessons for the present life from them. The first life reading's subject, in the tabulation reported by Godwin, was Cayce's own son Edgar Evans. Over the following twenty years, on Godwin's count, Cayce gave around 2,500 life readings — and no fewer than 700 of them placed a former life of the subject in Atlantis. In 1932 Cayce also consented to a deliberate series of readings on the deep past itself — the 364 series — which supplies much of the systematic Atlantis material: its geography, its factions, its destructions.

The mechanism by which a lost continent entered so many biographies deserves a plain statement, because it explains the corpus's shape. A life reading answered the client in front of it. When hundreds of clients, over two decades, were each told of an Atlantean incarnation, the readings accumulated a shared world — consistent in its furniture, its factions, and its chronology to a degree that believers read as corroboration and sceptics read as the natural product of one mind, one room, one set of expectations, and an organisation that indexed and cross-referenced every statement, allowing later readings to be given in a context already saturated with earlier ones. Both readings of the consistency are available; the archive itself cannot decide between them. What the archive can do — and what this article does — is report the composite faithfully, attributed to the readings, and then ask the only question evidence can answer: whether anything outside the corpus matches it.

VI. Atlantis as Asserted — Poseidia, the Law of One, and the Sons of Belial

Atlantis, in the readings, began — as the composite presents it — as a continent-sized land in the Atlantic, which successive catastrophes broke into islands. The readings name the principal remnants: Poseidia, the northern and last-surviving island, in the region of the Bahamas; Aryan, in the central Atlantic; and Og, in the west. Humanity in Atlantis, in the readings' telling, was immensely old — the story reaches back hundreds of thousands of years — and divided, across all its later history, into two enduring factions whose conflict is the moral engine of the whole narrative. The Children of the Law of One held to a single God, spiritual discipline, and the protection of the vulnerable. The Sons of Belial sought material power and self-gratification, and exploited a class of beings the readings call the "things" — in the composite, hybrid, part-animal creatures kept as labourers and worse, whose treatment is the readings' central Atlantean sin.

The debt of this picture to its predecessors should be noted here and developed in Section XIII: staged destructions and a final island called Poseidonis are Theosophical furniture (see Lemuria), and the single-source civilisation diffusing its gifts worldwide is Donnelly's architecture of 1882 (see Mu and Atlantis Reborn). What Cayce adds is not the frame but the interior: named regions, a priesthood, a theology of the Law of One, and thousands of individual biographies staged inside the frame, one per client. It is also worth recording what the readings' Atlantis is for, in its own terms. A life reading was a pastoral instrument: the Atlantean past life explained a present aptitude, a present fear, a present relationship, and carried a moral — most often, a warning against repeating, in this life, the misuse of power that had drowned the last one. Atlantis in the readings is less a place than a diagnosis. That does not make it history; but it does explain why, for the people who received these readings, its truth was felt in the present tense.

VII. The Firestone — Atlantean Technology as the Readings Describe It

The readings describe an Atlantis whose technology, in its high periods, resembled and in places exceeded that of the twentieth century: aircraft and submarines, electrical devices, forms of communication and transport at a distance, and — at the centre of the system — a great crystal the readings call the Tuaoi stone and, in later readings, the firestone. As the composite presents it, the firestone was housed in a special building, gathered and focused the energy of the sun, and supplied power for transport, industry, and healing. Its misuse is woven through the destructions: in Godwin's summary of the readings, the first catastrophe followed the turning of advanced technology to destructive purposes — against the great animals the readings say then overran the earth — and the second was precipitated when the firestone's power was turned too high. By the final period, in the readings' telling, crystal power was being used for coercion, and Poseidia had become, morally, what Plato's Atlantis was in miniature: a rich, armed, corrupted sea power awaiting its day and night of misfortune.

The historian's note on this section is short and is the same note sounded in the companion article: none of this technology is Plato's. Plato's Atlantis has canals, triremes, and orichalcum; the crystal power station, the aircraft, and the death-dealing rays enter the tradition with the twentieth century, in a country electrifying itself and learning to fly. That a trance discourse of the 1920s and 1930s should furnish its lost continent with the wonders of its own decade is exactly what the through-line of this hub would predict — each era adds what it wants (see Mu and Atlantis Reborn, §XVI). The readings' defenders reply, fairly, that Cayce disclaimed any reading of the Atlantis literature — his son Hugh Lynn stated that Cayce had not read Plato on Atlantis or the books about it — and that the question of influence is therefore open. It is open as a question about Cayce's reading habits. It is not open as a question about evidence: no artefact, site, or deposit corresponding to the readings' technological Atlantis has ever been identified, anywhere, and the ocean floors where it would have to lie have been mapped and sampled for half a century.

VIII. The Three Destructions — The Readings' Chronology

The readings do not sink Atlantis once. They break it in three destructions, and the tabulation of those destructions — made by Edgar Evans Cayce from his father's files — is the chronological spine of the whole Cayce Atlantis. In the standard summary, represented here by Godwin's: a first destruction around 50,000 BCE (some tabulations give 50,700 BCE), in which part of the continent went down and the land began its fragmentation; a second destruction around 28,000 BCE, preceded by the emigration of the Law of One's followers to refuges that the readings name across the world — Peru, the Yucatán, the Pyrenees, Egypt, and places in what is now the American West among them; and a final destruction of the last island, Poseidia, which the readings place at the horizon this article will meet repeatedly: Cayce cited 10,014 BCE in one tabulation for the final sinking, the process being complete, in Godwin's summary, by about 9500 BCE. The round figure the tradition carries — c. 10,500 BCE — is the horizon of the final catastrophe and of the Egyptian events of Sections X–XII, and it is the date around which the entire later controversy orbits.

Two honest cautions about the table. First, the readings were given across twenty years, in answer to thousands of different questions, and their internal dates are not perfectly uniform; the tabulations smooth them, and different A.R.E.-derived summaries smooth them slightly differently — which is why this article gives ranges and names its summariser rather than pretending to a precision the corpus does not possess. Second, the resemblance of the final horizon to Plato's chronology — Atlantis sinking some 9,000 years before Solon, which lands in the same millennium — is real, and cuts both ways in the literature: for believers, independent convergence; for sceptics, the clearest fingerprint of the tradition Cayce inherited. The archive records the resemblance. It does not adjudicate it.

IX. Bimini — The 1968–69 Expectation and the Stones Themselves

Cayce's connection with the Bahamian island of Bimini began in his own lifetime: he travelled there in 1927 in connection with a treasure venture, and readings of that period identified the island, in the composite, as the site of a temple of the Poseidians and as the highest ground of the sunken land still above water. The famous statement came in a reading given on 28 June 1940 (reading 958-3): that Poseidia would be among the first portions of Atlantis to rise again, and that this was to be expected in 1968 and 1969. This article's single verified quotation from the readings is that statement, as quoted in Godwin's scholarly summary:

Poseidia will be among the first portions of Atlantis to rise again — expect it in '68 and '69 — not so far away! READING 958-3, 28 JUNE 1940 — AS QUOTED IN JOSCELYN GODWIN, ATLANTIS AND THE CYCLES OF TIME

On 2 September 1968 — the first of the two named years — divers off Paradise Point, North Bimini, among them J. Manson Valentine of the Miami Museum of Science, found the formation since known as the Bimini Road: a pavement-like alignment of large, flat, roughly rectangular limestone blocks, about 0.8 kilometres long, in some five and a half metres of water, with a hooked turn at its southwestern end. The coincidence of place and year made it the most celebrated underwater site in the Atlantis literature, and the full account — the discovery, the surveys, and the coring — is given in the companion article Mu and Atlantis Reborn (§XI) and is summarised, not repeated, here. The geological assessment, from the detailed examination by Gifford and Ball and the coring work of Eugene Shinn and others, is that the Road is natural beachrock: a limestone of carbonate-cemented shell debris that forms in the intertidal zone, lithifies as a continuous layer, and fractures along straight joints into apparently laid blocks. The decisive observations were that no second course of blocks exists anywhere on the features; that bedrock lies close beneath the whole area; that no tool marks were found; and that cores through adjacent blocks show the rock's internal bedding running continuously from block to block — one layer, broken in place. Radiocarbon results reported from the coring placed the shell material at roughly 3,500 years and its cement at roughly 2,800 years — thousands of years younger than any date proposed for Atlantis.

// BIMINI — THE BALANCE SHEET, RESTATED FOR THIS CORPUS //

The 1940 statement was real, dated, archived, and fulfilled in its year as a matter of timing: something was found off Bimini in 1968. What was found is, on cores, bedding, and dates, a natural formation. A prophecy can score on its calendar and fail on its geology, and this one did both. For the method of this hub, Bimini is the Cayce corpus in miniature: the documented column is impeccable — the reading exists, its date is 1940, its text says 1968–69 — and the asserted column, tested at last against the physical world, does not hold.

X. Egypt as Asserted — The Migration and the Building on the Mounds

If Atlantis is the readings' lost world, Egypt is its heir — and the Egypt material is, by the A.R.E.'s own research files, the richest store of prehistory in the whole corpus. The Circulating File Archaeology: Egypt & Gobi, the Foundation's compilation of the Egypt extracts, opens with the observation that of all the prehistoric periods in the readings, Egypt dominates: more than 1,100 people were told, in their life readings, that they had lived there around 10,500 BCE. Assembled, those hundreds of individual statements compose a single narrative, and it is that narrative — the readings' narrative, attributed throughout — that this section and the next two set out.

As the readings present it, the story begins with the destructions. As Atlantis broke up, there came — in the paraphrase of reading 5748-6 as quoted in the A.R.E.'s own published research — an egress of peoples from the Atlantean land, who built a city near the edge of the Sahara. Egypt, in the readings, was not empty when they came: the same reading tradition holds that the land had known far older occupation — reading 341-9 is cited in the A.R.E. file for the statement that the country had been submerged for nearly a quarter of a million years since civilisation had last been in that portion of it — and that the newcomers conducted what the readings themselves describe as archaeological research, discovering ancient mounds and building upon them. The monuments, in reading 341-9's account as quoted by the A.R.E., were unearthed and added to from time to time, and many were buried again beneath shifting sands. The picture, in other words, is not of a civilisation founded from nothing in 10,500 BCE, but of one deliberately restored — by immigrants who understood themselves as heirs, excavating and rebuilding on foundations they believed immemorially old. It is a sophisticated piece of world-building, and it is worth noticing how much of the later alternative-Egyptology imagination — the monument repeatedly rebuilt, the knowledge older than the stones that carry it — is already present in it, in readings of the early 1930s.

At the centre of the restoration stands one figure, and he is the subject of the next section: the priest under whose direction, the readings say, the works at Giza were planned — and who, the readings say, Edgar Cayce himself had been.

XI. Ra-Ta — The Priest the Readings Say Cayce Had Been

The Ra-Ta narrative is anchored in Cayce's own file. The 294 series comprises the readings given for Cayce himself, and reading 294-8, given on 9 February 1924, is his own life reading — the document in which his incarnations, including an Egyptian high priest, were set out. Across the 294 series and the wider Egypt readings, that priest acquired a name, a biography, and a mission: Ra-Ta. As the A.R.E.'s published paraphrase, Egypt 10,500 B.C., presents the story — paraphrased there from the readings, and paraphrased again here — Ra-Ta was born among the tribe of Zu, in the region of Ararat, a child set apart from his people, who grew into a prophet and priest. Foreseeing catastrophe, he counselled migration: a company, led in the narrative by the king's son Arart with Ra-Ta as its spiritual head, journeyed to Egypt, where Arart became king — succeeded in the readings' genealogy by Araaraart — and Ra-Ta became high priest of a restored civilisation. Under his direction, the readings say, the works at Giza were laid out: reading 294-151 is cited by A.R.E. researchers for the statement that Giza was formed according to a plan Ra-Ta had worked out in the mount, in relation to the positions of the stars and the constellation Libra. The temples of the period — the readings name a Temple of Sacrifice and a Temple Beautiful — served, in the narrative, the healing and purification of the hybrid "things" and the teaching of the Law of One: the one God, the one law, that the Atlantean faithful had carried out of the drowning world.

// THE CLAIM, AS THE READINGS LEAVE IT //

The readings assert that the Great Pyramid and the Sphinx at Giza were built in the period of Ra-Ta, around 10,500 BCE, under his direction — some eight thousand years before the date Egyptology assigns them. Individual readings fill in the scene: the reading for subject [993], for example, placed that subject's past life in Egypt during the Great Pyramid's construction, with Ra-Ta as priest, at circa 10,500 BCE — and the same file tradition holds that still earlier pyramids preceded Ra-Ta's, built in an Egypt more ancient still. The claim is precise, dated, and attributed. It is also, as Section XVII sets out, contradicted by the whole independent evidentiary record of the Giza plateau.

Two scholarly cautions complete the section. The first is about the name: Ra-Ta is not an attested ancient Egyptian person. No inscription, king list, tomb, or text of any period knows a priest of this name directing works at Giza, and the name itself is not formed as Egyptian names of the Old Kingdom are formed. He exists in exactly one corpus — this one. The second caution is about the identification: that Cayce was Ra-Ta is itself a reading-internal claim, developed across the 294 series rather than delivered complete in 294-8, and later A.R.E. literature is frank that the whole Ra-Ta period was understood within the organisation as the pattern and precedent of Cayce's modern work — reading 294-197, quoted in the A.R.E.'s own booklet, states that Cayce's present period of activity is a replica of the Egyptian one. The narrative, in other words, does double duty in the tradition: it is offered as ancient history, and it functions as the founding charter of a twentieth-century organisation. A researcher can honour the second function while withholding assent from the first. That is what this hub does.

XII. The Hall of Records — Three Repositories and a Sealed Chamber at Giza

Of all the assertions in the corpus, one has driven more expeditions, more television, and more drilling permits than any other: the claim that the records of Atlantis survive, sealed, and can be found. The claim took its canonical form in a concentrated series of readings given from October to December 1933. As the readings present it, the Atlanteans, foreseeing the end, preserved their records in three repositories: one in the Atlantean land itself, sunk with Poseidia — the Bimini repository of Section IX; one in the Yucatán, overshadowed, in reading 2012-1's paraphrased terms, by a temple there; and one in Egypt — the Hall of Records. Reading 2012-1 (25 September 1939), quoted in the A.R.E.'s published research, names the three together: the one in the sunken Atlantean land, which the reading says will rise and is rising again; the one in Egypt, reached by the place of the records that leads from the Sphinx to the hall of records; and the one in the Yucatán land. Reading 5750-1 adds the tradition's most quoted summary of the doctrine, given by the A.R.E. in paraphrase here: that the three places are one, and that when the time of changes draws near, the three may be opened together — the records are one.

The Location, as Specified

The Egyptian hall is specified with unusual precision. Reading 378-16 (29 October 1933) is the locus classicus: as quoted in the A.R.E.'s published research and in scholarly quotation of it, the reading describes a record of Atlantis — its beginnings, its destructions, the sojournings of its peoples, and the building of the pyramid of initiation — preserved as copies from the sunken land, and places that record, in position, between the Sphinx and the river: as the sun rises from the waters, the line of shadow or light falls between the paws of the Sphinx, which was set later as the sentinel or guard, and the sealed chamber may not be entered from the connecting chambers from the Sphinx's right paw until the appointed time. Reading 5748-6, as quoted by the A.R.E., adds the architectural detail: with the storehouse or record house, where the records are still to be uncovered, there is a chamber or passage from the right forepaw to the entrance of the record chamber, or record tomb. Later readings of 1941, in the scholarly summary, describe the hall as containing records in both Atlantean and Egyptian writing, and imply that the hall itself was pyramid-shaped; reading 2329-3, quoted by the A.R.E., places an individual's tomb as part of the Hall of Records along the entrance from the Sphinx toward the pyramid — the hall, in that reading, being itself a pyramid of its own.

The claim's status can be stated in one sentence, and Sections XIV–XVI will spend three sections showing the work behind it: nothing matching Cayce's description has ever been found — not by the radar surveys of the 1970s, not by the seismic work of the 1990s, not by the drilling the Egyptian authorities permitted, and not by the full exploration in 1999 of the one genuine subterranean tomb near the Sphinx that hope fastened on. The Hall of Records is the best-tested claim in the entire Cayce corpus, because it is the most testable: a physical chamber, at a specified place, of specified contents. It has been looked for, with instruments, by people who wanted to find it. The looking is the subject of the afterlife.

XIII. Precursors — What Was Already in Print Before the Trance Spoke

A trance corpus does not speak in a vacuum, and the scholarly literature on Cayce — Fritze's Egyptomania (2016), Picknett and Prince, and Jason Colavito's The Legends of the Pyramids (2021) among it — has documented, carefully and without malice, how much of the readings' ancient world was already available in print before the readings gave it voice. The readings themselves, as Colavito notes, sometimes cite pre-existing esoteric literature by name. The Atlantis frame — a real civilisation behind Plato's story, destroyed in catastrophe, mother of Egypt and of the world's civilisations — is Donnelly's, from 1882. The staged destructions and the name Poseidonis for the last island are Theosophical. The hidden chambers beneath Giza have a longer pedigree still: Pliny reported that the people near the plateau in his day believed the Sphinx hollow, containing a king's tomb; medieval Islamic legend told of subterranean passages beneath the pyramids; and the legend of King Surid, who in medieval Arabic tradition built the Great Pyramid before the Flood precisely to preserve his civilisation's knowledge against the waters, was translated into European languages from the seventeenth century onward and cited by Donnelly himself. The Hall of Records, in other words, has an ancestor a thousand years old: a pyramid built as an ark for knowledge.

The most immediate precursors are the closest in time. In publications beginning in the late 1920s, H. C. Randall-Stevens described subterranean passages and initiation temples beneath the Giza plateau, reached after Atlantis's fall; Harvey Spencer Lewis's The Symbolic Prophecy of the Great Pyramid (1936) presented similar claims with strikingly similar diagrams, Lewis resting his on the records of his Rosicrucian order. Cayce's Hall of Records descriptions resemble these chambers closely enough that scholars have proposed derivation — a proposal the archive records as a scholarly argument, not a settled fact, since resemblance is not borrowing and Cayce's circle maintained he did not read this literature. What can be said without controversy is the point that matters methodologically: every major element of the readings' Atlantis and Egypt existed in the print culture of Cayce's lifetime before the readings assembled it. That does not disprove the readings — a tradition could, in principle, preserve truth through many channels. It does remove the argument from independent invention: the corpus cannot be treated as a witness sealed off from its century, because its century is legible on every page of it.

XIV. The Afterlife, I — The A.R.E. Goes to Giza, and the Lehner Story

What distinguishes the Cayce tradition from every other in this hub is that its custodians tested it. From the 1970s, the A.R.E. and the Edgar Cayce Foundation actively supported archaeological and geophysical work at Giza aimed, in significant part, at the Hall of Records. In 1978 the A.R.E. cooperated with SRI International in surveys around the Sphinx, using electrical resistivity and acoustical sounding to look for chambers in the bedrock. Anomalies were reported near the Sphinx's paws; test drilling found only natural fissures in the limestone. The pattern of the next twenty years was set in that first season: an instrument anomaly, honestly reported; a physical check; a natural explanation. To the A.R.E.'s lasting credit, it published and funded the checks anyway.

Mark Lehner — From Seeker to Egyptologist

The human emblem of this period is Mark Lehner. As a young man of twenty-one in 1971, Lehner — steeped in Cayce's works, and told by a reader that in a past life he had helped bury the Hall of Records beneath the Sphinx — travelled to Egypt believing, in his own later words, that his destiny was to find it. The Edgar Cayce Foundation funded his early path: his first book, The Egyptian Heritage (1974), published by A.R.E. Press, was a commentary on the Cayce Egypt readings, and he worked on the Cayce-funded geophysical survey of the Sphinx in 1977–78 alongside the young inspector Zahi Hawass. Then the plateau itself educated him. Lehner stayed, trained, took a Yale doctorate with a dissertation on the Sphinx (1991), directed the American Research Center in Egypt's Sphinx mapping project, and became — by the ordinary, gruelling route of excavation and publication — one of the leading field Egyptologists of his generation: mapper of the Giza plateau, excavator of the pyramid builders' town at Heit el-Ghurab, author of The Complete Pyramids (1997). His mature conclusion, stated openly and often, is that his initial notions could not stand up to the bedrock reality of Giza: the monuments are the work of the Fourth Dynasty Egyptians, in the third millennium BCE. The radiocarbon programme on pyramid materials in 1984 — funded, in an irony the record should savour, by friends and supporters of the Edgar Cayce Foundation — returned dates consistent with the Old Kingdom span, roughly 2589–2504 BCE for the Giza necropolis. No figure in this story better embodies the method this hub commends: he believed the claim, he went to the place, he learned the discipline the place required, and he reported what the place actually said — against his own hope. The A.R.E., for its part, has never disowned him, and he has never mocked where he began. Both halves of that sentence are to both parties' credit.

XV. The Afterlife, II — Schoch, West, and a Separate Geological Argument

In the 1990s the Hall of Records became entangled with a second claim, and this hub's discipline requires the two to be kept apart, because they are different kinds of thing. The second claim is the Sphinx water erosion hypothesis. Its lineage runs not from Cayce but from the French writer R. A. Schwaller de Lubicz, who in the 1950s speculated that the Sphinx's body showed water erosion and must therefore predate the floods that caused it; the idea was taken up by the independent writer John Anthony West, who in 1979 attributed the erosion to Nile flooding between 15,000 and 10,000 BCE, and who in 1990 brought the Boston University geologist Robert M. Schoch to Giza to give the claim a professional examination. Schoch concluded that the deep, rolling weathering profiles on the Sphinx and its enclosure walls were the signature of prolonged rainfall — precipitation weathering — which, given Egypt's climatic history, would require the monument's core to have been carved far earlier than the Old Kingdom: he first proposed a date range of roughly 7000–5000 BCE, and has in later statements pushed his minimum estimate back toward 9700 BCE.

Three separations must now be made, cleanly. First, Schoch's argument is a geological argument — about weathering profiles, seismic refraction, and climate history — made by a credentialed geologist who deliberately avoided the word "Atlantis" in formulating it. It is not a Cayce claim, it does not cite the readings, and it stands or falls on rock, not on trance. Second, it has nevertheless been answered on rock: mainstream geologists and Egyptologists — K. Lal Gauri, James Harrell, and others — attribute the enclosure's weathering to mechanisms that operate within the conventional chronology, including salt crystallisation in damp limestone, wetting from Nile flooding and from sand saturated after rain, and the differential erosion of the plateau's alternating hard and soft strata; and archaeologists point to the Sphinx's integration into Khafre's complex — its temple built of blocks quarried from the Sphinx's own enclosure, its alignment with Khafre's causeway — as context no redating has explained away. The hypothesis remains a fringe position, rejected by the great majority of Egyptologists and unaccepted by most geologists who have examined it. Third, the entanglement: the A.R.E. sent a member, Joseph Schor, as an official observer on Schoch's 1991 survey, hoping the geology would vindicate Cayce's date; the survey's geophysicist, Thomas Dobecki, reported seismic indications of a possibly man-made chamber beneath the Sphinx's right paw; and the 1993 NBC documentary The Mystery of the Sphinx fused the package — erosion, chamber, and Cayce's Hall — for an audience of millions. West, Bauval, and Hancock then adopted Cayce's 10,500 BCE itself as the Sphinx's date — a date Schoch's geology never gave them, drawn from the readings, not the rock. When a Cayce date is quoted as if it were a geological conclusion, the two columns of Section I have collapsed into one, and the reader should restore them.

// KEEPING THE ARGUMENTS APART //

Cayce: the Sphinx was built c. 10,500 BCE by Ra-Ta's people, and a Hall of Records lies by its right paw — a claim of trance, 1930s. Schoch: the Sphinx's weathering implies carving before 5000 BCE, perhaps much earlier — a claim of geology, 1991, disputed by other geologists. The two are often quoted as one claim. They are not one claim, they do not share evidence, and neither, on present evidence, has carried its field.

XVI. The Afterlife, III — Bauval, Hancock, and the Convergence on 10,500 BCE

The third strand of the 1990s synthesis was astronomical. Robert Bauval proposed, first in Discussions in Egyptology in 1989 and then in The Orion Mystery (1994, with Adrian Gilbert), the Orion correlation theory: that the three Giza pyramids were laid out as a terrestrial map of the three stars of Orion's Belt. In Keeper of Genesis (1996; published in the United States as The Message of the Sphinx), Bauval and Graham Hancock joined the correlation to the Sphinx: the lion-bodied monument, as they argued, faces its own celestial counterpart, Leo, at the spring equinox dawn — and the whole plateau, read as a sky-map, matches the heavens not of the Old Kingdom but of c. 10,500 BCE. Hancock's Fingerprints of the Gods (1995) carried the synthesis worldwide: Schoch's erosion for the Sphinx's age, Bauval's stars for its date, and Cayce — named openly in this literature as a source of the framework — for the civilisation that built it and the records it left behind. The full treatment of Hancock's wider argument belongs to the companion article Mu and Atlantis Reborn (§XIII); what belongs here is the observation that 10,500 BCE is Cayce's number. It entered the debate from the readings of the 1930s, and the geology and the astronomy were recruited to it afterwards — Schoch's original estimate stopped millennia short of it, and the Orion correlation's own geometry, as astronomers Ed Krupp and Anthony Fairall showed, does not yield it without rotating the sky-map against the ground plan and accepting angular mismatches of more than ten degrees.

The search, meanwhile, continued on the ground. From 1996 the Schor Foundation and Florida State University surveyed the plateau's cavities; in 1998 Egypt's Supreme Council of Antiquities permitted a test drilling into one detected anomaly near the Great Pyramid, which proved to be a natural cavity, and declined permission for further drilling. Attention then fastened on the "water shaft" — a genuine subterranean tomb west of the Sphinx, of the Late Period (c. 664–332 BCE), recorded by Selim Hassan in the 1930s but long flooded — which Zahi Hawass had pumped out and fully explored in 1999, partly in response to the public interest the Hall of Records claims had generated. It was a real tomb, of a real and much later period, and it contained no records. As the 1990s closed — the decade in which Cayce's adherents had expected, from the readings' pattern of change between 1958 and 1998, that the hall might be opened — the enthusiasm of the search visibly waned. The claim was not refuted by an argument. It was refuted, season by season, by instruments, drills, and an emptied shaft: the most honourable refutation a physical claim can receive, because it is the one the claim itself requested.

XVII. The Record at Giza — The Chronology as Attested

Against the readings' c. 10,500 BCE stands the chronology Egyptology actually holds, and it is not held on authority. It is held on the convergence of independent evidence classes, any one of which would be difficult to explain on the Cayce date and which together leave no room for it. The Giza complex was built in the Fourth Dynasty of the Old Kingdom, between roughly 2600 and 2500 BCE: the Great Pyramid under Khufu (completed c. 2560 BCE), the second pyramid and — on the Egyptological consensus — the Great Sphinx under Khafre (c. 2530 BCE), and the third pyramid under Menkaure (c. 2510 BCE). Quarry marks and work-gang graffiti name Khufu's crews inside the Great Pyramid itself. The journal of the inspector Merer, a contemporary papyrus logbook recovered at Wadi el-Jarf, records the transport of casing stone to "Akhet-Khufu" — the Great Pyramid by its own ancient name — in Khufu's reign. The builders' town at Heit el-Ghurab, excavated since 1988, preserves the bakeries, breweries, and barracks of the workforce, with pottery and sealings of the Fourth Dynasty throughout. Radiocarbon determinations on organic material from the monuments, including the Cayce Foundation-funded programme of 1984, cluster where the historical chronology predicts. And beneath all of it lies the negative fact: at 10,500 BCE, Egypt was inhabited by Epipalaeolithic hunter-gatherers, whose entire material record is one of microliths, hearths, and seasonal camps. Of a pyramid-building civilisation of that date — its quarries, its towns, its pottery, its dead — there is no trace, at Giza or anywhere else in the Nile Valley. For the plateau's own archive treatment, see The Old Kingdom in the Egyptology division, and the hub's Timeline.

Stated so, the contrast can look like a rout, and the archive should resist the tone of one. The Cayce claims about Giza were falsifiable — dated, located, physical — and they were falsified, in part by expeditions their own believers funded. Very little of what this hub files under Tier C submits to so clean a test, and there is something to respect in a tradition that put its central claim where a drill could reach it. The drill's answer is the record's answer. It is not the tradition's fault that the answer was no; it would be the tradition's fault, and its historians', to go on reporting the question as open.

XVIII. Method — How a Researcher Should Handle a Trance Corpus

The Cayce readings are, for the historian of modern religion, a gift: a claimed revelation with a stenographer. They are also a standing temptation to two opposite errors — credulity, which reads the transcript's solidity as the assertions' truth, and contempt, which reads the assertions' failure as the corpus's worthlessness. Both errors waste the material. The method this hub applies to Cayce generalises to every trance, channelled, and revealed corpus in the Tier C archive, and it can be stated as a short discipline.

1. DATE AND CITE IT AS A DOCUMENT

Use the archive's own apparatus: reading number, date, subject, conductor, stenographer. A claim that can be cited to 378-16, 29 October 1933, can be checked; a claim attributed to "Cayce" in general cannot.

2. SEPARATE THE COLUMNS

The document's existence, date, and internal consistency are facts about the twentieth century. The events it narrates are claims about the eleventh millennium BCE. Never let the first column's strength flow silently into the second.

3. READ IT IN ITS PRINT CULTURE

Establish what was available to be absorbed — Donnelly, Theosophy, the Surid legend, Randall-Stevens — before treating any element as independent testimony. Convergence with a printed source is evidence about transmission, not about Atlantis.

4. DISTINGUISH COMPOSITE FROM CORPUS

The seamless Atlantis of the popular books was assembled from 700 scattered statements across twenty years. Cite the assembly as an assembly, and the individual readings as readings; the smoothing is editorial, and sometimes it is where the meaning was made.

5. TEST WHAT IS TESTABLE, AND REPORT THE TEST

Where a corpus makes a dated, located, physical claim — a rising island in 1968, a chamber by a right paw — the physical check outranks all interpretation. Report the check's result with the same prominence as the claim.

6. LEAVE THE INNER EXPERIENCE ALONE

The record cannot adjudicate what Cayce experienced, and scholarship gains nothing by pretending otherwise. Fraud, trance, dissociation, and inspiration are diagnoses the archive is not equipped to issue. It issues dates, texts, and test results.

There remains the question of what the corpus is for, once its ancient history is set aside — and here the archive can end with the respect it promised at the outset. For its members, the readings are not primarily a prehistory. They are a pastoral literature: thousands of consultations in which frightened, ill, and searching people were told that their lives had meaning, that their suffering had a pattern, and that they were responsible, morally, for what they did with both. The Atlantis and Egypt material served that purpose in its own time — Atlantis as the warning, Egypt as the pattern of a life rebuilt on the Law of One — and it serves students of American religion now as one of the best-documented windows we possess into how a modern revelation is made, kept, and loved. Edgar Cayce lay down on a couch in Virginia Beach and described, in trance, a priest of 10,500 BCE directing the building of the Sphinx. The priest is not in the Egyptian record. The couch, the stenographer, the transcript, and the century of hope and drilling that followed are all in this one — and that record, kept apart from the telling as this hub keeps everything, is remarkable enough to need no embellishment.

The readings are a perfect archive of an unverified world. The archive is real. The world is the readings'. A researcher honours both by never confusing them. THE METHOD IN ONE SENTENCE — FOR THIS CORPUS AND EVERY ONE LIKE IT
