This is a Tier A article: everything in it rests on excavated, dated, and published evidence — archaeological sites, radiocarbon and luminescence chronologies, and ancient and modern DNA — and where scholars disagree, as they do, sharply, over the footprints at White Sands, the disagreement itself is reported with its positions attributed and its evidence weighed. No claim here depends on ancient legend or modern construction, and none is allowed to borrow strength from either.

That discipline needs stating plainly in this case, because the peopling of the Americas is also the subject of this hub's Tier C counterpart, Mu and Atlantis Reborn, where modern traditions have populated the Ice Age Americas with sunken motherlands and lost civilisations. This article is the attested counterpart to those claims: the same continents, the same deep past, reconstructed only from what has been dug, dated, and sequenced. The two accounts are kept apart by design, and the reader should keep them apart too.

A note on dates and terms. Ages are given as cal BP — calibrated years before present, "present" being 1950 by radiocarbon convention — unless a source is quoted in its own terms. The Last Glacial Maximum (LGM), the coldest phase of the last Ice Age, when the great ice sheets stood at their greatest extent, is placed by the White Sands literature at roughly 26,500 to 19,000 years ago. "Clovis" names an archaeological complex of fluted stone points and associated tools, not a tribe; "Beringia" names the now partly drowned landmass that joined Siberia to Alaska. Many Native nations hold their own accounts of their origins, placing their ancestors in their homelands from the beginning; this article addresses only the archaeological and genetic question of when and how the hemisphere's first populations arrived, and it does not adjudicate traditions that answer a different question.

I. The Question — The Last Continents

By about 45,000 years ago, modern humans had reached nearly every habitable corner of the Old World and had crossed open water to Australia. One double continent remained empty. North and South America — a quarter of the Earth's land surface, running from the Arctic to within a thousand kilometres of Antarctica — held no human beings at all until, at some point in the late Pleistocene, people crossed from northeastern Asia and spread through both continents with a speed that, on any chronology, counts as rapid. When that crossing happened, how many people made it, and which of two possible roads they took south past the ice sheets, are the three questions this article follows. They sound simple. They have occupied archaeology for a century, overturned one consensus already, and are being fought over again today at a dry lakebed in New Mexico.

The shape of the problem is set by geography and ice. Between Asia and the American interior stood two barriers in series. The first was water: the Bering Strait, which disappears whenever global sea level falls far enough, as it did for long stretches of the Ice Age. The second was ice itself: at the glacial maximum, the Laurentide Ice Sheet over Canada and the Cordilleran Ice Sheet over the western mountains merged into a near-continuous wall from the Atlantic to the Pacific, sealing Beringia and Alaska off from the rest of the continent. A population could therefore be in the Americas — on Alaskan soil, say — for thousands of years without being able to go anywhere. Much of the modern debate is really about that seal: when it formed, when it broke, and whether people found a way around its western edge, along the Pacific coast, before it broke at all.

The evidence comes in three kinds, and the history of the question is the history of their changing balance. The oldest kind is the artefact in its layer: a stone point among mammoth bones, a hearth, a butchered bone — dated by the radiocarbon in associated charcoal or bone, and believed exactly as far as its context is secure. The second kind is the trace without an artefact: a footprint, a coprolite, cut marks on bone — human presence attested by what a body did rather than what it made. The third kind, and the one that has transformed the field since about 2010, is the genome: DNA from living Indigenous peoples and from ancient skeletons, which records splits, mixtures, and isolations in a population's past whether or not that population left a single datable object. The strongest conclusions in this article are those where all three kinds agree. The sharpest fights — White Sands above all — occur where one kind of evidence runs far ahead of the others.

One more framing point, because it disciplines everything that follows. An archaeological "first" is a floor, not a date of arrival: the oldest found site can only show that people were present by that time, never that they were absent before it. Every generation of this debate has mistaken its current floor for the ceiling of the possible — Clovis-first did exactly that for fifty years — and the honest way to read the record assembled below is as a sequence of floors, each one excavated, dated, and argued over, with the true arrival somewhere at or before the oldest of them.

II. Clovis First — The Consensus and How It Was Built

The Clovis-first model was not a prejudice that happened to capture a field; it was built, find by find, on evidence that was genuinely strong — and understanding why reasonable scholars defended it for so long is necessary to understanding what it took to move them. Its foundation was laid in 1927 near Folsom, New Mexico, where excavators from the Colorado Museum of Natural History, working under Jesse Figgins, found a slender stone point lodged among the ribs of an extinct bison, Bison antiquus — and, crucially, left it in place until a visiting panel of senior scholars could see it there, undisturbed, with their own eyes. Folsom settled a question that had been bitterly contested for decades: human beings had indeed lived in North America alongside Ice Age animals. It also set the field's evidentiary standard — association demonstrated in situ, before witnesses — that every later claimant, Monte Verde and White Sands included, would be held to.

The second foundation stone fell a few years later at Blackwater Draw, near Clovis, New Mexico, where excavations in the early 1930s under Edgar B. Howard of the University of Pennsylvania found larger fluted points with mammoth remains in layers below the Folsom material. The Clovis point — lanceolate, bifacially flaked, with a channel-like flute struck from its base — became the signature of the earliest recognised culture in the hemisphere. Over the following decades, Clovis points and Clovis kill and camp sites turned up across North America, from coast to coast, and radiocarbon dating eventually compressed them into a strikingly brief window. No comparably convincing older site appeared. The conclusion drew itself: the makers of Clovis points were the first Americans.

The Model, Whole

In its mature form, Clovis-first was a complete and elegant story. The ancestors of Native Americans crossed Beringia from Siberia late in the Ice Age. They waited north of the ice, or passed as soon as they could, and then — sometime around 13,500 to 13,000 years ago — moved south through the ice-free corridor, the strip of deglaciated land opening between the Laurentide and Cordilleran ice sheets along the eastern flank of the Rockies. Emerging onto the northern Plains as specialised hunters of mammoth and other great game, they spread with extraordinary speed — Clovis points appear from Alberta to Mexico within a few centuries — reached South America, and, in the model's boldest extension (associated with the archaeologist Paul Martin's "overkill" hypothesis), hunted the continent's megafauna into the extinction that coincides with their arrival. One migration, one corridor, one technology, one wave: the model explained the apparent simultaneity of Clovis, the absence of anything older, and the extinctions, in a single gesture.

It also acquired, as long-lived consensuses do, a gatekeeping function. Claims of older occupation were announced regularly through the twentieth century — from the "American palaeoliths" of the 1870s onward — and nearly all collapsed on inspection: artefacts that proved to be naturally fractured stone ("geofacts"), bones broken by carnivores or trampling rather than butchers, charcoal from natural fires, or genuinely old layers contaminated by younger material worked down into them. Each collapse raised the bar for the next claim, and the bar was enforced, sometimes abrasively, by the field's senior figures. The result was a paradox that the next section resolves: the standard of proof that protected the field from a hundred false dawns also delayed its recognition of the one site that could meet it. That site was not in North America at all. It was in Chile, some 13,000 kilometres from the Bering Strait — which was precisely the problem.

// WHY DISTANCE WAS THE OBJECTION //

A site in southern Chile older than Clovis does not merely add a few centuries to a timeline. It requires people to have crossed Beringia, traversed the whole of North America, passed the ice sheets by some route, crossed the equator and the Andes' flank, and settled at the far end of South America — all before the date at which the model said the journey began. Monte Verde's age and its address were a single claim, and both halves had to be swallowed together.

A note on what Clovis-first got right, because the correction of an error should not erase the evidence that made it plausible. Clovis is remarkably widespread and remarkably brief — the best modern redating (Waters, Stafford & Carlson, 2020) confines it to about 13,050–12,750 cal BP, a span of some three centuries — and a technology that appears across a continent within three hundred years is a real phenomenon demanding explanation, whether its makers were firstcomers or, as §XIII argues, a later horizon spreading through a hemisphere already peopled. The model's error was not invention. It was inference: from "nothing older has been proved" to "nothing older exists." The proof, when it came, came from a peat bog.

III. Monte Verde — The Site That Broke the Barrier

Monte Verde lies on the banks of Chinchihuapi Creek in the Los Lagos region of southern Chile, some 50 km inland from the modern Pacific coast near Puerto Montt — and farther from the coast still in the late Pleistocene, when lower sea level pushed the shoreline some 90 km west. The site was found in the mid-1970s, when erosion and logging exposed bones and worked wood along the creek, and from 1977 it was excavated by Tom D. Dillehay, then of the Universidad Austral de Chile, with an interdisciplinary Chilean and international team. What the creek had preserved, under a cap of peat that sealed it in waterlogged, oxygen-free sediment, was something North American archaeology had never produced for so early a date: a settlement whose perishable half had survived.

Monte Verde II — The Occupation

The principal layer, designated Monte Verde II (MV-II), held the remains of a small community of perhaps twenty to thirty people. Its centrepiece was a tent-like residential structure some 20 metres long: a foundation of logs and planks, divided into about a dozen small rooms or cubicles, each with its own clay-lined hearth or brazier, its food remains, its tools. A short distance away stood a second, wishbone-shaped structure associated with mastodon bone and a concentration of medicinal plants — read by the excavators as a specialised workshop and healing place. Around and within the structures lay wooden lances and digging sticks, mortars, knotted cords and rope of twisted plant fibre, animal hides, stone tools, hearths, food debris of dozens of plant species from forest, coast, and distant inland zones — and a small human footprint preserved in the creek-side sediment. Radiocarbon dates on the layer's wood, charcoal, and bone clustered at about 12,500 radiocarbon years BP, which calibrates to roughly 14,500 cal BP — a full millennium and more before Clovis, at the wrong end of the hemisphere.

Two features of the assemblage carried weight out of proportion to their size. The first was seaweed: remains of nine species of marine algae, recovered from hearths and living floors and directly dated in a later study (Dillehay et al., Science, 2008) to between about 14,220 and 13,980 cal BP. The seaweed had been carried to the site from beaches and estuaries tens of kilometres away, and chewed cuds of it, mixed with other plants, were found in the wishbone structure. Seaweed is not a curiosity in this argument: it is short-lived, unmistakably marine, and directly datable, and it ties an inland settlement to the Pacific coast — the first material hint, from the far south, of the coastal orientation that §X would place at the centre of the peopling story. The second feature was the sheer ordinariness of the whole. Monte Verde II is not a kill site or a quarry stop; it is a settled camp of foragers with a broad diet — mastodon and other game, yes, but also wild potatoes, seeds, nuts, berries, and shoreline foods — practising plant medicine and living in built housing. Whatever the first South Americans were, by 14,500 years ago at Monte Verde they were not a blitzkrieg of mammoth hunters racing south. They were residents.

Twenty Years of Resistance

Dillehay reported the site's antiquity from the late 1970s, and the field's response, for roughly twenty years, was refusal. The objections were not frivolous, and they are worth listing at their real strength, because the Monte Verde fight defined the standard that every pre-Clovis claim — White Sands included — is still judged by. The stone tools were said to be too simple, some of them possibly natural. The association between artefacts and dated material was questioned: could younger objects have sunk, or older charcoal have been redeposited, in a creek-side bog? The radiocarbon dates themselves were audited for contamination. And beneath the technical objections sat the structural one from §II: a settlement older than Clovis at the far end of South America implied an entry into the hemisphere so much earlier, by a route so different, that the conservative reading of any single ambiguous object was always "not proven." Dillehay's team answered with volume — the site's final publication ran to two large monograph volumes (1989 and 1997) documenting stratigraphy, dating, and every class of find in exhaustive detail — and with patience.

The end came in January 1997, in the manner Folsom had pioneered seventy years earlier: by inspection, before witnesses. A delegation of twelve senior specialists — including prominent defenders of Clovis-first — travelled first to examine the artefacts and records, then to the site itself, much of which had by then been damaged by bulldozing, to inspect what remained of the stratigraphy. Their published conclusion, carried in American Antiquity later that year (Meltzer et al., 1997), was unanimous on the central question: MV-II is genuinely archaeological, and there is no reasonable ground to doubt its age of about 12,500 radiocarbon years BP. Later challenges — most extensively a 1999 critique by Stuart Fiedel, answered point by point by Dillehay — did not dislodge the consensus the visit had created. Monte Verde II is today the benchmark pre-Clovis site: the proof, accepted across the field, that people were living in South America at least a millennium before Clovis.

// MONTE VERDE I — THE LAYER THAT DID NOT CARRY //

Beneath and beyond MV-II, Dillehay reported a sparser, older component, Monte Verde I, for which dates around 18,500 cal BP have been proposed, and still older burned material from the vicinity has occasionally been cited. These claims have never won the acceptance MV-II won: the assemblage is thin, the association of the dated material with human activity is disputed, and Dillehay himself has treated the early material cautiously. This article therefore uses Monte Verde at its accepted strength — c. 14,500 cal BP — and lists MV-I among the open questions of §XIV. The distinction matters: it is the same discipline, applied by the same excavator, that makes MV-II's acceptance meaningful.

What Monte Verde changed, once accepted, was not only a date but a method. After 1997, the question was no longer whether anyone preceded Clovis, but by how much, and by which road — and claims that would once have been dismissed unread began to receive the full audit instead: excavation, redating, replication. Paisley Caves, Page-Ladson, and Cooper's Ferry (§XI) all entered the literature through the door Monte Verde opened, and the genetic standstill model of §V was developed in a field that now needed an earlier entry to explain. The Clovis barrier had functioned, in effect, as a dam on interpretation. Monte Verde broke it — not with a point in a ribcage, but with huts, hearths, seaweed, and twenty years of documentation.

IV. Beringia — A Country, Not a Bridge

Every version of the peopling story begins in the same place, and the place is persistently misdrawn. "Land bridge" suggests a narrow causeway, crossed in a journey. Beringia was a subcontinent: the broad plain — up to about 1,000 km from north to south at its greatest extent — exposed when Ice Age sea levels fell more than 100 metres below today's, joining Chukotka in Siberia to Alaska and the Yukon in a single landmass that also includes the now-drowned shelves of the Bering and Chukchi seas. The term covers, by convention, the whole unglaciated region from the Lena River in Siberia to the Mackenzie River in Canada. For most of the last 60,000 years some version of this country existed, dry, cold, and largely ice-free, while the continents to its south lay under ice sheets kilometres thick. Its story — its climate, its vegetation, its long human occupation, and its drowning — is told at full length in this hub's Drowned Landscapes; what matters here is the part Beringia plays in the peopling argument.

The first point is that Beringia was inhabitable — not a wasteland to be hurried across, but a homeland that could hold a population for millennia. The older picture of the land bridge as barren steppe-tundra, too poor in fuel and game to support people through the glacial maximum, has been revised by palaeoecology: sediment cores from the Bering Sea floor and from lakes on the now-emerged margins preserve pollen, plant macrofossils, and insect remains showing that central Beringia carried mesic shrub tundra — birch, willow, and alder scrub, with spruce in favoured refuges — through the LGM. Shrubs mean firewood; firewood, in a treeless world, means the difference between a place people can winter and a place they cannot. Hoffecker, Elias, and O'Rourke, in the synthesis that anchors this section (Science, 2014), drew the conclusion explicitly: a shrub-tundra refugium in central Beringia is precisely the kind of place where the genetic isolation described in §V could have happened — a population settled, not stranded, for the duration of the cold.

The second point is archaeological, and it is an absence with a reason. If people lived in central Beringia for ten thousand years, where are their camps? Mostly, under water. The heartland of the refugium — the low plain between modern Siberia and Alaska — was flooded as the ice sheets melted and sea level rose after about 13,000 years ago, and now lies beneath the Bering Sea. What survives on dry land is Beringia's eastern and western edges: in Siberia, the Yana Rhinoceros Horn Site and its successors show people hunting in the Arctic by about 32,000 years ago, well before the LGM; in Alaska and the Yukon, the oldest firmly dated occupations — Swan Point, and later Upward Sun River (§XII) — cluster around and after 14,000 years ago. Between those edges lies a gap in the site record exactly where the standstill model puts its population, and exactly where the sea now is. Absence of evidence, in a drowned landscape, is the weakest form of evidence of absence — a principle this hub applies again, in §X, to the drowned Pacific coast.

THE EXIT PROBLEM

To peopling the continents, it was not enough to reach Beringia. The way south — corridor or coast — was blocked by ice and sea at the LGM. The routes are the subject of §X.

Beringia also frames the question of who the first Americans were, genetically. The genomes of §V and §XII show a founding population formed from two sources: an East Asian lineage and the so-called Ancient North Eurasians, a Siberian population known from the 24,000-year-old Mal'ta child in south-central Siberia, whose ancestry contributes roughly a third of the Native American genomic inheritance. The mixture happened somewhere in northeastern Asia or Beringia itself, in the millennia before the glacial maximum. Beringia, in other words, is not only where the first Americans waited. It is, in part, where they became a distinct people — the place their genomes diverged from every Asian relative and began the separate history that the next section reads out of the DNA.

V. The Standstill — Ten Thousand Years in the Refugium

The genetic evidence for a long Beringian pause arrived before the palaeoecology that made it plausible. Through the 1990s, studies of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) — the small genome inherited only from the mother — had established that Native Americans carry a handful of founding maternal lineages (the haplogroups A2, B2, C1, D1, D4h3, and X2a) derived from Asian ancestors. In 1997 two Latin American geneticists, Sandro Bonatto and Francisco Salzano, proposed that these lineages had diversified in Beringia before dispersing. A decade later the proposal was formalised by Erika Tamm and colleagues (PLoS ONE, 2007), working with 623 complete mitochondrial genomes from the Americas and Asia. Their finding was a pattern of private mutations: changes present in the American founding lineages but in no Asian population — the signature of a population that had been genetically isolated, accumulating its own mutations, for thousands of years between leaving Asia and spreading through the Americas. Applying a molecular clock, they estimated that the American lineages had diverged from their Asian sisters more than 25,000 years ago, but had dispersed through the New World less than 15,000 years ago. Between those two bounds lay the standstill: on the order of ten thousand years, spent — the geography left only one candidate — in Beringia.

Whole-genome studies have since filled in the pause with people and dates. The founding population's ancestors split from East Asians around 36,000 years ago (the modelling of §XII gives 36 ± 1.5 ka), with gene flow between the diverging populations continuing until about 25,000 years ago. Between about 25,000 and 20,000 years ago the founding population received its Ancient North Eurasian component. Thereafter it was isolated — through the whole of the LGM — until its descendants began moving south of the ice sheets, on the genetic modelling, sometime between about 17,500 and 14,600 years ago, when the northern and southern branches of Native American ancestry diverged, probably already south of the ice (Waters, Science, 2019, summarising the genomic literature). The effective size of the isolated population was small — mtDNA modelling has suggested on the order of a few thousand breeding women, implying a total community in the low tens of thousands at most — and palaeoecological carrying- capacity estimates for shrub-tundra Beringia land in the same range. A small people, then, in a cold refugium, for longer than the whole of recorded history several times over.

// WHAT A "STANDSTILL" IS — AND IS NOT //

What the model claims: that between divergence from Asian ancestors (complete by c. 25,000 BP) and dispersal south of the ice sheets (c. 17,500–14,600 BP on the genomic modelling), the ancestral Native American population was genetically isolated, most plausibly in Beringia, and accumulated the private mutations its descendants still carry. What it does not claim: that the population was static, unchanging, or camped in one place — "standstill" describes gene flow, not culture or movement within Beringia. Its standing: the isolation itself is supported by every major genomic study since 2007; its duration and its precise location (central Beringia versus a Siberian refuge) are modelled quantities, and different models return different spans. This article uses the conventional c. 25,000–15,000 BP framing of the task the evidence is usually summarised by, and flags the genomic southward window separately where it matters (§X).

The standstill matters to this article for two reasons beyond its own elegance. The first is that it independently requires an early presence: a population cannot have been isolated in Beringia from 25,000 years ago unless its ancestors had already crossed northeastern Asia and reached the refugium before the LGM sealed the region — which makes the genetics, from 2007 onward, a standing argument that the hemisphere's peopling began well before Clovis, whatever the site record then showed. The second is that it sets the trap into which the White Sands dates would fall, and from which they would have to escape. If people were in New Mexico by 23,000–21,000 years ago — south of the ice, during the LGM — then either the standstill population leaked south far earlier than the genomic dispersal window allows, or a separate, earlier movement reached the interior Southwest and left the footprints, while the ancestors of all sampled Native American genomes came later. Neither option is impossible; ancient DNA has repeatedly revealed populations that left no descendants. But the tension is real, and §VIII returns to it, because it is the strongest external argument in the White Sands debate — stronger, in the end, than any argument about seeds.

First, though, the footprints themselves.

VI. White Sands — Footprints at the Glacial Maximum

White Sands National Park, in the Tularosa Basin of south-central New Mexico, is a gypsum dune field of unearthly whiteness — and, beneath and beside the dunes, the floor of a vanished Ice Age lake. Palaeolake Otero filled much of the basin during the late Pleistocene; as it shrank and dried toward the end of the Ice Age it left the broad playa called Alkali Flat, a mosaic of mudflats, shallow pools, and stream margins. Around that lake walked the Pleistocene megafauna — mammoths, giant ground sloths, camels, dire wolves, sabre-toothed cats — and their tracks, pressed into wet gypsum-rich mud and then buried, are preserved there in the tens of thousands. Park resource manager David Bustos first recognised the significance of the human tracks among them in 2009, and the years since have made White Sands the richest tracksite of its kind in the Americas: whole trackways recording behaviour — a human carrying a child, setting the child down, picking it up again; a giant sloth rearing and wheeling, apparently at a human approach — at a resolution no artefact scatter can match. Many of the surface tracks are "ghost tracks," visible only in particular conditions of moisture and light, and the survey methods developed for them are part of §IX.

The dated evidence comes from one intensively studied section: WHSA Locality 2, on the eastern margin of Alkali Flat, where an erosional scarp exposes the lake-margin sediments. There, in 2019–2020, a team led by geologist and ichnologist Matthew R. Bennett of Bournemouth University, with the U.S. Geological Survey's Kathleen Springer and Jeffrey Pigati, the National Park Service, Cornell University, and the University of Arizona, excavated a trench through the footprint-bearing sequence. The results were published in Science in September 2021 (Bennett et al., 2021).

The Claim

The excavated sequence is a stack of fine-grained lake-margin deposits, and within it the team documented 61 human footprints distributed across multiple distinct stratigraphic horizons — surfaces on which people had walked, separated by layers of sediment, and therefore by time. The prints are unmistakably human: bare feet, of modern anatomy, in trackways that include adults and, predominantly, adolescents and small children, judging by foot size. Interbedded with the footprint horizons — below them, above them, and in layers between them — lay dense bands of seeds of the aquatic plant Ruppia cirrhosa (spiral ditch grass), which the team radiocarbon-dated by accelerator mass spectrometry. Eleven aliquots of seeds, in correct stratigraphic order from the base of the sequence to the top, returned calibrated ages running from 22,860 ± 320 years BP at the bottom to 21,130 ± 250 years BP at the top. The footprints, bracketed by those seed layers, were therefore made between about 23,000 and 21,000 years ago — squarely within the Last Glacial Maximum, roughly eight thousand years before the oldest then-accepted site in the hemisphere, and at a time when the ice sheets to the north stood at or near their maximum extent and the ice-free corridor did not exist.

The paper's importance was not only its date. A footprint is the one class of evidence that cannot be redeposited, curated, traded, or dropped by a burrowing animal into an older layer: a trackway exists only where and when a foot pressed the ground. If the White Sands tracks are as old as their bracketing sediments, then human beings were present in the interior Southwest of North America — south of the ice sheets, a thousand kilometres from any coast — during the LGM, and every model of the peopling built on a post-glacial entry is wrong not in detail but in kind. The authors said so, in measured terms, and the paper landed accordingly. It was immediately clear, however, that the entire claim rested on a single load-bearing element: not the footprints, whose human origin no one disputed, but the dates of the seeds. And the seeds were of an aquatic plant. Within months, the critique arrived — aimed, precisely, at the water the plant had grown in.

VII. The Dating Fight, Round One — The Seed Critique

The critique of the White Sands chronology is the methodological centrepiece of this article, and it deserves to be stated the way this hub states every contested claim: at full strength, in its authors' own terms of argument, before any reply. It came in two connected publications — a formal Comment in Science (Madsen, Davis, Rhode & Oviatt, 2022) and a full assessment in Quaternary Research (Oviatt, Madsen, Rhode & Davis, 2022; journal issue 2023) — from a team combining a Quaternary geologist of the Great Basin (Charles G. Oviatt), archaeologists David B. Madsen and Loren G. Davis, and palaeoecologist David Rhode. Davis's presence is worth noting: the lead excavator of Cooper's Ferry (§XI), no defender of Clovis-first. The critique was not a rearguard action by the old consensus. It was a dating argument, and it was a good one.

The Reservoir Effect — The Core Objection

Radiocarbon dating measures the carbon-14 a once-living thing took from its environment. For a land plant, that carbon comes from the air, and the air's carbon-14 content at any past date is known from the calibration curve — the date is sound. But Ruppia cirrhosa is not a land plant. It grows submerged, in lake and wetland water, and it builds its tissues substantially from carbon dissolved in that water. Lake water in a limestone and gypsum basin like the Tularosa is fed by groundwater that has passed through ancient carbonate bedrock — rock whose carbon is millions of years old and entirely devoid of carbon-14. A plant photosynthesising from such water incorporates "dead" carbon, and its radiocarbon age comes out too old — by the amount of dead carbon in the mix. This is the freshwater reservoir effect, also called the hard-water effect, and it is one of the best-known traps in radiocarbon dating: aquatic materials from carbonate terrains are distrusted precisely because their apparent ages can exceed their true ages by centuries or millennia. Bennett and colleagues had acknowledged the issue in their paper and argued that the effect at Locality 2 was minimal. The critics' charge was that they had underestimated the plant's basic biology: Ruppia, Rhode argued, takes most of its carbon from the water column, not the atmosphere, and in the Lake Otero basin that water carried ancient carbon from the surrounding bedrock and from groundwater discharge.

The critics then did what strong critics do: they measured. Taking herbarium specimens of Ruppia cirrhosa of known collection age — modern plants, gathered within living memory — they had them radiocarbon-dated commercially. The plants returned apparent ages of roughly 7,400 radiocarbon years: specimens only decades old dated as if they had died in the early Holocene, an offset produced entirely by old carbon taken up in life. If an offset of anything like that magnitude applied to the White Sands seed layers, the footprints would not be 23,000–21,000 years old. They would be thousands of years younger — the critics calculated that a maximal reservoir correction could bring the trackway ages down toward the range of about 15,500–13,500 cal BP, squarely within the established pre-Clovis record and compatible with the genomic dispersal window. A second, independent line of attack ran alongside: even if the seeds' ages were accurate for the seeds, seeds are small, light, and mobile — they could have been reworked, eroded from older deposits and redeposited in younger sediment, in which case they would date the layer's ingredients, not the layer, and would give only a maximum age for the footprints. And a third critic, C. Vance Haynes Jr. — the dean of Clovis geoarchaeology — argued in PaleoAmerica (2021) that the tracks could in principle be far younger than their sediments if people had walked on an exposed older surface. The 2021 claim, the critics concluded, was possible but not demonstrated: a conclusion that would reorder the peopling of a hemisphere required dating material that did not carry a known, unquantified, millennia-scale error term.

The Reply — And Why It Did Not End the Matter

The White Sands team replied in the same venues (Pigati et al., Science, 2022), and their reply also deserves its strength. The dated seeds, they pointed out, did not come from the deep lake but from shallow, stream-fed and wetland contexts at the lake's margin, where water was thin, turbulent, and in close exchange with the atmosphere — conditions under which reservoir offsets shrink dramatically; the herbarium specimens, drawn from different waters, were no measure of Locality 2's hydrology. The seeds maintained perfect stratigraphic order — eleven aliquots, oldest at the bottom, youngest at the top, spanning two thousand years — which a wholesale reworking of older seeds into younger sediment would be unlikely to produce. And the site's wider chronology, from the lake's own sediment history, was consistent with an LGM age for the sequence. The exchange clarified everything except the answer. Each side's argument turned on quantities — the actual reservoir offset in that water, on that shore, 22,000 years ago — that neither side could measure directly in the ancient deposits. The debate had reached the point where only a different kind of date could settle it: a date on material that had never touched lake water at all.

// THE STANDARD THE CRITIQUE SET //

Stripped to its logic, the Oviatt team's demand was the Folsom standard of §II restated for geochronology: an extraordinary age must be carried by evidence without a known failure mode, verified by a method independent of the one under challenge. Bennett's team accepted the demand in terms — Pigati and Springer stated publicly that independent corroboration was exactly what the claim required to convince the field. Round Two, in §VIII, is the story of that corroboration being attempted properly: new materials, new methods, a different dating laboratory.

It is worth pausing on the sociology, because this hub has watched the same pattern at Monte Verde and will watch it again. In 1997 the burden of proof sat on Dillehay, and he discharged it by documentation and inspection. In 2021–2022 the burden sat on Bennett's team, and the critics — several of them pre-Clovis researchers themselves — specified exactly what discharging it would take: dates on terrestrial material from the same horizons, and a second, non-radiocarbon clock on the sediments themselves. That specification is what makes the White Sands fight, whatever its final outcome, a model of how such claims should be tested. The next section reports what happened when the test was run.

VIII. The Dating Fight, Round Two — Pollen, Quartz, and Mud

In October 2023, Science published the corroboration study: Pigati, Springer, and colleagues, "Independent age estimates resolve the controversy of ancient human footprints at White Sands" (Science 382, 2023: 73–75). Its design answered the critique point by point. Where Bennett et al. had dated an aquatic plant by radiocarbon, the new study dated two further materials by two different physical clocks, from the same stratigraphic horizons, processed at a different accelerator mass spectrometry facility from the original work.

Pollen — The Terrestrial Clock

The first new material was pollen — specifically, pollen of terrestrial, cone-bearing plants (pines and their kin) recovered from the very seed-layer horizons that had carried the 2021 dates. A pine tree photosynthesises from the air. Its pollen therefore carries the atmosphere's carbon-14 signature and is immune by construction to the freshwater reservoir effect: whatever old carbon haunted Lake Otero's water never entered a pine on the hills above it. The practical difficulty is mass: a pollen grain is microscopic, and an AMS radiocarbon measurement needs tens of thousands of grains of a single terrestrial type, separated from the sediment and from every other organic particle. The team used flow cytometry — a cell-sorting technology borrowed from biology — to isolate on the order of 75,000 pollen grains per sample. The pollen ages came back statistically indistinguishable from the seed ages from the same horizons: the footprint-bearing interval dated, on pollen, to the same span of roughly 23,000 to 21,000 years ago (individual determinations carrying the larger uncertainties honest pollen work entails, of the order of ±2,300–2,500 years). If the Ruppia seeds had been inflated by a reservoir effect of thousands of years, the pollen — which cannot share that error — should have come back thousands of years younger. It did not.

Quartz — The Luminescence Clock

The second new clock shared nothing with radiocarbon at all. Optically stimulated luminescence (OSL) dating measures the radiation dose accumulated by quartz grains since they were last exposed to sunlight: buried sediment is a slow battery, charged by background radiation, and the laboratory reading of its charge gives the time since burial — since the grains were last on a sunlit surface. OSL on quartz from within the footprint-bearing sequence returned a minimum age of about 21,500 ± 1,900 years for the interval: the sediments containing the tracks were last at the surface, and were buried, more than twenty thousand years ago. OSL is subject to its own failure modes — incomplete bleaching of grains at deposition chief among them — but they are different failure modes from radiocarbon's, and that independence is the point. Two clocks, built on different physics, fed by different materials, read by different laboratories, now agreed: the White Sands footprint horizons are of Last Glacial Maximum age. In the paper's own summary sentence:

The results show that the chronologic framework originally established for the White Sands footprints is robust and reaffirm that humans were present in North America during the Last Glacial Maximum.
— Pigati et al., Science 382 (2023): 73–75

The Third Material — Mud (2025)

The corroboration did not stop in 2023. A third study, led by geoarchaeologist Vance T. Holliday of the University of Arizona — himself a co-author of the 2021 paper, returning to test it — took a different stratigraphic route to the same horizons (Holliday et al., Science Advances, 2025). Holliday's team traced a previously unrecognised palaeolake Otero stratigraphy laterally into the track-bearing alluvium and radiocarbon-dated organic-rich lakebed mud — neither seeds nor pollen — at two laboratories independent of the original investigations. Twenty-six new mud dates documented a lake, wetland, and stream sequence spanning from before 23,600 to about 17,000 cal BP, with the muds of the track interval itself returning ages of roughly 20,700 to 22,400 years. The running total across the three studies is 55 radiocarbon dates on three materials — seeds, pollen, mud — from multiple laboratories, plus the OSL ages, all in stratigraphic order and all converging on the same two millennia. Expanded excavation reported in the most recent work has also extended the track-bearing sequence itself: some twenty footprint horizons are now recognised at Locality 2, with the refined chronology placing the sequence between about 23,500 and 21,700 years ago.

The Verdict, as the Literature Now Stands

This article's assessment, stated at the strength the published record supports, is as follows. The LGM age of the White Sands footprints is now the prevailing reading of the evidence, and it has earned that position the correct way. The specific mechanism of the 2022 critique — a multi-millennial reservoir offset in Ruppia — has been directly tested by materials that cannot suffer from it, and has failed: pollen and mud agree with the seeds, and an independent luminescence clock agrees with all three. To keep the footprints young, a critic must now posit not one error but a conspiracy of errors, of similar size and direction, across unrelated materials and methods — or retreat to the reworking hypothesis, under which seeds, pollen, and mud were all redeposited together in stratigraphic order, a scenario the 2025 lateral stratigraphy makes harder, not easier, to hold. The accompanying Perspective in Science (Philippsen, 2023) judged that even allowing for substantial errors, the combined data strongly indicate a human presence around the LGM, and subsequent syntheses have increasingly treated the White Sands age as established.

Three qualifications keep the verdict honest, and this article enters all of them. First, dissent continues in print: post-2023 papers have questioned the pollen pretreatment and the possibility that the dated grains include reworked older pollen, and the footprint team's detailed responses (including a full geochronological restatement in PaleoAmerica, 2024) are part of an ongoing exchange, not a closed file. Second, the tracks remain, so far, tracks alone: no artefacts, no hearth, no camp of LGM age has been found at White Sands, and Holliday himself has acknowledged in print and interview that the absence is a fair and unanswered question — though a lakeshore trackway is precisely the kind of place people cross without settling, and the surrounding playa has been only fractionally excavated. Third, the tension with the genomic dispersal window of §V is unresolved: people in New Mexico at 23,000–21,000 BP must either descend from an early movement out of the standstill population that the current genomic models do not show, or belong to a population that left few or no descendants in the sampled record. Ancient DNA has documented such vanished populations elsewhere in the hemisphere (§XII); the White Sands trackmakers may be their earliest known trace. None of the three qualifications restores the young age for the footprints. They mark where the argument has moved: from whether people were there at the LGM, to who they were and how they came. That is the same migration of the argument that followed Monte Verde — and it is what acceptance looks like while it is still completing itself.

IX. Reading a Footprint Horizon — Excavation and Dating in Practice

This hub's standing rule is maximum procedural detail for every repeatable physical process, and a footprint horizon is a process twice over: once when a foot made it, and once when an excavator reads it. This section sets out how the White Sands evidence was actually produced — the sequence of operations, in the order the teams performed them — because the dating fight of §§VII–VIII can only be judged by a reader who knows what a "horizon" is and how a date comes to be attached to it.

Step One — Finding Tracks That Are Barely There

Most White Sands tracks are invisible most of the time. The gypsum sediments differ so subtly in moisture retention and salt content between a footprint's compressed fill and the sediment around it that a track may appear at dawn after a damp night and vanish by noon — the "ghost tracks" of the park's literature. Surface survey therefore begins with timing and raking light, and proceeds to geophysics: ground-penetrating radar (GPR), dragged in grids over foam mats laid to protect the surface, detects the density contrast between track fill and matrix, including the compressed "undertrack" laminae a heavy foot drives into the layers below. In the method's demonstration study (Urban et al., Scientific Reports, 2019), GPR resolved 96 per cent of the human tracks in the survey area and all of the larger vertebrate tracks — without moving a grain of sediment. Promising anomalies are then recorded by photogrammetry and total-station survey, building a three-dimensional model of each track and trackway — length, width, depth, stride, pace — from which stature, speed, and (in the case of the smallest prints) the age class of the trackmaker are estimated.

Step Two — Excavating a Surface, Not a Layer

Excavation at Locality 2 proceeded by trench and by horizon. A trench is cut through the sequence to expose the full stratigraphy in section — the stacked lake-margin deposits, readable like the pages of a book — and the footprint horizons are identified in that section as discrete surfaces: thin partings where a once-exposed mudflat was walked on, then buried by the next flood's sediment. The excavator then works along a horizon rather than down through it, peeling back the overlying layer in plan to expose the surface as its makers left it, cleaning each print with brushes and soft tools — never trowelling across it, since the print is the surface. Prints are photographed and modelled in 3D before any sampling, because excavation destroys the horizon it reveals: the photogrammetric record is the permanent archive, and casts are taken of selected tracks. In section, a footprint appears as a deformation: sediment laminae bent down beneath the foot, the print filled with sediment of the next depositional event — and, at White Sands, in some cases seed layers themselves pressed and broken by the foot, which is as direct an association between track and datable material as the site can offer.

Step Three — Dating What the Foot Touched

A footprint contains no carbon and keeps no clock of its own. Every date for a track is a date for its horizon, obtained by bracketing: material from the layer below the surface gives a maximum age for the walk (the surface cannot be older than what it lies on), material from the layer above gives a minimum age (the surface cannot be younger than what buried it), and material within the surface layer itself — at White Sands, the Ruppia seed bands interbedded with the prints — dates the depositional episode in which the walking occurred. In practice the Locality 2 programme sampled seed bands at multiple levels through the trench section, dated each by AMS radiocarbon, and checked the results against stratigraphic order: a sound sequence must young upward, and the eleven 2021 aliquots did, spanning about two thousand years across the footprint stack. The 2023 programme repeated the operation on a second material (pollen, extracted by chemical digestion of the sediment and flow-cytometric sorting) from the identical horizons, and added OSL samples — sediment taken in opaque tubes to exclude light, so that the quartz grains' accumulated dose could be read in the laboratory as a burial age. The 2025 programme extended the bracketing laterally, dating mud from the same depositional system in trenches away from the tracksite, so that the chronology did not rest on a single column of sediment. Each of these operations is a separate chain of custody from ground to laboratory number, and the force of the final chronology is precisely that the chains do not share links.

X. The Routes South — Corridor or Coast?

Suppose a population is in Beringia by 25,000 years ago, and suppose its descendants are in Chile by 14,500. Between those two facts lies the ice. Two roads south have been proposed, and the modern debate is not really about which is prettier; it is about timing — which road was open when the travellers needed it.

The Ice-Free Corridor — Open Late, Viable Later

The corridor of the Clovis-first model is the strip of land freed as the Laurentide and Cordilleran ice sheets pulled apart along the eastern Rockies. Deglaciation chronology — from dated moraines, lake sediments, and cosmogenic exposure ages — places the physical opening of a continuous corridor at roughly 15,000 to 14,000 years ago: before that, the sheets were merged or the gap was blocked by proglacial lakes. But a physical gap is not a road. Freshly deglaciated land is a chaos of meltwater, unstable slopes, and bare till, and the corridor's bottleneck segment — the region of the former Glacial Lake Peace, in modern British Columbia and Alberta — has been cored and read directly. Pedersen and colleagues (Nature, 2016), using radiocarbon dates, pollen, macrofossils, and metagenomic DNA from lake cores in the bottleneck, found the corridor's ecological succession running late: steppe vegetation with bison and mammoth appears only by about 12,600 cal BP, open forest later still. A companion study of ancient bison DNA (Heintzman et al., PNAS, 2016) found northern and southern bison populations meeting in the corridor only around 13,000 years ago. The two studies converge on the same verdict: the corridor became a biologically viable migration route — with plants to eat, game to hunt, and fuel to burn along 1,500 km of travel — only around 13,800 to 12,600 years ago on the most generous readings, after Monte Verde, Page-Ladson, and Paisley were already occupied, and barely in time for Clovis itself. Later peoples certainly used the corridor, in both directions — the bison prove traffic, and some of it was northward. As the first road south, it is simply too late.

The Kelp Highway — The Coastal Alternative

The alternative runs along the water. The coastal migration model — proposed in modern form by archaeologist Knut Fladmark in the 1970s and long marginalised under Clovis-first — holds that maritime people moved from Beringia down the Pacific coast by boat, island- hopping and shore-hopping past the ice sheet's western edge, into the ice-free refuges of the Alexander Archipelago and the British Columbia coast, and onward south. Its ecological engine was named by Jon Erlandson and colleagues in 2007: the kelp highway (Journal of Island and Coastal Archaeology 2, 2007: 161–174). Kelp forests ring the North Pacific from Japan to Baja California, and resume, after a tropical break, along the Andean coast. They are among the most productive ecosystems on Earth — sheltering shellfish, fish, sea mammals, seabirds, and edible seaweeds themselves — and, crucially, they are the same ecosystem at every latitude: a people who knew how to live from a kelp forest in Beringia could live from one in Oregon or Peru with minimal relearning. By about 16,000 years ago, on Erlandson's reconstruction, the North Pacific coast offered an essentially unobstructed, sea-level migration route from northeast Asia into the Americas — thousands of years before the corridor became viable. The maritime capability the model requires is no longer speculative in principle: humans had crossed open sea to reach Australia by 50,000 years ago and the Ryukyu Islands by 30,000, and this hub treats that deep history of voyaging in Early Seafaring.

The coastal model's great weakness is the mirror of Beringia's: its evidence is drowned. The Pleistocene shoreline lies 100 metres and more below modern sea level; the camps of any coastal migration before about 13,000 years ago are, with rare exceptions on uplifted coasts, under the Pacific. What survives is circumstantial but accumulating: Monte Verde's imported seaweed (§III); Cooper's Ferry's position up the Columbia–Snake–Salmon river system, the first great ice-free river route inland from the coast (§XI); the stemmed-point tradition shared between the earliest western North American sites and late-glacial Japan; and the simple chronological fact that people were demonstrably south of the ice before the corridor could feed a traveller. Erlandson himself states the position with care — the kelp highway is a hypothesis that fits the dates and the ecology, not a proven itinerary — and this article adopts it at exactly that strength: the coastal route is the only currently proposed road consistent with the accepted site chronology, and its direct evidence is largely submerged.

// WHAT WHITE SANDS DOES TO THE ROUTES //

If the White Sands dates stand (§VIII), the route problem sharpens by an order of magnitude. People in New Mexico at 23,000–21,000 BP were south of the ice during the LGM, when the corridor did not exist in any form and the ice sheets stood at their maximum. Their ancestors must therefore have come down the coast before or during the glacial maximum — requiring a coastal movement out of Beringia substantially earlier than the c. 16,000 BP window the kelp-highway model usually assumes — and then travelled inland to the Tularosa Basin; or, less parsimoniously, crossed the ice itself. The footprints do not merely predate the corridor debate. They demand a pre-LGM chapter of the coastal story for which the direct evidence, if it survives anywhere, lies on the drowned continental shelf. This is the largest single consequence of the White Sands chronology, and the least excavated.

XI. The Pre-Clovis Roster — Four Sites at Their Evidence Strength

Monte Verde (§III) is the benchmark, but it no longer stands alone. Between 2008 and 2019, four further sites — three in North America, each found or re-examined under post-Monte-Verde standards — built the case that a hemisphere-wide human presence preceded Clovis by two to three thousand years. They are presented here in the order of their evidence, each at its own strength, because their strengths genuinely differ: one rests on DNA in dung, one on a knife in a flooded sinkhole, one on the most conventional and therefore most portable evidence of all — stone tools in dated strata.

Cooper's Ferry, Idaho — c. 16,000 BP

The strongest of the four, and the oldest widely accepted site in North America, is Cooper's Ferry, on a terrace of the lower Salmon River in western Idaho — the place the Niimíipuu (Nez Perce) know as the ancient village of Nipéhe. Excavated by Loren Davis of Oregon State University from 1997 and intensively from 2009, the site preserves hearths, storage and refuse pits, butchery debris, and a stone-tool assemblage in deeply buried loess. The 2019 report (Davis et al., Science 365: 891–897) presented radiocarbon dates on charcoal and bone from the lowest cultural layers and a Bayesian model placing the start of occupation between about 16,560 and 15,280 cal BP — conventionally summarised as c. 16,000 BP. A later report (Davis et al., Science Advances, 2022) described fourteen stemmed projectile points from a pit feature in the lowest deposits, dated to about 15,800 cal BP. The points belong to the Western Stemmed Tradition — smaller, stemmed rather than fluted, and technologically distinct from Clovis — and Davis has noted their resemblance to late Upper Palaeolithic stemmed points of Hokkaido, Japan, dated c. 20,000–19,000 BP, a resemblance he reads as a trace of the coastal movement of §X: people coming down the Pacific shore and turning inland up the Columbia, Snake, and Salmon rivers, more than 500 km from the sea. Cooper's Ferry matters disproportionately because its evidence is unglamorous: tools, features, and dates in stratigraphic order. It is the pre-Clovis claim a sceptic can check with a trowel.

Page-Ladson, Florida — c. 14,550 BP

At the opposite corner of the continent, Page-Ladson lies 9 metres underwater in a sinkhole in Florida's Aucilla River. In the late Pleistocene the sinkhole held a small pond on the Gulf Coastal Plain, and around it, at about 14,550 cal BP, people butchered or scavenged a mastodon. The site was first dug in the 1980s–1990s, when stone artefacts and a mastodon tusk bearing apparent cut marks were reported but widely judged ambiguous. Re-excavation in 2012–2014 by Jessi Halligan, Michael Waters, and colleagues — working underwater, recovering artefacts in place from undisturbed sediment — settled the question to the standard the first campaign could not (Halligan et al., Science Advances, 2016). The re-study confirmed the tusk's grooves as human cut marks from its removal, recovered a chert bifacial knife and flakes in the same layer, and built the date on seventy-one radiocarbon ages in a sealed, stratified sequence. Its proxy evidence carries a second finding: dung-fungus spores (Sporormiella) in the sediments show megafauna abundant around the pond until about 12,600 BP — meaning the first Floridians coexisted with the great beasts for some two thousand years, a datum against any simple blitzkrieg-extinction reading of the peopling. Page-Ladson's force is geographic: by 14,550 BP, people were not trickling through a corridor in Canada. They were butchering mastodons in Florida.

Paisley Caves, Oregon — c. 14,300 BP

The Paisley Five Mile Point Caves, wave-cut shelters above the Summer Lake basin in south-central Oregon, hold the most intimate evidence on this list: human coprolites — desiccated faeces — preserved in the caves' dry deposits. Excavated by Dennis Jenkins of the University of Oregon from 2002, the caves yielded coprolites that yielded, in turn, human mitochondrial DNA of haplogroups A2 and B2 — founding Native American lineages of Siberian derivation — in samples radiocarbon- dated directly to about 14,300 cal BP (Gilbert, Jenkins et al., Science, 2008). It was, at publication, the oldest human DNA recovered in the Americas, and it made Paisley the first pre-Clovis site whose evidence was a human body product rather than a tool. Its audit history is correspondingly sharp: critics (Poinar and colleagues, 2009, with others) questioned whether DNA could have leached down from younger coprolites or been introduced by later cave visitors' urine, and whether every specimen assigned as human was securely so assigned. The Paisley team's replies — including tests showing canid coprolites from the same layers carried no human DNA, and a 2012 report of Western Stemmed points in the caves dated to Clovis age and slightly beyond — have kept the site's pre-Clovis standing, and Paisley is generally counted among the accepted early sites. This article rates it a half-step below Cooper's Ferry and Page-Ladson: the dates are direct and the DNA is real, but the chain from molecule to occupation carries one more inferential link than a knife in a sealed layer does.

Monte Verde, Again — The Roster's Anchor

The fourth member of the roster is Monte Verde itself (§III), restated here for the comparison the roster exists to make. At c. 14,500 cal BP it is not the oldest of the four — Cooper's Ferry precedes it by some fifteen centuries — but it remains the most complete: a settlement, not a trace, with architecture, diet, medicine, and a direct coastal link in its seaweed. Read together, the four sites describe a pattern no single one could: by 14,500 BP people were present from Idaho to Florida to Chile; by 16,000 BP they were established on a Pacific-draining river system in the interior Northwest; and everywhere they appear, they appear as broad-spectrum foragers with regional toolkits — stemmed points in the west, a biface in Florida, pebble tools and wood in Chile — not as the uniform mammoth-hunting wave of the old model. Waters's synthesis (Science, 2019) names this interval the exploration period: dispersed populations, present between about 15,500 and 14,000 BP across both continents, from whom the recognisable regional complexes — Clovis among them — would emerge after 13,000 BP. The roster's collective strength should be stated plainly: even if White Sands were struck from the record tomorrow, the pre-Clovis peopling of the Americas by at least c. 16,000 BP would stand on Cooper's Ferry alone, and its hemisphere-wide spread by 14,500 BP on the other three.

XII. The Ancient Beringians — A Genome from Upward Sun River

The site of Upward Sun River (USR) lies at the confluence of the Tanana River and Upward Sun Creek in interior Alaska — eastern Beringia. Excavated by Ben A. Potter of the University of Alaska Fairbanks, it produced, first, the cremated remains of a small child in a house pit, dated to about 11,500 years ago (Potter et al., Science, 2011) — then, in 2013, beneath that level, the burials of two infant girls, interred together in a pit with grave goods of decorated antler and stone, at the same age of about 11,500 cal BP. The local Athabascan community named the older infant Xach'itee'aanenh t'eede gay — "sunrise child-girl" — and the research proceeded with the community's partnership. From her remains, a team led by J. Víctor Moreno-Mayar and Eske Willerslev sequenced a complete genome to about 17-fold coverage. The paper — Moreno-Mayar, Potter, and colleagues, Nature, January 2018 — is the single most informative ancient genome yet recovered for the peopling question, because of where its owner sits on the family tree.

USR1's genome is Native American — closer to all Native Americans than to any Asian population — but it belongs to neither of the two great branches (Northern and Southern) into which all other sampled Native American ancestry falls. It is basal to both: a third lineage, branching off before their split, which the authors named the Ancient Beringians. The demographic modelling built on that position produced the fullest population history the genomes currently support. The Ancient Beringians and the ancestors of all other Native Americans descend from a single founding population — one source, not successive unrelated waves — which split from East Asians about 36,000 years ago, exchanged genes with them until about 25,000 years ago, received Ancient North Eurasian ancestry between 25,000 and 20,000 years ago, and then divided: the Ancient Beringian lineage branched off about 22,000–18,000 years ago, while the lineage leading to everyone else later split, about 17,500–14,600 years ago, into the Northern and Southern branches — a split the authors locate south of the ice sheets. The Ancient Beringians themselves stayed in the north, in Beringian Alaska, where USR1's people were still living at 11,500 BP; afterwards they disappear as a distinct population, replaced or absorbed, on the paper's reading, by a back-migration of Northern-branch people into the far north.

ONE FOUNDING POPULATION

The genomes support a single source population for Ancient Beringians and all other Native Americans — against older multi-wave models in which the hemisphere was peopled by unrelated migrations. (Later, separate movements brought Palaeo-Inuit and Inuit ancestors across the Arctic in the Holocene; those are subsequent chapters, not rival foundings.)

A PEOPLE WHO VANISHED

The Ancient Beringians left no known descendants among living peoples. Their existence — proved by one infant's genome — is the standing demonstration that the Americas held populations the modern genetic record alone would never have revealed.

THE SPLIT HAPPENED SOUTH OF THE ICE

The Northern–Southern divergence at c. 17,500–14,600 BP is modelled as occurring after the founding population's descendants had passed the ice sheets — genomic support, independent of any site, for a southward movement in that window.

THE STANDSTILL, CONFIRMED IN BONE

USR1 is the standstill made flesh: a member of a lineage that had been in or near Beringia, genetically distinct, since before the LGM — still there, in Alaska, three thousand years after Monte Verde was occupied.

Two consequences of the Upward Sun River genome run through the rest of this article. The first is the single founding population result, which has held up across subsequent genomic studies: with the exception of the much later Arctic movements, the Indigenous peoples of the Americas descend from one ancestral population — the standstill population of §V — and their deep unity of descent is a genetic fact, however many routes and centuries its dispersal took. The second is the Ancient Beringians' disappearance, which cuts the other way as a methodological warning. Here is a whole branch of the founding population, present in Alaska for millennia, invisible in every living genome, known from a single burial. If a population that substantial can vanish from the descendant record, then the genomic dispersal window of §V — the split at 17,500–14,600 BP — is a date for the ancestors of surviving lineages, not a bound on every foot that ever crossed. The people who walked at White Sands at 23,000–21,000 BP, if the dates stand, need not contradict the genomes. They may simply have been, like the Ancient Beringians, a chapter that left no descendants — earlier, and known not from a genome but from footprints in gypsum mud. The same humility applies in reverse to this hub's treatment of symbolic life in Ochre and Symbol: the record preserves what it preserves, and the gaps are data about preservation, not about the past's emptiness.

XIII. Clovis Re-Seated — A Horizon, Not an Arrival

What, then, was Clovis? Stripped of its founding role, the Clovis complex remains one of the most striking phenomena in world archaeology, and it deserves a section of its own — re-seated, at its correct date and scale, in a hemisphere that was already old.

The phenomenon first. At about 13,050 cal BP, a distinctive technological package — the fluted lanceolate point, large bifaces, blade production, bone and ivory tools — appears in the record across an enormous range: from Alberta to northern Mexico, from the Pacific watershed to the Atlantic coast. And at about 12,750 cal BP, three centuries later, it is gone, replaced everywhere by regional traditions — Folsom on the Plains, stemmed points continuing in the West, fishtail points already established in the southern cone of South America by about 12,900 BP. Those bounds are the product of the most careful dating programme ever applied to an American complex: Waters and Stafford, auditing and re-measuring radiocarbon ages from Clovis sites across two studies (2007; Waters, Stafford & Carlson, Science Advances, 2020), retained only ages on securely associated bone, charcoal, and carbonised plant remains, and found thirty-two acceptable dates from ten sites falling between 11,110 and 10,820 radiocarbon years BP — the calibrated span of 13,050–12,750 cal BP. Clovis, on the best evidence, lasted about three hundred years.

Three hundred years is not a founding. A technology that ignites across a continent and burns out within ten generations is behaving like an idea moving through people — or like a population already distributed, already networked, adopting and then abandoning a shared toolkit in near-synchrony. The genomics point the same way. The one human burial directly associated with Clovis tools, the infant Anzick-1 from Montana (c. 12,600 BP), yielded a genome (Rasmussen et al., Nature, 2014) showing the Clovis child to be directly ancestral to many living Native Americans, particularly of Central and South America: the Clovis people were not a vanished first wave replaced by later arrivals, but members of the same founding descent as everyone else — a population of the exploration period's descendants, whose signature technology flared and faded within a much longer occupation. Meanwhile the stemmed-point makers of the West were Clovis's contemporaries, not its heirs, and South America's fishtail horizon is as old as Clovis itself. By 13,000 BP the Americas held at least three regional traditions at once. The picture that replaces Clovis-first is therefore not "someone else first." It is everyone already there: a hemisphere peopled for two to ten millennia, differentiating regionally, within which the fluted point had its brilliant three centuries.

The re-seating also re-opens, honestly, the question Clovis-first used to answer by definition: the megafauna extinctions. Clovis's disappearance at c. 12,750 BP coincides with the final loss of the North American mammoths and mastodons and the onset of the Younger Dryas cold reversal, and human hunting surely played its part in the great dying. But Page-Ladson's two thousand years of coexistence (§XI), and the longer coexistence implied by White Sands, rule out the simple story in which naive fauna met first-contact hunters and fell within centuries. The extinction was a terminal-Pleistocene event with climate in it as well as people — slower, patchier, and stranger than the overkill model allowed. Clovis keeps its drama. It loses only its priority — which, in the end, was never a property of the evidence, only of the order in which the evidence happened to be found: the Plains kill sites were conspicuous, datable, and dug first, and for fifty years the field mistook the first chapter it had read for the first chapter there was.

XIV. Assessment — What Is Settled, What Remains Open

The peopling of the Americas has been argued for a century, and the argument's centre of gravity has moved, in one professional lifetime, from 13,000 years to the edge of the Last Glacial Maximum. It is worth closing with the ledger kept exactly: what the evidence now settles, what it genuinely leaves open, and what would change the ledger.

Settled, at Tier A strength. The first Americans came from northeastern Asia by way of Beringia; no other origin is supported by any line of evidence. Their ancestors formed a single founding population, of East Asian and Ancient North Eurasian descent, which was genetically isolated — most plausibly in a Beringian shrub-tundra refugium — from about 25,000 years ago. People were present south of the ice sheets by at least 16,000 BP (Cooper's Ferry) and across both continents by 14,500 BP (Monte Verde, Page-Ladson, Paisley). The ice-free corridor cannot have been the first route south: it opened physically around 15,000–14,000 BP and became biologically viable later than the sites it would have had to feed. Clovis (13,050–12,750 cal BP) was a later technological horizon within an already-peopled hemisphere, made by people ancestral to living Native Americans. And at least one founding branch — the Ancient Beringians — lived and died in the north without leaving descendants in the sampled record, which licenses, in principle, other early populations that likewise left none.

Probably settled, still completing its acceptance. The White Sands footprints belong to the Last Glacial Maximum, c. 23,000–21,000 BP. The verdict of §VIII stands behind that sentence: three dating materials, two physical clocks, multiple independent laboratories, fifty-five radiocarbon dates in stratigraphic order. The remaining published dissent now concerns second-order questions — pollen pretreatment, reworking scenarios — rather than the reservoir mechanism that began the fight, and no young age for the tracks has been demonstrated by any measurement. This article records the White Sands age as the prevailing reading of the literature, with its file still open, which is the strongest status a five-year-old claim of this magnitude can honestly hold.

Genuinely open. Four questions remain, and they should be named without varnish. The entry date: if White Sands stands, people crossed south of the ice before or during the LGM — but by how long before, the record does not yet say; the gap between the footprints (c. 23,000 BP) and the next-oldest accepted site (c. 16,000 BP) is seven thousand years containing not one generally accepted site, and that silence is the largest single anomaly in the field. The route: the coastal model is the only fit for the chronology, but its early sites are drowned, its proof is circumstantial, and a pre-LGM coastal movement — which White Sands requires — is inferred, not observed. The early South American claims: a tier of proposed dates older than Monte Verde remains contested at every point — Monte Verde I (c. 18,500 BP, §III), the stone assemblages of Chiquihuite Cave in Mexico (claimed occupation from c. 26,500–30,000 BP; Ardelean et al., Nature, 2020), whose status as artefacts and whose stratigraphy are both disputed; Pedra Furada in Brazil, where claimed hearths and tools of 20,000 years and far older turn on the artefact-versus-geofact question that has dogged this field since the 1870s; cut-marked bones at Arroyo del Vizcaíno, Uruguay, proposed at c. 30,000 BP; and, as the limiting case, the Cerutti Mastodon claim of c. 130,000 BP from California, which the field has examined and, in its overwhelming majority, rejected — the standing demonstration that the standard of §§II and VII still excludes what cannot meet it. The trackmakers' identity: who the White Sands people were — an early offshoot of the founding population, or a separate movement that left no descendants — is a question only an LGM-age genome or an LGM-age site with artefacts could answer, and neither exists yet.

What would change the ledger is therefore easy to specify, which is the mark of a healthy field: one securely dated site in the 23,000–16,000 BP gap, anywhere in the hemisphere, excavated and published to the Monte Verde standard; one ancient genome from south of the ice older than 17,500 BP; or one demonstrated failure, by direct measurement, in the White Sands pollen or mud chronology. Any of the three would move this article's assessment, and this article would be rewritten accordingly. Until then, the story the evidence tells is this. A small people waited out the coldest millennia of the Ice Age in a shrub-tundra country that is now beneath the Bering Sea. Some of their kin — perhaps earlier than the genomes remember — walked a lakeshore in New Mexico while the ice stood at its greatest, most of them children, and left their footprints in the mud. Their descendants, by roads we can still only partly see, reached Idaho by 16,000 years ago, Florida and Chile by 14,500, and filled two continents — and one branch of the family stayed in the north and vanished, leaving a single buried infant to prove they had ever been. The last continents were not settled in a moment. They were settled in depth, over thousands of years, by people whose record we are still learning to read — in stone, in dung, in DNA, and, at White Sands, in the prints of bare feet on the shore of a lake that no longer exists.
