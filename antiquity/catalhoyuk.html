This is a Tier A article: everything in it rests on excavated, dated, and published archaeological evidence, and where scholars disagree — as they do, sharply, about the size of the population, the meaning of the famous "volcano" painting, and the reality of the town's celebrated equality — the disagreement itself is reported, with its positions attributed. No claim here depends on ancient legend or modern construction, and none is allowed to borrow strength from either. One discipline needs stating at the outset, because Çatalhöyük attracts mythology the way few sites do: the readings of its first excavator, James Mellaart — the "shrines", the mother goddess, the matriarchal town — are treated throughout as historiography, a layer in the site's modern history, and not as current consensus. Where this article reports what the ground shows, it follows the re-excavation of the site by the Çatalhöyük Research Project under Ian Hodder (1993–2017) and the specialist literature that project produced.

Çatalhöyük is a double settlement mound on the Konya plain of central Anatolia, in modern Turkey, built up on the alluvial fan of the Çarşamba river. Its East mound carries one of the longest and most densely built Neolithic sequences in the world: house upon house, for well over a thousand years, in a fabric so tight that there were no streets at all. People crossed the town on its roofs, climbed down ladders through holes in those roofs into their homes, cooked, slept, painted their walls, set the horned skulls of wild bulls into their benches, and buried their dead — hundreds of them, across the excavated sample — in pits cut through the floors they lived on. Then they filled each dead house in, and built its successor on the same footprint, on the stumps of the same walls.

The hub's index states the claim this article exists to test: that Çatalhöyük is "the standing proof that early urban life did not require kings." The excavated record does show something remarkable — a community of town-like scale and density with no palace, no temple precinct, no central storehouse, no chiefly burials, and no monumental architecture that anyone has identified in more than half a century of digging. But a proof is a strong thing, and §VIII of this article weighs the claim at its real strength: the positive evidence for an egalitarian organisation, the counter-evidence of the "history houses" and of change over time, and the standing weakness of all arguments built on absence — that authority which leaves no monument is authority the spade cannot see. The reader will find the claim largely sustained, and carefully qualified. That is the only form in which this tier prints it.

I. Two Mounds on the Konya Plain — The Place and the Sequence

Çatalhöyük — the name is modern Turkish, çatal, a fork, and höyük, a mound — lies on the southern Konya plain at roughly 1,000 metres above sea level, some 40–50 km southeast of the modern city of Konya, near the present-day village of Küçükköy in Çumra district. The settlement stood on the alluvial fan of the Çarşamba river, which in the Neolithic ran between what are now the site's two mounds and spread seasonally across the plain in a mosaic of channels, marshes, and dry ground. That mosaic was the town's larder and its building yard at once: river clay and marl for mudbrick and plaster, reeds and grasses for mats and baskets, wetland grazing and wildfowl at the door, and drier rises for fields within reach of the houses. The plain is ringed by hills that supplied timber, stone, and game; far to the northeast, on a clear day, the twin peaks of the volcano Hasan Dağı stand on the horizon — a landmark that matters to §VI of this article.

The East mound is the famous one. It rises some 20 metres above the plain and covers about 13.5 hectares, and excavation has distinguished eighteen levels of Neolithic building within it — level upon level of the same dense house fabric, each generation's town founded on the levelled remains of the last. The West mound, a few hundred metres away across the former river channel, is lower and smaller, and carries the settlement's Early Chalcolithic continuation: painted pottery, changed house forms, and a materially different, though related, community (§IX). The site as a whole was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2012, under criteria that cite, in the listing's own framing, its streetless neighbourhoods, its roof-access houses, and a social organisation read as largely egalitarian — a judgement §VIII examines rather than assumes.

The Dates — A Necessary Correction of the Round Numbers

Çatalhöyük's chronology comes in two generations, and this article keeps them apart because the difference is instructive. The conventional dates, established in the Mellaart era and still carried by the UNESCO listing, give the East mound as c. 7400–6200 BCE and the West as c. 6200–5200 BCE — the figures behind the round span, c. 7500–5700 BCE for the settlement as a whole, that this hub's timeline prints. The revised dates come from the Hodder project's programme of Bayesian radiocarbon modelling, in which hundreds of AMS determinations are ordered by the stratigraphy they came from, so that each date constrains its neighbours. Bayliss and colleagues (2015), modelling the start of the South Area sequence, placed the beginning of occupation on the East mound at 7165–7085 cal BCE (95% probability) — some three centuries later than the older estimate. Work on the mound's summit levels (Marciniak and colleagues, 2015) dates the abandonment of the last East mound houses to around 5950 BCE. New AMS dating of the West mound (Orton and colleagues, 2018) then dissolved the old picture of a clean succession: the two mounds were occupied together for a time, the focus shifting gradually, probably over a century or two around 6000 BCE, and the West mound occupation ran on to roughly 5600–5500 BCE.

The working chronology of this article is therefore: East mound, c. 7100–5950 BCE; West mound, beginning in the late seventh millennium and running to c. 5600–5500 BCE; a total settlement history of some fifteen to sixteen centuries on the East mound alone. Where the older round figures appear below, they are flagged as the older figures. The correction matters beyond pedantry: a town founded around 7100 BCE is not a pioneer outpost of the first farming spread but a mature, late foundation, established by people whose ancestors had farmed Anatolia for well over a millennium — at Aşıklı Höyük and Boncuklu, a short distance away, the region's earlier experiments in settled life can still be read. Çatalhöyük's achievement was not priority. It was scale, and duration.

How Many People? — The Population Problem

No number in this article is softer than the population, and the honest treatment is to show the machinery that produces the figures rather than to pick one. The classic estimate, built up in the Mellaart tradition and formalised by Craig Cessford for the Hodder project, runs as follows: measure the roofed area of a well-excavated level; count its houses; multiply by an assumed household — five to ten persons, on ethnographic analogy — and scale up to the whole mound. On those assumptions the peak population of the East mound comes out at 3,500 to 8,000 people, the range this hub's sources and most syntheses quote. Every step of the machinery, however, carries an assumption that can be challenged, and in 2024 Kuijt and Marciniak challenged nearly all of them at once.

Their critique, published in the Journal of Anthropological Archaeology, works from building biographies: houses were not all standing at once, not all roofed space was residential at any given moment, buildings spent part of their lives as ruins, stores, or animal pens, and the roofed fraction of the mound at any one time was perhaps only half to two-thirds of the whole. Rebuilding the estimate on those terms, they arrive at 600 to 800 people in an average year of the town's Middle phase (c. 6700–6500 BCE) — an order of magnitude below the classic peak. Other recent syntheses split the difference, quoting working ranges of roughly 500 to 5,000. The disagreement is not noise; it is the measurement. What survives every version of the arithmetic is the density: whether the town held eight hundred people or eight thousand, it held them in a continuous fabric of shared-wall houses with no streets, no plots, and no open public ground inside the built area — a crowding that has no parallel among ordinary farming villages of its millennium, and that frames every social question the site poses.

This article therefore speaks of "hundreds to thousands" where the argument does not depend on the count, gives the classic 3,500–8,000 where it reports the classic literature, and names the 2024 revision where the scale of the community is itself the point at issue. One comparison orients the range. The walled town of Jericho, a millennium older, held its thousands behind a wall and under a great tower; Çatalhöyük, at any point in its population range, held a comparable crowd with no wall, no tower, and no centre. How it held together at all is the subject of §§IV, V, and VIII.

II. Mellaart, 1961–65 — The Discovery and Its First Reading

The East mound was identified in November 1958, during a survey of the Konya plain by James Mellaart of the British Institute of Archaeology at Ankara, with David French and Alan Hall. Mellaart excavated for four seasons, 1961 to 1965 (the 1964 season was lost to the political fallout of the Dorak affair, an unrelated controversy over Bronze Age finds that ended his Turkish career). Working at a speed and on a scale that no modern project would attempt, his teams cleared more than 160 buildings in the southwest of the East mound and cut two trenches into the West mound, establishing the level sequence — his Levels XII to I, earliest to latest — on which all later work still builds. His synthesis, Çatal Hüyük: A Neolithic Town in Anatolia (1967), presented the site to the world, and its photographs of painted walls, horned benches, and corpulent figurines made Çatalhöyük famous within the decade.

The excavation itself was a genuine achievement, and the Hodder project's re-excavation has confirmed its stratigraphic skeleton: the agglutinative houses, the roof entry, the underfloor burials, the paintings, and the installations are all real, and all where Mellaart said they were. What has not survived is the interpretive superstructure he built on them, and the distinction between the two — the digging and the reading — is the single most important thing a modern account can preserve. The "Shrines"

Mellaart divided the buildings he cleared into ordinary houses and "shrines" — by his count some forty rooms whose richer decoration, paintings, reliefs, and bull-horn installations marked them, in his view, as cult buildings served by priests. The shrines anchored his whole reconstruction: a town of temples, in which religion was housed in dedicated architecture and mediated by specialists. The Hodder project's verdict, after excavating comparable buildings stone by stone and sample by sample, is that the category does not hold. Every "shrine" so far re-examined is a house: it has the full domestic kit — oven, hearth, storage bins, platforms, food-processing debris, burials — and its elaboration differs from its neighbours' in degree, not in kind. Buildings at Çatalhöyük form a continuum from the plain to the spectacularly decorated; the line Mellaart drew across that continuum, with temples on one side and homes on the other, is not in the ground. The consequences run through this whole article: the town's ritual life was not concentrated in a temple quarter. It was distributed, house by house, through the same rooms in which people cooked and slept — a finding that matters as much to the equality question (§VIII) as to the history of religion.

The Mother Goddess

Mellaart's second great construction was theological. The figurines of women — above all a seated figure flanked by two felines, found in a grain bin in a late level — he read as images of a Great Goddess, a mother deity of fertility and the harvest, heading the pantheon of what he presented as an essentially matriarchal society; the many sexless figurines he took as further symptoms of a culture in which, in his framing, the female principle dominated. Marija Gimbutas built the Çatalhöyük goddess into her wider vision of a goddess-worshipping "Old Europe", and through her the site became a place of modern pilgrimage for the Goddess movement. All of this is historiography now. The systematic re-study of the figurine corpus by Lynn Meskell, Carolyn Nakamura, and their colleagues (§VI) found that clearly female figures are a small minority of the whole — under three per cent of the diagnostic corpus — that most figurines are animals or unsexed abbreviated forms, and that they come overwhelmingly not from shrines or altars but from middens and fills, discarded with the rubbish. A goddess cult may have existed at Çatalhöyük; the figurines, counted rather than cherry-picked, do not evidence one. This article therefore uses "goddess figurines" only as the name of a debate, never as a description of a find.

// READING MELLAART TODAY //

Mellaart's 1967 volume remains the primary publication of the 1960s excavation and the source of its plans, photographs, and find records; it is cited in this article's Sources in exactly that capacity. Its interpretations — shrines, goddess, matriarchy, and the town-plan reading of the "volcano" painting (§VI) — are reported as his, attributed at every occurrence, and weighed against the re-excavation. That double treatment is not a courtesy. It is the archive's standing rule for any excavator whose synthesis outran his evidence: keep the record, retire the reading, and say plainly which is which.

After 1965 the mound slept for a generation. The site was protected, the finds went to the Museum of Anatolian Civilizations in Ankara — where the seated figure, the paintings' originals, and the great aurochs-hunt mural still anchor the Neolithic galleries — and Çatalhöyük entered the textbooks in Mellaart's version: the earliest city, the goddess town, the peaceful matriarchy. When excavation resumed in 1993, it resumed under a director, Ian Hodder, who had been Mellaart's student, and who came to the site with the explicit intention of testing the inheritance — including, as it turned out, most of his teacher's conclusions. The methods he brought with him are the subject of the next section, because at Çatalhöyük the method is part of the evidence: several of the findings this article relies on — the plaster micro-sequences, the burial biographies, the figurine statistics — exist only because the second excavation dug at a fineness the first never attempted.

III. The Hodder Project, 1993–2017 — Reflexive Archaeology

The Çatalhöyük Research Project, directed by Ian Hodder from 1993 to 2017 — twenty-five years of fieldwork, with the permit concluding in 2018 and Turkish-led work continuing since — is among the largest and longest archaeological projects ever run on a single Neolithic site. Its excavation areas re-opened and extended Mellaart's ground in the South Area of the East mound, opened new ground in the North Area, and returned in later seasons to the summit levels and to the West mound. Under the field direction of Shahina Farid, the project's excavation summaries established the fine stratigraphic framework — a re-lettered sequence of levels, South G to T and their northern equivalents — within which the Bayesian dating of §I and every building biography in this article are set. The project's publication output is correspondingly vast: the multi-volume Çatalhöyük Excavations monographs, specialist studies in every material from plaster chemistry to human ancient DNA, and Hodder's own synthesis, The Leopard's Tale (2006), the single most readable account of the site and the source this article leans on most.

The project's scale, however, is less distinctive than its method. Hodder came to Çatalhöyük as a founder of post-processual archaeology — the movement arguing that the past is not simply recovered but interpreted, and that the interpretation should therefore be documented as part of the record. The project practised what it called reflexive archaeology, briefly and factually: interpretation was pushed down to the trowel's edge, so that excavators recorded their readings of a deposit as they dug it, in diaries kept alongside the formal context records; specialists — bone, plant, pottery, plaster — worked on site, in daily "priority tours" of the trenches, rather than receiving finds in distant laboratories months later; the project's databases and diaries were opened to outside comment from early on; and multiple, sometimes conflicting, interpretations were published side by side rather than resolved into a single directorial voice — the "multivocality" the project made its watchword. One may or may not find the philosophy congenial. Its practical yield is not in doubt: a record of unusual depth and unusual auditability, in which the reasoning behind a claim can be traced back through diary, context sheet, and sample.

The method also changed what could be seen. Mellaart's teams had dug building by building; the Hodder teams dug within buildings at the scale of the single rake-out, the single replastering, the single dumped basket of ash. Micromorphology — the microscopic study of undisturbed sediment blocks — resolved floor and wall plasters into sequences of individual coats and sweepings (§V). Flotation recovered plant remains by the tens of thousands of samples (§VII). Every burial was excavated as a biography: position, wrapping, sequence of interment, later disturbance. It is not too much to say that the Çatalhöyük of this article — a town known through the life histories of individual houses — is a product of that fineness of digging, and that the older Çatalhöyük, of shrines and goddesses, was in part a product of digging too fast to see the houses for the rooms.

// TWO EXCAVATIONS, ONE MOUND //

The contrast is worth holding in mind throughout. Mellaart: four seasons, 160+ buildings, broad exposures, interpretation published as a single confident synthesis. Hodder: twenty-five seasons, fewer buildings, each excavated at micro-stratigraphic resolution with an army of on-site specialists, interpretation published as an open, multi-author archive. Where the two agree — the plan, the burials, the art — the evidence is as solid as Neolithic archaeology gets. Where they disagree, this article follows the slower dig, and says why.

A final fact of method belongs here because it disciplines everything that follows: only a minority of the East mound has been excavated at all. Mellaart's exposures and the Hodder project's areas together open a few hectares of the 13.5 — the rest is known from surface survey, geophysics, and coring, not from the spade. The town plan of §IV is real but partial; the absence of public buildings in §VIII is an absence within the excavated sample, extrapolated, reasonably but not certainly, to the whole. Tier A practice is to carry that caveat in the open, and this article does so at each point where the argument leans on it.

IV. The Agglutinative Town — Houses Without Streets

The plan of Çatalhöyük is the plan of no city planner. Houses stand wall to wall in a continuous fabric — agglutinative building, in the literature's term — each rectangular mudbrick house abutting its neighbours on every side, with its own set of walls even where a neighbour's wall already stood, so that party walls are doubled. Between the houses there are, in the classic levels, no streets, no lanes, and no courtyards at ground level: the only open spaces inside the settlement are the midden areas — patches of accumulated refuse, ash, and dung, used also for penning animals and for work too dirty for the house — which punctuate the fabric where a building has been abandoned or not yet rebuilt. The town's public surface was its roofscape: the flat roofs, at roughly common height within a neighbourhood, formed the streets, squares, and work yards of Çatalhöyük, and the excavated plan is best read as a map of cellars beneath a town that was actually lived one storey up, in the sun and the smoke, on surfaces that have not survived.

Movement followed from the plan. To visit a neighbour, one crossed roofs. To enter one's own house, one climbed a ladder to the roof and descended a second ladder through the roof opening — the house's only door, and also its chimney and its principal window. The opening stood, in the standard plan, over the southern part of the main room, directly above the oven and hearth, so that smoke rose past the ladder and out; the arrangement made every house defensible in a small way — draw up the ladder and the house is shut — though whether defence explains it, rather than the simple absence of any ground-level access in a streetless fabric, the evidence does not decide. Goods moved as people moved: grain, fuel, dung, carcasses, and building clay all came and went over the roofs and down the ladders, and the middens below the roofscape's edge received what the houses threw out. In the upper levels, after c. 6500 BCE, the fabric loosens — houses stand in clusters with gaps between, and something closer to lanes appears — a change that §VIII and §IX take up, since it tracks a loosening of the social fabric as well.

Anatomy of a House

Within the fabric, the individual house is one of the most standardised dwellings of the Neolithic world — a template repeated, with variations, in house after house and level after level, and set out here because §V and §VIII both argue from it. A typical house is a single storey of mudbrick on a footing of the levelled house beneath, its main room commonly around 25 square metres, with one or two small side rooms. The main room is zoned with a consistency that amounts to a grammar: the southern zone, beneath the roof hole, is the dirty working end — domed clay oven set against the south wall, a hearth on the floor beside it, ash, food debris, and the wear of daily traffic; the northern and eastern zones are the clean end — raised platforms of plastered mudbrick, kept swept and white, for sitting, working, and sleeping, and it is beneath these platforms that the dead are buried. The side rooms are for storage: clay bins built against their walls held grain, lentils, and peas; baskets, mats, tools, and stores filled the rest. Timber roof beams and posts, reed and clay roofing, wall niches, and — in the elaborate houses — benches and installations complete the inventory. Every house has its own oven, its own storage, its own platforms: the unit is functionally complete, a fact on which the equality argument of §VIII substantially rests.

Above the single house stands the neighbourhood. Bleda Düring's analysis of the Central Anatolian settlements (Constructing Communities, 2006) showed that the Çatalhöyük fabric is not uniform porridge: houses cluster in groups — his "clustered neighbourhoods", of the order of a few dozen households — separated by midden areas and slight breaks in the building line, and these clusters, he argued, were the town's operative social units above the household: the people whose roofs formed one continuous walking surface, whose dead and whose stores were near, and whose cooperation the daily logistics of a streetless town required. More recent spatial work by the project complicates any rigid version of the scheme — groupings overlap, shift between levels, and cross-cut one another — but the nested picture it supports is the one this article uses: household within house-cluster, cluster within sector, sector within town, with no level of the nesting marked by a building bigger or grander than the rest. The contrast the hub invites is with Göbekli Tepe, three millennia earlier and a day's journey east in its day: there, a community without farming built monuments and no town; here, a farming community built a town and no monuments. The Neolithic contained both experiments.

NO STREETS

In the classic levels the built fabric is continuous: no lanes, alleys, or plazas at ground level. Circulation, visiting, and much outdoor work happened on the roofs.

ONE DOOR, IN THE ROOF

Every house is entered from above, by ladder through the roof opening — simultaneously its door, chimney, and window, set over the oven end of the main room.

A GRAMMAR OF ZONES

South is dirty (oven, hearth, ash, traffic); north and east are clean (white platforms, display, sleep). The same grammar recurs in house after house, level after level.

MIDDENS BETWEEN

The only ground-level open spaces are middens — refuse, ash, and dung accumulations used for penning and dirty work, in the gaps of the house fabric.

V. The House as History — Rebuilding, Plaster, and the Dead Beneath the Floors

A Çatalhöyük house had a life, and the town's deepest habit was to make that life legible. Houses stood for roughly a human lifetime — estimates from the building biographies run to some fifty to eighty years — and at the end of a life, a house was not simply abandoned. It was closed: cleaned out, its roof timbers salvaged, its installations sometimes carefully removed or buried, its upper walls pushed in, and its shell filled with clean material to form a level platform. On that platform, on the stumps of the old walls and to the old alignments, the successor house was built — same footprint, same orientation, same internal grammar, often with its platforms and oven in the same relative places. Repeat the cycle for a millennium and the mound explains itself: 21 metres of houses, each generation standing quite literally on its predecessors. Continuity of place was not an accident of a crowded town; the care taken in closure and rebuilding, and the retrieval and re-deposition of objects and remains between houses, show it being maintained, house by house, as a practice.

Replastering — The Calendar on the Wall

Within a house's life, the maintenance rhythm was plaster. Floors and walls were coated in a fine white marl plaster, and the coating was renewed again and again: micromorphological study of wall sections counts the coats one by one, and the numbers are startling — dozens of replasterings in an ordinary house, and in extreme cases several hundred micro-layers within 20 cm of accumulated wall plaster, with counts up to around 450 coats reported for the most-renewed surfaces. Not every coat was a full re-whitening; many are hair-thin skim coats, and the rhythm they record — seasonal, perhaps in part annual, punctuated by heavier re-plasterings at life events — made the wall a calendar of the household's care. Platforms were kept the whitest: swept, clean, and renewed most often, the visible centre of the room. Paintings (§VI) belong to this cycle: a painting was made on fresh plaster, lived with, and in time plastered over, sometimes to be succeeded by another on the same wall. The imagery of Çatalhöyük was not permanent public art. It was domestic, renewable, and repeatedly buried by the household's own hand — which is why so much of it survives.

The Dead Beneath the Floors

The dead stayed in the house. The standard burial at Çatalhöyük is a primary inhumation beneath the floor: a narrow oval pit cut through the plaster — most often beneath the northern or eastern platforms of the main room — the body laid tightly flexed, frequently wrapped in matting or textile and sometimes set in a basket, the pit filled and the floor replastered over it, so that the household continued to sit and sleep above its dead. Infants and the very young were more often buried near the oven or in side rooms; the platform burials are disproportionately adults. The scale of the practice is best given in the project's own counts: the skeletal remains of at least 741 individuals were excavated from Neolithic contexts in the Hodder seasons of 1993–2017 alone (Larsen and colleagues, 2019), of whom sub-floor primary burials account for 471; with the Mellaart-era dead added, the excavated total runs to around 800 individuals. Burial was not a single event but a continuing relationship: floors were reopened for successive interments in the same house, some burials were later disturbed or partly removed, and a minority of the dead — about thirteen per cent of the assemblage — are secondary burials, loose or partly articulated remains re-deposited, sometimes with a primary burial. Skulls, in a small number of cases, were retrieved and kept: a few were plastered and painted, in the wider Anatolian and Levantine tradition this hub meets at Jericho, and one famous burial held a plastered skull cradled in its arms.

Two findings discipline the romance of the ancestor house. First, burial in a house was selective and uneven: some houses accumulated burials by the dozen — the most laden hold thirty, forty, in the extreme over sixty individuals across a building sequence — while many houses hold few or none. The dead of Çatalhöyük were not distributed one family to one house; they were gathered, disproportionately, into particular houses. Second, the gathered dead were not necessarily kin. Dental and ancient-DNA studies of co-buried individuals have repeatedly found that people buried together in a house were often not close biological relatives — membership of a house, and burial beneath its floor, appear to have been social facts, made by residence, affiliation, and practice, as much as genealogical ones. The house, in the project's phrase, made its people; it did not merely descend from them.

"History Houses"

The houses at the laden end of that distribution are the ones Hodder named "history houses". A history house is a building sequence that stands out on every axis of elaboration at once: rebuilt on its footprint more times than its neighbours, more thickly plastered, more richly installed and painted, and the repository of far more burials than the town's average — a house that accumulated, in Hodder's reading, the memories, remains, and prestige of a wider group, something like a lineage or sodality headquarters embedded in an ordinary street of roofs. The type specimen of the project's accounts, Building 1 in the North Area, held dozens of burials through its sequence; other sequences show the same signature of repeated rebuilding plus dense burial plus heavy installation. History houses matter to this article out of proportion to their number, because they are the strongest internal evidence against a flat reading of the town's equality: here, visibly, some houses were more equal than others — in symbolic capital, in ancestors, in the labour their upkeep absorbed. What they did not accumulate, on the project's analyses, is the subject of §VIII: no larger stores, no richer burials, no control of production has been shown for them. They were first in memory. Whether "first in memory" is a kind of power that leaves the equality claim intact, or the crack through which hierarchy eventually entered, is the sharpest question the site poses, and it is argued, not assumed, below.

// A HOUSEHOLD YEAR, RECONSTRUCTED FROM THE DEPOSITS //

Plaster: floors and walls re-whitened in thin coats through the year, platforms most often; a painting made on fresh plaster, lived with, and eventually covered. Fire: the domed oven and hearth in the southern zone, fuelled with wood and with dung cakes whose burnt residues fill the middens. Food: grain from the side-room bins, ground on stone querns, cooked as breads and porridges; clay balls heated and dropped into cooking vessels as a boiling technology. Death: when a household member died, the platform floor was cut, the flexed and wrapped body laid beneath it, and the floor made good — the room continuing in use above the new grave. Closure: at the house's end, salvage, clean fill, and rebuilding on the same walls. Every element above is a deposit type the project excavated, not a novelist's colour.

VI. The Imagery — Paintings, Reliefs, Bucrania, and Figurines

No Neolithic site has yielded imagery to match Çatalhöyük's in quantity, and none has suffered more from its own imagery's fame. The paintings, reliefs, and installations come from the same context as everything else in this article — the interiors of houses — and they reward being read there: as the furniture of domestic rooms, renewed and buried by the plastering cycle of §V, rather than as the programme of a temple. What follows takes the classes in turn, and gives the debates at the strength the evidence supports.

Wall Paintings

The paintings, in red, black, yellow, and white on fresh plaster, range from the geometric to the narrative. Geometric panels — grids, zigzags, and interlocking patterns that Mellaart compared to woven kilim designs — are the commonest class. Hand motifs occur, single and in rows. The narrative scenes are the famous ones: great deer and aurochs hunts, the animals rendered at large scale, ringed by crowds of small human figures who tease, bait, and leap around them — scenes the project reads less as records of subsistence (the hunted species were minor in the diet) than as the imagery of feasting and of dangerous, prestigious occasions. Most unsettling, and most reproduced, are the vulture scenes: huge vultures with outstretched wings descending on small headless human figures — widely, though not provably, read as excarnation, the exposure of the dead to birds, a practice that would sit coherently beside the retrieved skulls and secondary burials of §V. The paintings cluster in the elaborate houses and in the middle levels; they are absent from most rooms. Even the imagery, that is, was unevenly distributed — a datum §VIII weighs on both sides.

The "Town and Volcano" Panel — Both Readings

One panel requires its own hearing. From a Level VII house of c. 6600 BCE comes a painting, some three metres wide, whose lower register is a field of roughly eighty tightly packed rectangular cells and whose upper register carries a twin-peaked form spotted and streaked in red and orange. Mellaart's celebrated reading: the cells are a plan of the town — the earliest map known — and the twin-peaked form is Hasan Dağı erupting, the volcano some 130 km northeast of the site, drawn from the town it overlooked. The reading made the panel the most famous Neolithic painting in the world. It has never commanded the field. Stephanie Meece's counter-reading (2006), returning to what was reportedly Mellaart's own first impression, holds that the upper form is a leopard skin — spotted pelt, legs removed, laid over a geometric panel of a type found elsewhere at the site — and that the "town" is simply that geometric field, of the same family as the site's other pattern paintings. Leopards are everywhere in Çatalhöyük's art; maps and landscapes are nowhere else in it. That is the state of the iconographic argument, and on iconography alone the leopard has the better of it.

In 2014 the volcano acquired a witness from geology. Schmitt and colleagues, in PLoS ONE, dated a pumice deposit on Hasan Dağı's summit by (U–Th)/He zircon geochronology to 6960 ± 640 BCE — the first radiometric evidence of a Holocene explosive eruption of that volcano, overlapping the age of the level that held the painting. The result proves that an eruption happened within the town's lifetime and could have been witnessed; it does not and cannot prove that the painting depicts it. The honest summary, which this article adopts: the panel is a unique composition; the town-and-volcano reading is possible and now geologically unembarrassed, the leopard-skin reading is iconographically stronger; the painting's meaning is unresolved, and any account that presents it flatly as "the world's first map" is repeating Mellaart, not the evidence.

Reliefs and Bucrania

The walls also carried sculpture in plaster. Relief figures — splayed, front-facing forms modelled in relief on the walls of elaborate houses — were read by Mellaart as goddesses in the posture of giving birth; the project's re-examination notes that at least one celebrated example is better read as an animal form, a bear-like figure, and that the class as a whole resists his confident sexing. Beside them stand the plaster "breast" mouldings — rounded wall protuberances within which the excavators found, set into the plaster, the teeth of foxes and weasels, the jaws and tusks of wild boar, the claws of bears, and the beaks of vultures: wild animal parts, deliberately embedded in the house's body. And presiding over the class are the bucrania: the skulls and great horn cores of wild aurochs, plastered, sometimes painted, and installed in rows on benches, set into walls, or ranged along platforms — the horns of animals far larger than anything the household herded, brought into the cleanest zone of the room and kept there, in some houses through successive replasterings and rebuildings. Aurochs were feasted on, on the faunal evidence, at occasions that left whole-animal bone spreads in middens; the bucrania are the durable residue of those occasions, curated as the house's honours. If Çatalhöyük had a civic art, this was it — and it hung not in a temple but over the sleeping platforms of particular houses.

The Figurines and the Goddess Debate

The figurines close the section, because their re-study is the cleanest case in the archive of a famous interpretation dissolving under counting. The corpus assembled by the Hodder project numbers over 1,800 figurines and fragments, more than 1,000 of them diagnostic, in clay and stone. When Meskell, Nakamura, and colleagues classified the corpus whole (Meskell et al. 2008; Nakamura & Meskell 2009), the proportions inverted the textbook picture: the great majority are animals — quadrupeds, many of them cattle — or abbreviated human forms whose sex is not marked; figures that are clearly female comprise under three per cent of the corpus, and the emphasis in the human figures, where the body is modelled at all, falls on bellies, buttocks, and flesh — read by Nakamura and Meskell as the imagery of age, maturity, and accumulated substance rather than of fertility as such. The contexts invert the picture a second time: figurines come overwhelmingly from middens, fills, and abandonment deposits — made, handled, and thrown away in the course of daily life, often unfired or low-fired, many surviving only because a hearth accidentally baked them. They were not, as a class, enshrined.

The famous exception proves the rule's limits and is reported as such. The Seated Woman — a corpulent figure seated between two felines, hands resting on their heads, found by Mellaart's team in a grain bin in a late building (Level II) — is a genuinely fine and genuinely unusual object, and its findspot, in the household's store, invites the protective, abundance-related reading Mellaart gave it. But one object, however eloquent, is not a pantheon; the felines themselves are variously identified as leopards or lionesses; and against the single seated figure stand eighteen hundred counted others that do not say what she was made to say. Meskell's critique, which this article follows, is not that the figurines are meaningless but that their meanings were everyday — bound up with making, handling, ageing, animals, and the body — and that the mother-goddess reading survives in popular culture long after it has ceased to be the reading of the evidence. The goddess of Çatalhöyük is a twentieth-century artefact. The figurines are seventh-millennium ones, and they are more interesting than she is.

THE HUNT PAINTINGS

Aurochs and deer hunts, the great animals ringed by crowds of small baiting figures: the imagery of feasting and prestige occasions, not of the daily diet, in which the hunted species are minor.

THE VULTURE SCENES

Vultures descending on headless human figures — the site's most unsettling images, plausibly but not provably tied to excarnation and the curation of skulls.

BUCRANIA

Plastered aurochs skulls and horn cores installed on benches and walls: the curated honours of feasting houses, kept through replasterings and rebuildings.

FIGURINES, COUNTED

1,800+ figurines: mostly animals and unsexed abbreviated forms; clearly female figures under 3%; contexts overwhelmingly middens and fills, not shrines.

VII. Subsistence and Craft — The Farming Package and the Obsidian Roads

Çatalhöyük was a farming town, and its subsistence is among the best documented of any Neolithic community, thanks to the project's flotation programme — over ten thousand archaeobotanical samples across the sequence (Bogaard and colleagues, 2017). The crop package is the central Anatolian version of the Near Eastern founder set, adjusted in place over fifteen centuries: emmer and einkorn wheats as the staples, with free-threshing wheat and naked barley alongside, and lentils, peas, bitter vetch, and chickpeas among the pulses. The weed flora of the samples reads as intensive, small-scale husbandry: crops grown in fixed plots, autumn-sown, manured and hand-tended — garden agriculture at town scale, sustained, on the archaeobotanical argument, by continual adjustment of the crop mix rather than by expansion. Around the package stood a gathered margin that never disappeared: hackberries, almonds, pistachios, acorns, and the tubers of club-rush from the marshes. Dung — sheep and goat dung, burnt as fuel — runs through the deposits as both evidence and preservative, carrying seeds of the animals' grazing into the middens.

The animal economy inverts the imagery of §VI, and the inversion is one of the site's signature findings. The meat of daily life was domestic sheep and goat, dominant in the faunal assemblages throughout the sequence; the great aurochs of the paintings and bucrania were wild, hunted, and eaten at feasts rather than herded, and clearly domestic cattle appear only late — becoming significant after c. 6500 BCE, in the same late re-organisation that §IX follows. Deer, boar, equids, hare, fish, waterfowl, and eggs complete a diet in which the wild never became marginal. The bioarchaeology adds the cost side of the ledger: the skeletons record a community working hard — high workloads, periodic nutritional stress, and the infection load of dense living — with the pressures peaking in the crowded middle levels and easing as the settlement loosened after 6500 BCE (Larsen and colleagues, 2019). Farming at Çatalhöyük was not an idyll, and the bones say so.

Obsidian — The Cappadocian Connection

The town's cutting edge was imported. Çatalhöyük's tools are overwhelmingly of obsidian, the volcanic glass whose sharpness made it the prestige cutting material of the Neolithic, and geochemical sourcing (the long-running studies of Tristan Carter and colleagues) ties nearly all of it to the Cappadocian sources of Göllü Dağ and Nenezi Dağ, some 130–190 km to the northeast — the volcanic country of Hasan Dağı's neighbours. Obsidian arrived as prepared cores and was worked in the houses into pressure-flaked blades, daggers, projectile points, and — polished to a standard no other material of the age could take — the small obsidian mirrors that are among the site's most astonishing objects. The mechanism of supply is debated — direct procurement expeditions by townspeople, down-the-line exchange between communities, or both braided — but the fact of a sustained, centuries-long connection between a Konya-plain town and Cappadocian quarries is not. Obsidian is the clearest material proof that Çatalhöyük was never a closed world. The reader of this hub will meet the same glass, moved by sea to Melos and beyond, in Early Seafaring; the Çatalhöyük case is its overland counterpart, and together they map the exchange web — obsidian, shell from distant coasts, fine stone, timber — within which every "isolated" Neolithic town actually lived.

Textiles, Beads, and the Rest of the Craft Repertoire

The perishable crafts survive at Çatalhöyük by the usual accidents — charring, wrapping the dead, impressions in clay — and they repay attention precisely because they correct the stone-and-bone bias of the record. The textiles are the headline: woven fragments from the 1960s excavations and the Hodder seasons, among the earliest preserved woven fabrics known, were long identified as linen or wool. Re-analysis by scanning electron microscopy (Rast-Eicher, Karg, and Bender Jørgensen, Antiquity 2021) settled the sixty-year argument with a third answer: the fibres are bast from local oak — the inner bark fibre of the same oaks that timbered the roofs — consistent with the near-absence of flax seed in the samples. Tree bast, not imported flax, clothed the town: a whole fibre technology, invisible until someone looked at the threads under the microscope. Beside weaving stand basketry and matting (the wrappings of the burials), cordage, and the wooden vessels and boxes that survive as fragments.

The durable crafts are equally household-scaled. Beadwork in stone, shell, bone, and fired clay — necklaces and bracelets in the burials, bead blanks and drills in the houses — worked local materials and exotics side by side. Ground stone gave querns, axes, and palettes; bone gave awls, spatulas, and belt hooks; pottery, present from early in the sequence in plain forms, grows commoner and finer through the upper levels. A whisper of the next age runs through the late houses: small objects of native copper and of lead occur in the upper levels, worked cold as rare stones were worked — the threshold state of metallurgy this hub traces in First Metals. What the whole repertoire shares is its address. Production debris sits in houses and middens across the town; no workshop quarter, no attached specialist compound, and no central store has been identified in the classic levels. Craft at Çatalhöyük was skilled, sometimes virtuosic — and organised, so far as the evidence reaches, by households.

// THE DIET AND THE PICTURES //

Hold §VI and §VII together and a rule emerges: the animals Çatalhöyük ate (sheep, goat) are almost absent from its art, and the animals its art celebrates (aurochs, deer, leopards, vultures) were marginal or absent from its diet. Subsistence was domestic and daily; symbolism was wild and occasional. Any reading of the town that confuses the two registers — that makes it a town of goddess-worshipping cattle-herders on the strength of its walls — has mixed up the menu with the mythology, and this archive keeps them in separate columns.

VIII. The Equality Question — The Town Without Kings, Weighed

The index of this hub calls Çatalhöyük "the standing proof that early urban life did not require kings." This section argues that claim as carefully as the evidence allows — which means arguing both sides, and then stating what kind of proof, exactly, survives the argument. The question is not whether the town had a king in the later, palace-and-inscription sense: nobody claims it did. The question is whether a community of this scale and density organised itself without institutionalised hierarchy — without a stratum that commanded labour, stored the surplus, monopolised ritual, or buried richer than its neighbours — and what the ground can and cannot tell us about so inward a thing as power.

The Case For — Levelling You Can Excavate

The positive evidence is architectural, funerary, and biological, and it converges. Architecture: houses are strikingly uniform in size and equipment across the excavated levels — main rooms cluster around a common scale, and every house, plain or elaborate, has the same functional kit of oven, platforms, and storage. There is no big house. There is, in the excavated sample, no public or monumental architecture at all: no temple, palace, council house, granary block, or plaza building has been identified in either excavation era, and no central storage facility — each household stored its own grain in its own bins, in quantities proportioned to a household, not a tribute system. Burial: grave goods are modest and unsystematic — a necklace, a tool, a basket, often nothing — and they do not concentrate: the burials beneath the most elaborate history houses are not richer than those beneath plain ones, and no burial in the excavated hundreds stands out as a chief's. Bodies: the bioarchaeology finds men and women eating, on the isotopic evidence, substantially the same diet and carrying comparable workloads; health and nutrition do not sort the population into a fed elite and a labouring mass. Production: craft debris is dispersed through ordinary houses (§VII); obsidian, the prestige material, is everywhere. A society can hide many things from archaeology, but it cannot easily hide a palace, a royal tomb, a state granary, and a tribute economy simultaneously, across eighteen levels and two major excavations. Their joint absence is evidence, not merely silence.

Hodder's formulation of the finding deserves its currency: the middle, "classic" levels describe an aggressively egalitarian community — his term, developed in his 2014 summary of the project's results — in which equality was not a passive default but something actively maintained: by the levelling pressure of the house system itself, by the dispersal of ritual into every household rather than its concentration in a priesthood, by feasting that consumed surplus in public rather than storing it as wealth, and by the sheer friction of life at that density, in which any household's pretensions were lived, visibly, at arm's length from its neighbours' platforms. On this reading the town's famous uniformity is not the absence of politics. It is the politics.

The Case Against — Differentiation, and the Limits of Absence

The counter-case begins inside the project's own findings. History houses (§V) are differentiation, even if they are not stratification: some house-sequences monopolised the town's scarcest symbolic goods — ancestors, burials, paintings, installations, the labour of endless replastering — and a monopoly of memory is a form of rank, however it is dressed. If burial beneath a history house conferred standing, then standing was unequally held, and the equality of goods may have coexisted with an inequality of honour that the artefact record barely registers. Age and sex structure the burials: adults under the platforms, infants by the ovens; whatever equality obtained among household heads, the town sorted its dead by category, as every known society sorts its living. The bodies record violence: the skeletal series includes healed cranial injuries consistent with interpersonal blows — a community with rules, evidently, and with occasions on which they were enforced or broken at close quarters. And the plaster studies cut both ways: a 2024 archaeometric analysis of house plasters argues that the obsessive uniformity of the replastering regime functioned as a "symbolic adhesive" — an egalitarian façade, in its authors' pointed word, maintained over rising household competition. A façade implies something behind it.

Above these particulars stand two general cautions, and a Tier A account must print them in the same size type as the claim. First, negative evidence has a floor. Only a minority of the mound is excavated (§III); perishable wealth — textiles, feathers, stored food, livestock on the hoof — leaves almost nothing; and authority that works through kinship, ritual precedence, or oratory need build nothing at all. The correct statement is not "Çatalhöyük had no hierarchy" but "Çatalhöyük shows none of the material signatures by which hierarchy is recognised at comparable sites" — a strong statement, and a weaker one than the slogan. Second, the town changed. Hodder's own later work is the chief witness against a timeless egalitarian Çatalhöyük: after c. 6500 BCE, on his reading, houses grow larger and fewer, the fabric loosens, households manage their herds and stores with increasing independence, and — his words for the trend — social differentiation, competition, and economic specialisation become more pronounced, even as he maintains that the organisation remained egalitarian in character throughout. The levelling system, in other words, was a historical achievement with a lifespan, strongest in the crowded middle levels and visibly relaxing in the town's last East-mound centuries. The slogan "a town without kings" is truest of c. 6700–6500 BCE, and should be dated when it is used.

The verdict this article prints is therefore a layered one. Established: for well over a thousand years, a community of town-like density ran itself without palaces, temples, central stores, chiefly burials, or any excavated signature of institutionalised rank — the strongest such case the Neolithic world offers, and a permanent refutation of the claim that density requires kingship. Probable: its equality in the classic levels was actively maintained — "aggressively egalitarian" — rather than merely unchallenged. Not established: that the town knew no hierarchy of honour, age, or ritual precedence; the history houses argue otherwise, and the limits of negative evidence forbid the stronger claim. The comparison that fixes the scale of the achievement is forward in time: when this hub reaches the Ubaid and Uruk world, the temple household, the ration list, and the monumental precinct appear in the record unmistakably, in mudbrick that preserves exactly as Çatalhöyük's does. The spade can see kingship's furniture when it is there. At Çatalhöyük, for a millennium and more, it is not there — and that, stated at exactly that strength, is proof enough for one article.

IX. The West Mound — The Chalcolithic Shift and the End

The end of the East mound story is a drift, not a catastrophe, and it begins centuries before the last house was closed. From about 6500 BCE, on the East mound itself, the classic system relaxes: population falls back from its middle- level peak, the house fabric loosens into clusters with open ground between, houses grow larger and more complex even as they grow fewer, underfloor burial and the old house-continuity practices weaken, and smaller settlements begin to appear on the plain around the town. Domestic cattle join the herds; hulled barley joins the crops; households, on the isotopic and faunal evidence, manage their animals more independently and move through the landscape more. The "disaggregation", as the literature calls it, is the social fact of §VIII seen from the other side: the aggressively egalitarian, crowd-dense order of the classic levels giving way, over four or five centuries, to a looser, more autonomous, more mobile world.

The West mound is that world's local address. Occupation began there, across the Çarşamba channel, while the East mound was still inhabited: the thirty-three new AMS dates published by Orton and colleagues (2018) demonstrate the overlap conclusively, and recast the two mounds as one settlement whose focus shifted gradually, probably over one or two centuries around 6000 BCE — not a desertion and refoundation, and not the centuries-long hiatus that older literature sometimes inserted between them. The West mound community, Early Chalcolithic in the regional periodisation, differs from its parent in ways that are individually small and jointly decisive. Its houses are larger — some probably two-storeyed — and stand as architecture in a way the honeycomb never did. Its pottery is painted, in red-brown on pale slips, in quantity: the decorated serving vessel, made to be seen at a meal, replaces the plain ware of the East. And the classic symbolic system is gone from the excavated record: no underfloor burials, no wall paintings, no bucrania have been found on the West mound. The dead leave the house; the bull leaves the wall; the feast, to judge by the painted tableware, moves to the table. Diet and subsistence, by contrast, show strong continuity — the same crops, the same herds, less cattle eaten — a reminder that what changed was the social and symbolic order, not the farming.

The West mound occupation itself ends around 5600–5500 BCE, and with it Çatalhöyük: not destroyed — no destruction layer, no massacre, no sudden abandonment horizon has been found on either mound — but dispersed, into the smaller farming settlements that by then dotted the Konya plain and the wider region. The causes argued in the literature are, in descending order of this article's confidence: the long internal trend itself, fifteen centuries of crowding unwinding into household autonomy and mobility, a process visible from 6500 BCE onward and needing no external trigger; changes in the Çarşamba's channels and the local water and grazing balance, plausible and hard to date finely; and the rapid climate event of c. 6200 BCE (the "8.2 kiloyear event"), once routinely invoked for the East–West shift and now treated with caution, since the shift demonstrably began before it and proceeded gradually through it. The honest summary is the one the dates enforce: Çatalhöyük was not killed. It thinned, loosened, moved across its river, changed its pots and its burial customs, and finally dissolved into a landscape of villages — several of which, in the longer run of Anatolian prehistory, were its heirs.

// WHY THE END MATTERS //

The gradualness of the end is itself evidence for §VIII. States fall; Çatalhöyük faded. A community held together by palaces and garrisons leaves a collapse horizon when the centre fails. A community held together by households, neighbourhoods, and habit can simply — over twenty generations — stop reassembling itself in one place, and that is what the two mounds record. The manner of the town's ending is the last exhibit in the case for how it was governed while it lived.

X. What Çatalhöyük Does and Does Not Show

What Çatalhöyük shows, at full Tier A strength, is a complete social experiment preserved in mudbrick. A farming community founded around 7100 BCE on a river fan of the Konya plain grew into one of the largest and densest settlements of the Neolithic world and sustained itself, on the East mound alone, for some fifteen centuries. It built a town with no streets and lived on its roofs; it organised production, storage, craft, and ritual through households and neighbourhoods; it buried some eight hundred of its excavated dead beneath the floors of its houses and rebuilt those houses, generation after generation, on the same walls; it painted hunts and vultures, set the horns of wild bulls over its sleeping platforms, wove cloth from the bast of oaks, cut its tools from Cappadocian obsidian carried 150 kilometres and more, and left — across eighteen levels and two major excavations — no palace, no temple, no central storehouse, and no chief's grave. As a demonstration that early town life could be organised without kings, it stands.

What it does not show must be entered in the same hand. It does not show a mother-goddess religion or a matriarchy: those are Mellaart's constructions, retired by the counting of the figurines and the re-excavation of the "shrines", and they survive in this article only as historiography. It does not show the world's first map: the town-and-volcano panel is a unique and unresolved image, its leopard-skin rival reading unrefuted, its volcano merely possible. It does not show a population figure: 3,500–8,000 and 600–800 are both in print, from defensible methods, and the spread between them is the present state of knowledge. It does not show a utopia: its bones record workload, disease, and healed head wounds; its history houses record an inequality of honour that coexisted with its equality of goods; and its last centuries record the levelling order itself relaxing, differentiating, and finally dispersing. Nor does it show a beginning: farming, herding, sedentism, and even dense agglutinative building all predate it in Anatolia and the Levant, at Aşıklı, at Boncuklu, at Jericho. Çatalhöyük's distinction is not that it was first. It is that it was most — the largest, longest, densest, and now the most finely excavated instance of the Neolithic town before cities.

Perhaps that is why the site will not stay in the past. Mellaart made it a goddess town; the Goddess movement made it a pilgrimage; the textbooks made it the first city; this hub's index makes it a proof about kings. Each age reads its own question into the honeycomb, and the honeycomb — plastered, repainted, rebuilt — absorbs the reading and outlasts it. The archive's duty is narrower and, in the end, more respectful: to keep the excavated town and the projected ones apart, to print the equality claim at the strength the houses, burials, and bodies actually support, and to leave the roofs, where the life of the place was actually lived, honestly unexcavated in the imagination. Thousands of people, or hundreds — the arithmetic is still open — climbed down ladders into white rooms, ate bread from their own bins, slept above their own dead, and needed no one above them to make a town. Attested, dated, and debated in the open literature, that is what the ground at Çatalhöyük says. It is enough.
