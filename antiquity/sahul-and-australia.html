This is a Tier A article: everything in it rests on excavated, dated, and published archaeological, palaeoecological, and genetic evidence, and where scholars disagree — as they do, sharply, about the age of Madjedbebe and the cause of the megafaunal extinctions — the disagreement itself is reported with its positions attributed. No claim here depends on ancient legend or modern construction, and none is allowed to borrow strength from either. One date in this article, the c. 65,000 BP occupation of Madjedbebe, is presented throughout as what it is in the literature: a published claim with published critics and published replies, not a settled fact.

Sahul is the name archaeologists give to the Pleistocene continent formed, at low sea level, by Australia, New Guinea, and Tasmania joined in a single landmass. It was never joined to Asia. Between Sahul and the Asian mainland shelf of Sunda lay Wallacea, an archipelago of deep-water islands separated by straits that no fall in sea level ever closed. Whoever first reached Sahul therefore crossed open sea — repeatedly, over legs measured in tens of kilometres — and did so earlier than any other securely evidenced sea crossing anywhere in the world. That single geographical fact makes the peopling of Sahul a founding event in the history of seafaring, and its date a founding question in the history of our species.

What followed the crossing was not a visit but an occupation without interruption. Within a few thousand years of the earliest dates, people were living in the highlands of New Guinea, on the continent's southwestern and southeastern coasts, in its arid interior, and across the cold plain that is now Bass Strait, in Tasmania. They ground seed and ochre, hafted edge-ground axes, painted rock walls, cremated and ochre-buried their dead in ceremonies among the earliest ritual burials known anywhere, and lived alongside — and, on one contested body of evidence, preyed upon — the giant marsupials and birds of the Pleistocene continent. This article walks that record in order: the continent, the crossing, the contested first date, the spread, the art, the burials, the extinctions, and the fire — and it closes where the archaeology itself closes, with continuity: the deep past documented here is the deep past of the world's oldest continuous living culture, and it is written about in that knowledge throughout.

I. A Continent Joined — Sahul, Sunda, and the Sea Between

Begin with the map as it was, not as it is. Through most of the last hundred thousand years, global sea level stood far below its present stand — by roughly 120 metres or more at the Last Glacial Maximum, and by several tens of metres at the time of the first crossing, around 65,000–50,000 years ago. Land now drowned stood dry. In the west, the Malay Peninsula ran unbroken into Sumatra, Java, and Borneo: that exposed shelf continent is Sunda, and a traveller from mainland Asia could walk its whole length. In the east and south, Australia, New Guinea, and Tasmania stood as one: New Guinea joined to the Australian mainland across the plain now drowned by the Torres Strait and the Gulf of Carpentaria — where a great enclosed lake, Lake Carpentaria, occupied the gulf's basin at low stands — and Tasmania joined to Victoria across the broad, cold Bassian Plain now beneath Bass Strait. That joined continent is Sahul, roughly a third again as large as modern Australia alone, much of the addition being the now-submerged shelves off its northern and northwestern coasts — the first country the voyagers landed on, and country no archaeologist can excavate, a loss this hub treats in Drowned Landscapes.

// THE CROSSING //
Always by sea

Even at the lowest sea levels, reaching Sahul required multiple open-water legs, the longest modelled at c. 90–100 km.

Between Sunda and Sahul lay the islands that belong to neither: Wallacea, named for Alfred Russel Wallace, whose line — running between Bali and Lombok and between Borneo and Sulawesi — marks the great faunal boundary between the animals of Asia and the animals of Australasia. Wallacea's islands are separated from each other and from both shelves by straits cut through deep water. That depth is the decisive fact of this entire article. The shelves of Sunda and Sahul are shallow, and a fall of a hundred metres joins them to their continents; the Wallacean straits are hundreds of metres deep, and no Pleistocene sea-level fall — not even the maximum of the last glaciation — ever drained them. There was never a land bridge to Sahul. Not at 65,000 years ago, not at 20,000, not ever, in the whole span of human existence. The first Australians did not walk in, and they did not drift in on a shoreline that later sank beneath them as a causeway. They crossed water.

How much water is a question of route and of date, because the legs shorten as sea level falls and the exposed shelves widen. The modelling literature (§V) converges on the shape of the answer even where it argues about the route: a voyage to Sahul meant a chain of crossings through Wallacea — a series of island hops, most of them in the range of roughly 20–30 km, with at least one long final leg to the Sahul shore. For the southern route, from Timor or Roti to the then-emergent islands of the Sahul Banks off the northwest coast, that final leg is modelled at about 87–100 km even with the shelf exposed. For the northern route, through Sulawesi and the northern Moluccas to the Bird's Head of New Guinea, the individual legs are generally shorter but more numerous. Either way, the minimum statement is the one the geography forces: at the lowest sea levels of the age, the shortest single crossings between Wallacean islands and the Sahul coast ran to several tens of kilometres of open sea, and the full passage required making such a crossing not once but repeatedly, island after island, and then doing it with enough people, arriving alive together, to found a population. A single castaway does not colonise a continent. The genetics and the demographic modelling (§V) both say the founding was plural.

Two consequences follow, and they organise everything after this section. First, the peopling of Sahul is the earliest well-evidenced episode of deliberate open-water voyaging in the world — earlier, on any dating, than the settlement of the Mediterranean islands or the crossing to the Americas' offshore worlds — and the hub treats its technology and seamanship in the companion article Early Seafaring, to which this article defers on watercraft and defers gladly: no Pleistocene boat from Wallacea survives, and what can be said rests on the modelling and the geography, not on a hull. Second, because the landing coasts themselves are now under the sea, the archaeological record of the occupation begins inland, at the oldest surviving sites on the raised ground — and the oldest and most argued-over of those sites is a sandstone shelter on the western edge of the Arnhem Land plateau, in Mirarr Country in what is now the Northern Territory: Madjedbebe.

II. Madjedbebe — The Shelter on the Arnhem Land Escarpment

Madjedbebe — spelled Malakunanja II in the earlier literature, a name the reader will meet in every paper before 2017 — is a rock shelter at the foot of the Arnhem Land escarpment, at approximately 12°30′ S, 132°53′ E, some twenty metres above sea level, near the modern town of Jabiru and the edge of Kakadu National Park. The shelter sits in Mirarr Country, and the modern excavations were conducted in formal partnership with the Gundjeihmi Aboriginal Corporation, the Mirarr traditional owners exercising, in the excavators' own reporting, significant operational control over the research on their land. That partnership is not a courtesy detail. It is part of the site's modern history, and it stands in deliberate contrast to the older history of Australian archaeology — including the removal of the Mungo ancestors told in §IX — in which excavation proceeded without the consent of the people whose past was being excavated.

The shelter's deposits are sands: a basal unit of culturally sterile orange sand, overlain by the occupation deposits — a pink sand unit carrying the lowest artefacts, grading upward into brown sands and, at the top, a Holocene midden rich in shell and bone. The site was excavated first in 1973, and then in 1989 by a team associated with Rhys Jones, whose excavation recovered more than 1,500 stone artefacts from the lowest levels, together with silcrete flakes, ground ochre, and a grindstone. The sediments around those lowest artefacts were dated by thermoluminescence to around 60,000–50,000 years ago — but with total uncertainties of 16,000–20,000 years, and the claim that Madjedbebe was Australia's oldest site, at perhaps 60,000 years, was contested from the moment it was made: critics questioned whether the artefacts had stayed where they were dropped, in loose sand subject to disturbance, and whether they truly belonged with the dated grains around them. Two of the 1989 sediment samples were later re-dated by the then-new single-grain OSL method, and those ages supported the early chronology — but the argument did not close. It waited, for more than twenty years, on a better excavation.

The better excavation came in 2012 and 2015, when a team led by Chris Clarkson of the University of Queensland — with dating specialists Zenobia Jacobs and Richard Roberts, lithics specialist Richard Fullagar, and a large interdisciplinary team — opened twenty 1 × 1 m squares adjacent to and enclosing the earlier trenches, digging to a maximum depth of 3.4 m. The scale of recording is the first thing to grasp, because the whole later debate turns on it: the three-dimensional coordinates of approximately 11,000 artefacts and other features — hearths, burials, pits — were recorded individually, and samples were collected throughout for dating, geoarchaeology, and plant-remains analysis. The artefacts were not spread evenly through the sand. They were concentrated in three dense bands, separated by sparser deposits, and each band carried its own raw-material and technological character — the stratigraphic pattern of repeated occupation episodes, not of a churned deposit in which everything has slid together. The lowest dense band, designated phase 2, lay at 2.60–2.15 m depth and contained an in situ hearth. It is the contents of that band, and the age of the sand that holds it, on which the claim of §III stands.

The Lowest Assemblage — What Was in the Sand

The phase 2 assemblage is made mostly from quartzite, silcrete, mudstone, and dolerite, and its inventory matters as much as its age, because it is not the toolkit of a shipwrecked handful but a full occupation assemblage. It includes flakes and cores of several forms, among them faceted discoidal cores and thinning flakes; whole and fragmentary edge-ground hatchets — axes whose cutting edge had been shaped by grinding, a technology then unknown anywhere in the world at anything like this age; grinding stones, including stones used for grinding seed; ground ochre, including a ground ochre "crayon" and pieces of ground yellow ochre found with fragments of sheet mica wrapped around them — mica being a reflective additive, ground ochre mixed or accompanied by a substance whose only evident purpose is to make the pigment glitter; and residue and macrofossil evidence for the use of fuel wood and a range of plant foods — seeds, tubers, and Pandanus nuts — from the eucalyptus and monsoon vine-thicket country around the shelter. Later specialist study of the grinding stones (Hayes and colleagues, 2022) traced grinding-stone use at the site continuously through the sequence, and archaeobotanical work (Florin and colleagues, 2021) documented changing plant-food use across the full span the excavators claim for the site.

Read at face value, then, the lowest band at Madjedbebe contains, together: the world's oldest known edge-ground axe technology; seed-grinding; and pigment prepared with a reflective additive — grinding, hafting, and ochre, the three threads this article follows through Warratyi (§VI), the painted Kimberley (§VIII), and the ochre burials of Lake Mungo (§IX) — present, together, in the first occupation layer of the continent's most discussed site. Everything depends, therefore, on the age of the sand. The next section gives the dating programme the space it needs, because at Madjedbebe the method is the argument.

// WHY THIS SHELTER, OF ALL SHELTERS //

Madjedbebe's claim does not rest on its being uniquely old in kind — other northern sites, Nauwalabila among them, have carried early claims of their own — but on the conjunction of depth, density, and dating effort: a thick sand deposit at the escarpment foot, occupied repeatedly, in which the lowest artefact band could be excavated afresh, piece-plotted artefact by artefact, and dated by dozens of OSL samples through the profile. Sites that might be older exist on the drowned shelves and cannot be dug. Madjedbebe is the oldest claim that can be tested — which is precisely why it, and not a quieter site, carries the argument.

III. Dating Madjedbebe — OSL as Procedure, and the 65,000-Year Claim

Radiocarbon cannot reach Madjedbebe's lowest levels: beyond roughly 50,000 years, too little carbon-14 survives in any sample for the method to return a finite age, and the basal deposits in any case hold little dateable charcoal. The 2017 chronology therefore rests on optically stimulated luminescence — OSL — and the method must be understood as a procedure, not invoked as a label, because every later criticism of the site is a criticism of what OSL does and does not date.

OSL, Step by Step

Quartz sand grains are natural dosimeters. Ionising radiation from the surrounding sediment — from the decay of uranium, thorium, and potassium in the deposit, and from cosmic rays — steadily knocks electrons in the quartz crystal into trapped states, where they accumulate for as long as the grain lies buried in darkness. Sunlight empties the traps: a grain exposed to daylight on a surface is, in the jargon, bleached — its clock reset to zero. The procedure follows from the physics. Sediment samples are collected in the field in opaque tubes, so that no light reaches them after collection. In the laboratory, under controlled low light, individual quartz grains are stimulated with light and the luminescence they emit is measured: the brightness records the burial dose, the total radiation the grain absorbed since it was last bleached — that is, since it was last on a sunlit surface and was then buried. Separately, the dose rate of the surrounding sediment is measured — how much radiation the deposit delivers per thousand years. Age is the quotient: burial dose divided by dose rate gives the time since the grain was last exposed to sunlight. At Madjedbebe, the team dated the sediments by single-grain OSL: thousands of grains measured one by one, so that grains whose doses betray disturbance, incomplete bleaching, or intrusion can be identified statistically rather than averaged invisibly into a bulk age — the refinement that had already, in its early form, supported the 1989 chronology, and that the 2017 programme applied at a scale of dozens of samples through the profile, with ages combined in a Bayesian stratigraphic model that uses the known order of the layers to constrain the age of each.

What OSL dates, stated exactly, is the sand: the last sunlight exposure of the grains among which the artefacts lie. It does not date the artefacts themselves. The inference from sediment age to occupation age therefore carries a load-bearing assumption — that the artefacts and the dated grains entered the deposit together and have stayed together. If artefacts sank, were trampled down, or were carried down by burrowing creatures into older sand, OSL would faithfully return the age of the older sand and the association would be false. The Clarkson team's paper is best read as two arguments bolted together: a dating argument, that the phase 2 sands were last bleached around 65,000 years ago; and an integrity argument, that the phase 2 artefacts belong in those sands. The first argument has attracted little direct attack. The second is where the fight of §IV takes place.

The Integrity Argument — Refits, Hearths, and Trampling

The integrity argument, as published, has three main timbers. First, refitting: broken silcrete artefacts whose pieces could be fitted back together were found within the dense bands — fourteen refits in the lower band and three in the middle band — and none between bands; the median vertical distance between refitted pieces was 10.6 cm. If the deposit had been churned over metres, conjoining pieces would be scattered across bands; they are not. Second, patterning: there is no size-sorting of artefacts with depth — small pieces are not systematically deeper than large ones, as gravity-driven sinking through loose sand would tend to produce — burnt artefacts concentrate in the dense bands rather than between them, and an intact hearth survived in phase 2, a feature that wholesale disturbance would be expected to disperse. Third, experiment: the team ran modern trampling experiments at the site, walking over artefacts laid on the surface, and measured how far trampling actually moves pieces in this sand — movement of the order of centimetres to about ten centimetres, consistent with the refit distances and far short of the distances required to carry a younger assemblage down into 65,000-year-old sand. On these three timbers the team concluded that post-depositional vertical movement at Madjedbebe is restricted to roughly ten centimetres, that the three dense bands are genuine occupation horizons, and that the phase 2 band lies in sand dated to about 65,000 years ago.

The published conclusion, in the paper's own abstract (Clarkson et al., Nature 547 (2017): 306–310), is that human occupation at Madjedbebe "began around 65,000 years ago," with the distinctive assemblage of §II, and that the evidence sets a new minimum age for the arrival of humans in Australia and for the dispersal of modern humans out of Africa. The claim landed with corresponding force. It pushed the peopling of Sahul some fifteen thousand years earlier than the previously secure floor of about 50,000 years; it placed people in Australia while Neanderthals and Denisovans still lived across Asia (§VII); it lengthened the overlap between people and megafauna (§X); and it implied a dispersal out of Africa early enough to sit awkwardly with the then-standard genetic chronologies of that dispersal. A claim that rearranges four debates at once invites examination in all four. The examination arrived the following year, and §IV gives it in full — because a Tier A article does not protect its most interesting claim from its critics.

IV. The Challenge — O'Connell et al. 2018, and the Replies

The critique appeared in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences in August 2018: James O'Connell, Jim Allen, and seven colleagues, "When did Homo sapiens first reach Southeast Asia and Sahul?" (PNAS 115 (2018): 8482–8490). Its frame is the whole arc from South China to Sahul, and its conclusion about Madjedbebe is stated without softening: reviewing the regional evidence, the authors find that an age estimate of more than 50,000 years for the site "is unlikely to be valid," and that the data offered in support of a pre-55,000-year dispersal of modern humans through the region are not compelling. The paper's positive position is that anatomically modern humans spread from Africa in the main at about 50,000–55,000 years ago, and that Sahul was reached within that movement — the chronology the genetic evidence, on their reading, independently favours.

The Site-Formation Argument

The heart of the critique is not the OSL physics but the sand. Madjedbebe sits in a regionally extensive sand sheet in a tropical environment of pronounced wet and dry seasons, and O'Connell and colleagues catalogue the post-depositional processes such substrates undergo: rain-splash erosion, subsurface washing (eluviation), creep — and, most notably, bioturbation by termites. Termites move sediment on a vast scale in northern Australia; their abandoned galleries collapse and vanish, leaving little visible trace in a sand profile, while the processes as a whole, on the extensive geomorphological literature the authors cite, facilitate the downward movement of stones — including artefacts — into deeper, older deposits. An artefact that has sunk a metre through bioturbated sand will sit among grains OSL dates to 65,000 years, and no measurement of the grains can expose the substitution. The authors press the precedent of Nauwalabila, another Arnhem Land sand site where early claims beyond 50,000 years were abandoned once the effects of such disturbance were demonstrated — a site, they argue, whose fate Madjedbebe's may repeat. They further dispute particulars: the purported occupation "pit" in phase 2 is read as a dripline gutter worn by water falling from the shelter's edge; and they note what they present as an inconsistency in the excavators' handling of the deposits below phase 2 — the phase 1 artefacts, which the Clarkson team itself attributed to post-depositional settling from above. If settling explains the artefacts beneath the claimed horizon, the critics ask, why may it not explain the horizon itself?

The Replies

The excavators' defence, in their responses and in the team's subsequent work, returns to the three timbers of §III and adds a fourth: specificity. Refits do not cross bands; there is no size-sorting; hearths survive intact; trampling experiments bound the movement that feet can cause. Against the termite mechanism specifically, the reply is that a mechanism possible in tropical sands generally must still be shown at this site, in this profile — and that the Madjedbebe profile lacks the diagnostic products the mechanism leaves where it has operated at scale. That point was later examined independently: a geoarchaeological assessment of how termite stone lines can be distinguished from genuine artefact horizons (Ward and colleagues), taking Madjedbebe and Nauwalabila as its case studies, concluded that neither site contains a termite stone line — while cautioning that both sites have complex formation histories and that bioturbation must be treated as part of every tropical site's sedimentary history, not waved away. The team's positive programme continued in parallel: residue and use-wear studies of the Madjedbebe grinding stones reported continuous grinding-stone use across the claimed 65,000-year span (Hayes et al. 2022), and the archaeobotanical sequence was published as a record of plant-food use running from the earliest levels (Florin et al. 2021) — work that presupposes the chronology and so cannot independently prove it, but that deepens the assemblage the chronology carries. Later syntheses by participants on both sides (Allen and O'Connell's subsequent reviews among them) have continued to argue the matter at the level of the whole regional dataset — how many sites, dated how well, must exceed 50,000 years before the floor itself moves.

// THE VERDICT THIS ARTICLE PRINTS //

This archive does not declare a winner, because the literature has not. The position stated here is the one the evidence, as published, supports: Madjedbebe's c. 65,000 BP occupation is the earliest widely discussed date for the peopling of Sahul, advanced by a large, well-published dating and integrity programme, and contested at its edges — the contest turning not on the OSL ages of the sand, which few dispute, but on whether the lowest artefacts belong with the sand that dates them. The uncontested floor, accepted by the critics themselves, is an occupation of northern Australia by about 50,000 years ago. Between that floor and the 65,000-year claim lies fifteen thousand years of live scientific argument, reported here as argument — with the excavators' tests, the critics' mechanisms, and the independent geoarchaeological assessment all on the record. Readers who meet the 65,000-year figure elsewhere stated as simple fact, or dismissed as simple error, are meeting advocacy, not the literature.

V. The Crossing — Routes Through Wallacea

However the Madjedbebe argument settles, the crossing itself is not in doubt — only its date within a range and its route within a pair. The framework was set in 1977 by Joseph Birdsell, who mapped the possible passages from Sunda to Sahul as two families of routes. The northern route runs from Borneo across to Sulawesi, then east through the northern Moluccas — the islands of the Sula and Banggai chains, Buru, Seram and their neighbours in the various reconstructions — to a landfall on the western end of New Guinea, around the Bird's Head and the island of Misool. The southern route runs along the Lesser Sunda chain — Bali, Lombok, Flores, Alor — to Timor or Roti, and then across the long final leg to the exposed Sahul shelf off what is now the Kimberley coast of northwestern Australia. Every later model is a refinement, a re-costing, or a re-dating of Birdsell's pair. The debate is not whether people island-hopped through Wallacea; it is which chain they hopped, and the two chains make different predictions — a northern entry peopling New Guinea first and Australia from the north-east, a southern entry landing directly on the Australian northwest — that the distribution and dating of the earliest sites might, in principle, decide.

Least-Cost Modelling — Kealy, Louys, and O'Connor

The most cited modern treatment is the least-cost pathway modelling of Shimona Kealy, Julien Louys, and Sue O'Connor (Journal of Human Evolution 125 (2018): 59–70). Extending earlier out-of-Africa least-cost models through Wallacea, the authors digitised Birdsell's routes and costed movement across reconstructed landscapes at 65,000 and 70,000 years ago — costing slope, visibility between islands, access to fresh water, and the difficulty of each sea leg. Their result was emphatic: the models recover overwhelming support for the northern route, with a landing on present-day Misool Island at the western end of New Guinea, and only minimal support for a southern crossing — recovered in the 70,000-year models as a possible passage from eastern Timor. Two riders in the paper deserve the emphasis the authors gave them. The northern Wallacean islands crossed by the favoured route are, archaeologically, among the least explored in the region: the model points at a map gap, not at a confirmed chain of camps. And the better-studied southern islands, Timor included, have produced no occupation dates comparable in age to the earliest dates claimed for Sunda and Sahul — an absence that cuts against the southern route as the first passage, though absence on islands whose Pleistocene coasts are drowned is, as always in this region, weaker evidence than it looks.

Voyage Modelling — Timor, Drift, and Intent

The southern route has its own modelling defence. Bird and colleagues (Quaternary Science Reviews 191 (2018): 431–439), working from new bathymetry and drift modelling, showed that from about 70,000 to 10,000 years ago a chain of habitable, resource-rich islands stood emergent off the northwest Sahul coast — now mostly submerged — visible from high points near the Timor–Roti coast and as close as about 87 km. Accidental drift arrival from Timor–Roti was improbable at any period, they found; but purposeful voyages in the summer monsoon were very likely to succeed, over four to seven days. The demographic evidence points the same way: genomic estimates of the founding population — put by the modelling literature at figures of the order of a thousand or more founders for long-term viability, and in the Timor study's citation at more than 72–100 individuals at minimum — are taken to indicate deliberate, repeated colonisation rather than a single accidental rafting. Bird, Bradshaw, and colleagues (Scientific Reports 9 (2019): 8220), combining coastal viewshed analysis — which islands can be seen from which, building on the intervisibility work associated with Kasih Norman and colleagues on both route families — with ocean drift modelling and population projection, put a number on the accident hypothesis: the probability of reaching Sahul by random drift is below five per cent unless at least forty adults are washed off an island at least once every twenty years, whereas choosing a departure time and making even minimal headway — half a knot — toward a destination transforms the odds. Their conclusion, that the settlement of Sahul was not an accident and that its people could plan and make open-sea voyages lasting several days by at least 50,000 years ago, is now the modelling mainstream — with arrival via New Guinea judged more likely than via northwestern Australia in their runs, and later continental-scale simulations (Bradshaw and colleagues, 2021) finding the southern entry through Timor potentially dominant in the peopling of the continent's interior. The models, in short, agree on intent and disagree on the door.

What kind of craft made the crossings, this article does not speculate beyond the literature: no Wallacean watercraft survives from the Pleistocene, and the candidates canvassed in the seafaring literature — bamboo rafts, bark and bundled-reed craft, dugouts — are assessed in Early Seafaring. Two fixed points may be stated here. The crossings were made by people whose descendants, within the span of the earliest sites, were grinding seed, hafting axes, and working ochre (§II): the voyagers were not a marginal population but a fully equipped one. And the route passed the painted caves of Sulawesi — whose art, dated to at least 51,200 years for a narrative scene at Leang Karampuang, is treated in this hub's Ochre and Symbol and is not re-treated here: it is enough to register that the corridor to Sahul was itself inhabited and painted by the people, or the close kin of the people, who made the crossing. Sahul was not reached from an artless world. It was reached from a world already making images — which is the world §VIII finds waiting, in paint, on the far shore.

VI. The Spread — Interior, South Coast, and the Plain to Tasmania

A landing is not a continent. The second fact of Sahul's occupation — after the crossing itself — is the speed and completeness with which the whole landmass was taken up. By about 50,000 years ago, on dates accepted across the Madjedbebe argument by both sides, occupation is attested in the highlands of New Guinea, where the Ivane Valley sites (Kosipe and its neighbours) place people in mountain country at roughly 49,000–44,000 years ago; on the far southwestern coast, at Boodie Cave on Barrow Island and at Devil's Lair near the Leeuwin coast, both carrying occupation around 50,000–48,000 years ago; in the southeast, where the Willandra Lakes record (§IX) begins around 50,000–46,000 years ago; and across the north, where Madjedbebe's contested claim stands at the head of a cluster of Arnhem Land and Kimberley sites. Continental-scale modelling of the spread (Bradshaw and colleagues, 2021) finds the peopling of every corner of Sahul achievable within a few thousand years of entry — an establishment rate of the order of a kilometre a year — and consistent with entry at either door, or both. The pattern the dates describe is not a slow seep from a beachhead. It is the rapid occupation, by people who already knew how to live from coasts, rivers, and ranges, of a continent whose every environment was new and none of whose animals had ever seen a human being.

The Arid Interior — Warratyi, c. 49,000 BP

The interior was long assumed to be the last country taken up — the desert heart imagined as a barrier crossed late, when climate allowed. Warratyi rock shelter, in the northern Flinders Ranges of South Australia, in Adnyamathanha Country some 550 km north of modern Adelaide and 200 km inland, overturned the assumption. Excavated after its chance discovery in 2011 by Adnyamathanha elder Clifford Coulthard, working with archaeologist Giles Hamm, the shelter yielded more than 4,300 artefacts and a bone assemblage spanning occupation from about 49,000 to 10,000 years ago (Hamm et al., Nature 539 (2016): 280–283). The basal occupation, at around 49,000 years ago, stands some ten thousand years earlier than the previously reported settlement of the arid zone. Within the shelter, stratified and reliably dated, lay the only such association in Australia: artefacts more than 46,000 years old alongside the remains of extinct megafauna, including the giant marsupial Diprotodon optatum — animals that cannot have climbed into the elevated shelter unaided, and whose presence there is evidence of human transport, of people hunting or scavenging the giants and carrying the parts home. And the shelter's cultural sequence runs ahead of the continent's supposed timetable at every level: the earliest-known use of ochre in Australia and Southeast Asia at or before 49,000–46,000 years ago; gypsum pigment at 40,000–33,000; bone tools at 40,000–38,000; hafted tools at 38,000–35,000; backed artefacts at 30,000–24,000 — each, in the excavators' assessment, up to ten thousand years older than any previously known occurrence. The interior, Warratyi says, was not a late refuge. It was settled within a few thousand years of the landing, by people innovating in it from the start.

Tasmania — Across the Bassian Plain

Tasmania required no second crossing. At low sea level its northern coast was simply the far side of the Bassian Plain — a broad, cold, grassy lowland, threaded by rivers, across which people walked south from what is now Victoria. The Tasmanian record, from the caves and shelters of the southwest and the central highlands, is among the richest Pleistocene records in Sahul: occupation at Warreen Cave in the Maxwell River valley from about 34,000–35,000 years ago; occupation of the Jordan River valley in the southeast from around 40,000 years ago on preliminary dating; and at Kutikina Cave on the Franklin River, deposits of about 19,000–20,000 years ago that demonstrated — against an older assumption of late settlement — that people were living in the Tasmanian highlands through the cold of the Last Glacial Maximum itself, hunting wallabies in a periglacial landscape, the southernmost human population on Earth at that time. Then the sea came back. Between roughly 14,000 and 12,000 years ago the rising ocean drowned the Bassian Plain and cut Tasmania off as an island, and its people — the ancestors of the Palawa — remained isolated from the mainland for some eight thousand years, until European vessels arrived in the eighteenth century. The Bassian Plain is the gentlest of this article's drowned landscapes, and the most poignant: a homeland remembered in the geography of a strait, its sites and pathways now under Bass Strait (see Drowned Landscapes).

NEW GUINEA HIGHLANDS

Ivane Valley occupation c. 49,000–44,000 BP: people in high mountain country within millennia of the earliest continental dates.

THE FAR SOUTHWEST

Boodie Cave (Barrow Island) and Devil's Lair, c. 50,000–48,000 BP: the continent's opposite corner occupied at the same horizon as the north.

THE ARID INTERIOR

Warratyi, c. 49,000 BP: settlement of the dry interior ten millennia earlier than previously reported, with megafauna in the shelter and ochre from the base.

TASMANIA

Occupation across the Bassian Plain by c. 40,000–35,000 BP; glacial-maximum highland living at Kutikina; isolation after the plain drowned, c. 14,000–12,000 BP.

VII. An Older Inheritance — Denisovan Ancestry in Sahul's Peoples

One passage, stated carefully, belongs to the genetics. The peoples of Sahul carry, alongside the Neanderthal ancestry shared by all populations outside Africa, the highest proportions of Denisovan ancestry known in any living populations: on the order of three to five per cent of the genomes of Aboriginal Australians and Papuans, in the published estimates, derives from Denisovans — the archaic population known principally from DNA recovered at Denisova Cave in Siberia and from a mandible on the Tibetan Plateau. The Denisovan ancestry in Sahul's peoples comes substantially from a lineage deeply divergent from the Siberian genome itself, and genomic analyses resolve more than one pulse of Denisovan admixture in the ancestry of Papuan populations. Where the admixture happened is not directly observed; the distribution of the ancestry — highest east of Wallace's Line, in the peoples of New Guinea, Australia, and Island Southeast Asia — places the encounters in Southeast Asia or Wallacea, in the course of the dispersal that ended at Sahul, and its presence in both Papuan and Aboriginal Australian genomes shows that it preceded the separation of those populations from one another. The ancestors of the first Sahul peoples thus met, and had children with, an archaic people whom archaeology has barely found — a meeting written in living genomes and nowhere, yet, in a Wallacean fossil. The Denisovans themselves, and the whole archaic company of the dispersal, are treated in this hub's Neanderthals and Denisovans; this article records only what their inheritance in Sahul establishes: that the crossing of §V was made by a population already carrying, in its blood, the memory of another kind of human.

VIII. Painted Country — Arnhem Land, the Kimberley, and the Gwion Problem

Australia holds one of the world's great bodies of rock art, and its two premier provinces face each other across the north: Arnhem Land, the sandstone escarpment country of Madjedbebe itself, and the Kimberley, in the far northwest of Western Australia. Both preserve long, superimposed sequences — wall after wall painted, repainted, and painted over, style succeeding style in a relative chronology built from superimposition, subject matter, and weathering. Both also preserve the central frustration of rock-art science: the pigments are mostly ochre, iron oxide, which contains no carbon and cannot be radiocarbon dated, and the art's antiquity must be established indirectly or not at all. What can be stated at Tier A strength is therefore a scaffold of dated points within a much larger, undated whole — and the scaffold, properly read, is impressive enough.

In Arnhem Land, a fragment of charcoal drawing from the great painted shelter of Nawarla Gabarnmang has been radiocarbon dated to about 28,000 years ago — for a time the oldest securely dated rock art in Australia, and a minimum for an Arnhem Land painting tradition whose dynamic human figures and great superimposed galleries are, on stylistic grounds, argued to run far deeper. The Madjedbebe evidence (§II) shows the raw practice — ground ochre, prepared pigment, a reflective additive — present in the occupation deposits from the earliest claimed horizon: whatever the age of the oldest surviving painting, the making of pigment is as old as the occupation itself. In the Kimberley, the stylistic sequence runs from an earliest phase of large naturalistic animals — the Irregular Infill Animal Period — through the celebrated Gwion figures to the later Wanjina tradition, which continues, repainted and renewed, into the living culture of the Kimberley's traditional owners.

The Gwion Gwion Paintings — and Why They Would Not Date

The Gwion figures — called Bradshaw paintings in the older literature, after Joseph Bradshaw, the pastoralist who reported them to science in 1891, and known as Gwion Gwion, Kiro Kiro, or Kujon in the names of the traditional owner groups — are among the most distinctive images in world rock art: slender human figures in mulberry- coloured pigment, finely painted, in elaborate ceremonial dress, with long headdresses, tassels, and material culture — boomerangs, spears, dilly bags — rendered in detail. For a century they were also among the most argued-about, precisely because they would not date: their pigment carries no carbon, their style sits in the middle of the Kimberley sequence, and estimates of their age swung across tens of thousands of years on stylistic argument alone. An early attempt to date them by OSL on overlying mud wasp nests suggested a minimum age beyond 17,000 years for one figure — a result that stood, unreplicated and much debated, for two decades.

The Wasp-Nest Method — Finch et al. 2020, and the Kangaroo of 2021

The resolution, when it came, came from the wasps. Mud wasps build their nests on the same sheltered rock faces the artists painted, and they build them, in part, of mud flecked with tiny specks of charcoal gathered from the country around. A nest built over a painting must be younger than the painting: radiocarbon date the charcoal in the nest, and the painting is at least that old — a minimum age. A nest built under a painting, later painted over, gives the reverse bound: the painting is younger than the nest — a maximum age. Where nests occur on both sides of one motif, the painting is bracketed. Damien Finch and colleagues applied the method at scale to the Gwion style (Science Advances 6 (2020): eaay3922): twenty- four radiocarbon ages on mud wasp nests overlying or underlying pigment from twenty-one Gwion motifs at fourteen rock shelters. The result redrew the Gwion chronology: while one nest date implies a minimum age of about 17,000 years for a single motif, the great body of the dates supports the Gwion style having been produced in a relatively narrow period around 12,000 years ago — at the very end of the Pleistocene, as the last glaciation released its grip and the seas were rising toward the Kimberley coast. The Gwion fluorescence, on this evidence, belongs not to the first peopling but to the world the first peoples' descendants made under the pressure of a transforming climate — a finding that disciplines a century of speculation in both directions at once.

The same team's second result reached deeper. Turning to the earlier, naturalistic animal phase, Finch and colleagues (Nature Human Behaviour 5 (2021): 310–318) radiocarbon dated twenty-seven mud wasp nests constraining sixteen motifs at eight shelters, and found the naturalistic paintings proliferating between about 17,000 and 13,000 years ago. One painting — a two-metre kangaroo in dark mulberry pigment on the sloping ceiling of a shelter above the Drysdale River, in Balanggarra Country — was bracketed by three nests beneath and three nests above, and dated to between 17,500 and 17,100 years ago, most likely about 17,300: the oldest radiometrically dated in situ rock painting in Australia. State the scaffold exactly, then, as this article leaves it: pigment-making in the occupation deposits from the earliest horizon claimed at Madjedbebe; a dated charcoal drawing at c. 28,000 BP in Arnhem Land; dated naturalistic painting in the Kimberley by c. 17,300 BP; the Gwion fluorescence at c. 12,000 BP; and, beyond the dated points, provinces of painting whose true depth the methods cannot yet reach. The antiquity of Australian rock art beyond 17,300 years is an argument from the ochre in the ground. It is a good argument. It is not a date, and this article does not print it as one.

// MINIMUM, MAXIMUM, BRACKET //

The wasp-nest method dates nests, not paint — the same association logic as OSL at Madjedbebe (§III), in miniature and in reverse. An overlying nest yields only a floor: the painting is older than the nest, by an unknown margin. An underlying nest yields only a ceiling. Only the rare motif with nests on both sides — the Drysdale River kangaroo is the type case — receives a true bracket. Most Kimberley "dates" in the press are floors wearing the clothes of ages; the Finch papers themselves are careful, and this article follows the papers.

IX. Lake Mungo — Cremation, Ochre, and Return

Lake Mungo is a dry lake in the Willandra Lakes region of southwestern New South Wales — a chain of Pleistocene lake basins that filled and dried with the glacial cycles, and whose eastern shores carry great crescent dunes, lunettes, in which the record of the lakeside world is stratified. Human occupation of the Willandra begins, on the 2003 dating programme, around 50,000–46,000 years ago, when the lakes were full and the country around them rich. But Mungo's fame is not its tools. It is its dead — two burials, found in the lunette within a few hundred metres of each other, that stand among the earliest ritual burials known anywhere in the world.

Mungo Lady — The Cremation

In 1968, geologist Jim Bowler found burnt human bone eroding from the Mungo lunette. The remains — designated Lake Mungo I, and known as Mungo Lady — proved on analysis to be those of a young woman who had been cremated: the burning pattern on the bones shows a body first burned, then deliberately smashed, then burned a second time, before the remains were gathered and buried, with ochre. It is, on the consensus chronology below, the earliest known cremation in the world — evidence, at about 40,000 years ago, of a multi-stage funerary rite, conducted in sequence by people who survived her: burning, breaking, burning again, gathering, burying. Nothing about the rite is expedient. Every stage is a choice.

Mungo Man — The Ochre Burial

On 26 February 1974, shifting sand in the same lunette exposed a second burial, Lake Mungo III — Mungo Man. The skeleton, of a man who died at about fifty years of age, his joints arthritic and his teeth heavily worn, had been laid out in ceremony: placed on his back, his knees bent, his hands positioned together over the groin with the fingers interlocked, beside the remains of a fire — and the body sprinkled with red ochre, a pigment whose nearest source lay a long distance from the lake. The posture is arranged, the ochre is imported, and the whole is among the earliest documented examples of such a burial rite anywhere: a community, 40,000 years ago, laying its dead down in a prescribed attitude and anointing the body with coloured earth carried in for the purpose.

The Dating — From 62,000 to a Consensus 40,000

Mungo's chronology had its own long argument, and its resolution is the model of how such arguments should end. Early radiocarbon results placed Mungo Lady far younger than the burials' stratigraphy suggested; a 1999 estimate, by electron spin resonance and uranium-series methods, put Mungo Man at about 62,000 years — a figure that would have made him a second pillar, beside the then-claimed Madjedbebe ages, of a very early peopling. In 2003, Bowler led a multidisciplinary team — geologists, dating specialists from four laboratories, archaeologists — that re-dated the burials and the lunette stratigraphy by multiple methods, OSL foremost (Bowler et al., Nature 421 (2003): 837–841). Their consensus: both burials date to about 40,000 years ago, within about two thousand years either way; human occupation of the lake shore reaches back to 50,000–46,000 years ago; and Mungo Man cannot be older than about 50,000. The 62,000-year estimate was set aside — by evidence, in print, with the discoverer of both burials leading the team that set it aside. This article cites Mungo at c. 40,000 BP for the burials, and notes the episode because it is the standing answer to a fair question about §IV: Australian archaeology's early dates are not protected doctrine. They are tested, and where they fail, as Mungo's 62,000 did, they fall.

The Return — Repatriation, 1992 and 2017

The burials' modern history must be told as plainly as their ancient one. Both sets of remains were removed to Canberra, to the Australian National University, without the consent of the Willandra Lakes traditional owners — the Barkandji (Paakantyi), Mutthi Mutthi, and Ngiyampaa peoples, who regard the Mungo dead as their ancestors — and the distress that removal caused is well documented in the repatriation record. Mungo Lady was returned to Lake Mungo in 1992, into the keeping of the traditional owners. Mungo Man remained in Canberra for a further twenty-five years, through decades of campaigning by the three groups; in 2015 the university relinquished the remains and apologised for the manner of their taking. In November 2017 the remains of Mungo Man, together with those of 104 other ancestors from the Willandra Lakes, were formally handed over and carried home across New South Wales, stopping for smoking ceremonies in the towns where Willandra custodians live, and arrived at Mungo on 17 November 2017 to a public welcome ceremony of the traditional owners. The remains are in the keeping of the traditional owners, and their final resting arrangements rest with them. This article reports the return in those terms — factually, and as the close of the Mungo story that the archaeology is entitled to tell: the ancestors were excavated by science, dated by science, and, at the end of a long insistence by their descendants, brought home from science. Any account of what Mungo Man means now belongs to the Barkandji, Mutthi Mutthi, and Ngiyampaa peoples, and is not this archive's to write.

X. The Giants Depart — Megafauna and the Extinction Debate

The continent the voyagers entered was stocked with giants. Sahul's Pleistocene megafauna — conventionally, animals above about 44 kg — included Diprotodon optatum, a wombat relative the size of a hippopotamus and the largest marsupial that ever lived; the short-faced kangaroo Procoptodon, taller and far heavier than any living roo; the marsupial lion Thylacoleo; giant echidnas and wombats; the great monitor Varanus priscus; and Genyornis newtoni, a flightless bird of about two metres and some 200 kg, a grazing giant of the inland plains. By about 40,000 years ago, essentially all of them were gone. The extinctions fall, on every chronology, within the span of the human occupation — after the crossing, before or around the Last Glacial Maximum — and the question of what killed the giants is the second great open argument of this article, after Madjedbebe. The positions, as published, are these.

The Human Case

The comprehensive review of the evidence by Johnson, Roberts, Brook, and seventeen colleagues (Proceedings of the Royal Society B 283 (2016): 20152399) surveyed the dating, the palaeoecology, and the archaeology, and concluded that multiple independent lines of evidence point to direct human impact as the most likely cause of the extinctions. Modelling by Saltré and colleagues (2016), hindcasting 659 fossil ages against high-resolution climate records, found the extinctions broadly synchronous among genera and independent of aridity and climate variability, estimating disappearance Australia-wide some thirteen thousand years after human arrival, with shorter coexistence in some regions. The Lynch's Crater record (§XI) shows megafaunal abundance — tracked by spores of the dung fungus Sporormiella — stable through earlier climatic upheavals and crashing around 41,000 years ago, in the human span and before the local climate shift. And an offshore core from southwestern Australia (van der Kaars and colleagues, 2017) records regional megafaunal collapse between about 45,000 and 43,100 years ago, within four thousand years of human dispersal, on the authors' reading ruling out climate as the primary cause. Direct evidence of interaction exists but is rare: the Warratyi association (§VI) — megafauna remains carried into a shelter with artefacts — is the type case, and the burnt eggshell of the next paragraphs is the most ambitious claim of all.

The Burnt Eggshells — Miller et al. 2016, and Its Critics

Gifford Miller and colleagues (Nature Communications 7 (2016): 10496) assembled Genyornis eggshell from nearly two thousand localities across the continent and identified, at more than two hundred sites, fragments with diagnostic burn patterns: blackened at one end and not the other, with a steep gradient of amino-acid decomposition across a few centimetres of shell — total decomposition at the burnt end, shading to none at the unburnt end — and clustering in tight groups a few metres across, as if discarded around a hearth. A wildfire, the authors argue, cannot produce such gradients: it heats a landscape broadly and evenly, or not at all. The patterns are consistent, instead, with eggs gathered, cooked in transient human fires, and the shell tossed in and around the fire's edge. Dating by three methods confines the burnt eggshell to a window between about 53,900 and 43,400 years ago, most likely before 47,000 — and burnt emu eggshell, the control case, appears at the same horizon and continues to the present, while Genyornis eggshell itself is never found younger than about 45,000–50,000 years. The proposed mechanism is elegant and modest at once: not the hunting of adult birds, but the harvesting of their eggs, season after season, suppressing reproduction until the giant failed. Critics have pressed the argument at its joints — the security of the eggshell's attribution to Genyornis, the possibility of natural fire regimes producing localised burning, and the general scarcity of kill sites for any megafaunal species — and the exchange continues in the literature. What the eggshell establishes beyond dispute is narrower than its headline and still considerable: Genyornis shell was burned, in a patterned way, at hundreds of sites, only during the window when people and the bird coexisted.

The Climate Case — and the Standing of the Debate

The alternative has serious advocates and a serious argument. Wroe, Field, and colleagues, reviewing the fossil record, have argued for a staggered extinction: that a majority of the megafaunal species blamed on humans cannot in fact be shown to have survived into the human span at all — on their count, only a minority of species are securely present after 50,000 years ago — and that the last several hundred thousand years of Australian climate were a long escalation of aridity and variability, culminating within the last glacial cycle, against which extinctions spread over time need no human author. On this reading, people arrived on a continent whose giants were already failing, and added, at most, a minor superimposition. The honest summary of the debate's standing, and the one this article prints, is the shape of the Johnson review's own caution: the timing evidence — extinctions in the human window, on a continent whose fauna had survived many previous glacial cycles — makes a human role probable in the judgement of the largest review teams, while the mechanism (hunting, egg harvest, habitat burning, or some braid of all three with climate stress) remains unresolved species by species, direct kill evidence remains vanishingly rare, and a credentialed minority continues to argue that climate did most of the work before people arrived. Tier A prints the debate at that strength: a human role, likely; a sole human cause, unproven; a pure climate cause, against the run of the recent reviews but not extinguished.

XI. Fire and Landscape — The Fire-Stick Thesis and the Charcoal Record

No people on Earth are more associated, in the literature, with the deliberate use of landscape fire than the Aboriginal peoples of Australia — and few associations need more careful handling, because the ethnographic fact, the historical thesis built on it, and the palaeoecological record are three different things. The ethnographic fact is secure: at European contact, Aboriginal groups across the continent practised skilled, purposeful burning — small, cool, mosaic fires, set at chosen seasons, to renew grass, ease travel, drive and attract game, and care for particular places — documented in early settler accounts and in living traditional practice. The term for the system was coined by Rhys Jones in 1969: fire-stick farming.

The thesis built on the fact is Bill Gammage's The Biggest Estate on Earth (2011): that the burning was not local and occasional but continental and systematic — that through precise, repeated fire, Aboriginal management made the whole country a single tended estate, a mosaic of grass and open forest maintained in that state by burning alone, such that the dense forests Europeans later watched advance were the product of the management's removal after 1788. Gammage's book is a work of historical scholarship built on settler testimony and landscape reading, and it has been both celebrated and contested — contested most sharply on its universality: regional studies have found country, such as the dense river-flat forests of the Hawkesbury–Nepean, that does not fit the tidy mosaic, and ecologists have questioned how far a regime documented at contact may be projected backward across fifty millennia of climate change. This article therefore flags the Biggest Estate, exactly as its critics and its careful readers do, as a thesis: a powerful account of the contact-era landscape whose extension to the whole continent and the whole occupation is an argument, not a measurement.

The measurement, such as it is, comes from charcoal. Lake and swamp sediments across Australia preserve microscopic charcoal in stratified sequence — a running record of how much burning the country around each site underwent. Two results discipline the fire story. At Lynch's Crater in northeast Queensland, the 130,000-year core analysed by Rule and colleagues (Science 335 (2012): 1483–1486) shows the sequence in its most quoted form: Sporormiella — the megafauna proxy — crashes at about 41,000 years ago, and charcoal rises only after, by about a century, followed by the replacement of rainforest by fire-tolerant sclerophyll vegetation. On the authors' reading, the order matters: the giants fell first, the ungrazed grass built up as fuel, and fire followed the extinction rather than causing it — a result that cuts against the version of the fire thesis in which burning killed the megafauna, while still placing a transformed fire regime in the human period. At the continental scale, syntheses of the charcoal record (Mooney and colleagues) find no single step-change in burning at the horizon of human arrival: biomass burning tracks climate — glacial aridity, Holocene moisture, El Niño variability — more faithfully than it tracks any plausible curve of population, and statistical comparison of the charcoal synthesis against the archaeological radiocarbon record over the last 20,000 years (Williams and colleagues, 2015) finds only weak correlation between people and fire at broad scales. The palaeoecological verdict, stated at its proper strength, is this: Aboriginal burning at local and regional scales is ethnographically certain and archaeologically plausible deep into the past; a continent-wide, arrival-dated transformation of the fire regime is not what the charcoal shows. Fire was a tool in Sahul from the hearths of Madjedbebe onward. Whether it was ever a single system, and when, the sediments decline to say — and Gammage's estate remains, in this archive's classification, a thesis to be argued, not a record to be cited.

XII. What Sahul Shows — An Assessment

Strip the contested edges away, and state what remains — because what remains, on the floor that critics and excavators share, is extraordinary enough. By about 50,000 years ago, beyond serious dispute: human beings had crossed open sea, repeatedly, to reach a continent no one had ever reached; had spread through its every environment, from highland New Guinea to glacial Tasmania, from the southwestern forests to the arid interior; were grinding seed and ochre, hafting tools, and carrying pigment across country; and were burying their dead — within ten thousand years of that floor, at Mungo — with cremation rites and ochre ceremony that stand among the earliest such burials in the world. On the contested claim, add fifteen thousand years and the world's oldest edge-ground axes at the first horizon. The argument between 50,000 and 65,000 is an argument about how early the extraordinary began. It is not an argument about whether it did.

What Sahul does to the larger story is the assessment this hub exists to make. A persistent habit of older synthesis treated the great human capacities as late inventions, flowering in the European Upper Palaeolithic or later: seafaring a Holocene skill, symbolism a sudden revolution, ritual a garnish on survival. Sahul refutes the habit at every point, on evidence from the far side of the world from the caves where the habit was formed. Seafaring: the crossing to Sahul is the earliest demonstrated open-water colonisation anywhere, demanding craft, provisioning, and intent, tens of thousands of years before the Mediterranean was island-hopped. Symbolism: the voyagers came from a corridor — Sulawesi — where narrative painting is dated beyond 51,000 years, and they carried pigment practice in their first assemblages, reflective mica and all; the far shore's painted provinces, dated where dating is possible to 28,000 and 17,300 years and argued on good grounds to run deeper, are a continuation, not a beginning. Ritual: at 40,000 years, at Mungo, a cremation conducted in stages and a body laid out with interlocked hands under imported ochre — funerary behaviour as elaborate as anything of its age on Earth, performed at the end of the longest journey our species had ever made. None of these capacities waited for Europe, for farming, or for cities. They crossed the sea with the first voyagers, because — Sahul's deepest lesson — they were never inventions at all. They were luggage: part of what the people who left Africa were already carrying, everywhere they went.

And the occupation did not end. The record of this article — shelters reoccupied across forty millennia, painting traditions renewed wall over wall into living memory, languages, law, and ceremony carried in unbroken transmission — is the deep past of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples and of the peoples of New Guinea: the world's oldest continuous living culture, whose connection to the country documented here is not an archaeological inference but a continuity its custodians have always asserted and the excavated record, century by century, keeps confirming. The archaeology of Sahul is therefore written, in this archive, under a closing discipline that is also a fact: these are not vanished worlds. Madjedbebe is Mirarr Country, excavated with Mirarr authority; the Kimberley galleries are the heritage of living traditional owner groups, dated with their consent and participation; the Mungo ancestors are home, in their descendants' keeping. The first crossing was made some fifty to sixty-five thousand years ago. The occupation it began has never paused — and the people who can say what it means, across its whole length, are still here to say it.
