This is a Tier A article: everything in it rests on excavated, dated, and published archaeological evidence, and where scholars disagree — as they do about the domestication status of Jiahu's rice, the meaning of its incised signs, the early dates claimed for millet at Cishan, and the social order of the Yangshao villages — the disagreement itself is reported with its positions attributed. No claim here depends on ancient legend or modern construction, and none is allowed to borrow strength from either. Two disciplines govern the whole article and are worth naming at the outset. First, cultivation is not domestication: tending a wild plant, planting it, and harvesting it are behaviours; domestication is the measurable, heritable change in the plant that those behaviours eventually produce, and the two can be separated by millennia. Second, sign use is not writing: an incised mark can be real, deliberate, and meaningful without recording speech, and this article holds the Jiahu signs to exactly that standard — the same standard this hub applies to the Vinča marks in Origins of Writing.

The Neolithic, as the older textbooks told it, was a single invention with a single address: farming began in the Fertile Crescent, pottery and settled villages followed from it, and the rest of the world received the package, later and derivatively, by migration or imitation. East Asia refutes that story three times over, and this article is organised around the three refutations. Its pottery is the oldest in the world — fired clay vessels at Xianrendong Cave in Jiangxi are dated to about 20,000 years ago, made by Ice Age foragers some ten thousand years before anyone in the region farmed. Its most famous forager culture, the Jōmon of Japan, built sedentary villages, shell middens, lacquer workshops, and settlements of hundreds of people across more than ten thousand years without staple agriculture. And its farming, when it came, grew from its own plants: broomcorn and foxtail millet in the dry north, rice in the wet centre and south — domestications with no western progenitor, worked out on East Asian ground from East Asian wild grasses.

Three bodies of evidence carry the argument. The Jōmon sequence of the Japanese archipelago (from c. 14,000 BCE) supplies the forager proof: among the world's earliest pottery, pit-house villages, and at Sannai-Maruyama (c. 3900–2200 BCE) a settlement of a scale that forced archaeology to separate the idea of complexity from the fact of farming. The early village of Jiahu in Henan (c. 7000–5700 BCE) supplies the densest single site: early rice, the oldest playable musical instruments known anywhere, tortoise shells bearing incised signs whose status this article examines at length, and — named here in its site setting, and treated comparatively elsewhere in this hub — the earliest chemically attested fermented beverage in the world. The Yangshao culture of the Yellow River (c. 5000–3000 BCE) supplies the mature farming village: the ditch-enclosed, plaza-centred settlements of Banpo and Jiangzhai and the painted pottery whose manufacture this article follows step by step. Read together, they are not the Far Eastern chapter of a Near Eastern book. They are a second book.

I. An Independent Trajectory — Not an Echo

Independence, in archaeology, is a claim about evidence, not a sentiment about regions, and it has to be earned trait by trait — the same discipline this hub applied at Mehrgarh, where the grains proved western and the cattle proved local, and the site's originality turned out to be the originality of combination. East Asia's case is stronger and cleaner, because for its defining traits the western connection is not merely unproven but impossible on the chronology. Pottery in China and Japan predates the earliest Near Eastern pottery by several thousand years; there was no western pottery tradition in existence to copy. Millet and rice have no wild progenitors in western Asia at all: the plants themselves are East Asian, and their domestication could only have happened where they grew. Whatever moved along the long routes across Central Asia in later millennia — and much did, in both directions — the foundations were laid separately.

The sequence also runs in an order the old model did not predict. In the textbook Near Eastern progression, farming comes first and pottery follows — the earliest farming villages of the Levant and Anatolia are famously aceramic, making no pottery for their first millennia, exactly as at Mehrgarh's Period I. In East Asia the order is reversed: pottery precedes farming by a span longer than the whole of recorded history. The reversal is not a curiosity. It tells us what pottery was for in its first home — not a farmer's storage technology but a forager's cooking technology, a way of boiling, simmering, and extracting more from wild foods — and it breaks, at the root, the idea of a single Neolithic "package" whose parts must travel together. Sedentism, too, comes loose from farming in the East Asian record: the Jōmon sat still for ten thousand years on fish, nuts, and game. Village life, fired clay, plant management, and eventually plant domestication were assembled in East Asia piece by piece, in an order of East Asia's own — which is what an independent trajectory means.

One boundary should be drawn before the detail begins. "East Asia" in this article means the Japanese archipelago and the river systems of what is now China — the Yellow River and its tributaries in the dry loess north, the Huai and Yangtze systems in the wetter centre and south. The Korean peninsula's contemporary Jeulmun sequence, with its own early comb-pattern pottery, belongs to the same broad world and is noted where the comparison matters, but its full treatment belongs with a Korea the hub has not yet built. Within the stated ground, the article follows the evidence in chronological order: the pottery prologue, the forager achievement, the first farming villages, and the threshold — deliberately not crossed — where the Neolithic gives way to the ranked, walled world that leads toward early China.

II. The Pottery Prologue — Xianrendong and the Order Reversed

Xianrendong Cave — the name means "Cave of the Immortals" — is a limestone cave in Wannian County, Jiangxi Province, some 100 kilometres south of the Yangtze River, with the companion rockshelter of Diaotonghuan nearby. Excavated in campaigns from the 1960s onward, the cave preserves a long Late Palaeolithic sequence: stone, bone, and shell tools, the debris of hunting and gathering at the end of the Ice Age — and, in its lower layers, fragments of fired clay vessels. Claims for very early pottery at the cave were made for decades, but the dating was contested, because a sherd cannot be radiocarbon dated directly and the age of the layer around it is only as secure as the stratigraphy. The question was settled, as far as current evidence settles anything, by a targeted re-excavation: in 2009 an international team led by Wu Xiaohong of Peking University reopened trenches into the intact pottery-bearing deposits and dated the contexts themselves, sample by sample.

The result was published in Science in 2012 (Wu et al., "Early Pottery at 20,000 Years Ago in Xianrendong Cave, China," Science 336: 1696–1700). Radiocarbon ages for the contexts of the earliest sherds fall between 20,000 and 19,000 calendar years before present — two to three thousand years older than any other pottery then known, and squarely within the Last Glacial Maximum, the coldest phase of the Ice Age. The sherds themselves are fragments of large, bag-shaped vessels; many bear scorch marks, the blackening of use over fire. The makers were mobile hunter-gatherers. No plant or animal domestication of any kind is associated with these layers, and none would appear in the region for roughly ten thousand years. The entry already stands on this hub's timeline; this article gives it its context.

What Pottery-Before-Farming Means

The Xianrendong date rearranges the logic of invention. Pottery is heavy, fragile, and useless to people who move constantly and carry everything — or so the older reasoning ran, tying ceramics to the sedentary farmer's need to store a harvest. The East Asian evidence shows the reasoning was backwards. The first potters were foragers who returned to favoured places — caves, river terraces, shell-rich coasts — often enough, and stayed long enough, for a breakable cooking vessel to pay its way. What a pot offers a forager is not storage but transformation: prolonged boiling renders foods that cannot be eaten raw or merely roasted — extracting grease and marrow from crushed bone, detoxifying bitter nuts, simmering shellfish, small fish, and wild grains into digestible form. Wu and colleagues propose exactly such uses for the Xianrendong vessels, and the scorch marks on the sherds are the use-wear of cooking, not of a granary. Fired clay, on this evidence, begins as kitchen equipment for people who had no fields.

Nor is Xianrendong an isolated flash. At Yuchanyan Cave in Hunan, pottery-bearing contexts are dated to about 18,000 years ago (Boaretto et al., PNAS 2009); early pottery follows across the region in the millennia after the glacial maximum — in south China, in the Russian Far East, and in the Japanese archipelago, where the Incipient Jōmon sherds of §III reach back toward 16,000 years ago. The pattern is regional and sustained: a broad East Asian world of late-glacial and early post-glacial foragers took up pottery thousands of years before the Near East did, and used it, for most of that span, without farming at all. When farming did arrive in East Asia, it arrived in communities that had been potters for longer than civilisation has yet lasted. The pot came first. Everything else in this article happens in a world where that was already true.

// A DATE FOR A CONTEXT, NOT FOR A SHERD //

The Xianrendong chronology dates the sediments and associated charcoal and bone of the sherd-bearing layers, established by micromorphological study of the stratigraphy — not the sherds themselves, which contain no datable carbon of their own. That is the strongest method available for early ceramics anywhere, and the Science team's stratigraphic controls are the reason the date commands assent; but the form of the claim should be kept exact: pottery occurs at Xianrendong in contexts dated to 20,000–19,000 cal BP. Claims for still earlier pottery elsewhere in the region, resting on looser associations, are not made in this article.

III. Jōmon Japan — The Long Sequence

The Jōmon period of Japan is conventionally dated from about 14,000 BCE to the first millennium BCE — a span of more than thirteen thousand years, longer than the distance from the first Egyptian pyramids to the present day twice over. The name means "cord-marked": it derives from the rope-impressed decoration of the pottery, noted in 1877 by the American zoologist Edward S. Morse at the Ōmori shell mounds in Tokyo, and it names the period after its most durable artefact. Archaeologists divide the span into six phases — Incipient, Initial, Early, Middle, Late, and Final — defined largely by pottery typology and anchored by radiocarbon. The phases are a filing system, not a story: across thirteen millennia and an archipelago running from Hokkaidō to the Ryūkyū Islands, Jōmon communities were always regional, always various, and never a single people marching in step. What unifies them is a way of life: pottery-using, increasingly sedentary hunter-fisher-gatherers, exploiting the forests and seas of the islands with a sophistication that grew, over the millennia, into one of the most durable forager adaptations on earth.

The setting explains the durability. As the Ice Age ended, sea level rose and cut the archipelago off from the continent; broadleaf deciduous forest — beech, oak, chestnut, walnut — spread over the hills of northeastern Honshū and southern Hokkaidō, producing reliable autumn harvests of nuts, while the meeting of cold and warm currents offshore made the coasts rich in fish and shellfish, and the rivers ran with salmon. It was, by forager standards, a generous landscape: storable plant staples from the forest, protein from the sea, game in between. Jōmon communities built their whole long history on the management of that generosity — on storage pits for nuts, on processing technology for bitter and toxic plant foods (acorns and horse chestnuts require leaching before they can be eaten), on fishing gear, boats, and weirs — and on staying put. Junko Habu's Ancient Jomon of Japan (Cambridge, 2004), the standard English synthesis, frames the period's central fact precisely: sedentism and cultural complexity developed here within a hunting-gathering economy, sustained not by domesticated staples but by the intensive, knowledgeable use of wild ones.

The earliest pottery deserves its own sentence of care. Sherds from Ōdai Yamamoto I in Aomori, at the northern tip of Honshū, have been dated by association to roughly 16,000 years ago — among the oldest ceramics known anywhere, though younger than Xianrendong's (§II). Habu notes that most Japanese scholars long assumed pottery must have been introduced from the mainland; the accumulating dates reversed the presumption, and the current picture is of an early pottery horizon shared across East Asia — south China, the Amur basin, and the archipelago — within which the precise geography of first invention cannot presently be resolved and need not be: the Tier A claim is the horizon itself, forager-made and pre-agricultural, not a single birthplace. What Japan adds to that horizon is continuity: nowhere else was the forager-pottery adaptation sustained, elaborated, and documented for so long.

IV. Sedentary Foragers — Villages, Middens, Lacquer, and Dogū

The Jōmon settlement form is the pit-house village: dwellings dug partly into the ground — a circular or rectangular pit, roofed on posts with thatch — clustered, in the larger settlements, around open spaces, and accompanied by storage pits, burials, and working areas. Villages of a handful of houses are the norm through much of the sequence; villages of dozens, occupied and rebuilt across centuries, become common in the Early and Middle phases. The sedentism was real but should be stated exactly: many settlements were occupied year-round or near it, some were seasonal stations within a settled territory, and mobility never vanished from the system. What the record will not support is the old picture of Jōmon bands wandering at random. A community that digs hundreds of storage pits, buries its dead in formal cemeteries beside its houses, and rebuilds on the same terrace for a thousand years is sedentary in every sense that matters to archaeology — and it did so, for most of the sequence, without a single domesticated staple crop.

Shell Middens — Archives of the Diet

Along the coasts, Jōmon life left its most visible monument: the shell midden, an accumulated mound of discarded shell — clam, oyster, and other shellfish — mixed with fish bone, animal bone, broken tools, and pottery. Middens such as Ōmori, where Morse's 1877 excavation effectively founded Japanese archaeology, are not rubbish heaps in the dismissive sense; they are stratified archives of diet, season, and craft, and their alkaline shell matrix preserves bone that Japan's acid soils elsewhere destroy — including, at some sites, human burials and the remains of the Jōmon's domestic dogs, buried with a care that marks them as companions and hunting partners rather than food. The middens also chart the economy's reach: inland sites received marine shell and dried fish, coastal sites received inland stone, and obsidian from sources such as Kōzushima island and the Shinshū highlands, jade from Itoigawa, and asphalt and lacquer moved across the archipelago in exchange networks hundreds of kilometres long. The Jōmon world was connected, and its connections ran on forager logistics.

Lacquer — A Chemistry of Patience

Of all Jōmon crafts, lacquer is the most technically demanding, and among the earliest attested anywhere. Lacquer is the sap of the lacquer tree (Toxicodendron vernicifluum), tapped by scoring the bark, collected, filtered, and homogenised, then applied in thin coats that cure not by drying but by enzymatic polymerisation in warm, humid air — a process requiring controlled conditions, repeated coats, and weeks of work for a finished object, with a raw material that is itself a potent skin irritant. At Kakinoshima B in Hokkaidō, ornaments coated in red lacquer were recovered from a pit grave dated to about 9,000 years ago (c. 7000 BCE) — the oldest lacquered objects known in the world, reported as such in the site literature. The attribution carries a noted caveat, recorded in the analytical literature: the Kakinoshima pieces were identified as lacquer without the full chemical confirmation that later pyrolysis studies have applied to other early pieces, and this article reports them at that strength. The broader Jōmon lacquer tradition — combs, vessels, bows, and ornaments from waterlogged sites across the following millennia, chemically confirmed in multiple cases — is not in doubt. A forager society mastered one of the most unforgiving coating chemistries of the pre-industrial world, and used it for beauty.

Dogū — Described Plainly

The dogū are clay figurines — hand-modelled, fired, ranging from a few centimetres to some 40 cm tall — produced from the Early Jōmon onward and most numerous and elaborate in the Middle, Late, and Final phases of northern and eastern Japan. Most represent a human-like figure, conventionally read as female on the evidence of breasts and emphasised hips on many examples; the best-known Late and Final Jōmon forms have large, protruding eyes that modern viewers compare to goggles, heart-shaped or triangular faces, and bodies covered in cord-marked and incised patterning. They are very frequently found broken, and often in refuse or midden contexts rather than in graves or shrines. That is the description. What dogū were — the literature offers healing or childbirth figures, dolls, ritual objects deliberately broken in use, representations of spirits or ancestors — the evidence does not decide, and the confident modern readings, whether "fertility goddess" or anything stranger, go beyond it. This article records the figurines as a major, long-lived Jōmon art form whose function is unresolved, and notes the one hard pattern in the data: a sedentary forager society sustained figurine-making, as it sustained lacquer and fine pottery, for thousands of years. Symbolic elaboration did not wait for farming either.

V. Sannai-Maruyama — The Large-Settlement Case

Sannai-Maruyama, in Aomori Prefecture near the northern end of Honshū, was found in 1992 when construction of a baseball stadium cut into the site; the prefecture cancelled the stadium, excavated on a large scale, and preserved the ground — an outcome Japanese archaeology is entitled to be proud of. The settlement was occupied for roughly seventeen centuries, c. 3900–2200 BCE, spanning the Early and Middle Jōmon, on a terrace between river and bay. Its excavated remains are the fullest picture of a large Jōmon community anywhere: hundreds of pit-house foundations, large pillar-supported buildings including a long structure over 30 metres in length, raised-floor storehouses, storage pits in quantity, roads — a packed-earth thoroughfare some 12 metres wide in places — formal burial areas with adult graves ranged along the roads and a separate zone of child burials in pottery jars, and great mounds of accumulated refuse rich in pottery, stone, bone, and plant remains. Population estimates for the settlement at its height commonly run to several hundred people. In 2021 the site was inscribed, with the other Jōmon sites of northern Japan, on the UNESCO World Heritage List.

The building that made the site famous is its six-pillar structure: six enormous postholes, evenly spaced in two rows, each holding a chestnut post about a metre in diameter. The posts' regularity — the spacing is uniform enough to have prompted speculation about standardised measurement — implies a large roofed or tower-like building of communal scale, raised by coordinated labour with stone tools. Its function is unknown: watchtower, assembly hall, and ritual structure have all been proposed, and this article asserts none of them. What the postholes prove without interpretation is organisation — timber felled, transported, shaped, and erected at a scale beyond any household, in a community with no chiefs anyone can identify in its burials, no metal, and no crops. Beside the structure, the exchange evidence widens the picture: jade and obsidian from distant sources, lacquerware, and amber show Sannai-Maruyama plugged into the archipelago-wide networks of §IV at their northern end.

The Chestnut Question — Management or Cultivation?

The settlement stood in chestnut country, and chestnut dominates its plant remains — as food, in storage pits full of nuts, and as timber, in posts like the six pillars. The debate is whether that dominance is an accident of local forest or a human product. Pollen evidence around the site shows chestnut rising to unusual prominence during the occupation, and archaeobotanical study has argued that the inhabitants managed the surrounding forest — protecting, encouraging, and selectively using chestnut (and other useful trees) so systematically that the groves around the village amount to a tended landscape: arboriculture without fields. Genetic study of chestnut remains has been brought to bear on both sides, and the strong claim — deliberate planting and selection amounting to cultivation — remains argued rather than settled. The careful statement, and the one this article makes, distinguishes the levels: that Jōmon communities used chestnut intensively is certain; that they managed the forest to favour it is the prevailing reading of the pollen and assemblage evidence; that they domesticated it, in the genetic sense claimed for cereals, is not demonstrated. Sannai-Maruyama's people also used a few genuinely cultivated plants in small quantities — bottle gourd and others attested in the assemblage — as supplements at the margin of a forager economy, which is exactly the point: cultivation at the margin, sedentism and complexity at the centre.

Sannai-Maruyama's lesson for this hub is the Jōmon's lesson at full scale: complexity without agriculture. A settlement of hundreds, maintained for seventeen centuries, with monumental communal building, formal roads and cemeteries, craft production in lacquer and jade, and exchange reaching hundreds of kilometres, rested on fish, game, and managed forest. The same theme is sounded, in a farming key, at Çatalhöyük, where a town of thousands likewise shows no palace, no temple quarter, and no ruling house anyone can find: in both places the equation of social scale with hierarchy and of settlement with farming fails, and fails on excavated ground. The Jōmon simply fail it for longer, and without the crops.

// WHY THE NORTH KEEPS ITS SECRETS POORLY — AND WELL //

Japan's acid volcanic soils destroy bone and wood at most sites, which is why the Jōmon skeletal record is thin and why middens and waterlogged deposits carry so much of the evidence. Sannai-Maruyama's waterlogged lower layers preserved wooden artefacts, plant remains, and lacquer that would have vanished on a dry terrace. The record's unevenness cuts both ways: absence of a material at a Jōmon site is weak evidence, and the richest sites are rich partly because they preserved. Population and complexity estimates in this section are read with that caution.

VI. Jiahu — The Village on the Huai

Jiahu lies near Wuyang in Henan Province, in the upper Huai River valley — the border country between China's dry millet-growing north and its wet rice-growing centre — east of the Funiu Mountains, between the floodplains of the Ni River to the south and the Sha River to the north. The settlement is an irregular oval roughly 275 by 260 metres, some 55,000 m² in extent, occupied, on the radiocarbon evidence published with the flute study (twenty determinations, calibrated), from 7000 to 5700 BCE in three sub-periods (c. 7000–6600, 6600–6200, and 6200–5700 BCE). It was identified in 1962 and excavated in campaigns from the 1980s onward under the Henan Provincial Institute, with Juzhong Zhang as its leading excavator. Only a small fraction — about 4–5 per cent — of the site has been excavated, and that fraction has yielded an inventory out of all proportion to its size: 45 house foundations, 370 storage or cellar pits, 9 pottery kilns, more than 300 graves, and thousands of artefacts in bone, pottery, and stone (Zhang et al., Nature 1999).

The village was, from its first phase, sedentary and organised: semi-subterranean and surface houses, dedicated pottery kilns, storage pits, and cemeteries in which the dead were buried with goods — tools, pottery, ornaments, and, in a number of graves, the tortoise shells and bone flutes of §§VIII–IX. Its material culture is correspondingly rich: polished stone tools including harvesting implements, bone tools of many kinds, pottery in a developing repertoire of jars and basins (the vessels whose residues §X examines), and evidence of animals managed as well as hunted, with pig and dog among the domesticates present in the assemblage as the sequence proceeds. The subsistence base remained broad — deer, fish, freshwater shellfish, turtles, water chestnut, acorns, and other wild plants bulk large in the remains — with rice present from the early phases as one resource among many. Jiahu, in the excavators' phrase as reported in the Nature paper, was an organised, well-established sedentary village of long duration in the very early Chinese Neolithic: not a camp on the way to farming, but a settled community experimenting, over thirteen centuries, with the plants and animals around it.

That border-country setting is the key to everything that follows. Jiahu sits near the northern climatic limit of wild rice and at the southern edge of the zone where the northern millets would be domesticated; it is the kind of ecotone where two of the world's great grain traditions could meet in a single village's stores. The four bodies of evidence that made Jiahu famous — its rice, its flutes, its signs, and its fermented beverage — are treated in the next four sections in ascending order of contention: the flutes, whose evidence is the soundest and strangest; the rice, whose domestication status is genuinely open; the beverage, chemically attested and briefly handled here; and the signs, where this article's discipline is tested hardest.

VII. Jiahu — Early Rice and Its State of Evidence

Rice at Jiahu is attested by three independent kinds of remain. Carbonised grains occur in the site's deposits — one such grain supplied one of the twenty radiocarbon dates of the flute paper — and rice husks were used as temper in pottery. Phytoliths — the microscopic silica bodies that form in plant tissues and survive where the plant itself decays — were recovered from Jiahu sediments in a 1995 study (published in Chinese Science Bulletin) that identified fan-shaped and other phytolith forms diagnostic of rice, including forms from the grain husks, in the site's early levels, and assigned the cultivated material to the japonica type of Oryza sativa. And the residues of §X identify rice as an ingredient of the village's fermented beverage, which presupposes rice available in quantity worth brewing. Taken together, the three lines establish beyond reasonable dispute that Jiahu's people used rice, from the site's early centuries, as a significant resource. They do not, by themselves, establish what kind of rice it was.

The open question is domestication status, and it is open for a principled reason: the traits that distinguish domesticated rice from wild rice — above all the non-shattering rachis, the mutation that stops the ripe grain falling from the stalk and so makes the plant dependent on human harvest and sowing — are read from spikelet bases and grain metrics in large, well-preserved assemblages, and Jiahu's rice assemblage, recovered largely before systematic flotation became standard in Chinese archaeology, is not of that character. The site's excavators, notably Zhang Juzhong with Wang Xiangqun, argued from the grains and phytoliths that Jiahu's rice was already a cultivated, early domesticated japonica, and proposed the upper Huai as a centre of rice cultivation in its own right, north of the Yangtze heartland. The wider archaeobotanical synthesis — Dorian Fuller and colleagues' work on the protracted, millennia-long domestication of rice in the Yangtze valley, where the shift to non-shattering forms can be tracked grain by grain and was not complete until roughly 4000 BCE — counsels a different framing: at 7000–5700 BCE, rice anywhere was at most in the early stages of a long domestication process, and Jiahu's material is best described as rice under cultivation or intensive use while domestication was still under way. Reviews of the 10,000–7000 BP rice evidence (e.g. Pan 2023) likewise treat Jiahu as an early and important node in the rice story whose precise status — wild-collected, cultivated wild, or incipiently domesticated — the surviving samples cannot finally resolve.

This article therefore states the Jiahu rice claim at its defensible strength: rice was used and, on the phytolith and grain evidence, very probably cultivated at Jiahu from the seventh millennium BCE — among the earliest such evidence anywhere; whether that rice was morphologically domesticated, and whether the upper Huai was an independent centre of domestication or the northern reach of the Yangtze process, remain open questions in the published literature. The caution is not a diminishment. A village brewing with rice (§X) at the plant's northern limit, a thousand years before the Yangtze sequence shows domestication completed, is evidence enough that the rice story was wide, early, and regional — and Jiahu's place in it is secure at exactly that strength.

// HOW A PHYTOLITH IS READ //

Rice leaves and husks deposit distinctive silica bodies: fan-shaped (bulliform) cells from the leaf, double-peaked cells from the husk. The laboratory procedure reported for Jiahu — oxidising the sediment sample, dissolving carbonates in dilute acid, and floating the phytoliths out in a heavy liquid — isolates them for microscopy, where their shapes are matched against modern reference collections of wild and cultivated rice. The method identifies the plant robustly; the step from husk phytoliths to the plant's domestication status is the inferential step on which the debate of this section turns, because husk form alone does not carry the non-shattering trait.

VIII. The Bone Flutes — Manufacture and Acoustic Study

In September 1999, Nature published the report that made Jiahu world-famous: Zhang Juzhong, Garman Harbottle, Wang Changsui, and Kong Zhaochen, "Oldest playable musical instruments found at Jiahu early Neolithic site in China," Nature 401 (1999): 366–368. The excavations had produced six complete bone flutes, together with fragments of some thirty more, from radiocarbon-dated layers spanning the site's occupation. The complete instruments come principally from burials of the site's middle sub-period. They are, in the paper's own carefully hedged words, what may be the earliest complete, playable, tightly dated multi-note musical instruments known. Earlier candidate flutes exist — the fragmentary Neanderthal-associated bone from Divje Babe is the famous case — but they are fragments whose status as instruments is itself disputed. The Jiahu flutes are whole, provenanced, dated, and playable. That conjunction is the claim, and it has stood.

Manufacture — A Crane's Wing, Worked

The flutes are made from the ulna — the great wing bone — of the red-crowned crane (Grus japonensis), a large, hollow, thin-walled bone whose natural tube requires a minimum of hollowing to become a wind instrument. The published dimensions of the complete flutes run to roughly 20–25 cm in length and about 1.1 cm in bore. The maker's procedure can be reconstructed from the instruments themselves: select a straight ulna; cut it to length and clear the bore; lay out the finger holes along one face; drill them — the complete flutes carry five, six, seven, or eight holes in their main row — and finish the surface. Two details show the craft thinking. First, the hole spacing is not mechanically even: it is adjusted, hole by hole, as a flute-maker adjusts for pitch, because the acoustic length of a stopped tube does not map linearly onto the bone. Second, the best-preserved flute carries, beside its seventh hole, a tiny additional hole — interpreted by the investigators as a correction, drilled after a first test showed the note off pitch: a tuning adjustment, made by ear, by a maker who could hear the error and knew the remedy. A second flute was found broken and anciently repaired, its two halves bound for continued use — an instrument valued enough to mend. These are not whistles or toys. They are the products of an iterative craft tradition, in which instruments were tested, corrected, and kept.

The Acoustic Study — Playing a 9,000-Year-Old Flute

The Nature team did the thing archaeology rarely gets to do: they played the evidence. The best-preserved flute, catalogued M282:20 — seven main holes plus the small correction hole, free of cracks — was sounded under laboratory conditions, its tones measured with a stroboscopic pitch analyser (a Stroboconn) under the supervision of Huang Xiangpeng of the Chinese Academy of Arts' music research institute. Because the flute is an open-ended, vertically held tube sounded by blowing across its rim, pitch depends on the player's embouchure angle; the team therefore recorded two players, each blowing at upward and downward angles, to map the instrument's producible scale rather than a single performance. The measured tone series formed a coherent scale of the kind that permits melody, not merely isolated notes. The authors compared the scale with the two earliest documented Chinese tonal systems — the six-tone Qing Shang and seven-tone Xia Shi scales — and stated the limit of the comparison exactly: those systems are documented six thousand years later, and whether the Jiahu scale is ancestral to either cannot be concluded from one instrument. Their positive conclusion is the one this article adopts: the carefully selected tone scale of M282:20 shows that the Neolithic musicians of the seventh millennium BCE could play not just single notes, but music — organised, repeatable pitch, produced on an instrument designed for it.

The flutes' contexts complete the picture, stated with the same care. They come from burials, some of them among the richer furnished graves of the cemetery — instruments buried with their players, or with those entitled to them. What the music was for — ceremony, dance, healing, entertainment, rites involving the cranes whose bones sing in them — the evidence does not say, and this article does not guess. What can be said is structural: a farming-border village of the seventh millennium BCE sustained a craft of instrument-making refined enough to tune by ear and correct by drilling, and valued its instruments enough to repair them and to bury them with the dead. Music is among the oldest attested arts on earth because Jiahu kept its flutes.

IX. Tortoise Shells and Incised Signs — Sign Use, Not Yet Writing

Jiahu's graves also held tortoise shells — carapaces and plastrons, in some burials in sets, the shells containing small pebbles — placed with the dead in a manner that invites comparison, across five thousand years, with the tortoise-shell divination of Shang China. On some of these shells, and on a few other objects, are incised signs. The corpus, as published in the paper at the centre of this section — Li Xueqin, Garman Harbottle, Zhang Juzhong, and Wang Changsui, "The earliest writing? Sign use in the seventh millennium BC at Jiahu, Henan Province, China," Antiquity 77 (2003): 31–44 — comprises, on the strictest scrutiny, eleven definitely incised signs: nine on tortoise shells and two on bone, with a somewhat larger number (sixteen or seventeen groupings in some counts) claimed when less certain marks are included. The signs are single marks, not sequences: among them forms resembling a human eye, a sun-like mark, and simple stroke configurations. The shells bearing them date to the site's middle sub-period, around 6600–6200 BCE.

The Antiquity paper's own position is more careful than the headlines it generated ("'Earliest writing' found in China," as the BBC headed its report). Li Xueqin — the leading palaeographer of his generation — and his colleagues noted the resemblance of individual Jiahu signs to individual characters of the oracle-bone script of the late Shang (c. 1250 BCE onward), the eye-like sign to the later character for "eye" among them, and the suggestive fact that the signs occur on tortoise shell, the Shang divination medium. But their stated conclusion, which this article quotes in substance because it is the correct strength, is that the signs are interpreted not as writing itself, but as features of a lengthy period of sign use which led eventually to a fully fledged system of writing. Sign use, on their own showing: deliberate, conventional marks, made and presumably understood within the community, standing at the head of a five-thousand-year Chinese tradition of graphic marking that eventually — at Banpo, Dawenkou, and beyond (§§XII, XV) — issues in writing.

The Critics — And Why the Hub Sides with the Caution

The critical response is substantial, and a Tier A article must give it at full weight. Sinologist William G. Boltz, reviewing the Neolithic sign evidence, argued that the resemblances between isolated Neolithic marks and later characters carry no evidential force by themselves: simple geometric and pictorial forms recur independently, and — decisively — the Jiahu signs show no meaningful order of repetition or concatenation: they never combine into sequences, never recur in patterns that suggest they record words, and give no evidence of the phonetic borrowing by which a sign system becomes a script. Qiu Xigui, the standard authority on Chinese palaeography, states the criterion the critics apply: only when symbols are consciously used to record words forming sentences has the development of script begun — and of that, at Jiahu, there is no evidence. Other cautions are narrower but real: some claimed marks are shallow or ambiguous enough that unintentional damage or working marks cannot be excluded for individual cases (the Antiquity authors themselves reduced the corpus on scrutiny, from seventeen claimed groupings to eleven certain signs), and the five-thousand- year gap between the Jiahu shells and the oracle bones is bridged by sign traditions, not by any demonstrated line of transmission for these particular marks. The pebble-filled shells' resemblance to Shang divination apparatus is likewise a resemblance: no heating, cracking, or inscription-for-the-crack is attested at Jiahu, and divination is not claimed here.

The adjudication this article prints is therefore threefold, and each part is stated at its own strength. Certain: the eleven signs are deliberate human marks, incised on tortoise shells and bone in the seventh millennium BCE, among the earliest attested sign use in East Asia. Plausible, and the excavators' own reading: the signs are conventional symbols within a local sign-using practice, of the kind that, sustained across Neolithic China, eventually issued in writing. Not established: that the signs are writing, proto-writing in the strong sense, or direct ancestors of any specific oracle-bone characters. The reader who wants the general framework — what separates a mark, a sign system, and a script, and how the same questions are handled for Vinča and for the Indus — will find it in this hub's Origins of Writing, where Jiahu takes its place in the comparative table under the same rules applied here.

The Antiquity paper is titled "The earliest writing?" — with its question mark. Popular reporting habitually drops the mark and converts a hypothesis about sign use into a discovery of writing. This hub restores the question mark wherever the Jiahu signs are mentioned, including its timeline: the signs are entered as sign use, Tier A as marks, contested as writing. X. The Fermented Beverage at Jiahu — Named in Its Setting

The fourth of Jiahu's famous evidence is handled in this article as the coordination of the hub requires: named, placed in its site setting, and sourced — with its comparative and chemical treatment reserved to the rider section of Göbekli Tepe, where early fermentation is weighed across sites. The finding itself is this. Chemical analyses of residues absorbed into the pottery of sixteen vessels from Jiahu's domestic contexts — jars and basins of the kind the village used daily — identified, in thirteen of the sixteen, the consistent signature of a mixed fermented beverage of rice, honey, and fruit (hawthorn fruit and/or grape), the fruit attested by tartaric acid and its salts at concentrations pointing to hawthorn or grape, the rice and honey by their own chemical markers and by the archaeobotanical record of the site. The earliest analysed vessels belong to the site's first sub-period, c. 7000–6600 BCE. The paper is McGovern et al., "Fermented beverages of pre- and proto-historic China," Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 101 (2004): 17593–17598 — Patrick McGovern of the University of Pennsylvania Museum leading an international team that included Jiahu's excavator, Zhang Juzhong.

In its site setting, the result completes the village portrait of §VI. The beverage was not an import or a ritual exotic: it was made in the village's own jars, from the village's own border-country ingredients — the rice of §VII, wild fruit from the hills, honey from the forest — by the seventh millennium BCE, at roughly the same horizon at which barley beer and grape wine were emerging in the Near East, and on present chemical evidence slightly before the earliest attested grape wine there. A community that brewed was a community with surplus worth fermenting, vessels worth sealing, and occasions worth marking; that the same village tuned crane-bone flutes (§VIII) and incised its tortoise shells (§IX) is a conjunction the reader may weigh without this article's help. What the chemistry does and does not prove — the method, the markers, the cross-site comparison with Anatolia and the Caucasus, and the limits of residue inference — is set out in the Göbekli Tepe rider, and is not duplicated here.

XI. Millet Domestication — Cishan and the Debate over Early Dates

North China's Neolithic rests on two grains, both East Asian domestications from local wild grasses: broomcorn (common or proso) millet, Panicum miliaceum, from a widespread wild Panicum; and foxtail millet, Setaria italica, from the wild green foxtail, Setaria viridis, which still grows as a weed across northern China. The two are small-grained, fast-growing, drought-tolerant cereals fitted to the loess country — thin soils, summer rain, long dry winters — where rice cannot grow. Together they fed the Yangshao villages of §XII and, in time, the first Chinese cities. The question this section adjudicates is not whether the millets are Chinese domestications — no serious position denies it — but when the domestication happened, and on that question the literature holds two positions a full two thousand years apart.

Cishan — The Storage Pits and the 2009 Claim

The type evidence comes from Cishan, in Wu'an, Hebei, on a terrace where the loess meets the North China Plain. Excavated in 1976–1978, Cishan yielded 88 storage pits filled with an extraordinary quantity of grain remains — the excavators estimated more than 50,000 kg of crop in the pits as found — preserved largely as silicified husk material, the grain itself having decayed. The remains were first reported as foxtail millet, on size alone, and dated by associated charcoal to about 8,200 years ago; both the identification and the dating were long doubted, because the grains were never morphologically documented and the charcoal's association with the grain was insecure. In 2009, Lu Houyuan and colleagues returned to the site and published the re-analysis in PNAS: "Earliest domestication of common millet (Panicum miliaceum) in East Asia extended to 10,000 years ago," PNAS 106 (2009): 7367–7372. Working from newly excavated pits, the team identified the husk phytoliths — silica patterns in the husk epidermis that differ diagnostically between the two millet species — together with biomolecular markers, and concluded that the early Cishan grain was broomcorn millet, not foxtail; foxtail appears only in small quantity after about 8,700 years ago. New radiocarbon dating placed the broomcorn-bearing pits between about 10,300 and 8,700 cal BP (c. 8300–6700 BCE). Their conclusion: common millet was the earliest dry-farming crop in East Asia, domesticated — their word — independently in semi-arid northern China by 10,000 years ago, its drought resistance suiting it to lead the way.

The Debate — Identification, Dating, and the Meaning of "Domesticated"

The 2009 paper resolved two real problems — it demonstrated the species identification properly for the first time, and it dated new samples rather than relying on the 1970s charcoal — and its broomcorn identification has been widely accepted. The debate concerns what the result means, and it has three fronts, each with named positions. First, the dating: the earliest Cishan contexts rest on a limited number of determinations, and the site's stratigraphy — pits dug down from later surfaces into earlier deposits — makes association the standing difficulty; critics in the critical archaeobotanical literature (Stevens & Fuller 2017, taking a systematically sceptical approach to early Chinese crop dates) treat claims from before about 6000 BCE as requiring direct dates on the crop remains themselves. Second, the identification of status: a husk phytolith identifies the species; it does not show that the plant was morphologically domesticated — grain-size and shattering evidence, of the kind that tracks rice domestication, is what the morphometric school (Fuller and colleagues; the 2020 morphometric model for Panicum domestication in Vegetation History and Archaeobotany) requires, and on that evidence the measurable domestication of broomcorn appears as a protracted process still under way in the seventh–sixth millennia BCE at sites such as Xinglonggou, rather than a completed fact at 8000 BCE. Third, the model: Bettinger and colleagues, arguing from the same early record, proposed rapid domestication under intensive cultivation; the morphometric school answers with the pattern established for wheat, barley, and rice — domestication as an evolutionary process of two to three thousand years of unconscious selection, in which cultivation long precedes the domesticated form.

The adjudication printed here follows the distinction this article announced at the outset. Secure: millet — broomcorn first, foxtail following — was cultivated and stored in bulk in northern China by the seventh millennium BCE at the latest (Cishan's later pits, and the wider Peiligang–Cishan horizon, are not in dispute), and its domestication from Chinese wild grasses is an independent East Asian achievement. Claimed, and partly supported: broomcorn use at Cishan reaching back toward 10,000 BP, on phytolith identification and context dating (Lu et al. 2009). Not settled: that the plant of 10,000 BP was domesticated in the morphological sense, as opposed to a wild or incipiently cultivated grass under intensive use — the morphometric evidence presently favours a protracted domestication completing millennia later. The hub's timeline accordingly enters Cishan at the strength of cultivation and storage, with the domestication date flagged as debated. East Asia's independence does not need the earliest date to be true; it is fully proved by the seventh-millennium record alone.

// TWO MILLETS, TWO CLOCKS //

Broomcorn millet (Panicum miliaceum): the pioneer — earliest at Cishan on the phytolith evidence, extremely drought-tolerant, fast to mature; spreads west across the steppe in later millennia, reaching Europe by the second millennium BCE on the current direct-dating consensus. Foxtail millet (Setaria italica): appears in small quantities at Cishan after c. 8700 BP and becomes, by Yangshao times, the prestige and staple grain of the north — the millet of the classical Chinese texts. The sequence matters: the Yangshao granaries of §XII were filled by a two-millet system that had been assembled, species by species, over thousands of years.

XII. Yangshao — Banpo and Jiangzhai: The Planned Village

The Yangshao culture (c. 5000–3000 BCE) is the mature Neolithic of the middle Yellow River — thousands of sites across Henan, Shaanxi, and Shanxi, named for the village of Yangshao in Henan where Johan Gunnar Andersson's team first excavated in 1921. Its people were millet farmers (with pigs and dogs, hunting and fishing in support), living in villages of a form so consistent that the form itself is evidence: a residential core of semi-subterranean houses around open communal space, enclosed by a ditch, with cemeteries and pottery kilns placed outside the enclosure. Two excavations give the form its classic documentation. Banpo, at the eastern edge of modern Xi'an, discovered in 1953 and excavated 1954–1957, is the type site of the early (Banpo) phase and has been a museum site since 1957. Jiangzhai, some 20 km east of Xi'an in Lintong, excavated over eleven seasons in 1972–1979, is the most completely exposed early Yangshao village known — a settlement excavated, uniquely for its period, essentially to its limits.

Banpo — Ditch, Houses, Kilns, Cemetery

Banpo's excavated settlement covers some 5–6 hectares within a ditch five to six metres wide — read variously as defence, drainage, and stock control, and probably serving more than one of those ends. Inside stood the houses: round or square in plan, semi-subterranean, their floors sunk about a metre below the contemporary surface, walls of wattle plastered with mud on timber frames, roofs thatched and steeply pitched, a central hearth and posts carrying the roof. Houses occur singly and in small clusters; one building substantially larger than the rest has been read since the first reports as a communal or assembly structure. Outside the ditch lay the working and mortuary zones: pottery kilns, and the cemetery, where adults were buried in single or collective graves and children — a pattern repeated across the Yangshao world — were buried in painted pottery urns, at Banpo in ground nearer the houses. Population estimates for Banpo at any one phase commonly run between one and five hundred, hedged by the fact that only part of the settlement was excavated and phases overlap. The excavators also recovered the farming kit in quantity — stone axes and spades, bone and stone harvesting knives, grinding equipment — and the charred millets of §XI in its storage pits.

Jiangzhai — The Whole Village, Excavated

Jiangzhai's first phase (radiocarbon c. 4700–3600 BCE for the early occupation) preserves what Banpo's partial excavation can only suggest: a complete village plan. The settlement — its residential core within an area of roughly two hectares inside a total site many times larger — was ringed by a ditch. At its centre lay an open plaza of some 4,000 m², with animal pens upon it. Around the plaza stood about 120 houses arranged in five residential groups; each group was organised around one large house — the largest reaching some 124 m² of floor — with medium houses (roughly 20–40 m²) and small houses (sufficient for a small family) clustered about it, together with the group's storage pits (more than 300 across the village) and hearths. All the houses faced inward, toward the plaza. Kilns stood apart from the houses, and the cemetery — 376 burials excavated, single and multiple — lay outside the ditch in three distinct sections. Storage capacity, as Peterson and Shelach's re-analysis (2012) quantified it, could have held millet enough for hundreds of people: the plan is a working diagram of a community that stored communally, built to a shared template, and organised its space in nested groups — household, cluster, village — legible in the ground. Their re-analysis also complicates the first excavators' tidy picture: the five groups are not identical copies of one another, differing measurably in activity emphasis and in accumulated surplus, so that Jiangzhai shows variability and the beginnings of economic differentiation within its communal frame — a finding §XIV takes up.

XIII. Painted Pottery — Pigments, Firing, and the Motif Sequence

Yangshao pottery is the most celebrated ceramic tradition of the Chinese Neolithic, and its manufacture is recoverable in unusual procedural detail because every stage left evidence: clay preparation in the settlement debris, kilns outside the ditches, wasters and misfires in the kiln dumps, and the finished vessels by the thousand. The standard account here follows Li Wenjie's technological studies of Neolithic pottery-making and the syntheses of Liu Li (The Chinese Neolithic, 2004) and Underhill's Companion to Chinese Archaeology (2013).

Forming — Coil, Mould, and the Slow Wheel

Vessels were built by hand: clay prepared by levigation and tempering, formed by coiling — ropes of clay stacked and smoothed — by moulding over or inside a form for standard shapes, and by beating with a paddle against an anvil held inside the wall to thin and shape the body. The surfaces were then scraped, smoothed, and burnished. Rotational devices enter the sequence slowly and late: the common statement that "the Yangshao did not use the potter's wheel" is true of wheel-throwing — no Yangshao vessel was thrown from a lump on a fast wheel — but turntable-assisted finishing, turning the vessel while smoothing and painting it, is attested in the tradition's later phases and becomes general toward the end of the period; the fast wheel belongs to the Longshan world of §XV. The distinction matters procedurally: Yangshao regularity of form was achieved by moulds, templates, and the turntable, not by the wheel, and the painted decoration of the next paragraph was laid out on a vessel the painter could rotate steadily by hand.

Painting and Pigments — Before the Fire

The painted decoration was applied before firing, to the dried, burnished surface. The ground is the vessel's own body: a fine red-firing clay, often given a slip of refined clay that fires to a warm buff-red. Onto this ground the painter worked in two principal pigments: black, from iron- and manganese-bearing mineral pigments (iron oxide with manganese compounds, on the analyses summarised in the technological literature), ground fine and suspended in water; and white, from a pale clay or calcareous pigment, used more sparingly and chiefly in certain phases and regions; red pigment (haematite) supplements the black in some wares. The painting was done freehand with a soft brush — the swelling and tapering of the lines preserves the brush's loading and lift — in single confident passes: the great sweeping bands, nets, and arcs of the Banpo basins were not sketched and corrected but laid down by painters whose motor habits were formed over thousands of vessels. Because the pigment is mineral and the firing oxidising, the design fires into permanence: black on red, the Yangshao signature, is a chemistry of iron under an oxidising kiln atmosphere, not a surface paint that can flake away.

Firing — The Upright Kiln

Yangshao kilns, excavated at Banpo and elsewhere, are small updraft kilns: a firebox below, a perforated floor, and a chamber above in which the vessels stood, the hot gases drawn up through the load. Firing temperatures on the evidence of the wares themselves ran to roughly 900–1000 °C — sufficient to harden the body and fix the pigments while keeping the atmosphere oxidising for the red ground. Kilns were communal installations, grouped outside the ditch in their own quarter at both Banpo and Jiangzhai — a placement that speaks of fire risk managed collectively and, very probably, of production organised above the household level, though whether by specialists, by kin groups in rotation, or by whole-village firings the evidence does not say. The waster heaps — warped, bloated, and fused vessels — are the industry's failure rate made visible, and their quantity at the kiln sites confirms production well beyond a single household's needs.

The Motif Sequence — Banpo to Machang

The decoration follows a legible development across the culture's two thousand years, and the sequence is the archaeologist's clock. In the early Banpo phase the characteristic designs are broad black bands carrying naturalistic and stylising fish — whole fish, fish heads, frogs — and deer, together with the famous human-face-and-fish design of the Banpo basins, in which a round face, its head crowned with a triangular headdress, is flanked by fish. Through the middle Miaodigou phase the naturalism dissolves into pattern: petal and leaf forms, arcs and hooked lines in flowing, interlocking compositions of great elegance, the animals gone into pure geometry. In the late phases, westward into the Majiayao tradition of Gansu and Qinghai (often treated as late Yangshao or as its successor culture), the painting reaches its technical summit — vast swirling black compositions covering whole vessels — before the final Machang phase simplifies again into bold, rapid, sometimes almost sketch-like figures. What the motifs meant — totem, tally, myth, or ornament — is not recoverable from the pots, and the popular readings (the face as a "shaman," the fish as a clan emblem) are modern attributions, flagged as such here and asserted nowhere in this article. The pots also carry, at Banpo and elsewhere, small incised marks — some twenty-two distinct signs at Banpo — made before firing on individual vessels: pot-marks, most plausibly of maker, owner, or batch, and subject to exactly the discipline of §IX. They are sign use in a mature farming society, and no more writing than Jiahu's.

XIV. Yangshao Social Organisation — The Matriarchy Reading as Historiography

For a generation, the standard description of Banpo in textbooks around the world was a single word: matriarchal. The reading deserves a section of its own in this article — not because it is the current position, but because its history is the cleanest case in East Asian archaeology of an interpretation imposed by a framework rather than drawn from the ground, and Tier A discipline requires the hub to show its workings. The framework was nineteenth-century evolutionary theory as transmitted through Engels's The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State (1884), itself built on Morgan's account of Iroquois society: human society was held to pass through fixed stages, of which an early stage of mother-right — matrilineal descent, matrilocal residence, women at the centre of the kinship order — preceded the patriarchal family. Introduced into Chinese theoretical thought in the 1930s and dominant in the archaeology of the 1950s, when Banpo was excavated, the scheme supplied the conclusion before the evidence was examined: an early farming village must be matrilineal, and the excavators — working in good faith within the paradigm of their time — read the site accordingly.

The evidence marshalled for the reading, stated fairly, was this: collective burials in the Banpo cemetery were interpreted as clan burials organised by maternal kin, with sex-segregated groupings taken to reflect a matrilineal order; one unusually rich burial, of a child of about four, furnished with fine pottery and ornaments, was identified as a girl and read as evidence of the honoured status of females; and the village plan — communal, undifferentiated, without evident chiefly houses — was held to fit a matriarchal clan commune. Each element has since failed under scrutiny, on grounds the modern literature (summarised in Liu 2004 and in the Jiangzhai re-analyses) states plainly: the sex of a four-year-old skeleton cannot be reliably determined, so the rich child's sex — the linchpin of the status argument — is unknown; collective and sex-grouped burial occurs in societies of many kinship types and diagnoses none; and nothing in house form, grave goods, or skeletal evidence at Banpo or Jiangzhai discriminates matrilineal from patrilineal or bilateral organisation. Descent rules are among the things archaeology is worst at seeing, and at Yangshao sites they are, on present evidence, unseen.

What the evidence does support, this article states instead. Early Yangshao villages were corporate communities organised in nested kin-like groups — the five residential clusters of Jiangzhai are the ground plan of some segmentary order, whatever its descent rule — with communal storage, communal works (ditch, kilns, plaza), and burials that show modest differentiation by age and sex but no chiefly stratum: the current consensus description is a broadly egalitarian village society. The Jiangzhai re-analysis (Peterson & Shelach 2012) adds the necessary complication: the five groups differ in surplus and activity, households differ in assemblages, and the seeds of economic inequality are present within the communal frame — so "egalitarian" names a starting condition under slow strain, not a utopia. The matriarchy, accordingly, is retired in this article to where it belongs: the historiography of archaeology, as a documented episode in which a powerful theory wrote a society's family life for it. The Yangshao dead keep their kinship rules. The villages keep everything else.

The Banpo matriarchy is this hub's East Asian instance of a failure mode it has met before: a framework — diffusionism at one site, a king-list chronology at another — supplying answers the evidence never gave. The corrective is not to invert the claim (nothing here asserts a Yangshao patriarchy, for which the evidence is equally absent) but to return each question to the strength of its own record. Where the record is a ground plan, report a ground plan. Where it is silent, the article is silent with it.

XV. Toward Complexity — Dawenkou and the Longshan Horizon

This section is an outline only, and is labelled as such. It marks the threshold at which this hub's remit ends: the developments it names lead, within a few centuries, to the ranked societies, walled towns, and ultimately the early states of China, whose treatment belongs to a future division of this archive. Nothing in it is argued here; it is a signpost, drawn from the standard syntheses (Liu 2004; Underhill 2013), so that the reader can see where the Neolithic story of this article is heading and where this article stops.

East of the Yangshao world, in Shandong, the Dawenkou culture (c. 3800– 2600 BCE) shows the first clear hardening of social difference in the region's cemeteries: graves diverge sharply in size and furnishing, some with coffins, pig skulls and mandibles deposited as wealth, fine pottery, and jade, others with almost nothing — differentiation the Yangshao cemeteries of §XIV conspicuously lack. Dawenkou also yields a small corpus of large incised emblem-like signs on pottery, the most writing-like marks of the Chinese Neolithic, whose status is debated under the same discipline as §§IX and XIII and is not adjudicated here. The Longshan horizon (c. 2600–1900 BCE) that follows across the north brings the full threshold assemblage: walled towns of rammed earth, fast-wheel black pottery of eggshell thinness, jade working of the first rank, scapulimancy — divination by heat-cracked shoulder bones, the direct ancestor of the Shang oracle-bone practice — and cemeteries in which rank is unmistakable. Between Longshan and the Bronze Age states lies the world of Erlitou and the early dynasties. That world is literate history's antechamber, and it is not Before Kemet's ground. The signpost stands; the article turns back to its own assessment.

XVI. Assessment — Three Independent Proofs

The Neolithic was not one invention. East Asia proves it three times, on three different kinds of evidence, and the proofs are independent of one another — each would stand if the other two fell.

The first proof is pottery-first. At Xianrendong, fired clay vessels were made and used for cooking by Ice Age foragers in contexts dated to 20,000–19,000 years ago; at Yuchanyan and across the early Jōmon, the practice spreads and sustains. Pottery in East Asia precedes farming by some ten thousand years and precedes Near Eastern pottery by several thousand. There was nothing to copy and no package to receive: the technology was invented here, for foragers' purposes, in a sequence that reverses the textbook order. A trait cannot be an echo of something that did not yet exist.

The second proof is forager sedentism. The Jōmon built pit-house villages, cemeteries, storage systems, lacquer workshops, exchange networks, and — at Sannai-Maruyama — a settlement of hundreds maintained for seventeen centuries, on fish, game, and managed forest, without staple agriculture. Sedentism, complexity, craft specialisation, and symbolic elaboration — the social contents the old model reserved for farmers — are all attested in East Asia in their absence. Whatever the Neolithic is, it is not a single door through which societies pass in one order; the Jōmon walked around the door for ten thousand years and built on the other side of the wall.

The third proof is separate domestications. When East Asia did farm, it farmed its own plants: broomcorn and foxtail millet from northern wild grasses, rice from the wetlands of the centre and south — domestications whose progenitors grow only here, whose archaeobotanical sequences (Cishan's pits, the Yangtze's spikelet bases, Jiahu's border-country rice) are documented on East Asian ground, and whose products fed the Yangshao villages and, in time, a civilisation. The debates this article has reported — over Cishan's earliest dates, over Jiahu's rice, over the tempo of domestication — are debates within that independence, about its chronology and mechanism. No position in them derives an East Asian grain from a western field.

What East Asia does not show should be entered with the same plainness this hub applies at Mehrgarh. It does not show writing in the Neolithic: Jiahu's signs are sign use, Banpo's are pot-marks, and the script arrives with the Shang, five thousand years after Jiahu — a gap this article has refused to paper over at every occurrence. It does not show a matriarchal (or any archaeologically visible) kinship order in the Yangshao villages. It does not show domesticated rice at Jiahu, or domesticated millet at 10,000 BP at Cishan, at the strength those claims are popularly printed; it shows cultivation and use, with domestication following its own slow clock. And it does not show isolation: from the Jōmon's obsidian routes to the later traffic across the steppe that carried broomcorn west and, eventually, wheat east, East Asia was connected — connected, but not derived. Its Neolithic is a second, independent answer to the question the post-glacial world asked everywhere at once: how to live, settled and stored and symbol-making, in one place. The Fertile Crescent answered with wheat, sheep, and aceramic villages that learned pottery late. East Asia answered with pots before ploughs, villages before crops, and grains of its own. Both answers are attested. Neither is the other's echo.
