This is a Tier B article: Ancient Tradition. Its objects are ancient textual systems — omen compendia, a diagnostic handbook, therapeutic recipe collections, and incantation series — preserved on cuneiform tablets and studied here as texts and as documented practices. That double description is the article's discipline, and it needs stating plainly at the outset, because the material invites confusion in both directions.

On one side stands what can be reported as documented practice: that scholars copied, catalogued, and consulted these series; that a diviner slaughtered a sheep, inspected its liver in a fixed order, and delivered a written report; that a healer visited a sick man's house, worked through his symptoms from head to foot, and pronounced whether he would live or die; that drugs were pounded, sifted, boiled in beer or oil, and applied as drinks, salves, bandages, fumigations, and enemas. All of this is attested in the tablets, letters, reports, colophons, and catalogues, and it is reported here as practice, with its sources named.

On the other side stands what the systems themselves claimed. The omen series claim that the gods write signs into the sky, the liver, and the newborn child, and that the trained reader can recover their verdicts. The medical series claim that sickness is sent — by a god, a ghost, a demon, a witch, a broken oath — and that the right incantation, spoken over the right preparation at the right hour, can lift it. The scholarly tradition claims, further, that the whole body of knowledge descends from the gods themselves, delivered before the Flood by the antediluvian sages, the apkallu. Those are the tradition's claims. This article reports them, in the tradition's own terms, and labels them as claims wherever they appear. It does not endorse the efficacy of a single prescription, and it does not narrate a single omen's prediction as an event that happened.

What remains, held apart in that way, is not diminished. It is, on the contrary, one of the most remarkable intellectual constructions of the ancient world: a literate, systematising culture that spent well over a thousand years turning observation into lists, lists into series, and series into libraries — and that left the working manuals behind, in clay, for us to read. Mesopotamia's scribal sciences are the subject. Their procedures, given here in the fullest detail the sources allow, are the evidence.

I. What This Article Is — Tier B and Two Kinds of Truth

Every hub in this archive badges its articles, and the badge is a promise about how the sentences inside may be read. A Tier A article promises excavated, dated, published evidence. A Tier C article promises a modern construction, documented at its real date and in its makers' own terms. This article is Tier B, and its promise is narrower and stranger than either: the things it describes are neither dug out of the ground as objects nor invented in modern times. They are systems written down by ancient scholars — and a written system makes two kinds of statement at once, which the reader must never allow to merge.

The first kind is procedural and institutional, and it is as checkable as anything in Tier A. A series has a number of tablets, an order, an incipit, a catalogue entry, a colophon naming its copyist or its royal owner. A profession has titles, a training, a place at court or in a temple household. A rite has a sequence: these words, these materials, this hour of the night. Statements of this kind — the Diagnostic Handbook runs to forty tablets; the Maqlû ceremony occupied a single night; the extispicy report was written and could be checked against a second sheep — rest on the tablets and on the modern editions named in the Sources, and this article makes them at full strength.

The second kind is the system's own claim about the world, and it is reported, never adopted. When a tablet says that a lunar eclipse portends the death of a king, the article's sentence is that the series assigns that apodosis to that protasis. When a diagnostic entry names the "hand of a ghost" as the cause of a seizure, the article reports the entry's aetiology in the entry's own language, attributed. When the tradition traces its learning to Ea, god of wisdom, speaking through the seven antediluvian apkallu, the article presents that descent as what it is in the sources: the scholars' own account of where their knowledge came from — a claim about origins, made by the tradition, preserved in its catalogues and lists of sages. The companion article on the Antediluvian King Lists reads the kingly side of that same deep past under the same rule.

// A NOTE ON VERBS THROUGHOUT //

"The series says," "the entry assigns," "the handbook pronounces," and "the tradition claims" are the normal forms in this article wherever a prediction, a cause, or a cure is at issue. "The diviner slaughtered," "the scribe copied," and "the healer bandaged" are the normal forms wherever a documented human action is at issue. The distinction is not pedantry. It is the whole tier system in miniature, applied sentence by sentence.

One further boundary belongs here. Modern readers meet this material under modern words — "astrology," "magic," "medicine," "science" — and every one of those words cuts the corpus at a joint the corpus itself does not have. The Mesopotamian scholars organised their learning not by our disciplines but by series and by crafts: the craft of the āšipu, the craft of the bārû, the scribal art itself, ṭupšarrūtu. Francesca Rochberg's The Heavenly Writing (2004), a foundation of this article, argues the point at length for the celestial sciences: omen-reading, observation, and mathematical astronomy were one scribal culture, practised by the same men, copied onto the same library shelves. Jean Bottéro, in Mesopotamia: Writing, Reasoning, and the Gods, makes the complementary argument for the list itself: the Mesopotamian list — of signs, of words, of omens, of symptoms, of drugs — is not a primitive failure to reach theory. It is the characteristic instrument of a reasoning that worked by exhaustive arrangement. This article follows both guides. It will speak of "medicine" and "divination" because English must, but it will keep returning to the units the sources themselves use: the scholar, the series, the tablet, the entry, the procedure.

The corpus, finally, in outline. Three great omen compendia organise the readable world: Enūma Anu Enlil, the celestial omens; Šumma ālu, the terrestrial omens of city and house; and Šumma izbu, the omens of anomalous births (§§V–VII). One solicited oracle stands apart from the unsought signs: extispicy, the inspection of the sacrificed sheep's entrails, practised by the bārû (§VIII). Two medical corpora organise the sick body: the Diagnostic Handbook, Sakikkû, which reads symptoms and pronounces outcomes (§IX), and the therapeutic texts, which prescribe (§§XI–XII). Between and around them stand the incantation series — Maqlû, Šurpu, and their companions — where diagnosis, ritual, and drug meet (§XIV). Behind them all stands the institution that trained the men, kept the tablets, and copied them for a millennium and more (§§XVI–XVII). The article takes the scholars first, because in this world the knowledge and the knowers cannot be separated.

II. The Two Scholars — The Āšipu and the Asû

A sick Mesopotamian of means did not send for "a doctor." He sent, in the developed practice of the second and first millennia BCE, for one or both of two differently trained experts, whose titles the tablets keep distinct even where their work overlapped. The āšipu — the word is also written with its older or more vernacular doublet mašmaššu — was the incantation and ritual expert: the scholar who diagnosed, pronounced the prognosis, identified the supernatural sender of the illness, and deployed the spoken and enacted remedies — incantations, rituals, amulets, purifications — that his craft commanded. The asû was the practitioner of the drug-lore: the expert in medicinal substances and their preparation, who compounded and applied the potions, salves, bandages, and other physical treatments that the therapeutic texts prescribe. Older English scholarship rendered the pair, too neatly, as "exorcist" and "physician," and then, more neatly still, as "magic" and "medicine." The tablets support the titles. They do not support the neatness.

// THE ĀŠIPU //
Incantation & ritual expert

Also called mašmaššu. Diagnosed from the Diagnostic Handbook, pronounced prognosis, identified the divine or demonic sender, and treated by incantation, ritual, amulet, and purification — his craft is āšipūtu.

// THE ASÛ //
Expert in drugs & preparations

The practitioner of the materia medica: compounding, preparing, and applying potions, salves, bandages, suppositories, and enemas. His craft is asûtu. The Code of Hammurabi already regulates his fees and liabilities for operations.

// THE BĀRÛ //
The diviner of the entrails

The third scholar of this article: the haruspex who put questions to Šamaš and Adad and read the answer in the sacrificed sheep's liver and lungs (§VIII). His craft is bārûtu.

// THE ṬUPŠARRU //
The scribe-scholar

Underlying all three crafts: the literate scholar, trained in the scribal art (ṭupšarrūtu), without whose copying, cataloguing, and commentary none of the series would exist at all.

The Old Picture — Two Professions, Two Worlds

The classic modern account descends from Edith Ritter's influential 1965 study of the two healing professions, and it drew a bright line. On one side, the āšipu: a temple-linked scholar-priest whose world was supernatural aetiology — gods angered, demons seizing, ghosts afflicting, witches working — and whose instruments were the word and the rite. On the other, the asû: a more secular craftsman whose recipe texts, strikingly bare of gods and demons, treat symptoms with substances, and whose world was the pharmacy. On this reading, Mesopotamia had two medicines — a magical one and an empirical one — running in parallel, and the recipe texts' silence about the supernatural was the signature of the second. The reading had real evidence behind it. The therapeutic prescriptions, in their thousands, do indeed proceed with barely a glance at aetiology: if a man suffers such-and-such, pound these drugs, he drinks them in beer, he will recover. And the Diagnostic Handbook, the āšipu's book, does indeed name a god, ghost, or demon in entry after entry. The division of the texts is genuine. The question is what it divides.

Geller's Revision — One Corpus, Overlapping Crafts

Markham J. Geller's Ancient Babylonian Medicine: Theory and Practice (2010), the anchor study for this section, works through the evidence for both professions — the titles in the lexical lists, the colophons that name copyists and owners, the catalogues of what each craft must master, the letters that show practitioners at work — and arrives at a deliberately less tidy picture, which subsequent scholarship has broadly worked within even where it argues the details. Its main points, as Geller states them, are these.

First, the two titles for the ritual expert are not two jobs. Āšipu and mašmaššu are, in Geller's analysis, registers of one profession: āšipu the learned, prestigious term of scholarship and literature, mašmaššu the term of more general use — and the English "exorcist," applied to either, is far too narrow for a craft whose curriculum, set out in the so-called Exorcist's Manual (the catalogue KAR 44), runs from temple ritual and the purification of divine statues through diagnostics to the whole incantation corpus. Exorcism, in the modern sense of expelling a possessing spirit, was one procedure among many in a much larger ritual technology.

Second, the crafts overlap in the texts themselves. The āšipu did not merely pray over patients: the diagnostic craft was his, the prognosis was his, and therapeutic prescriptions — drugs included — circulated within his lore as well as the asû's; colophons and catalogues refuse to sort the medical corpus into two sealed libraries. JoAnn Scurlock's Sourcebook for Ancient Mesopotamian Medicine (2014), which translates the corpus whole, accordingly reverses the old English labels altogether, rendering āšipu as "physician" — the scholar-diagnostician whose role-set comprised diagnostician, prognostician, exorcist, and healer — and asû as "pharmacist," the compounder of the drugs the diagnosis called for. The reversal is itself the lesson: the Akkadian titles name competences and trainings, not the modern boundary between a doctor and a priest.

Third — and here Geller both keeps and qualifies the old picture — the two crafts did maintain distinguishable profiles, and could stand as alternative, even competing, systems of explanation at the bedside. The āšipu's characteristic question was who sent this, and what will happen; the asû's characteristic act was preparing what would treat it. A patient, or a family, could consult both, in sequence or together, and the letters from the Assyrian court — where kings kept both kinds of expert on staff and played their opinions against each other — show exactly that consultation pattern at the highest level. What the evidence will not bear is the inference from the recipe texts' silence to a secular worldview: a prescription is a workshop document, written to be used, and its omission of aetiology shows its genre, not its author's theology. Geller's own summary position — that Babylonian "magic" and "medicine" are best treated as complementary and inseparable aspects of one healing culture, distinguishable as crafts but not separable as worldviews — is the position this article adopts, attributed to him.

// THE SURGEON IN THE LAW CODE //

The asû of the early second millennium could also be a cutter. The Code of Hammurabi (c. 1754 BCE) fixes the fees for operations performed with the bronze lancet — on an eye, a wound, a bone — and the penalties when the patient dies or loses the eye, graded by the patient's status. No surgical treatise survives from Mesopotamia, and the therapeutic corpus is overwhelmingly conservative — bandages, oils, drugs — but the law code's clauses are documentary proof that operative intervention belonged, at least at that date, to the asû's regulated craft.

A third scholar completes the cast, though his patient was usually the king rather than the sick man: the bārû, the diviner, master of extispicy (§VIII) and interpreter of the unsought signs as well. At the Neo-Assyrian court of the eighth and seventh centuries BCE — the best-documented scholarly court of the ancient world, thanks to the letters and reports preserved from Nineveh — the king's experts included āšipus, asûs, bārûs, astrologer-scribes, and lamentation priests, corresponding with the throne in a steady traffic of reports, quotations from the series, and recommendations. The king did not experience these as separate magics and medicines. He experienced them as his ummânu — his master scholars (§XVI) — each reading the same written world with a different instrument. To understand the instruments, the article turns first to the claim that underwrote them all: that the writing being read was, in the end, the gods' own.

III. The Claim of Descent — Ea, the Gods, and the Apkallu

Mesopotamian scholarship did not present itself as invention. It presented itself as inheritance — and the inheritance had a named source, a named channel, and a named moment after which, in Berossus's later Greek summary of the tradition, nothing fundamentally new was discovered. The source was Ea — Sumerian Enki — god of the sweet waters of the Apsû, of wisdom, of craft, and of effective ritual knowledge, patron of Eridu, the city the King List names first. The channel was the seven apkallu, the antediluvian sages: semi- divine culture-bringers, in the iconography often fish-cloaked or bird-headed, whom the tradition placed beside the first kings before the Flood as the teachers of humankind. The moment was the Flood itself, after which the semi-divine sages were succeeded, in the lists that pair sages with kings, by human scholars — the ummânu — who stood to the apkallu as heirs to founders.

The claim is not a vague piety; the scholarly corpus states it in its own documentary forms. The Catalogue of Texts and Authors — a library catalogue that assigns the great series to their authors — credits bodies of learning to divine and antediluvian authorship: diagnostic knowledge is there presented as coming "from the mouth of Ea," written down, in the catalogue's phrase, at his dictation by Adapa, the first and greatest of the sages. Colophons and catalogues mark certain series as niṣirtu — secret, restricted knowledge, "the secret of the gods" — to be shown by the knowing to the knowing, and withheld from the uninitiated. Esagil-kīn-apli, the very human editor of the Diagnostic Handbook (§IX), frames his own editorial work within the same architecture: the forty tablets of his series answer, in the tradition's reckoning, to the number of Ea, the god who gave mankind diagnostic knowledge. The editor edits; the knowledge descends.

Tier discipline requires the next sentence, and the article makes it without flinching: this descent is the tradition's claim about itself, and it is reported here as such. No tablet excavated at Eridu was dictated by a god; no fish-cloaked sage has been dug out of a pre-Flood stratum, for there is no such stratum to dig. What archaeology and philology can document is the claim's career: that first-millennium scholars believed their series descended from divine and antediluvian sources, wrote that belief into their catalogues, used it to authorise their editions and to restrict their circulation, and carved the apkallu on palace walls and buried their figurines under floors as protectors of the houses the knowledge served. A claim believed, institutionalised, and acted on for a thousand years is a historical fact of the first order — about the believers. About the Flood-age sages themselves, it is evidence of nothing but the claim. The Antediluvian King Lists article treats the paired lists of kings and sages, and Berossus's fish-man Oannes — the Greek dress of Uanna, the first apkallu — in full.

The claim also did practical work. If the series are transcripts of a divine writing-system — Rochberg's "heavenly writing," the gods' script across sky, liver, and body — then the scholar's task is not to test the system but to read it correctly: to copy it exactly, arrange it exhaustively, comment on its difficulties, and guard it. That is precisely the behaviour the tablets display. And if sickness is a message as well as an affliction, diagnosis is a branch of reading too — the āšipu at the sick man's door doing with symptoms what the astrologer does with eclipses. The corpus's unity is the claim, at work.

THE SOURCE — EA / ENKI

God of the Apsû, of wisdom and of ritual efficacy. In the tradition's own catalogues, diagnostic and incantatory knowledge issues from Ea; the Diagnostic Handbook's forty tablets answer to his sacred number.

THE CHANNEL — THE SEVEN APKALLU

The antediluvian sages, Adapa first among them, placed beside the pre-Flood kings as teachers of humankind. Their figurines were buried apotropaically in houses; their images guarded palace walls.

THE HEIRS — THE UMMÂNU

After the Flood, in the lists' scheme, human master scholars succeed the sages. Historical ummânu — court scholars, series editors — are the claim's institutional reality (§XVI).

THE SEAL — NIṢIRTU, "SECRET"

Colophons restrict the great series as the secret knowledge of the gods, to be transmitted from the knowing to the knowing. Esotericism here is not a modern overlay; it is in the tablets' own paratext.

IV. The Omen as a Sentence — Protasis and Apodosis

Before the individual compendia, their shared grammar. Nearly every entry in every series in this article — celestial, terrestrial, teratological, extispicy, diagnostic — is built as a single conditional sentence of two clauses, for which modern scholarship uses the Greek-derived terms protasis and apodosis. The protasis, introduced by šumma, "if," states the sign: an observed, observable, or — the point matters — merely conceivable state of the world. The apodosis states what the sign, in the system, portends or pronounces: for the king, the land, the army, the harvest, the householder, or the patient. "If X, then Y" is the atom of Mesopotamian divination, and the compendia are its molecules, built by the tens of thousands.

The building method is systematic variation, and it explains both the compendia's size and their strangest entries. Take a sign-bearing object — the moon, a liver lobe, a newborn's ear — and vary its parameters one by one: month by month, day by day; right against left; above against below; white, black, red, green-yellow, variegated; present, absent, doubled, displaced. Each combination receives its entry, and its apodosis is assigned by the system's internal logic: by analogy (the lion's ear to the strong king), by wordplay between the Akkadian or Sumerian terms of protasis and apodosis, by the great polarities of right (favourable) against left (unfavourable) and light against dark, and by precedent — the remembered association of a sign with a famous king's fate, the so-called historical omens embedded especially in the extispicy tradition. The method guarantees completeness, and completeness guarantees impossibilities: eclipses on days of the month when eclipses cannot occur, births no midwife ever saw, liver configurations no sheep ever grew. The compendia contain them anyway, because the list, as Bottéro saw, aims at the exhaustive ordering of the possible, not at a register of the observed. A modern reader who asks of every entry "did this really happen?" is asking the list a question it was never built to answer.

The entries were made to be used, and their physical form serves the lookup. Within a tablet, entries are grouped by protasis, similar signs together; each entry is marked off by the single vertical wedge (the sign DIŠ) at its head; tablets are numbered within their series and identified by incipit — the first words of the first entry — and catalogues listing the incipits in order served as the system's finding device. When a sign occurred — an eclipse, a malformed lamb, a pig crossing the āšipu's path to a patient's house — the scholar turned to the series, found the protasis, and read off the apodosis. The surviving reports of the Assyrian court scholars show the practice in action: a phenomenon is reported to the king with its series entry quoted verbatim, the quotation doing the argumentative work. The compendium was not a book to be read through. It was an instrument to be consulted, and its authority in consultation was the authority of the written tradition itself.

// HOW TO READ AN OMEN ENTRY IN THIS ARTICLE //

Where this article quotes an entry, the quotation is short, confined to a named published translation, and identified as that translator's rendering. Where it paraphrases, it says the series "assigns," "pairs," or "pronounces" — never that the predicted event occurred. Apodoses concerning kings and lands belong to the state divination of a court culture; apodoses concerning householders and patients belong to the private consultation. The two scales coexist in the same series and are distinguished, where it matters, in the text.

One more distinction organises what follows: signs unsought against signs solicited. The omens of §§V–VII are unsought — the world offers them, and the art is recognition and lookup. Extispicy (§VIII) is solicited: a framed question, a provided sheep, an answer written for that question into that animal. Diagnosis (§IX) stands between: the symptoms are unsought, but the visit is a consultation, and the handbook opens not with a symptom but with a journey — the scholar already reading signs on his way to the sick man's house. The compendia follow, from the sky downward.

V. Enūma Anu Enlil — The Heavens in Some Seventy Tablets

The celestial omen series takes its title, in the Mesopotamian manner, from its opening words: Enūma Anu Enlil — "When Anu and Enlil…," the great gods who, in its prologue's framing, ordained the signs of heaven and earth. In its received first-millennium form it runs to some seventy tablets and roughly seven thousand omens — the figures are conventional because the series is reconstructed from many incomplete copies, Nineveh's above all, and because tablet divisions vary between recensions. Its materials are older than its form: celestial omens are attested already in the Old Babylonian period, in the early second millennium BCE, and the series grew by the same accretion, arrangement, and late-second-millennium editing that the other great series underwent. What the first-millennium scholar held was not a composition but a canon: the sky, edited.

The Four Sections

The series is organised by the divine owners of the phenomena, in four great sections. The first, Tablets 1–22 in the usual reconstruction, belongs to Sîn, the moon: the moon's appearances, its horns, its halo, its light and darkening — and, as a coherent sub-series within it, the lunar eclipses, the most dreaded of all celestial signs, whose apodoses run to the death of kings and the fall of lands. The second, Tablets 23–39, belongs to Šamaš, the sun: the sun's appearance at rising, its colour and marks, and again a block of eclipse omens. The third, Tablets 40–49, belongs to Adad, the storm god: thunder, lightning, rain, wind, cloud, and earthquake — weather admitted to the celestial series because it, too, is written in the sky. The fourth, Tablets 50–70, belongs to Ištar: the planets — Venus first and most fully — and the fixed stars, their risings, settings, conjunctions, and colours. Within each section the variation machine of §IV runs at full power: the same eclipse worked through every month, every day, every watch of the night, every quadrant of the moon's disc, each with its assigned land and its assigned fate.

Embedded in the lunar section is the series' most famous single document: the so-called Venus Tablet of Ammi-ṣaduqa (Tablet 63 in the received numbering), which pairs observations of Venus's appearances and disappearances, attributed to the reign of a king of the First Dynasty of Babylon in the seventeenth century BCE, with their omens. Modern scholarship has mined it for chronology; the ancient scholars who copied it for a thousand years valued it as the series in miniature — observation and apodosis bound together under a king's name. It is treated, with the rest of the Babylonian sky tradition, in the companion article Babylonian Sky Science, to which this article defers for the astronomy proper: the diaries, the goal-year texts, and the mathematical schemes that grew, in Rochberg's demonstration, from the same scribal culture as the omens and were never, in that culture, a separate "science" set against them.

Whom the Omens Concern — and What Was Done About Them

The apodoses of Enūma Anu Enlil are overwhelmingly public. They concern the king, the land, the army, the harvest, the neighbouring lands and their kings — the celestial series is state divination, and its natural home is the court. That home is documented in unusual fullness: the letters and reports of the astrologer-scholars of Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal (seventh century BCE) quote the series constantly, report observed phenomena against its entries, and advise the king on what the entries require. What they could require was drastic. For the most dangerous lunar-eclipse apodoses — the death of the king — the system provided an institutional answer in the substitute king ritual: a stand-in was enthroned, dressed, and treated as king for the danger period (in the sources, up to about a hundred days), to draw the apodosis onto himself, while the true king withdrew under the title of "farmer." The substitute was afterwards put to death and buried with royal honours — the apodosis, in the system's logic, honourably discharged. More routinely, unfavourable signs of every class could be met with namburbi rituals — "undoing" rites, studied in Erica Reiner's Astral Magic in Babylonia (1995) — whose whole purpose was to avert an announced evil before it landed: the omen stated a verdict, and the ritual appealed it to the gods who had issued it, Šamaš and Ea above all. The system, that is, was never fatalistic in practice. A verdict written by the gods could be petitioned, by the gods' own procedures, for reversal — and the petition, too, was a written series, copied and catalogued like the omens it answered.

// THE HEAVENLY WRITING //

Rochberg's governing image, from the series' own idiom: the gods write the signs — the sky is a tablet, the phenomena are its script, and celestial divination is a branch of literacy. The image is the tradition's, not the modern scholar's decoration: Akkadian calls the starry sky šiṭir šamê, "writing of the heavens," and the same verb of writing serves for the marks in a liver and the symptoms on a body. One script, three surfaces — that is the intellectual unity of this article's whole corpus.

A worked example, paraphrased, fixes the form. Across the lunar-eclipse tablets, the series works an eclipse through its variables — date, watch of the night, quadrant first darkened, the shadow's direction, the disc's colour — and distributes the threatened fates among the four great lands, Akkad, Subartu, Elam, Amurru, quadrant answering to land. The king received the report with its recommended response, a namburbi or, at the extreme, the substitute: in form, a legal consultation — a code consulted, a case stated, a remedy prescribed from the same shelves.

VI. Šumma Ālu — The City, the House, and Everyday Signs

If Enūma Anu Enlil is the sky edited, Šumma ālu ina mēlê šakin — "If a city is set on a height," from its first entry — is the lived world edited. It is the largest of the omen series: in its fullest reconstruction, more than a hundred tablets — the catalogue tradition counts up to about 120 — carrying, by the modern editors' reckoning, well over ten thousand omens (some estimates run toward thirteen thousand). Terrestrial omen collections are attested from the Old Babylonian period; the series in something like its received shape is documented from the eleventh century BCE onward, and its fullest copies, once again, are Nineveh's. Its first entry sets its scale and its tone at once, in Sally Freedman's edition of Tablets 1–21:

If a city is set on a height, living in that city will not be good.
— Šumma ālu, Tablet 1, opening entry, in the edition of S. M. Freedman, If a City Is Set on a Height (1998)

From the city, the series descends through the whole fabric of ordinary life, and its table of contents is a map of what could carry meaning. Houses: their foundations, doors, walls, and beams; the creatures that enter them and the sounds heard in them. Animals in their dozens of tablets: snakes, scorpions, lizards, ants and other insects; dogs, pigs, cattle, sheep, donkeys, horses; the wild animals that stray into settled space; fish, frogs, and birds — the animal omens of Tablets 22–40 and 63–79 are among the series' largest blocks, now the object of the Vienna Bestiarium Mesopotamicum project. Fire, water, and weather at ground level. Human behaviour: dreams' daytime counterparts, sexual acts and their occasions, speech, laughter, stumbling, the body's involuntary signs. Nothing is too small. A moth in a man's house, a crow calling before an army on campaign, a snake seen at a particular hour of a particular day — each has, or is given, its entry and its apodosis, and the apodoses here are characteristically private where the celestial series' were public: they concern the householder, his family, his property, his health, his standing — the fortunes of the bēl bīti, the head of the house, in whose name the consultation was made.

Two features of Šumma ālu deserve emphasis against caricature. The first is that the series is not a chaos of superstitions but an arrangement, thematic tablet by thematic tablet, worked by the same variation method as the sky: the snake varied by place, hour, colour, direction of movement, and behaviour, entry by entry, until the permutations are spent. Whatever else it is, it is scholarship — the exhaustive cartography of a meaningful world, executed with the list as its instrument. The second is that the series already contains, inside itself, the germ of its own mitigation: a strand of entries appends to the dire apodosis the counter-rite — if a man sees such-and-such and wishes not to suffer its announced outcome, let him do such-and-such — the namburbi logic of §V operating at household scale. The terrestrial world announced more evils than the celestial one, because it was closer; and it is in Šumma ālu's world — the house, the street, the road to a patient's door — that the medical practice of §§IX–X will be found already reading signs before it ever reaches the patient.

The series also marks the omen culture's social breadth: the terrestrial signs occurred to everyone, and consulting a scholar about a snake or a household prodigy was an ordinary transaction of urban life. The scribal science was an elite literacy (§XVI), but its clientele, at this level, was the city itself.

VII. Šumma Izbu — Anomalous Births

The third great compendium reads the sign at its most intimate and most disturbing: the malformed newborn. Šumma izbu — "If an anomaly…" — takes its name from its subject, the izbu, the anomalous birth, human or animal, that departs from the expected form: parts missing, parts doubled, parts displaced, parts belonging to another species, colours wrong, births multiple or conjoined. In its received form the series runs to twenty-four tablets and, by the usual reckoning, some two to three thousand omens; Erle Leichty's edition, The Omen Series Šumma Izbu (1970), remains the standard point of entry. Birth omens are among the oldest attested — Old Babylonian collections precede the series by the better part of a millennium — and the received form, like its companions, is a late-second- to early-first-millennium editorial achievement, preserved most completely at Nineveh. Its editor is unknown; modern discussion places the systematisation somewhere between about 1100 and 700 BCE.

How the Series Is Built

The twenty-four tablets are a composite, and their seams are instructive. An opening block (Tablets 1–4) derives from an older series on pregnancy and birth, Šumma sinništu arâtma, "If a woman is pregnant." The izbu proper — malformed human births — occupies the central tablets (roughly 6–17), arranged, like the Diagnostic Handbook and by the same scholarly instinct, from head to foot, and within that order by the colour sequence white, black, red, green-yellow, variegated. Individual tablets take up single organs in turn — horns, eyes, ears among them. The closing tablets (18–24) pass to the animals: lambs and sheep (Tablet 5, set oddly early, may be the oldest stratum of all), goats, cattle and donkeys, horses, pigs, dogs, and the wild animals. The scale of the apodoses shifts with the subject: the human tablets speak, like the celestial series, to king and land — the malformed child is a public sign, a verdict on the realm — while the animal tablets speak increasingly to the owner of the flock or herd, the householder whose fortune the birth in his byre concerns.

A single tablet's run of entries shows the variation method at its most legible. Tablet 3 works, among other features, the newborn's ears; in the translation presented by Eleanor Robson from Leichty's edition, the sequence includes:

If a woman gives birth and the baby has lion's ears: there will be a strong king in the land. If a woman gives birth and the baby has no right ear: the prince's days will end.
— Šumma izbu, Tablet 3, in Leichty's edition, as presented by E. Robson (Cambridge, 2011)

The logic is on the surface, and Robson's presentation names its two engines: analogy — the lion's feature to the lion-like king — and the right/left polarity, by which the right side carries the favourable value, so that damage on the right portends ill and the corresponding damage on the left can portend long days for the prince. Nothing here is random, and nothing is observed in any modern sense: the entries are generated, ear by ear and side by side, by a grammar the scholars commanded completely. That generativity is the finding. A system that can produce the omen for a birth that never occurred is not a notebook of cases. It is a theory of the sign, expressed as a list.

The Series in Use — Reports to the King

That the system was used, and used at the highest level, is documented in the scholars' correspondence. Reports to the Assyrian king record anomalous births across the realm — an eight-legged piglet, preserved in salt by the man whose sow bore it and reported by name; twin calves and foals; conjoined lambs; a lion, in the report's claim, born to a cow — each matched against the series and its apodosis laid before the throne. One damaged letter, much cited, carries the system's honest limit: a scholar explains to the king that the series does not contain every possible anomaly — the gods could still surprise the best-prepared library. The remark is precious evidence, and this article weighs it as such: the scholars knew their compendium was a human arrangement of the divine writing, complete in intention, incomplete in fact. The royal astrologer Balasi, writing to Ashurbanipal about this very series, adds the professional's corollary — in the letter as modern editions render it, Šumma izbu is difficult to interpret, and no one who has not had it pointed out to him by a teacher can understand it. The series was a book; the book required the school. §XVI takes up the school.

VIII. Extispicy — The Bārû and the Sheep's Liver

All the signs so far were unsought. Extispicy — the inspection of the entrails, above all the liver, of a sacrificed sheep — inverts the relation: the questioner asks, and the gods answer, in flesh. The practitioner is the bārû, "the one who sees," whose craft, bārûtu, was in the Old Babylonian period already a learned profession with its own manuals, its own clay teaching models, and its own place at court — the diviner Asqudum served Zimri-Lim of Mari (eighteenth century BCE) as chief bārû and royal counsellor, and the Mari letters preserve extispicy at work in the day-to-day government of a kingdom a thousand years before Nineveh. In its standardised first-millennium form, the extispicy omen corpus itself became a series — the Bārûtu, arranged in ten chapters — consulted like the others, and the queries addressed to the sun god survive as a literature of their own. No procedure in this article is documented in fuller procedural detail, and none better deserves the step-by-step treatment the hub's method requires.

The Gods Addressed, and the Question Framed

Extispicy was a judicial act before it was a technical one. The gods addressed are Šamaš, the sun, lord of justice and of divination, and Adad, the storm god, together invoked as "lords of divination" and "lords of the decision." The questioner — king, official, or private client acting through the bārû — first framed the question, and framing was everything: an extispicy question is not "what will happen?" but a precisely bounded proposition, set within a stated period, to which the gods are asked for a firm yes or no. The surviving tamītu texts — oracle queries from the Assyrian court — show the framing at its most formal: the matter stated in full, the time limit stated, the alternatives stated, and the gods asked, in the query's recurring plea, for a firm positive answer. Vague questions were a procedural fault. The oracle answered what was asked.

The Procedure, Step by Step

The rite, reconstructed from the ritual texts, the query texts, the reports, and the Bārûtu corpus, proceeded in a fixed order:

1. Purification and preparation. The bārû prepared himself ritually — the diviner who approached the gods as a court approached a judge had to be clean, correctly dressed, and in the ritual texts' prescription, free of disqualifying impurity. The place was prepared, the gods invoked, and prayers of the ikribu type — the standard prayers accompanying the rite — were recited, presenting the questioner and the question.

2. Presentation of the question and the animal. The sheep — in the normative descriptions a sound, unblemished animal, a lamb or ram provided by the questioner — was brought before the gods, and the question was put, in the ritual's logic, into the animal: the gods are asked to place their answer, their written verdict, in this sheep's entrails. The animal is thus not a symbol of the answer. In the system's own terms, it is the answer's tablet — the medium into which Šamaš and Adad write.

3. Slaughter and opening. The sheep was slaughtered and opened, and the entrails exposed — the liver above all, with the lungs, the gall bladder, and the coils of the intestines all within the inspection's scope. Speed and order mattered: the features were read in the opened animal, in a sequence the manuals fix.

4. The inspection, feature by feature. The bārû then read the liver as the compendia taught him to read it — in a fixed order, conventionally beginning with the feature called the manzāzu, the "Station," a mark on the left lobe, and proceeding through the named features in their canonical sequence to the coils of the colon at the end: the "Path," the "Palace Gate," the gall bladder and its states, the "Finger" (the caudate process) and its shape, the lobes' surfaces, their marks, holes, crosses, and weapon-shaped signs, each feature assessed for presence, absence, size, position, and side — right favourable, left unfavourable, in the system's polarity, with the complication that "right" and "left" invert their reference according as the feature concerns the questioner or his adversary. Every feature was a protasis; the manuals supplied its apodosis; the inspection was, in form, a lookup conducted in flesh.

5. The tally and the verdict. The favourable and unfavourable features were counted against each other — the reports and manuals work with the balance of the signs — and the verdict pronounced: favourable, unfavourable, or, in the honest cases the texts acknowledge, not clear enough to act on. A verdict was a property of the whole configuration, not of any single mark: the craft lay in the weighing.

6. The check — a second sheep. A single extispicy was not, for weighty matters, the end. The procedure provided for repetition: a second animal, a check inspection (the technical term is piqittu), against the first. The Mari correspondence shows kings ordering checks and diviners reporting confirmations and contradictions alike. A system that builds in replication is making a methodological claim about itself, and this article records the claim as procedure — the strongest internal evidence that the practitioners understood their verdicts as corrigible readings, not as inspirations.

7. The written report. The result was committed to clay. Old Babylonian extispicy reports — terse, dated, signed documents listing the features found and the verdict — survive in quantity from Mari and elsewhere, and the first-millennium court queries preserve question and answer in formal pairs. The report closed the procedure and entered the archive: divination, in Mesopotamia, left a paper trail in baked earth, auditable by the king and by posterity.

The Clay Livers — Teaching Models and Memory

The craft taught itself with models. Clay models of the sheep's liver — hand-sized, shaped to the organ, their surfaces divided into zones and inscribed with the omens belonging to the features of each zone — survive from the Old Babylonian period, most famously a group from the palace at Mari and the celebrated Sippar model now in the British Museum, its surface ruled into fifty-five compartments with peg-holes by which features could be marked. Some models carry "historical" omens, attaching a configuration to the fate of a famous king — the liver that "was thus" when a named catastrophe befell a named ruler — the profession's memory cast in clay. The models are, for this hub's purposes, the single clearest artefacts of the whole scribal-science culture: they are simultaneously teaching aids, reference summaries, and claims about precedent — a syllabus you can hold in one hand. That Babylonians knew more of sheep anatomy than of human — Geller's dry observation, from the absence of any human dissection tradition in the sources — is the models' corollary: the one interior the culture opened systematically was the sacrificial animal's, and it opened that interior for a thousand years.

// WHAT EXTISPICY IS EVIDENCE FOR //

The procedure documents a disciplined, replicable, archived consultation practice, with framed questions, fixed inspection order, tallying rules, confirmation by second inspection, and written reports. It does not document that the verdicts were true. The article reports the discipline; the truth of the verdicts was the tradition's claim, and is labelled accordingly wherever an apodosis is quoted or paraphrased in these pages.

IX. The Diagnostic Handbook — Sakikkû

With the Diagnostic Handbook the omen sentence turns upon the human body. The work's Akkadian title is Sakikkû — from Sumerian SA.GIG, conventionally rendered "symptoms," more literally the "sick" or "diseased" cords or sinews — and its incipit names its user and its scene: "When the āšipu goes to the house of a sick person." In its received form it runs to forty tablets and more than three thousand entries, each in the omen form: if the patient shows this sign, then this is the diagnosis — characteristically naming the sender, the "hand" responsible — and this the prognosis. It is the most consequential medical text to survive from the ancient world before Hippocrates, and unlike the Hippocratic corpus it comes with its editor's name attached.

Esagil-kīn-apli and the Editorial Act

The editor was Esagil-kīn-apli of Borsippa, ummânu — chief scholar — to the Babylonian king Adad-apla-iddina (reigned 1067–1046 BCE), in the eleventh century BCE. His editorial work is documented with a fullness almost unique in ancient literature: two surviving catalogues of the handbook — one Neo-Assyrian, one Neo-Babylonian — list the incipit and line-count of every tablet, and between the catalogue of Sakikkû and that of its twin series, the physiognomic omens Alamdimmû, they preserve the editor's own manifesto. In it, as the modern editions render his words, Esagil-kīn-apli states that the diagnostic omens had since long ago not been organised into a new edition — that they lay "tangled like threads," with no master edition — and that he has now assembled and ordered them. The sentence deserves its fame. It is the voice of an editor who knows he is editing: older materials, disordered by transmission, are to be given a received form. The forty-tablet Sakikkû of the first millennium — the text Heeßel's Babylonisch-assyrische Diagnostik (2000) edits and analyses — is that form, and the tradition remembered whose form it was: the Uruk List of Sages and Scholars, written centuries later, still pairs Esagil-kīn-apli with his king. At the same time, and characteristically, the catalogue tradition credits the work's authorship to Ea (§III): the human editor orders what the god authored. Both statements are in the sources, and this article reports both, at their different levels.

The Architecture — Six Sub-Series, Head to Foot

The forty tablets fall into six sub-series of unequal length, and their order is the handbook's intellectual signature:

TabletsSub-SeriesOrganising Principle
1–2 The approach Omens met on the way to the patient's house and at his door — the diagnosis begins before the patient is seen
3–14 The inspection The body examined a capite ad calcem, from head to foot — the first seven of these twelve tablets are devoted to the head alone
15–25 The course in time The illness classified by its duration and its behaviour across days; fever and its presence or absence
26–30 Neurological syndromes Seizures, epilepsy in its named varieties, stroke, paralysis — the great territory of the "hand" of god, ghost, and demon
31–34 Fever and skin Continued fevers; skin lesions and their colours and distributions
36–40 Women and infants Gynaecology, pregnancy, and the illnesses of infants and children (some tablets of the sequence are lost)

Within the great inspection sub-series, the ordering runs deeper than head-to-foot. Entries are grouped by symptom and then permuted by the handbook's axes: the side affected — left against right, with the polarity of the omen tradition — and the colour of the sign, in a fixed sequence (red or brown, yellow or green, black, white, and the intermediate hues). The result is a diagnostic matrix: for a given part and a given sign, the handbook aims to have an entry for every combination the axes generate. Whether any practitioner ever met the full permutation in a patient is beside the point, and the handbook does not care: like the omen series from which it descends, it is built for coverage. The sick body, like the sky, is to be completely legible — every configuration already written, somewhere, in the forty tablets.

Prognosis — The Characteristic Output

What the handbook characteristically produces is not a prescription — it contains almost no treatments at all — but a prognosis: he will recover; he will linger and then recover; his days will be long but he will die; do not approach him, he will die. The verdicts are stated in the apodosis with the same flat authority as an eclipse omen's, and they include the system's most socially significant output: the identification of the cases not to take. Entries whose prognosis is hopeless can direct, in effect, that the patient not be treated — a provision that protected the practitioner from the consequences of a death foretold, and that tells the modern reader the prognosis was a public, consequential act, not a private opinion. Between the diagnosis — the naming of the sender, "hand of such-a-god," "hand of a ghost," "hand of mankind" (§XV) — and the prognosis, the handbook's entry completes the āšipu's characteristic work: to say what this is, whose it is, and how it will end. Treatment, where treatment follows, belongs to the therapeutic corpus (§§XI–XII) and could be undertaken by either craft. The division of the written corpus — a diagnostic book that does not treat, recipe books that barely diagnose — is the textual fact from which the two-professions debate of §II starts, and Heeßel's structural analysis remains its fullest modern treatment.

A note on the first two tablets, the corpus's most honest pages. Before the āšipu sees the patient, the handbook reads his journey: the animals, persons, and objects met on the way, the state of the house at his arrival — each an entry, each already diagnostic. A parti-coloured pig met en route carries a prognosis for the patient not yet seen. The handbook's logic is stricter than the modern charge of superstition allows: there is no boundary at the sickroom door, because there is no boundary anywhere — the world is one text, and diagnosis is the reading of all of it that bears on this patient, from the street to the skin.

X. A Diagnosis in Practice — How a Case Proceeded

The corpus, assembled into a single consultation, yields a procedure as definite as extispicy's — and reconstructing it, step by step from the handbook, the therapeutic texts, the incantation series, and the letters, is the best way to see the scribal sciences as their clients saw them: not as books, but as a visit. What follows is a composite, built only from documented elements; no single tablet narrates a whole case, and this article does not pretend otherwise.

1. The summons and the journey. The āšipu is called to the house. His handbook's first tablets govern the walk itself: what he meets, what he sees at the door and in the courtyard, is read as the case's preamble. He arrives, in the system's terms, already informed.

2. The approach and the inspection. The handbook's second tablet-group opens with the approach to the patient and the third begins the inspection proper, from the head downward: the head's heat and pain, the eyes, the ears, the nose and mouth, the neck, the trunk, the limbs, the skin — colour, temperature, moisture, movement, sensation, the state of the body's surfaces and orifices. The examination is visual and manual; the corpus knows no instruments of examination and no opening of the human body, and its diagnostic vocabulary is correspondingly a vocabulary of the surface, refined to an extraordinary fineness of distinction.

3. The history in time. The middle sub-series govern what the modern clinician would call the history: how many days the illness has run, how it behaves through the day, whether fever attends it and of what pattern. Duration is itself diagnostic in the handbook — the same symptom on the first day and on the fifth is not the same entry.

4. The diagnosis — naming the hand. From the configuration of signs the āšipu names the sender: a named god, the patient's own god or goddess withdrawn, a ghost — of the family, of a stranger, of one dead violently or unburied — a named demon, the consequence of a broken oath or an incurred curse, witchcraft worked by human hands, or, in the entries' own residual category, the "hand of mankind," affliction from human and natural causes without a named supernatural sender (§XV). The naming is not decoration on the diagnosis. It is the diagnosis, because it selects the remedy: each sender has its counter-procedure.

5. The prognosis. With the diagnosis, the verdict on outcome: recovery, recovery after lingering, death, or the refusal of the case. The prognosis is pronounced from the handbook's entries and is, in the court letters, reported upward with the same formality as an eclipse report — the king's āšipus prognosticate for the king, and their letters show them hedging, revising, and defending verdicts exactly as the astrologers do.

6. The treatment plan. Where the case is taken, treatment proceeds on the two tracks of the two crafts, usually together: the therapeutic track — drugs prepared and administered as §XII describes, by the asû or by the āšipu himself — and the ritual track: the incantations and rites directed at the named sender, from the short bedside incantation spoken over the drug itself to, in the gravest attributions, the great night-long series of §XIV. Prayers of the šu'illa type, "raised hands," work to reconcile the patient with his personal god, whose withdrawal the aetiology so often presupposes.

7. The follow-up in the text. The therapeutic prescriptions themselves encode the follow-up: they are written as sequences — if he does not improve, a second preparation; if that fails, a third — with alternative drugs noted against unavailability or cost, and with the prognosis restated at the prescription's end: he will recover. The case, like the extispicy, closes in writing.

Set beside extispicy, the consultation shows the system's deep symmetry. Both are framed consultations with a written instrument — the liver, the body — read in a fixed order against a written corpus, tallied into a verdict, checked and revised, and closed with a written record. The difference is the direction of the question. The bārû asks the gods about the future and reads the answer in an animal opened for the purpose. The āšipu asks the signs about a present affliction and reads the answer on a body opened by illness itself. In the scholars' own understanding, both readings are of the same script. The next sections turn from the reading to the remedies the reading prescribed.

XI. The Therapeutic Corpus — Materia Medica

Against the Diagnostic Handbook's forty tablets of prognosis stands the therapeutic corpus: the recipe literature, preserved in the great modern collection known by its German edition's siglum, BAM (Die babylonisch-assyrische Medizin in Texten und Untersuchungen, Köcher, 1963–1980), and now read most accessibly in Scurlock's Sourcebook (2014). Its scale matches the diagnostic corpus: hundreds of tablets and fragments, thousands of prescriptions. Its organisation is the mirror of the handbook's — where Sakikkû proceeds by the patient's signs, the therapeutic series proceed by the seat of the affliction, head downward in their turn: texts for the head, the eyes, the ears, the teeth and mouth; for the chest and lungs; for the stomach and belly; for the kidneys and the rectum; for the limbs; for the skin — the same a capite ad calcem instinct, applied to cure rather than to verdict. The recent identification, within the Nineveh material, of a structured therapeutic compendium — the "Nineveh Medical Encyclopaedia" of the BabMed project's work under Geller: twelve treatises in some fifty chapters, each prescription built of diagnosis, preparation, administration, and prognosis — confirms that the recipe literature, too, was edited into series, not merely accumulated.

The Prescription as a Form

A therapeutic prescription is a small procedural text with a fixed anatomy, and its fixedness is its information. It opens with the indication, in the omen's own grammar: "If a man suffers from such-and-such…" — the symptom or named illness, stated in a clause. It continues with the ingredients, listed with their quantities, in shekels and in handful-measures and in the recipe's own relative terms. It prescribes the processing — pound, crush, sift, mix, boil, steep, dry — in the imperative, second person, the voice of a master to a practitioner. It specifies the vehicle and the administration: he drinks it in beer, in wine, in oil, in water; you anoint him; you bandage him; you fumigate him; you pour it into his anus. And it closes, characteristically, with the outcome in the apodosis position: "he will recover." The recipe is an omen sentence that has learned to cook: protasis the affliction, apodosis the cure, and between them, in place of a prediction, a procedure.

The Three Kingdoms of the Pharmacy

The ingredients — the materia medica — are drawn, in the prescriptions and in the pharmacological lists that support them, from all three kingdoms of the Mesopotamian natural world. Plants dominate overwhelmingly: seeds, roots, barks, leaves, flowers, resins, and gums of garden herbs, field plants, and trees — licorice, myrrh, cedar, juniper, tamarisk, date palm, willow, and a long roll of Akkadian and Sumerian drug names whose bearers §XIII will have to leave, in honesty, partly unnamed in modern terms. Minerals form the second kingdom: salts and alums, sulphur, bitumen, lapis and other stones ground to powder, shells and coral-like substances, the earths and clays of named places. Animal products form the third: fats and oils, milk and cheese and honey, blood, bone, horn, hair, wool, dung and urine in the strand of the corpus modern scholarship calls, after the German, Dreckapotheke — "filth-pharmacy" — some of whose outlandish ingredient names are now argued, notably against Köcher's older "secret names" hypothesis, to be alternative, vernacular, or punning names for ordinary substances rather than a code. The pharmacological literature that organised this wealth was itself serial: the great drug-list Uruanna, a multi-tablet synonymy of drug names and their equivalents, re-edited — its colophon proudly records — under Ashurbanipal himself, who is said there to have checked the drugs and their equivalents, removed duplicates, and arranged what older handbooks had lumped together; and the herbal Šammu šikinšu, "The plant, its appearance…," which identifies drugs by describing the plant's look, its habitat, and the time and manner of its gathering — a field guide, in list form, to a pharmacy that began in gardens, in the wild, and in the import trade.

PLANTS

The bulk of the materia medica: seeds, roots, barks, leaves, resins. Gathered wild, grown in medicinal gardens, and imported — the texts name provenances and substitutes where a drug is costly or out of season.

MINERALS

Salts, alum, sulphur, bitumen, ground stones and shells. Mineral drugs are characteristically ground, levigated, and used in salves, washes, and fumigations.

ANIMAL PRODUCTS

Fats, milk, honey, blood, bone, wool — and the Dreckapotheke strand, whose foul names are now widely read as alternative or vernacular drug names, not as a secret code (see §XIII).

THE LISTS BEHIND THE RECIPES

Uruanna, the drug synonymy; Šammu šikinšu, the descriptive herbal; the Pharmacist's Handbook and plant glossaries translated in Scurlock's Sourcebook — the reference shelf without which no prescription could be compounded twice the same way.

One absence must be registered, because popular accounts supply it from imagination: the corpus knows the drugs' preparation intimately and their theory hardly at all. No Mesopotamian Galen explains why a drug works by qualities and humours; the prescriptions do not argue, they instruct. What theory the system has lives elsewhere — in the aetiology of the diagnosis (§XV), which names the sender, and in the analogical logic of the lists, which sorts the world's substances by name, appearance, and equivalence. The long trial-and-error by which effective treatments were found — and some of the corpus's standbys, licorice and turmeric among them, have real pharmacology, as Scurlock's plant-by-plant studies argue — is nowhere recorded. The texts are the end of that process, frozen at the moment of the recipe. §XII opens the recipe up.

XII. Making Medicines — Preparations as Procedures

The hub's standing rule for this archive is maximum procedural detail for every repeatable physical process, and the therapeutic corpus rewards the rule: a Mesopotamian prescription is nothing but procedure. The operations below are the corpus's own repertoire, in the corpus's own sequence — from the raw drug to the patient's body — as the Nineveh Medical Project's synthesis of the techniques and Scurlock's translated texts document them.

Processing — From Drug to Ingredient

The raw materials were first reduced. Seeds, roots, and minerals were pounded and crushed — in mortars of stone or wood, with pestles the texts name — and ground to graded fineness, to flour-fineness where the recipe demands it. Powders were sifted through sieves of reed or rush of graded mesh, the coarse returned and the fine kept. Ingredients were weighed — in shekels, the recipe quantities running from fractions to dozens of ingredients in a single compound — or measured in the handful and the vessel-measures of the workshop. Drying, roasting, and charring prepared other drugs; steeping and maceration, in water, beer, wine, milk, or oil, over stated times — overnight, for days, sealed — prepared others again. The recipes are exact about sequence and about simultaneity: what is pounded together, what is added after, what must not boil, what must be left under the stars. A prescription is a timetable as much as a formula.

The Vehicles and the Forms

Processed drugs reached the patient in a defined set of forms, which the corpus deploys with a consistent, if never fully explained, matching to the seat of the illness. Potions: the drug decocted or steeped in beer — the commonest vehicle — in wine, in water, in milk, or in oil, and drunk, often on an empty stomach, often in repeated doses across days. Decoctions proper: ingredients boiled down in a stated liquid, sometimes in the small copper pot the texts specify, the liquid strained and administered warm. Salves and ointments: the drug ground into a base of oil, fat, or wax, and smeared or rubbed on the part — the standard form for skin, eye, and ear, and for the massage that accompanies the great rituals. Poultices and bandages: the preparation spread on cloth or applied as a paste and bound on — bandaging is among the corpus's commonest verbs — for swellings, wounds, bites, and, in the multi-form treatments, for internal complaints treated from outside as well as in. Washes and baths: decoctions poured over the patient or used to bathe the part. Fumigations: the drug burned on a brazier or in a fumigation vessel, the smoke directed to the patient, the room, or the body's orifice concerned — a form that stands, in the corpus's own practice, exactly on the medicine/ritual boundary, since fumigation also expels demons. And the internal forms that surprise modern readers least once the corpus's logic is granted: tampons and suppositories, the drug formed with fat or dough and inserted; and enemas, decoctions poured or injected into the rectum by tube — prescribed, in the texts' distribution, characteristically for the diseases of the belly and the lower body, as the external forms are for the surface, though the corpus cheerfully combines all of them against a single stubborn case.

The combination is the point, and one prescription type — paraphrased here from the stomach texts (BAM 575) as the Nineveh project's synthesis presents them — shows the full orchestra at work on a single piercing pain of the stomach. The patient drinks two pounded drugs in beer. A third drug is boiled down in oil and poured into his anus. Leaves of three plants are heated in water and he is washed with it. Juices of two further plants are poured into his anus. And a final pair of aromatics is boiled in a small copper pot, smeared on cloth, and he is bandaged with it. Drink, enema, wash, enema, bandage: five administrations, three routes, one indication, in one paragraph of clay. Whatever the modern pharmacologist makes of the ingredients, the practice documented here is not a charm muttered over a herb. It is a treatment protocol — sequenced, multi-modal, and written to be repeated by another practitioner, in another house, from the tablet alone.

Timing, Repetition, and the Second Prescription

The protocols run in time as the diagnoses do. Administrations are timed — at evening, at morning, before eating, for three days, for seven — and the ritual calendar enters the pharmacy without embarrassment: preparations are left overnight under the stars, compounded at stated hours, administered with the incantation spoken at the moment of application, the drug and the word timed to land together. Failure is provided for in writing: the series' prescriptions chain alternatives — if he does not recover, this; if not this, that — and note cheaper or more available substitutes against the first choice's cost, a practitioner's realism embedded in the canon itself. The corpus even preserves its quality marks: preparations are labelled in places as tried or proven, and one eye salve circulates under the authority-tag of a famous name — the "eye salve of Hammurabi" of the later copies. The tags are the tradition's claims for its recipes, and are reported as such; but the existence of the tagging is a fact about the tradition, and it is the fact of a craft that distinguished, in its own way, the tested from the merely inherited.

// THE WORKSHOP INVENTORY //

The procedures imply, and the texts name, a workshop: mortars and pestles of stone and wood; sieves of graded mesh; measuring vessels; the small copper pot of the decoctions; cloth for bandages and straining; tubes for the enema; braziers for the fumigation; vessels for steeping and sealing. Surgical instruments are the marked exception — the lancet belongs to the law code's asû (§II), not to the recipe corpus, whose cutting is confined to the drug, never, in the surviving texts, to the patient.

XIII. The Names We Cannot Read — The Plant-Identification Problem

Everything in §§XI–XII is documented procedure. One thing is not documented, and this article's honesty requires a section of its own: we often do not know what the drugs were. The prescriptions name their ingredients in Akkadian and Sumerian, in a pharmacological vocabulary built up over two thousand years. A modern reader who wants to know whether a given recipe "worked" must first answer a prior question — what plant, what mineral, what substance does this name denote? — and for a large part of the vocabulary that question has, at present, no secure answer.

The reasons are structural, and the scholarship — the plant-name studies surveyed in Maddalena Rumor's recent state-of-the-field review among them — states them plainly. The ancient identification literature is itself lists: Uruanna equates names with names, synonyms with synonyms, in chains that can circle without ever touching a description; Šammu šikinšu describes appearances, but in the compressed idiom of the list, and many of its descriptions fit several plants or none now known. Names shift across the two millennia of the corpus's life; the same name can denote different plants in different places and periods, and the same plant can carry a pharmacopoeia of names — learned, vernacular, foreign, and punning. The older method of identification, by etymology and cognate-hunting through the Semitic languages, produced confident equations that later work has repeatedly had to withdraw. Where a name is anchored by an unbroken tradition, by a description that fits one plant only, or by the plant's products surviving in the record, the identification can be secure — licorice (šūšu), turmeric (kurkanû), and a modest company of others stand on such ground, and Scurlock's plant-by-plant method — comparing every ancient use of a securely identified drug with its documented modern pharmacology — is built expressly to work only where the anchor holds. Beyond that company, the honest vocabulary of the field is perhaps, possibly, and unidentified, and it applies to a substantial fraction — by any count, the majority — of the drug names in the corpus.

// WHAT THIS ARTICLE THEREFORE DOES NOT DO //

It does not translate Akkadian drug names into Latin binomials except where the identification is presented as secure in the scholarship it cites. It does not assess any prescription's efficacy, because efficacy cannot be assessed for a formula whose ingredients cannot be named. And it treats popular lists of "Babylonian herbal remedies" that render every drug name as a familiar modern herb as what they are: modern constructions wearing ancient dress — the Tier C of the pharmacy, however venerable the tablets they decorate.

The problem cuts the other way as well, and fairness requires the admission. The unidentified names are not evidence that the drugs were imaginary or the practice a sham. They are evidence of a broken transmission — a learned vocabulary whose living referents died with the language that carried them. The practitioners who compounded the recipes knew exactly which plant each name meant: they gathered it, bought it, grew it, and described it in their herbal for the apprentice who did not yet know. The loss is ours, and it is philology's, and the correct scholarly posture — the one this hub applies to every tier — is to state the identification at exactly the strength its evidence has reached, plant by plant, and no further. The procedures of §XII survive entire. The pharmacy that filled them survives in part. Both statements are true, and the article has now made both.

XIV. Incantations at the Boundary — Maqlû and Šurpu

Between the handbook that diagnoses and the recipes that treat stands the third arm of the āšipu's craft: the great incantation series, in which the named sender of an illness is confronted by word and rite together. Two of them — the two the tradition itself canonised most fully — stand at the exact boundary this article has been tracing, and they divide the boundary between them by aetiology. Maqlû answers illness sent by human malice: witchcraft. Šurpu answers illness sent by the patient's own incurred guilt: the broken oath, the unknown sin. Both are called, in Akkadian, "Burning" — and in both, what burns is the vehicle onto which the evil has been transferred.

Maqlû — The Night Against the Witch

Maqlû, in its mature form — composed, on Tzvi Abusch's analysis in his edition, The Witchcraft Series Maqlû (2015), in the early first millennium BCE from a much shorter morning rite — comprises eight tablets of incantations, nearly a hundred in number, and a ninth, ritual tablet that cites each incantation by incipit and prescribes the acts that accompany it. Abusch's foundational demonstration, now the consensus frame, is that the series is not an anthology but a single ceremony: performed through one night, in the month of Abu (July/August), when the traffic of ghosts between the worlds was held to make the rite's targets most reachable. Its patient is characteristically a man of standing who attributes his illness, impotence, losses, or social ruin to kišpū — witchcraft worked against him by a warlock or, in the texts' stereotyped pairing, a witch.

The ceremony's three movements, as the ritual tablet orders them, proceed with the night. In the evening, the gods of the night are invoked as a court is convened, and the case is stated: the patient appeals to Šamaš, judge of gods and men, against his unknown accusers. Through the night's first great sequence (Tablets I–IV), figurines of the witch and warlock — made for the rite, in clay, dough, wax, and other materials the instructions specify — are tried, condemned, and destroyed: burned in the brazier before Girra, the fire god who executes the sentence, drowned in black liquid, laid face down and crushed. The destruction is juridical in form: the figurine stands for the witch in court, and the fire carries out a verdict, not a metaphor. Through the second sequence (Tablets V to VII, 57), the direction reverses — from the destruction of the sender to the release and protection of the patient: his house is fumigated, his body massaged with the prepared oils, the witchcraft is peeled away from him in the incantations' great recurring image, as garlic is peeled. Toward dawn, the final incantations accompany the patient's washing; Nusku, god of the lamp, is greeted as the night's protector, then Šamaš himself at sunrise; and the ceremony closes with the restored man contemplating his own image in a bowl of pure water — himself, returned to himself, the witchcraft undone. Drugs, fumigations, massage, washing: the whole therapeutic repertoire of §XII is inside the rite, timed to its hours and spoken over at every step. To ask whether Maqlû is "medicine" or "magic" is to ask the corpus to observe a boundary it never drew.

Šurpu — The Undoing of the Oath

Šurpu, edited by Erica Reiner (Šurpu: A Collection of Sumerian and Akkadian Incantations, 1958), is the rite for the sufferer who does not know what he has done. Its presiding concept is the māmītu: the oath that, once sworn, becomes an active power — the curse that seizes a man who has broken an oath, transgressed a taboo, or offended against the moral and social order, whether or not he knows it. The series, canonised at Nineveh in nine tablets (its materials older, its compilation placed by scholarship in the middle Babylonian period), is built for that ignorance. Its second tablet recites an exhaustive confessional catalogue — offences against gods, against parents and elders and neighbours; false weights and moved boundary stones; the yes spoken with the mouth and the no held in the heart; taboo foods eaten, taboo contacts incurred — naming every sin the patient might have committed, so that the right one, whichever it is, is named somewhere in the rite. The list is a diagnostic instrument for guilt, built by the same exhaustive method as the omen lists: coverage as cure.

The rite's central act gives the series its name. In Tablets V–VI, the patient takes in his hands, one by one, the vehicles of transfer — an onion, a tuft of wool, a cluster of dates, a mat's fringe, goat hair — each first wiped over his body or handled as the incantation directs, so that the guilt passes into it, and each then surrendered to the fire with its formula: as this is peeled, plucked, unravelled, and burned, and never returns to its place, so may the sin be peeled from the patient and never return. In Reiner's translation, the garlic formula runs, in excerpt: "My illness, my weariness, my guilt, my crime, my sin, my transgression — be peeled off like this garlic, so that the fire-god, the burner, consumes it today." The remaining tablets marshal the gods in long litanies as releasers, address Marduk-Asalluḫi, the divine āšipu whose consultation with his father Ea is the mythology of the craft itself (§XV), and close by consecrating the rite's own instruments. Reiner's dry observation, much quoted since, that burning plays a smaller part in the series than its title promises, is itself instructive: the fire is the destination of the transfer, but the work of Šurpu is verbal and juridical — confession, enumeration, appeal, release.

MAQLÛ — AGAINST THE WITCH

Eight tablets of incantations plus a ritual tablet; one night in Abu; figurines of the witch tried and burned before Girra; the patient fumigated, massaged, washed, and restored at dawn. Aetiology: kišpū, witchcraft sent by human enemies.

ŠURPU — AGAINST THE OATH

Nine tablets; an exhaustive confessional list for the sin the patient cannot name; transfer of guilt to onion, wool, dates, and garlic, burned before the gods. Aetiology: māmītu, the broken oath and the incurred curse.

THE SHARED GRAMMAR

Both series proceed by transfer and destruction: the evil is named, loaded onto a vehicle, and annihilated under divine judgement — with drugs, oils, and fumigations administered inside the rite, not beside it.

THE OTHER SERIES

Maqlû and Šurpu stand at the head of a shelf: Udug-ḫul ("Evil Demons"), Sag-gig-ga-meš ("Headache"), the namburbi corpus, and dozens more, catalogued in the Exorcist's Manual as the āšipu's required mastery.

XV. Hand of Ghost, Hand of God — The Language of Aetiology

The Diagnostic Handbook's diagnoses are cast in a single idiom, and the idiom is a whole theory of disease in two words: "hand of…" — qāt such-a-one. Hand of Ištar. Hand of Šamaš. Hand of Sîn. Hand of the patient's god, hand of his goddess. Hand of a ghost — specified, in entry after entry, with a casuistry of the dead: the ghost of his father, of his mother, of his brother; a ghost of one who died by violence, by drowning, by hunger; a roaming ghost, a ghost with no one to care for it. Hand of a named demon — the utukku, the alû, Lamaštu who seizes infants, the lilû and lilītu of the night. Hand of curse, hand of oath, hand of witchcraft. And, at the idiom's edge, hand of mankind — the affliction laid to human agency and the natural course of things, the nearest the handbook comes to a sender-less diagnosis, and a category whose very existence inside the idiom shows the idiom straining toward completeness.

The "hand" is not a metaphor the modern reader may dissolve into "cause." It carries the system's physics: the sender has laid hold of the patient — the seizure idiom runs through the corpus, demons "seize," diseases "grip," the hand "touches" — and treatment is correspondingly a release: the hand lifted, the grip loosened, the sender appeased, exorcised, or paid off. The personal god's role completes the circuit. Each person stood, in the aetiology's background, under the protection of a personal god and goddess; offence — sin, taboo-breach, the incurred māmītu — could drive the protectors away, and the unprotected man lay open to every hand that passed. Much of the ritual corpus, the šu'illa prayers above all, is therefore reconciliation-work: restoring the personal god to the patient's side, so that the hands lose their opening. The handbook's neurology sub-series (§IX) is the idiom's densest territory — the seizure disorders, with their sudden seizures and falls, are assigned across a fine grid of divine, ghostly, and demonic hands, distinguished by the attack's timing, its form, and its course, in entries whose clinical observation of the phenomena modern neurologists have repeatedly, and with due caution, admired.

Behind the whole idiom stands its mythology, in the dialogue the incantations rehearse without tiring — the pattern scholarship calls the Marduk–Ea dialogue. Asalluḫi-Marduk, the divine āšipu, sees the afflicted man and goes to his father Ea in the Apsû: "Father, such a sickness has seized him — I do not know what to do." And Ea answers, in the formula's fixed words: "My son, what do you not know? What can I add to your knowledge?" — and then adds it: the procedure, the drug, the rite, dictated from the Apsû to the god who will perform it, and through him to the human āšipu who performs it now, speaking the god's part. The dialogue is the aetiology's charter and the descent-claim of §III in narrative form: every correct treatment was once Ea's answer to Marduk's question, and the practitioner who recites it is not inventing a cure but re-issuing one. This article reports the dialogue as the tradition's account of its own authority — which is to say, as the most revealing document in the corpus of how the corpus wished to be understood.

// AETIOLOGY WITHOUT ANATOMY //

The system explains disease entirely by senders and seizures, and it does so without any doctrine of the body's interior: no human dissection, no anatomy beyond the surface and the sacrificial animal's opened trunk (§VIII). Geller's formulation stands: the Babylonians knew more about the anatomy of sheep than of human beings. The aetiology's sophistication is real — but it is a sophistication of agency, not of structure: who sent it, for what, and what will release it, asked of a body read, like everything else in this world, from its signs.

For the archive's wider map: the "hand of a god," the unquiet ghost, the diagnosable witch are the local, literate form of an aetiological world the whole ancient Near East shared — approached from the narrative side in the companion articles on Gilgamesh and the Antediluvian King Lists. What is specifically Mesopotamian is not the belief. It is the filing system: the hands enumerated, permuted, and shelved in forty tablets, so that a seizure at a stated hour, on a stated side, with a stated course, already has its sender named and waiting in the canon.

XVI. The Institution — Edubba, Ummânu, and the Scholarly House

Systems of this size do not maintain themselves. Behind the series stands an institution with three levels — a schooling, a profession, and a household economy of tablets — and the sources let all three be described.

The Edubba and the Making of a Scribe

Training began in the edubba, the "tablet house" — the scribal school, best documented for the Old Babylonian period, whose curriculum the school texts themselves preserve: the sign-lists and syllabaries first, then the great lexical lists that taught Sumerian by theme — trees, animals, stones, professions, places — then proverbs, model contracts, hymns, and mathematics, the whole crowned, for the scholarly minority who continued, by the specialised curricula of the crafts. The Exorcist's Manual (KAR 44) is such a curriculum preserved entire: a catalogue of the compositions "fixed for study and reading" for the mastery of āšipūtu, running from temple ritual through the diagnostic series to the whole incantation shelf — a syllabus that lets the modern reader watch a profession define itself by its reading list. The bārû and the astrologer had their corresponding canons; the asû's training is the least documented of the three, and the sources suggest what the colophons confirm for the others — that after the school, the real education was apprenticeship within a scholarly family, the craft descending, like the tablets, from father to son.

The Ummânu and the Scholarly Families

At the institution's summit stood the ummânu, the master scholar: counsellor to kings, editor of series, the human successor, in the lists' scheme (§III), of the apkallu. Esagil-kīn-apli (§IX) is the type — chief scholar to his king, remembered by name for eight centuries. Below the summit, the profession reproduced itself in houses: the best excavated example is the family of Kiṣir-Aššur of Assur, seventh century BCE, whose house library — some hundreds of tablets, overwhelmingly the āšipu's corpus — preserves, in its colophons, the training in progress: tablets copied by Kiṣir-Aššur as a junior apprentice (šamallû ṣeḫru), as apprentice, and as master in his turn, the exercises, excerpts, and full series of a career in clay. Houses like his — at Assur, at Babylon, at Uruk, where scholarly families can be followed for generations — were the system's real infrastructure: part school, part practice, part archive, holding the tablets as capital and the competence as inheritance. The late Babylonian temples inherited the role as the cuneiform world contracted, and it is in temple libraries — at Babylon, Borsippa, Sippar, Uruk — that the series were still being copied, with undiminished exactitude, in the last centuries BCE, by the last generations of men who could read them.

Commentaries — The System Thinking About Itself

A corpus copied for a millennium accumulates a second literature: the commentaries, which gloss the series' difficult words line by line, equate the rare drug name with the known one, and explain an apodosis by the wordplay that generated it — philology, pharmacology, and hermeneutics in one genre. It was this living voice that Balasi (§VII) insisted on: the series cannot be understood by one to whom it has not been pointed out. The tablets were the system's memory. The pointing-out was its mind.

XVII. Ashurbanipal's Library — The Vehicle of Survival

Nearly everything in this article is known because one king collected it and one city's burning preserved it. Ashurbanipal of Assyria (reigned 668 to about 631 BCE) assembled at Nineveh, in the palaces on the Kuyunjik mound, the largest tablet collection of the ancient world: more than thirty thousand tablets and fragments in the modern count of the excavated material, gathered by royal order from the temples and scholarly houses of Assyria and, above all, of Babylonia — sought out by the king's agents, copied by his scribes, seized in war, and stamped with his colophon. The king, who boasted in his inscriptions of his own scribal training — of reading obscure Sumerian and Akkadian, of mastering the craft his predecessors had left to servants — presents the collecting, in the library's own paratext, as a scholar's act. In the medical colophon preserved on the Nineveh therapeutic material, as the BabMed project presents it, the king declares that he wrote, checked, and collated tablets of medical prescriptions "from cranium to toenail" and placed them in his palace for his consultation and reading. The phrase is a colophon's boast and is reported as such; the collection it boasts of is an excavated fact.

For this article's corpus, Nineveh's role is specific and decisive. The received forms of Enūma Anu Enlil, Šumma ālu, Šumma izbu, the Bārûtu, Sakikkû, Maqlû, and Šurpu — the series as §§V–XV describe them — are known in their fullest extent from Nineveh copies, and the library's catalogues and acquisition records let the collecting itself be studied: which series were taken, in how many tablets, on clay and on the wooden writing-boards that have perished where the clay endured. Around the collection stands its working staff, the court scholars whose letters and reports — the astrologers quoting Enūma Anu Enlil, the āšipus prognosticating, the diviners reporting — fill the state archives and supply this article's evidence for the system in use. When Nineveh fell in 612 BCE, the burning palaces baked the library's clay hard; the tablets slept in the ruin until Layard's and Rassam's excavations of 1849–1853 brought them to the British Museum, where George Smith's 1872 reading of the Flood tablet announced to the modern world what the ruin had kept. The companion article on Gilgamesh tells that recovery from the epic's side.

Two cautions close the account. Nineveh is a seventh-century Assyrian window on a largely Babylonian tradition of far greater age, and the copies from Assur, Babylon, Borsippa, Sippar, Uruk, and Sultantepe correct the Nineveh picture wherever they survive. And survival is not innocence: a royal library collected what a royal court valued — the state omens and the āšipu's canon entire, the asû's recipes more partially, the unlettered healing of village and household not at all. The corpus is the scribal sciences as the scribes preserved them — its glory as evidence, and its limit as history.

XVIII. What This Corpus Is and Is Not

What the corpus is evidence for can now be stated at full strength, because the article has kept its claims sorted. It is evidence for a literate, systematising medicine-and-divination culture of extraordinary duration and self-consciousness: a culture that organised the sky, the city, the birth, the liver, and the sick body into written series of conditional entries; that edited those series, named its editors, and catalogued its shelves; that trained its practitioners in schools and scholarly houses, examined them against a syllabus, and set them to copy the canon as the price of mastering it; that conducted its consultations by fixed procedures — framed question, ordered inspection, tallied verdict, confirming second inspection, written report — and its treatments by written protocols of pounding, sifting, boiling, drinking, anointing, bandaging, fumigating, and purging, sequenced in time and provided with alternatives against failure. It is evidence, further, for a genuine observational deposit inside the system: the handbook's symptom descriptions, the seizure entries' clinical fineness, the recipes' tried-and-proven tags, and the drug tradition's demonstrable pharmacological standbys all record centuries of attended experience, however the system theorised what it attended to. And it is evidence — the tier's own contribution — for what that culture claimed: that the world is a divine text, that sickness has senders, and that knowledge itself descends from Ea through the sages before the Flood. The claims are documented as claims, in the claimants' words, and their thousand-year career is a fact of intellectual history whatever their truth.

What the corpus is not evidence for must be stated with the same plainness. It is not evidence that the omens' predictions were fulfilled, that the handbook's prognoses were accurate at any measurable rate, or that the prescriptions cured the conditions they name: no outcome records, no case series with follow-up, no statistics of any kind survive against which such efficacy could be tested — and for the majority of the drug names, §XIII has shown, the ingredients themselves cannot now be securely identified, so the test cannot even be begun. The efficacy claims in the tablets — "he will recover," the "tried" and "proven" tags, the salve that carries a great king's name — are the tradition's claims, reported in this article and endorsed nowhere in it. Nor is the corpus evidence for the descent it claims: the apkallu are the tradition's founders as the tradition presents them, and the archive's Tier B rule — the claim recorded, the claimant named, the event never narrated — has governed every mention of them here. Nor, finally, is the corpus a picture of all healing in Mesopotamia: it is the palace-and-temple, literate, male, urban stratum of a world that also contained the midwife, the bone-setter, the village herb-woman, and the family's own remedies, of whom the tablets say almost nothing because the tablets were not theirs.

The balanced judgement is the one the anchor scholarship supports. Bottéro: a reasoning by the list, organisational before it is experimental. Rochberg: one scribal culture of reading and computing, in which our line between astronomy and astrology does not exist. Geller: magic and pharmacy as crafts within one healing world. What stands at the end is neither a primitive medicine to be patronised nor a lost wisdom to be envied, but a complete, documented, self-aware system for reading affliction and answering it — wrong, where it is wrong, in ways it wrote down carefully enough for us to follow, and right about the thing it understood best: that the sick person must be read, whole and in order, from the head to the foot and from the street to the skin, before he can be helped. The forty tablets are still on the shelf. The reading is still legible.
