I. What This Article Is — And What It Is Not

This is a Tier B article: Ancient Tradition. That label governs everything that follows. Tier B does not mean "attested fact," and it does not mean "modern invention." It means that the material here survives in ancient texts, written by ancient scribes, in ancient languages, and that this article treats those texts as texts — what they say, how they are built, who copied them, and what later readers have made of them.

The central text is the composition modern scholars call the Sumerian King List: a Sumerian-language list of cities, kings, and reign lengths, running — in its fullest surviving form — from the first kings before a great flood down to the kings of Isin in the early second millennium BCE. Alongside it stand the Mesopotamian flood narratives: the Sumerian flood story centred on Ziusudra, the Akkadian poem of Atrahasis, and the flood account embedded in Tablet XI of the Epic of Gilgamesh, where the hero is called Utanapishtim.

Tier discipline, in practice, means this: when the King List assigns a reign of 28,800 years to Alulim, this article reports that the list assigns 28,800 years to Alulim. It does not narrate Alulim as a historical person who reigned for 28,800 years. It does not narrate him as a historical person at all. The reign lengths are the texts' claims. They are presented here as claims — remarkable, patterned, and historically revealing claims about how Mesopotamian scribes imagined their own deep past — but claims nonetheless.

// A NOTE ON LANGUAGE THROUGHOUT //

Verbs matter in this article. "The list says," "the poem tells," "Berossus reports," and "the scribes assigned" are the normal forms here. Where a scholar's interpretation is given, the scholar is named. Where a number comes from a specific manuscript, the manuscript is named. No reign length, flood date, or antediluvian lifespan in this article should be read as the Archive's statement of historical fact.

That restraint is not scepticism for its own sake. It is what makes the texts interesting in the first place. A list that opens before the flood, totals 241,200 years for eight kings, and then — after a single line about water — resumes with reigns that shrink, dynasty by dynasty, toward ordinary human spans is doing deliberate literary and political work. Reading it as a failed attempt at modern chronology misses the work entirely. Reading it as a scribal composition, with a structure, a mathematics, and a purpose, recovers it.

II. The King List as a Text

The Sumerian King List is not a chronicle in the modern sense. It does not narrate events, describe battles in detail, or record omens, treaties, and building works year by year. It is, at its core, a list — and the list form is the point. City, king, reign length. City, king, reign length. Subtotal. Transition. Next city.

The composition is written in Sumerian, the older literary and administrative language of southern Mesopotamia, even in copies made long after Akkadian had become the spoken language of the region. That choice of language is itself traditional: Sumerian was the language of the scribal schools, of hymns, proverbs, and learned compositions, and a king list in Sumerian carried the authority of that learned world.

No single "original" of the King List survives, and scholars do not treat any one manuscript as the text. What survives is a family of copies — tablets and prisms, complete and fragmentary, from several cities — that agree in structure and in much of their content while differing in names, figures, and whole sections. The modern text that readers encounter, in Thorkild Jacobsen's 1939 edition or in the Oxford Electronic Text Corpus of Sumerian Literature (ETCSL), is a composite: an editorial reconstruction built by comparing the witnesses, not a transcription of any one object. Section X below takes up those differences in detail, because they are essential to honest reading.

Dating the Composition

The earliest known witness to the list belongs to the period of the Third Dynasty of Ur (Ur III, c. 2112–2004 BCE). Piotr Steinkeller published an Ur III manuscript of the list in 2003, confirming that the composition's basic structure predates its earliest previously known copies. Jacobsen, working earlier, placed the original composition around the time of Utu-hegal of Uruk, the king credited in tradition with driving out the Gutians — that is, at the threshold of the Ur III age. The fullest copies, including the Weld-Blundell Prism discussed below, are Old Babylonian, from the early second millennium BCE.

Two cautions follow from those dates, and both are regularly dropped in popular retellings. First, the copies are separated from the reigns they describe — even the historical reigns — by centuries. Second, the antediluvian section, the part of the list this article is chiefly about, is not present in the same form in every witness, and Jacobsen argued on grammatical and structural grounds that it is a later addition to the composition. That argument is presented in Section VI. It does not make the section unimportant. It makes it a datable act of scribal composition in its own right: at some point, learned scribes decided the list needed a beginning before the flood, and they built one.

III. How the List Is Built — One Kingship, Moving City to City

The list's governing idea can be stated in one sentence, and the Ashmolean Museum's summary of its prism states it plainly in paraphrase: dominion over the land, determined by the gods, could be exercised by only one city at a given time, and only for a limited period. Kingship — in Sumerian, nam-lugal — is a single office. It descends from heaven. It resides in one city. That city falls, or is defeated, and kingship is carried to the next city. There is never a vacancy in principle, and never a rival: the list admits no simultaneous kingships.

Each dynastic block follows the same formula. Kingship comes to a city. Its kings are named, each with his reign length, sometimes with a filiation ("son of") or a short epithet. The block closes with a subtotal: so many kings, so many years. Then comes the transition formula — in the post-flood sections, the city was defeated (in some manuscripts, "smitten with weapons") and the kingship was taken to the next city. The ETCSL composite translation renders the antediluvian transitions more simply, with the city "falling" and kingship being "taken" onward, and marks some of those readings with a query, a small sign that the underlying text is not everywhere certain.

THE UNIT OF THE LIST

The list's true subject is not any individual king. It is kingship itself — one office, heaven-sent, moving through history city by city. Individual kings matter as holders of the office and as entries in its unbroken succession.

THE FORMULA

City receives kingship. Kings are listed with reign lengths. A subtotal is given. The city falls. Kingship is carried to the next city. The repetition is the argument: the same pattern, from Eridu to Isin, across the flood itself.

WHAT THE FORMULA EXCLUDES

Historically, the cities of early Mesopotamia often held power at the same time. Kish, Uruk, Ur, and Lagash overlapped. The list irons that simultaneity into a single sequence. That is not an error to be corrected; it is the composition's thesis, and Section XVII examines what the thesis was for.

THE FLOOD AS HINGE

The flood is the list's great dividing line. Kingship descends from heaven, runs through five cities, is interrupted by the flood, descends from heaven again, and resumes at Kish. One office, two descents, one history.

Because the alternative modern title for the composition — Chronicle of the One Monarchy — captures exactly this doctrine, it is worth keeping in mind while reading the tables below. The numbers are spectacular, but the structure is the argument.

IV. The Witnesses — Manuscripts, Prisms, and Fragments

The King List survives in more than a dozen manuscripts — counts vary with how fragments are grouped, and figures of sixteen or more witnesses are commonly cited — written in Sumerian cuneiform on clay tablets and on prisms, principally from Nippur, with others associated with Kish, Larsa, Isin, and Susa, among other provenances. Most of the surviving copies are Old Babylonian school or library products: the list was a learned text, copied and recopied in the scribal tradition.

The first fragment was published in 1906 by Hermann Volrath Hilprecht, and a second in 1911 by Jean-Vincent Scheil. Stephen Langdon published the great Weld-Blundell Prism in 1923. It was Jacobsen, in 1939, who first collated the witnesses then known into a critical edition — comparing their variants line by line rather than printing any one copy as definitive. The ETCSL composite translation follows that comparative method: its notes name the manuscripts (by the sigla of Vincente 1995) wherever witnesses differ, and state that, unless otherwise noted, the numerical data follow manuscript WB — the Weld-Blundell Prism.

// READING A COMPOSITE //

When a translation prints a reign length without comment, that does not mean every manuscript agrees. The ETCSL text is full of bracketed variants — one manuscript's 960 against another's 900, and so on. The antediluvian figures happen to be comparatively stable in the WB tradition, but stability in one tradition is not unanimity across all of them.

The Ur III manuscript published by Steinkeller deserves separate emphasis. It is roughly three centuries older than the Weld-Blundell Prism, it was associated with the court of Shulgi of Ur, and — crucially — in its form of the list, kingship descends and lands at Kish, after the flood's position in the later copies, without the developed antediluvian opening. Several of the copies Jacobsen worked from — three from Nippur and one from Susa, in his account — likewise began with Kish. The pre-flood kings, the most quoted part of the entire composition, are absent from its oldest stratum. Section X returns to what that means.

V. WB 444 — The Weld-Blundell Prism

The best-known witness, and the one from which the familiar antediluvian figures are drawn, is the Weld-Blundell Prism, museum number AN1923.444 in the Ashmolean Museum, Oxford — cited by Assyriologists as WB 444. It is a four-sided baked-clay prism, a little under 20 cm tall and about 9 cm across, inscribed with two columns of Sumerian cuneiform on each face, with a hole running through it from end to end.

The Ashmolean describes it as the most extensive and most complete surviving version of the list. It was presented to the museum by Herbert Weld-Blundell in 1923 and published the same year by Stephen Langdon in the Oxford Editions of Cuneiform Texts. Its provenance is given carefully by the museum as "probably Larsa," in southern Iraq; Weld-Blundell assembled his collection largely by purchase, and the prism's exact findspot is not securely documented. Popular accounts that state a firm excavation provenance go beyond the record.

The prism's text runs from the antediluvian kings through the dynasties of Kish, Uruk, Ur, Akkad, and Ur III, and ends with the kings of Isin — its last named king is Sin-magir of Isin, who reigned around 1827–1817 BCE. Because Sin-magir is given an eleven-year reign in the text, the prism is usually dated to about 1800 BCE, in or near the final year it records. A closing line beneath a double ruling at the foot of the last face names the scribe: Langdon read it as "by the hand of Nur-Ninsubur"; Jacobsen read the name slightly differently, as a possession line. Even the colophon, in other words, is a reading, not a certainty — a fitting emblem for the whole object.

The Ashmolean's own characterisation of the prism is worth quoting in paraphrase because it sets the scholarly tone for this whole article: the list, the museum states, is not history as we would understand it, but a combination of myth, legend, and historical information — and its gallery presentation has described it, memorably, as ancient political spin. Section XVII examines that judgement and the scholarship behind it.

VI. Jacobsen and the Standard Edition

Thorkild Jacobsen (1904–1993), the Danish-American Assyriologist, published The Sumerian King List in 1939 as Assyriological Studies 11 (University of Chicago Press). It remains the standard critical edition and the starting point for all subsequent work: a collation of the witnesses, a reconstructed text, a translation, and a sustained argument about how the composition was assembled.

Jacobsen's most consequential argument for this article concerns the antediluvian section itself. Comparing the manuscripts, he observed that several copies — including witnesses from Nippur and Susa — begin with Kish and have no kings before the flood at all. He further argued from the text's grammar and formulas: the pre-flood section moves between cities with a different transition formula from the post-flood sections, and its subtotals use plural verb forms even for cities with a single king, a habit the older sections do not share. His conclusion, as it is usually summarised from the 1939 edition, was that the role of the antediluvian section in the tradition could no longer be in doubt: it is a later addition.

That conclusion should be reported precisely. Jacobsen did not argue that the antediluvian kings were invented from nothing in the Old Babylonian period, still less that the flood tradition itself was late. Flood stories, king lists, and traditions of primeval kings circulated in Mesopotamia in several forms, and other compositions — the Sumerian flood story, the Instructions of Shuruppak tradition, the later Berossus — preserve related material independently. Jacobsen's argument is about this composition: the pre-flood opening, as an opening to the King List, was attached to a list that had originally begun after the flood, at Kish. Later scholarship, including Steinkeller's publication of the Ur III manuscript, has broadly confirmed the picture of a list that grew at both ends — extended forward to reach each new ruling dynasty, and eventually backward, across the flood.

// WHY THE ADDITION MATTERS //

An addition is not a footnote. Whoever attached the antediluvian section gave the list a new beginning: kingship no longer merely resumed after a catastrophe, it originated before one, in the same five cities the flood story names as the first cities. The list and the flood narrative were drawn together deliberately. That editorial act — datable, motivated, and visible in the manuscripts — is itself a piece of history, even though the reigns it records are not.

VII. The Opening — Kingship Lowered from Heaven

The list does not begin with a creation account, a genealogy, or a date. It begins with an event: kingship descends. In the ETCSL composite translation (t.2.1.1), verified against the Oxford text for this article, the opening runs — in paraphrase of its structure, with the verified wording given in the pull-quote below — as follows: after the kingship descended from heaven, the kingship was in Eridug; in Eridug, Alulim became king. The phrasing often quoted in popular works as "when kingship was lowered from heaven" renders the same Sumerian opening; translations differ in wording, and this article uses the ETCSL rendering, which it actually consulted, rather than mixing renderings from different editions.

"After the kingship descended from heaven, the kingship was in Eridug. In Eridug, Alulim became king; he ruled for 28800 years." — The Sumerian King List, lines 1–39, ETCSL translation t.2.1.1 (Oxford)

Three features of the opening deserve attention. First, kingship is a thing that can descend, reside, fall, and be carried — an institution with a location, not an abstraction. Second, its first earthly seat is Eridug (Eridu), the ancient southern city associated with the god Enki and, in the flood story's own account of the first cities, the first city founded. The list and the flood story share a geography of origins. Third, there is no king before Alulim and no time before the descent: the list's history begins when the office arrives. Whatever the scribes imagined before that moment, the list does not say.

After the flood, the formula repeats. In the ETCSL translation, the post-flood section opens by saying that after the flood had swept over, and the kingship had descended from heaven, the kingship was in Kish. The second descent is structurally necessary: the flood has emptied the office's earthly seat, so the office must be given again. The symmetry — descent, succession, flood, descent, succession — is the composition's frame, and every dynasty that follows, down to historical kings with reigns of a decade or two, is placed inside it.

The subtotals are the list's own. Eridu's two kings are totalled at 64,800 years; Bad-tibira's three at 108,000 years; Larak, Sippar, and Shuruppak have one king each. The section then closes, in the ETCSL translation, with the summary that in five cities eight kings ruled for 241,200 years — and then the flood line, quoted in full in Section XI.

Notes on Individual Figures

Alulim is, in the list's presentation, the first king ever: the first holder of the descended office, at Eridu. He is not attested as a historical ruler in Early Dynastic inscriptions, and Assyriologists treat him as a figure of the list tradition. A separate composition, the Ballad of Early Rulers, also names him — and assigns him a different reign (36,000 years), an early reminder that even ancient witnesses did not agree on the figures.

Dumuzid, the shepherd, carries the list's only occupational epithet in this section. The name Dumuzid (Tammuz) belongs elsewhere in Mesopotamian tradition to a shepherd god associated with fertility and the cycle of the seasons; the list also knows a different Dumuzid, "the fisherman," among the post-flood kings of Uruk. Whether the antediluvian shepherd and the god are to be identified is a question the list itself does not answer, and this article does not settle it.

En-men-dur-ana of Sippar has attracted particular comparative attention, because Sippar was the city of the sun god, and later Mesopotamian tradition remembered Enmeduranki — a form of the same name — as a primeval king taken into the gods' confidence and shown heavenly knowledge. That association has often been compared with the biblical Enoch, seventh in the Genesis list, who is credited with 365 years — the number of days in a solar year — and is said to have walked with God. The comparison is presented, with its proper cautions, in Section XVIII.

Ubara-Tutu of Shuruppak is the last king before the flood, and his position matters beyond the list. In Gilgamesh Tablet XI, the flood hero Utanapishtim is introduced as the son of Ubar-Tutu (in Akkadian form, Ubara-Tutu). The list and the epic thus lock together at exactly the flood's edge: the last antediluvian king is the flood hero's father. Shuruppak, his city, is the flood hero's city in every major version.

IX. The Numbers — Sar, Ner, and Sexagesimal Reckoning

The antediluvian reigns look arbitrary until they are read in the number system in which they were written. Mesopotamian scribal mathematics was sexagesimal — base-60 — and its named units for large numbers included the soss (60), the ner (600, ten sosses), and the sar (3,600, ten ners, or sixty sosses). Read in those units, every antediluvian reign resolves into whole numbers of sar and ner, as the table in Section VIII shows: 28,800 is 8 sar; 36,000 is 10 sar; 43,200 is 12 sar; 21,000 is 5 sar and 5 ner; 18,600 is 5 sar and 1 ner; and the grand total, 241,200, is exactly 67 sar.

Scholars draw two conclusions from that pattern, and both should be attributed rather than asserted as this article's own discovery. First, the figures are constructed: they are round numbers in the scribes' own system, built from its largest familiar units — the numerical equivalent of writing a reign in round thousands. Numbers of this shape are what one expects from scribes composing an impressive, ordered past, not from records of observed reigns. Second, the pattern is meaningful within the system: the sar was a unit of grandeur, and assigning a primeval king eight, ten, or twelve sar is a statement about the scale of the age, made in the mathematical language the scribal schools themselves used. The numbers are, in that sense, scribal mathematics doing literary work.

Unit Value Relation
Soss 60 60 units
Ner 600 10 sosses
Sar 3,600 10 ners; 60 sosses
Sar-u (greater sar) 36,000 10 sar — the unit of Berossus's largest reigns, see Section XVI

A persistent popular literature attempts to rescue literal chronology from these figures by dividing them — by 12, to turn "years" into months, by 360, to turn them into days, or by other factors — until plausible reign lengths emerge. Such schemes should be reported for what they are: modern reinterpretations, not readings found in the texts. The texts present the figures as years of reign, in round sexagesimal units, and the scholarly discussion — from Jacobsen onward — treats them as schematic and ideological numbers, not as garbled records awaiting a conversion key. Where individual scholars have proposed specific numerological readings, those proposals are theirs, and are labelled as such in the literature. This article follows the mainstream presentation: the pattern of the numbers is the evidence, and the evidence points to composition, not transcription.

// 67 SAR //

241,200 = 67 × 3,600. The total is as constructed as its parts. A scribe totalling observed reigns does not arrive, reign after reign and total after total, at whole numbers of his largest unit by accident.

It is also worth noting what changes after the flood. Post-flood reigns in the earliest Kish dynasty are still large — 1,200 years, 960, 900 — but they are no longer uniformly round sar figures, and they include odd, specific-looking numbers and even fractions of months and days in the dynastic totals. The mathematics itself narrates the transition: from the pure, large units of the primeval age toward the messy specificity of remembered history. Whether that gradient was designed or accumulated, it is one of the list's most effective literary devices.

X. Variants — The Lists Do Not Agree With Each Other

Any honest account of the antediluvian kings must reckon with the fact that "the" list is a modern editorial convenience. The witnesses differ, and their differences are not confined to spelling. They include which sections exist at all.

THE UR III WITNESS

The oldest known manuscript, of the Ur III period and published by Piotr Steinkeller in 2003, presents the list beginning at Kish — the first post-flood city in the later copies — without the developed antediluvian opening. The pre-flood section is missing from the list's oldest stratum.

THE NIPPUR AND SUSA COPIES

Of the copies Jacobsen collated in 1939, several — three from Nippur and one from Susa, in his account — likewise began with Kish and had no kings before the flood. Jacobsen's argument for the section as a later addition rested in part on exactly this distribution.

DIFFERING TOTALS

Where antediluvian sections do survive in variant forms, their kings, cities, and totals are not everywhere identical; totals substantially larger than WB's 241,200 years are reported in the variant tradition. The 241,200-year figure is WB's figure — the most complete witness's, and the one the ETCSL prints — not a universal figure of "the" list.

DIFFERING REIGNS

Individual reigns vary between witnesses throughout the list, as the ETCSL's bracketed variants show on nearly every line of the post-flood sections, and independent compositions such as the Ballad of Early Rulers assign Alulim a different reign from the list's. Ancient scribes transmitted a tradition, not a fixed table.

None of this diminishes the texts. It locates them. The King List was a living scribal composition across at least three centuries of copying, extended forward as dynasties changed and backward as its frame was completed. The antediluvian section, on the mainstream scholarly account following Jacobsen, belongs to that growth: it is the moment the list acquired a beginning worthy of its structure — kingship descending before the flood, in the flood story's own first cities, in numbers built from the scribal system's grandest units.

XI. After the Flood — The Reigns Shrink

The flood occupies exactly one line of the King List. In the ETCSL translation, the antediluvian section closes with its subtotal and then, without narrative, description, cause, or hero, the text says the flood swept over — and the next line begins the second descent of kingship, at Kish. The list does not tell the flood story. It assumes it. The story itself lives in other compositions, taken up in Sections XII to XV. Within the list, the flood is a hinge, not an episode: the boundary between the age of sar-long reigns and everything after.

"In 5 cities 8 kings; they ruled for 241200 years. Then the flood swept over." — The Sumerian King List, close of the antediluvian section, ETCSL translation t.2.1.1

After the hinge, the numbers descend by stages. The first post-flood dynasty, at Kish, is credited in the WB tradition with 23 kings ruling 24,510 years, 3 months, and 3½ days — its first king, Gushur, is assigned 1,200 years, and individual reigns remain in the hundreds for much of the dynasty. Among its later kings stands En-me-barage-si, assigned 900 years — a figure of particular interest because, unlike the antediluvian kings, Enmebaragesi of Kish is also attested in contemporary inscriptions and is generally treated by historians as a real ruler of roughly the 27th–26th century BCE. The list, that is, does not switch from fiction to fact at a single line. It shades: legendary scale, then exaggerated scale around figures some of whom existed, then — by the dynasties of Akkad, Ur III, and Isin — reign lengths that match the historical record closely enough to be checked against it.

Uruk's dynasty illustrates the middle of the gradient. Its kings include Lugalbanda, assigned 1,200 years, and Gilgamesh — "whose father was a phantom," in the ETCSL rendering of his epithet, lord of Kulaba — assigned 126 years. A reign of 126 years is still beyond a human span, but it is an order of magnitude below the antediluvian figures and two orders below his own dynasty's founder figures. By the time the list reaches Ur-Namma of Ur, founder of Ur III, it assigns him 18 years — his historical reign. The long diminuendo from 43,200 to 18 is the list's quietest and most persuasive piece of composition.

XII. Ziusudra — The Sumerian Flood Hero

The earliest surviving Mesopotamian flood narrative is Sumerian. The composition modern scholars call the Sumerian Flood Story — ETCSL 1.7.4, also widely known by Jacobsen's title for it, the Eridu Genesis — is preserved principally on a tablet from Nippur, excavated by the University of Pennsylvania expedition and recognised by Arno Poebel in the early twentieth century, with further fragments from Ur and a later bilingual fragment from the library of Ashurbanipal. The main Nippur tablet is dated to around 1600 BCE. Roughly a third to two-thirds of the text is lost, in varying estimates, and the surviving portions are heavily broken — a fact every summary of the story should carry with it.

What survives, in the ETCSL translation, has a clear shape. After the creation of humankind and animals, kingship descends, the divine rites are perfected, and the first five cities are founded and assigned to their gods: Eridu to Nudimmud (Enki), Bad-tibira to the Mistress (Inana), Larak to Pabilsag, Sippar to Utu, and Shuruppak to Sud. These are the King List's five antediluvian cities, in the same order. The two compositions share the tradition's map of the first age.

Then, after a break, the gods resolve on destruction. The hero is Zi-ud-sura — Ziusudra, a name understood as "life of long days" — presented as a king and a gudug priest, humble and reverent, standing day by day at his devotions. The god Enki, bound by an oath the gods have sworn, does not address Ziusudra directly. In the ETCSL translation's paraphrasable sense, he speaks to a wall, with Ziusudra standing at its side listening: a flood will sweep over the land; the decision that the seed of mankind is to be destroyed has been made; Ziusudra is to heed the instructions that follow. The boat-building instructions themselves fall in a lacuna. The flood, when the text resumes, lasts seven days and seven nights. Afterward Ziusudra opens the boat to the sun god Utu's light, prostrates himself, and sacrifices oxen and sheep in abundance. In the closing preserved movement, An and Enlil grant him life like a god's — the tradition's explanation, within its own terms, for the hero's unending lifespan.

// ON RECONSTRUCTION //

Because so much of the Sumerian flood tablet is lost, popular retellings silently fill its gaps from the later, fuller Akkadian versions — Atrahasis and Gilgamesh XI — and present the composite as "the Sumerian story." Bendt Alster's work on the Sumerian flood tradition, and the ETCSL's own presentation with its gaps and queries marked, model the honest alternative: what survives is remarkable enough, and its missing third is part of the evidence, not an embarrassment to be papered over.

Ziusudra's city is Shuruppak — Ubara-Tutu's city, the last city of the King List's antediluvian section. The Sumerian tradition thus places its flood hero exactly where the list leaves off: in the final reign before the waters. Later tradition, including the Instructions of Shuruppak, remembered Shuruppak as a seat of primeval wisdom, and the flood hero as its king.

XIII. Atrahasis — The Akkadian Flood Poem

The fullest early telling of the flood is the Akkadian poem modern scholars call Atrahasis, after its hero, whose name means "exceedingly wise." Composed in the Old Babylonian period — the best-preserved early copy is dated to the reign of Ammi-saduqa in the 17th century BCE — the poem runs to three tablets and roughly 1,245 lines in the standard edition of W. G. Lambert and A. R. Millard (1969), and is most widely read today in Stephanie Dalley's translation in Myths from Mesopotamia (Oxford University Press, 1989).

Atrahasis is not only a flood story. It is a complete account of why human beings exist, why they multiply, why the gods turn against them, and why — after the flood — human life is permanently restructured. Its opening movement, in paraphrase, begins among the gods themselves: the lesser gods labour, rebel at their workload, and humankind is created to carry the labour in their place. Humanity multiplies. And then the poem gives its famous, unglamorous motive for catastrophe: noise. In Dalley's translation's sense, the land bellows like a bull; the god Enlil is kept from sleep by the uproar of humankind; and Enlil moves, step by step, to reduce the noise — first plague, then drought and famine, each attempt frustrated by Enki's quiet countermeasures on humanity's behalf, delivered through his servant Atrahasis.

The flood is Enlil's final measure, undertaken after the lesser measures fail, and bound by an oath imposed on Enki himself. Enki circumvents the oath as in the Sumerian version — speaking not to the man but to his house's wall — and Atrahasis builds the vessel, loads it, and rides out a flood that the poem, in its third tablet, sets at seven days and seven nights of storm. Afterward, Atrahasis sacrifices; the hungry gods gather to the offering; Enlil rages at the survival — and is answered, by Enki and by the mother goddess, with the poem's settlement: humanity will continue, but under new limits — mortality, barrenness for some, and celibate priestly classes are presented as the permanent checks that replace annihilation. The flood, in Atrahasis, is the event that explains why human life has the shape it has.

Two scholarly points, associated in the modern literature with Piotr Michalowski's and Bendt Alster's generation of readers among many others, deserve emphasis. First, Atrahasis reframes the Sumerian material: the flood is no longer an unexplained divine decision but the climax of a long argument about labour, population, and noise, conducted between gods with opposed interests. Second, the poem's hero is not a king in the King List's mould but a wise servant — "exceedingly wise" is his whole epithet — whose survival is an act of one god's disobedience, not of royal right. The tradition is already, in its earliest full form, arguing with itself about what the flood meant.

XIV. Utanapishtim — Gilgamesh, Tablet XI

The most famous Mesopotamian flood account is a story inside a story. In Tablet XI of the Standard Babylonian Epic of Gilgamesh — the version traditionally attributed to the scholar Sin-leqi-unninni and known from seventh-century BCE copies from Ashurbanipal's library at Nineveh, among other witnesses — the hero Gilgamesh, grieving Enkidu and terrified of his own death, has crossed the waters of death to find the one man who was granted unending life: Utanapishtim, "the Faraway," son of Ubar-Tutu of Shuruppak. Andrew R. George's critical edition, The Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic (Oxford, 2003), and his Penguin translation, The Epic of Gilgamesh (1999), are the standard modern points of entry; Dalley's Myths from Mesopotamia also translates the tablet.

Utanapishtim's narration, in paraphrase following George's edition, closely tracks Atrahasis — at points, scholars note, nearly word for word — while reshaping it as personal testimony. The great gods resolve on the flood; Ea (Enki) betrays the secret to the wall of Utanapishtim's reed hut; the hero is told to tear down his house, build a boat of equal length and breadth, roofed over, and load it with the seed of all living things, his family, craftsmen, and goods. The storm lasts six days and seven nights. The boat grounds on Mount Nimush (Nisir in older translations). After six days held fast, on the seventh Utanapishtim releases three birds in sequence: a dove, which returns finding no perch; a swallow, which likewise returns; and a raven, which sees the waters receding, feeds, and does not return. He releases the animals, sacrifices on the mountain, and the gods gather to the sweet savour of the offering. Enlil, furious, is rebuked — and in settlement blesses Utanapishtim and his wife, making them like the gods, to dwell far away at the mouth of the rivers.

The tablet's purpose inside the epic is explicit in its structure. The flood story is told to demonstrate that Utanapishtim's immortality was a unique, unrepeatable grant, made in circumstances that will never recur — not a technique Gilgamesh can learn. The point is driven home immediately afterward: challenged simply to stay awake for six days and seven nights, Gilgamesh falls asleep at once, and Utanapishtim's wife bakes a loaf for each day of his sleep so the failure cannot be denied. The man who cannot conquer sleep will not conquer death. When George Smith first translated the Nineveh flood tablet and announced it to the Society of Biblical Archaeology on 3 December 1872, the sensation it caused — a flood account older than Genesis, in a Babylonian epic — permanently changed the study of both the Bible and Mesopotamia. The tablet he read, now British Museum K.3375 and its joins, remains the single most consequential cuneiform discovery for the comparative study taken up in Section XVIII.

XV. The Ark Tablet — Irving Finkel's Round Boat

In 2009 the British Museum's cuneiform curator Irving Finkel was shown a small clay tablet — about the size of a mobile phone, some sixty lines of Old Babylonian cuneiform, dated to roughly 1900–1700 BCE — that had been brought from the Middle East decades earlier by RAF veteran Leonard Simmons and kept by his family. Finkel's translation and study appeared as The Ark Before Noah: Decoding the Story of the Flood (2014). The object, privately owned and published through Finkel's book, is now known as the Ark Tablet.

The tablet is a version of the Atrahasis flood story — its hero is Atrahasis, its warning god is Enki — but its distinction is practical. Where other versions move quickly from warning to storm, the Ark Tablet lingers on boatbuilding, with quantities. In Finkel's translation, in paraphrase: the god instructs the hero to draw out the boat on a circular plan, its length and breadth equal; the vessel is to be a giant coracle — the round river boat of Mesopotamia, a basket of coiled palm-fibre rope stiffened with wooden ribs and waterproofed inside and out with bitumen — scaled up to a base area of about 3,600 square metres, a circle some 65–70 metres across with walls about six metres high. The tablet specifies the rope, the ribs, and two kinds of bitumen in stated amounts, and its closing preserved lines bring the wild animals aboard in pairs.

Finkel's central point is worth stating in his terms, paraphrased: the tablet does not invent a new kind of vessel. It takes the ordinary Mesopotamian coracle — a boat type still in use on Iraq's rivers into the modern period — and enlarges it to the scale the story requires. The "ark," in this oldest detailed specification, is round because Mesopotamian river boats were round. The long, ship-shaped ark of later Western illustration belongs to a different boatbuilding world. A television production in 2014 built a scaled-down round boat following Finkel's reading, with boatbuilders in Kerala, India — an experiment in the tablet's plausibility as instructions, at a fraction (reported variously as a fifth to a third) of the tablet's stated size.

WHY THE ARK TABLET MATTERS FOR THIS ARTICLE

The King List gives the flood one line. The Ark Tablet gives the boat sixty. Together they show the range of the tradition: the same flood could be a chronological hinge in a scribal list, a theological argument in a poem, a personal testimony in an epic, and a builder's specification on a palm-sized tablet. "The Mesopotamian flood story" is a library, not a text.

XVI. Berossus — Ten Kings and 432,000 Years

The tradition's last great ancient retelling comes not in cuneiform but in Greek, some fifteen centuries after the Weld-Blundell Prism. Berossus (Berossos), a Babylonian priest of Bel writing under Antiochus I in the early third century BCE, composed the Babyloniaca, a history of Babylonia in Greek for a Hellenistic audience. The work is lost as a whole and survives in fragments and summaries transmitted through Alexander Polyhistor, Josephus, Eusebius, and the Byzantine chronographer Syncellus, among others — a transmission chain every statement about Berossus should acknowledge.

In Berossus's second book, as the fragments report it, the period before the flood contains ten kings reigning a total of 120 sar — 432,000 years. Their names, in the Greek forms the fragments preserve, are: Aloros, Alaparos, Amelon, Ammenon, Amegalaros (Megalaros), Daonos, Eudorachos, Amempsinos, Opartes (Otiartes), and Xisuthros. Several correspond recognisably to the King List's figures under Greek dress — Aloros to Alulim, Otiartes to Ubara-Tutu — while the list has grown from eight kings to ten, and its total from WB's 241,200 years to a rounder, grander 432,000: in sexagesimal terms, 120 sar, or twelve of the greater sar of 36,000.

The tenth king is the point of the expansion. Xisuthros is the Greek form of Ziusudra's name — the flood hero himself, promoted in Berossus's list from the son or successor of the last king to the last king in his own right. In the flood account the fragments preserve from Berossus, the god Cronus — Ea/Enki, under interpretatio graeca — warns Xisuthros in a dream that mankind will be destroyed by a flood on the fifteenth day of the month. Xisuthros is told to bury the writings containing the beginnings, middles, and ends of things at Sippar, the city of the sun, so that civilisation's knowledge can be recovered afterward; he builds the vessel, takes aboard his family and provisions and animals, releases birds to test the waters as in Gilgamesh XI, grounds on a mountain in Armenia in Berossus's geography, sacrifices — and is taken to live with the gods, while his companions recover the buried writings from Sippar and refound Babylon. The detail of the buried books is Berossus's own emphasis, and a priestly one: what survives the flood, in his telling, is not only life but the written record. For a Babylonian priest explaining his tradition's antiquity to a Greek king, no emphasis could be more pointed.

Berossus also shows the tradition still doing chronological work in the Hellenistic age: after the flood, the fragments assign eighty-six kings a further span of roughly 33,000–34,000 years down toward the historical dynasties. The numbers remain schematic, the structure remains the King List's, and the audience has changed completely. That is the measure of the composition's durability: a Sumerian scribal frame, still carrying Babylonian claims about the deep past in Greek prose two thousand years after Eridu.

XVII. What Were the Lists For?

If the antediluvian reigns are not records, what are they? Scholarship offers several answers, which are complementary more often than competing. Each is attributed here to the line of scholarship that argues it.

1. Legitimation — History as Charter

The most influential answer is Piotr Michalowski's. In "History as Charter: Some Observations on the Sumerian King List" (Journal of the American Oriental Society 103, 1983), Michalowski argued — in paraphrase — that the list works as a charter: by presenting kingship as a single, heaven-sent office passed from one city to the next for a limited time, it makes whichever dynasty currently holds the office the lawful heir of every earlier power in the sequence. A reigning king's claim does not need to be argued on its merits; it needs only to be placed at the end of the queue. The Ashmolean's presentation of WB 444 makes the same point for its prism specifically: the list ends with Isin, and its structure implies that Isin's rulers are the rightful successors of the powerful kingdoms of the past. Jacobsen's earlier account is compatible in direction: he treated the composition as a political document of the Ur III–Isin world, not as a neutral chronicle.

2. The One-Monarchy Doctrine — Against Civic Rivalry

Closely related is the reading of the list as an intervention in the rivalries of the Mesopotamian city-states. In reality, Kish, Uruk, Ur, Lagash, and their peers competed, overlapped, and remembered their own pasts proudly and separately. The list denies that plurality in principle: there was only ever one kingship, held by one city at a time, granted and withdrawn by the gods. A city that has lost kingship has not been robbed by a rival; its allotted term has ended, as every term ends. The doctrine simultaneously flatters the current holder — the gods' present choice — and consoles every former holder: their greatness was real, heaven-sent, and is a matter of record in the same list that records its passing. Few political fictions have served so many masters so politely.

3. Scribal Mathematics and School Tradition

A third answer looks not to palaces but to schools. The list was copied for centuries in scribal training contexts; its antediluvian numbers are exercises in the sexagesimal system's largest units as much as they are claims about the past. On this reading, part of what the list is for is the formation of scribes: a canonical sequence to be mastered, totalled, and varied, carrying the profession's own memory of itself. The flood hero's world, in Berossus's version, is saved by buried books; the list's world is preserved by copied tablets. Both are scribal values, stated as history.

The school evidence behind that reading is unusually concrete, and it can be reconstructed because the exercises themselves survive. Niek Veldhuis, in Elementary Education at Nippur (1997), rebuilt the order of the Old Babylonian elementary curriculum at Nippur from the physical tablets: on the common type II tablet the teacher's model stood on the obverse and the pupil's copy beside or behind it, while the reverse repeated a text learned earlier — so the pairing of obverse and reverse on thousands of tablets lets a modern reader recover the sequence in which lessons were taken. That sequence began with the making of wedges and single signs, ran through syllable exercises and lists of personal names, and arrived at the great thematic lexical lists — the ur5-ra series of trees, wooden objects, vessels, animals, and the rest of the ordered world — with metrological and mathematical lists introduced alongside the more advanced lexical work.

Mathematics, in that curriculum, was not an afterthought. Christine Proust's study of the Old Babylonian school tablets (Cuneiform Digital Library Journal 2009:1) shows metrology and sexagesimal place-value notation taught as the first level of the mathematical curriculum itself: pupils learned ordered lists of capacities, weights, surfaces, and lengths, then the tables that convert between measures, then numerical tables — multiplications and reciprocals — in the base-60 notation Section IX describes. A scribe who had totalled a list in sar and ner had first totalled capacities and fields in exactly this training. Eleanor Robson's study of House F at Nippur (Revue d'Assyriologie 95, 2001) gives the institution an address: a small private house in use as a school in the 1740s BCE, which yielded nearly fifteen hundred tablet fragments — about half of them the by-products of elementary teaching — and a courtyard basin of wet clay in which finished exercises were crumpled and recycled. Beyond the elementary stage, pupils moved to model contracts, proverbs, and literary compositions in Sumerian, a language by then learned, not spoken at home. The King List's surviving copies are products of that world: Old Babylonian school and library tablets, written by scribes whose formation consisted, in large part, of copying canonical sequences accurately — and whose profession's prestige rested on the antiquity and order of what they copied.

4. A Theology of Kingship

Finally, the list is a theological statement, and should not be reduced to propaganda alone. Its first and most repeated claim is that kingship is not a human invention: it descended from heaven, it is withdrawn and reassigned by divine decision, and no dynasty holds it by nature. Even the flood cannot abolish it — it can only interrupt it, after which it descends again. For the cultures that copied the list, that was a claim about the structure of the world, and the immense antediluvian numbers belong to it: the office is older, grander, and more durable than any holder of it, including the holder copying the list.

// HOLDING THE ANSWERS TOGETHER //

Legitimation, civic doctrine, scribal training, and theology are not four rival explanations between which a reader must choose. A single composition can charter a dynasty, discipline rival cities, train the scribes who copy it, and teach a doctrine of kingship — and the King List's fifteen centuries of copying suggest that it did all four, for successive audiences who needed different parts of it most.

XVIII. Genesis and the Antediluvian Patriarchs — A Comparison, Carefully Framed

No comparison in this field is more often made — or more often mishandled — than the comparison between the Mesopotamian antediluvian traditions and the opening chapters of Genesis. This section makes the comparison the way comparative scholarship frames it: as a relationship between traditions, documented in texts, with the similarities and the differences both stated plainly. It takes no position on the theological truth of either corpus; that is not a question cuneiform evidence can settle, and this Archive does not pretend otherwise.

The Genesis List

Genesis 5 presents ten generations from Adam to Noah: Adam, Seth, Enosh, Kenan, Mahalalel, Jared, Enoch, Methuselah, Lamech, and Noah. Each entry gives the patriarch's age at the birth of his named son, his remaining years, and his total lifespan. In the Masoretic (Hebrew) text, the lifespans are: Adam 930 years, Seth 912, Enosh 905, Kenan 910, Mahalalel 895, Jared 962, Enoch 365, Methuselah 969 — the longest in the chapter — Lamech 777, and Noah 950 in total (Genesis 9:29), with the flood falling in Noah's six-hundredth year. Summed by the chapter's own begetting ages, the Masoretic chronology places 1,656 years from Adam to the flood. The ancient versions differ among themselves: the Samaritan Pentateuch's figures total 1,307 years, and the Greek Septuagint's roughly 2,242 years — a reminder that Genesis's numbers, like the King List's, were transmitted in variant forms and should be cited by version.

What the Comparison Shows — And What It Does Not

The structural parallels are real and were recognised as soon as the cuneiform texts were read: a sequence of primeval figures of extraordinary longevity, in a fixed order, terminated by a flood; a hero warned in advance who survives in a vessel with his family and with animals; birds released to test the waters; a mountain grounding; a sacrifice after the waters fall; and a marked decline in longevity afterward. The chain of shared details — warning, vessel, birds, mountain, sacrifice — is too specific, in scholars' widely held judgement, to be coincidence: the traditions belong to one ancient Near Eastern world of flood storytelling, in which the Mesopotamian versions are attested in writing many centuries before the biblical text reached its final form. Individual correspondences have also been proposed — most famously between En-men-dur-ana / Enmeduranki of Sippar, the seventh king, associated with heavenly knowledge and the sun god's city, and Enoch, the seventh patriarch, credited with 365 years and a walk with God; and between the flood heroes themselves, Ziusudra, Atrahasis, Utanapishtim, Xisuthros, and Noah. Such correspondences are proposals within comparative scholarship, of varying strength, and are presented here as such.

The differences are equally real, and honest comparison keeps them in the same paragraph as the parallels. The Mesopotamian figures are kings with reigns; the Genesis figures are fathers with lifespans and begetting ages. The Mesopotamian totals are vastly larger and built from base-60 units; the Genesis figures, whatever their own schematic patterns, are not sar arithmetic. The Mesopotamian flood, in Atrahasis, is sent because humanity is too loud and is opposed from within the divine council by Enki; the Genesis flood is a moral judgement on human corruption by a single God, followed by a covenant. The Mesopotamian hero is granted unending life and removed from the world; Noah remains mortal, dies at 950, and his descendants repopulate the earth under blessing. Comparative scholarship's mainstream framing — a shared inheritance, reworked in each tradition toward its own theology — accounts for both the parallels and the differences. Claims of direct transcription in either direction, and claims of total independence, both go beyond what the texts show.

// ON LOCAL FLOODS AND UNIVERSAL CLAIMS //

Excavations in southern Iraq — at Ur, Kish, Shuruppak, and Uruk — have found water-laid silt layers, and catastrophic river flooding was a recurring reality of life between the Tigris and Euphrates. Those layers date to different periods and do not combine into evidence for a single universal deluge. The texts themselves make theological and literary claims, not geological ones, and this article reports the flood as the traditions present it: as their pivotal primeval event. What any reader makes of that, theologically, is the reader's own work.

XIX. The Bundahishn — Iran's Bounded Twelve Thousand Years

The Genesis comparison of Section XVIII stays inside one ancient Near Eastern world of flood storytelling. This section widens the frame to a third system of numbered deep time, from Iran: the Zoroastrian Bundahishn — the name means "Primal Creation" — a Middle Persian (Pahlavi) compilation whose subject is nothing less than the whole career of the world, from its creation to its end, told inside a fixed number: 12,000 years, in four periods of 3,000. Set beside the King List's 241,200 years for eight kings before a single flood, and beside the 4,320,000 years of a single Indian mahāyuga in The Yuga Cycles, the Bundahishn's total is strikingly small. That smallness is the point of the comparison. The Iranian numbers are not trying to outbid anyone's antiquity. They are a bound: a timetable within which evil is admitted into the world, fought, and finally expelled from it.

The Text — A Pahlavi Compilation

The Bundahishn, as D. N. MacKenzie's Encyclopaedia Iranica entry describes it, is a major Pahlavi work of compilation: mainly a detailed cosmogony and cosmography based on the Zoroastrian scriptures, with legendary history besides. It survives in two distinct recensions. The shorter, Indian Bundahishn was the first to reach European readers, in E. W. West's English translation in Pahlavi Texts, Part I, volume 5 of the Sacred Books of the East (1880). The fuller Greater, or Iranian, Bundahishn — about twice the Indian recension's extent, in thirty-six chapters in the numbering of its modern edition — was published with transliteration and English translation by B. T. Anklesaria in 1956, under the title Zand-Ākāsīh. This section's account of the scheme follows those published translations and the Iranica summaries of them, in paraphrase; no wording is quoted from the Bundahishn here.

// SEATED AT ITS COMPILATION //

Tier B discipline applies to this text exactly as to the King List. The Bundahishn is seated here at its real date: a compilation of the 9th century CE — its final redaction belongs to the circle of the priest Zādspram, known to have been active about 880 CE, on MacKenzie's account — assembled from older, Sasanian-era materials, themselves drawing on Avestan scripture and its traditional commentary (the Zand), including material traced to the lost Dāmdād nask. The scheme it preserves claims primordial antiquity. The book that preserves it does not have that antiquity, and this article does not lend it. A 9th-century compilation carrying a Sasanian world-scheme is evidence — for the scheme's Sasanian and post-Sasanian life. It is not a Bronze Age document, and nothing in this section treats it as one.

The Frame — Ohrmazd, Ahriman, and Limited Time

The scheme's frame is a confrontation. In the account the Encyclopaedia Iranica's cosmogony article summarises from the Bundahishn and the related Selections of Zādspram, in paraphrase: at the beginning Ohrmazd — the Middle Persian form of Ahura Mazdā — dwelt on high, in pure light; Ahriman, the hostile spirit (Avestan Angra Mainyu), dwelt in the depths, in darkness; between them was the void. Ohrmazd, aware of his adversary, first fashioned his creations in a spiritual state. Ahriman attacked, refused an offer of peace, and the two entered into a pact to wage their contest for a limited period, in a defined arena: this world. Finite, numbered time is thus, in this system, a weapon agreed in advance — the duration of a war whose outcome Ohrmazd already foresees. The cosmic drama that fills the 12,000 years is often summarised in three stages — creation (bundahišn), mixture (gumēzišn), and separation (wizārišn) of evil from good — and the numbered chronology divides that drama into four.

Period Years of the Scheme What the Tradition Places in It
1. The spiritual creation 1–3,000 Ohrmazd shapes his creations in a spiritual (mēnōg) state, in the form of bright white fire (Bundahishn 1.44, as summarised in the Encyclopaedia Iranica cosmogony article); they remain in that state for 3,000 years while Ahriman fashions his demonic counter-creations in the darkness.
2. The material creation, unassailed 3,001–6,000 After the pact, Ohrmazd recites the Ahunwar prayer; Ahriman, shown his ultimate defeat, falls back in stupor for the whole period. Ohrmazd fashions the creations in material (gētīg) form — sky, water, earth, the single plant, the uniquely created bull, the first man Gayōmard, and fire — motionless, the sun standing still in mid-sky.
3. The mixture — Ahriman's assault 6,001–9,000 Ahriman renews his attack: he pierces the sky, pollutes the waters, withers the plant, kills the bull and Gayōmard, and taints fire with smoke. Movement, death, and generation enter the world. Good and evil are now mixed in every created thing — the condition the tradition calls gumēzišn, and the condition of the present world.
4. The final period — from Zoroaster to the Renovation 9,001–12,000 At the period's opening, in the scheme's own placement, Zoroaster receives and brings the revelation. The last 3,000 years are divided into three millennia, each closing with a saviour born of Zoroaster's line: Ušēdar at the end of the first, Ušēdarmāh at the end of the second, and at the end of the third the final Saošyant, who brings the contest to its close.

The end of the fourth period is the Frashokereti (frašō.kərəti) — the Renovation, the "making wonderful" of the world. In the tradition's account, in paraphrase: the dead are raised, humanity passes through an ordeal of molten metal that purifies rather than merely judges, Ahriman and his creations are defeated and expelled, evil is separated from good for good — the wizārišn, the separation, that closes the drama — and the world is restored to the perfection of its creation, no longer mixed, no longer mortal, and no longer inside the bounded 12,000 years at all. Limited time was created for this ending. When the ending arrives, the limit has done its work.

Three Numbered Deep Times, Side by Side

Three ancient systems, three uses of number. All three are numbered, exact, and schematic — none of the three totals is an accumulated record, and in each case the pattern of the numbers is the evidence of deliberate construction. They are alike in that. In scale, and in what the numbers are for, they could hardly differ more.

The scale differences repay a moment's arithmetic. The Bundahishn's whole world, creation to Renovation, lasts about one twentieth of the King List's antediluvian section alone (241,200 ÷ 12,000 ≈ 20) — and the King List's flood, which ends that section, is for the Iranian scheme not even a marker: its dividing lines are an assault and a revelation, not a deluge. Against the Indian scale the contrast is exact: one mahāyuga is the Bundahishn total multiplied by 360 (12,000 × 360 = 4,320,000). The numeral 12,000 itself stands in both systems — in India as 12,000 divine years, each worth 360 human years; in Iran as 12,000 years simply, the whole of finite time. The same figure, doing opposite work: in the yuga scheme it is one turn of a wheel that has turned before and will turn again; in the Bundahishn it happens once, runs in a straight line, and ends in a renovation that is final. No borrowing between the systems is asserted here, in either direction — the comparison is of structures, documented in their own texts, not of descent.

Read together, the three systems also mark three different relations between number and hope. The King List's numbers look backward, lending the present an ancestry too large to argue with. The yuga numbers look around, locating the reader inside a turning order larger than any single life. The Bundahishn's numbers look forward: because the total is bounded and its periods are counted, a reader inside the mixture — the third period's world, or the fourth's — can know that the contest has a remaining term, and that the term is finite. Eschatology, in this scheme, is arithmetic with the end left in.

XX. How to Read These Texts

The antediluvian king lists reward a particular kind of reading, and this closing section gathers the article's discipline into a short set of working rules for any reader who meets these texts elsewhere — in museums, in popular books, or online, where they are quoted constantly and sourced rarely.

1. ASK WHICH WITNESS

"The King List says" is incomplete without "in which copy." The 241,200-year total is WB 444's. Other witnesses differ, omit the section, or begin at Kish. A claim that cannot name its manuscript is not yet a claim about a text.

2. ASK WHICH TRANSLATION

"Kingship was lowered from heaven" and "the kingship descended from heaven" are different translators' English for the same opening. Quote the edition you opened, name it, and do not blend renderings. This article quotes the ETCSL, because the ETCSL is what it read.

3. READ THE NUMBERS IN BASE-60

Before marvelling at 28,800, divide it by 3,600. The answer — 8 sar — is the scribe's number. The pattern of whole sar and ner is the single most informative fact about the antediluvian figures, and it is invisible in decimal translation.

4. KEEP THE HERO'S NAMES APART

Ziusudra, Atrahasis, Utanapishtim, and Xisuthros are related — and they are not interchangeable. Each name belongs to a language, a composition, and a theology. Collapsing them into "the Sumerian Noah" erases a thousand years of retelling, which is the most interesting part.

5. ATTRIBUTE THE INTERPRETATIONS

Legitimation is Michalowski's argument and the Ashmolean's presentation; the later-addition argument is Jacobsen's; the round coracle is Finkel's translation. Ancient texts have ancient claims and modern readers. A careful article keeps all three layers labelled.

6. LET THE FLOOD BE A HINGE

In the list, the flood is one line — and everything turns on it. The tradition knew exactly what it was doing with that economy. Reading for structure, not only for spectacle, is how a list of impossible numbers becomes one of the most durable historical arguments of the ancient world.

Kept apart in this way — the record, the tradition, and the telling — the antediluvian lists need no exaggeration. Eight kings, five cities, 241,200 years of claimed reign, a single line of water, and then history, shrinking reign by reign toward the world the scribes themselves lived in: that is already, exactly as the texts present it, one of the great constructions of the ancient imagination.
