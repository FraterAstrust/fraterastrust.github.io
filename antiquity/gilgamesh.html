This is a Tier B article: Ancient Tradition. Everything in it rests on ancient texts — poems in Sumerian and Akkadian, written in cuneiform on clay tablets, copied and recopied by ancient scribes — and it treats those texts as texts: each version is seated at the date its composition or its manuscripts actually carry, each claim is attributed to the text that makes it, and every direct quotation is taken from a named, published modern translation and credited to its translator. No claim here depends on treating the poem as a report of events, and none is allowed to borrow the authority of archaeology it does not possess.

That discipline matters for Gilgamesh more than for almost any other subject in this division, because the epic invites a confusion its own first lines encourage. The poem is about a king of Uruk whom the tradition places in the third millennium BCE — in the age this hub's Ubaid and Uruk article treats from the ground. But the poem itself is not a third-millennium document. It is a layered work: independent Sumerian poems of about 2100 BCE, a unified Akkadian epic of about 1800 BCE, and a standardised twelve-tablet edition credited by Babylonian tradition to a scholar named Sîn-lēqi-unninni and dated by modern scholarship to roughly 1300–1000 BCE. The antiquity the poem claims — a hero of the deep past, tidings from before the Flood — is the tradition's claim, and this article labels it as such wherever it appears. The antiquity the poem has is the stratigraphy of its manuscripts, and that is old enough, and strange enough, to need no inflation.

This article also tells the story whole. The epic is famous in fragments of itself — the Flood of Tablet XI above all, which this hub's Flood Traditions article owns and treats at full length. Here the Flood takes its proper place as one movement among twelve: after the making of Enkidu, the journey to the Cedar Forest, the insult to Ishtar, and the death that sends a king walking to the edge of the world; and before the plant, the serpent, and the return to the walls of Uruk with which the poem deliberately closes. Read whole, Gilgamesh is not a flood story with adventures attached. It is a poem about mortality that contains a flood — and the difference is the subject of this article.

I. Tier B — A Poem, Not a Chronicle

Begin with what the object is. The Epic of Gilgamesh is a narrative poem — in its fullest ancient form, some three thousand lines of Akkadian verse distributed over twelve clay tablets — preserved not in any author's autograph but in dozens of fragmentary copies, made in scribal schools and palace libraries across more than a thousand years, and reassembled by modern scholarship from pieces held in museums from London to Baghdad. About two-thirds of the standard version's lines have been recovered; the rest are lacunae — gaps where the clay is broken — which editors mark and translators bridge, honestly or otherwise, according to their practice. When this article quotes the poem, it quotes A. R. George's Penguin translation, The Epic of Gilgamesh (1999; Penguin Classics edition 2003), the reading text companion to his two-volume critical edition of 2003, and it says so at the quotation. Stephanie Dalley's translation in Myths from Mesopotamia (Oxford, 1989; revised edition 2000) and Benjamin R. Foster's in The Epic of Gilgamesh (Norton, 2001; second edition 2019) are the other standard English doors into the poem, and the differences among the three — in how much broken text each restores, and how — are themselves part of the evidence a careful reader is handling.

The poem's hero requires the same care. That a king named Gilgamesh — in Sumerian, Bilgames — was remembered at Uruk is the tradition's own testimony, repeated in the Sumerian King List, in offering lists, and in the poems themselves. Whether a historical king of that name reigned at Uruk in the early third millennium BCE is a question the surviving contemporary record does not answer: no inscription of his reign, no monument, no administrative tablet naming him has been recovered from his supposed lifetime, and scholars who accept a historical kernel behind the figure do so as an inference from the persistence and specificity of the tradition, not from a document. This hub treats the King List's Gilgamesh entry — his phantom father, his impossibly long reign — in The Antediluvian King Lists, under that article's own rules. Here it is enough to fix the posture: the epic is evidence for what Mesopotamians of the second and first millennia BCE told, taught, and copied — which is a great deal — and it is not a chronicle of a reign in the third.

// THE RULE OF THIS ARTICLE //

Dates in this article are dates of texts: of compositions, editions, and surviving copies. When the poem says its hero lived before the Flood, that is the poem speaking, and it is reported as the poem speaking. When a tablet is dated to the seventh century BCE, that is a manuscript, and it is reported as a manuscript. The two kinds of date are never allowed to stand in for each other.

One more preliminary, because it shapes everything that follows. There is no single ancient object called "the Epic of Gilgamesh" in the way there is a single printed book of that name today. There is a tradition: Sumerian poems that are not yet an epic, an Old Babylonian epic that is not yet standard, a Standard Babylonian edition that later scribes copied with variants, and peripheral versions in other languages that paraphrase freely. Jeffrey H. Tigay's The Evolution of the Gilgamesh Epic (1982) — still the classic study of the poem's growth — made that layered growth its subject, comparing the surviving stages line by line to show what each age added, dropped, and re-thought. This article follows Tigay's and George's stratigraphy throughout: the next three sections walk the layers in order, before the story itself is told, so that the reader always knows which Gilgamesh is on the page.

II. The Stratigraphy — A Poem in Layers

The epic's history, as modern scholarship reconstructs it from the manuscripts, has four great strata. They are summarised here and treated one by one in §§III–VI.

// STRATUM ONE //
Sumerian poems, c. 2100 BCE

Five independent poems about Bilgames of Uruk, composed in the age of the Third Dynasty of Ur or its aftermath; surviving mostly in Old Babylonian school copies.

// STRATUM TWO //
Old Babylonian epic, c. 1800 BCE

The first unified Akkadian epic, known by its opening words, "Surpassing all other kings"; surviving in scattered tablets and fragments only.

// STRATUM THREE //
Standard Babylonian, c. 1300–1000 BCE

The twelve-tablet edition credited to Sîn-lēqi-unninni, known by its opening words, "He who saw the Deep"; the form all later copies follow.

// STRATUM FOUR //
The copies, to the late 1st millennium

First-millennium manuscripts from Assyria and Babylonia — above all the seventh-century BCE library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh — are the survival vehicle through which the poem reached the modern world (§XV).

Two features of this stratigraphy deserve emphasis before the detail. The first is the gap between hero and poem. Even the earliest stratum — the Sumerian poems of about 2100 BCE — stands centuries after the era in which the tradition sets its king, in the Early Dynastic age of Uruk's first greatness. The poems were never contemporary reportage; from their first recoverable moment, Gilgamesh was already a figure of the remembered past, a hero of long ago around whom stories had gathered. The second is the direction of growth. The tradition does not decay from a perfect original; it builds. Episodes that begin as separate Sumerian tales are woven into a single Akkadian arc; a flood story belonging to another poem is drawn in and made the arc's climax; a frame is fitted around the whole that turns a hero's adventures into a meditation on what a mortal life can leave behind. Each stratum is an act of authorship, and the editors are, in Tigay's demonstration, as creative as the poets.

A note on the dates themselves. They are scholarly working dates, and their authority differs by stratum. The Sumerian poems are dated by the language and cultural setting of their composition and by the date of their surviving copies; the Old Babylonian epic by the script, language, and provenance of its tablets; the Standard Babylonian edition by Babylonian tradition's attribution and by comparison of its text against the older versions — Tigay's method — which shows the standard text reworking Old Babylonian material that must precede it. None of these dates comes from a colophon saying "written in this year," and the range given for Sîn-lēqi-unninni — some three centuries wide — is the honest measure of what the evidence fixes. This article prints the ranges, not false precision.

III. The Sumerian Poems — Bilgames, King of Uruk

The tradition begins in Sumerian, and it does not begin as an epic. Five poems about Bilgames — the Sumerian form of the hero's name, of which "Gilgamesh" is the later Akkadian form — survive as independent compositions: Gilgamesh and Aga, in which Aga of Kish besieges Uruk and is defeated; Gilgamesh and Huwawa, in two versions, telling the expedition to the Cedar Forest; Gilgamesh and the Bull of Heaven; Gilgamesh, Enkidu and the Netherworld, in which Enkidu descends to the land of the dead; and The Death of Gilgamesh, in which the hero, dying, is promised remembrance and a place among the judges of the dead rather than the life he wanted. The poems are generally dated to the age of the Third Dynasty of Ur, around 2100 BCE, though they survive almost entirely in copies made by scribal students some three centuries later, in the Old Babylonian schools — chiefly at Nippur — where Sumerian, by then a learned language no longer spoken in the street, was taught through its classics.

Three things about these poems matter for everything after. First, they are separate: no ancient manuscript joins them into one life of the hero, and their episodes do not agree in the way chapters of a single work agree — the Gilgamesh who defies Aga is a city king in a political tale, the Gilgamesh of the Netherworld poem a master whose servant Enkidu is still alive at a point where other tellings have buried him. Second, Enkidu's status differs from the later epic's: in the Sumerian poems he is Gilgamesh's servant and companion, not the wild man created as the king's equal and opposite; the friendship of equals, on which the Akkadian epic turns, is a later reimagining. Third, the poems already contain, in seed, the epic's two great preoccupations: the winning of a name that outlasts death — the Cedar Forest expedition is undertaken, in the Sumerian telling as in the Akkadian, to establish exactly that — and the confrontation with death itself, in the Netherworld poem's bleak inventory of how the dead fare and in the Death of Gilgamesh's direct refusal of the hero's hope. The Akkadian poets did not invent their themes. They inherited them, and then they did something new with them: they made them one story.

// WHY UR III? //

The kings of the Third Dynasty of Ur claimed descent from Gilgamesh and honoured him as a divine forebear; the dynasty's founder's successors presented themselves as his kin and, in the case of Shulgi, as his brother. That royal investment is the usual explanation offered in the literature (George 2003) for why poems about a long-dead king of Uruk were composed, or at least collected and prized, at exactly this period: the hero had become dynastic ideology. The poems are, among other things, what a court wanted told about its ancestor.

It is worth pausing on the evidentiary shape of this stratum, because it is the pattern of the whole article in miniature. We possess these poems because schoolboys copied them, on clay, as exercises — and because the copies were buried and the clay endured. What a tradition teaches its children is among the strongest evidence of what it values, and by 1800 BCE the schools of Mesopotamia were already teaching Bilgames. But school copies are not archives of fact about 2700 BCE. They are the literature of 1800 BCE looking backward, in a language already ancient to its copyists, at a king already ancient to its poets.

IV. The Old Babylonian Epic — "Surpassing All Other Kings"

Sometime around 1800 BCE — the early second millennium, the Old Babylonian period — an Akkadian poet, or a succession of them, did the decisive thing: the separate tales were drawn together into a single epic in the Semitic language of Babylon, organised as one life with one arc, from the king's tyranny and the making of his friend, through the Cedar Forest and the Bull of Heaven, to Enkidu's death and the hero's terrified wandering. The poem was known, as Mesopotamian works were known, by its first words: Šūtur eli šarrī, "Surpassing all other kings" — a title that announces a hero's boast where the later edition will announce a sufferer's wisdom. That difference of opening is the difference of the versions in miniature, and Tigay's comparison of the strata rests heavily on it.

The Old Babylonian epic survives badly, and the manner of its survival should be stated plainly. No complete copy exists. What we have is a scatter: two substantial tablets known by their modern homes as the Pennsylvania tablet and the Yale tablet, which together carry much of the Cedar Forest journey; a tablet from Sippar preserving the ale-wife Siduri's famous counsel to the wandering hero (§XI); and a handful of smaller fragments and school excerpts. From these pieces — supplemented, cautiously, by what the later standard version demonstrably reworks — scholars reconstruct the Old Babylonian poem's outline. The reconstruction is good enough to show the epic's shape and its great scenes; it is not good enough to quote the version entire, and any modern "Old Babylonian Gilgamesh" a reader meets is a mosaic with the joins showing, in George's edition honestly marked.

What the fragments do establish, beyond reasonable dispute, is the transformation itself. In the Old Babylonian telling, Enkidu is already the wild man: formed from clay, living with the beasts, humanised by the woman Shamhat, and set against the king as his match before he becomes his friend. The friendship is the poem's centre of gravity, and death is its engine: it is the Old Babylonian epic, on Tigay's analysis, that first binds the Cedar Forest expedition, the Bull of Heaven, and the death of Enkidu into a causal chain, and that sends its hero out beyond the known world to ask the one immortal man how death may be escaped. Whether the flood story already stood in the Old Babylonian epic as it stands in the standard version is not established — no Old Babylonian Gilgamesh tablet preserving Utnapishtim's tale has been recovered — and the positions are taken up in §V and §XII, where they belong. What is certain is that by about 1800 BCE, in Akkadian, the epic exists: Gilgamesh has become a poem.

V. The Standard Babylonian Version — Sîn-lēqi-unninni

The form in which the world reads Gilgamesh — twelve tablets, a prologue praising the hero's hard-won wisdom, the flood set into Tablet XI, the return to Uruk's walls closing the poem — is the work of a late second-millennium revision that Babylonian tradition itself credited to a named man. Later scholarly lists from Uruk preserve the name Sîn-lēqi-unninni: a scholar and exorcist-priest (mašmaššu) of Uruk, from whom learned families of the city later claimed descent, and to whom the tradition assigned the epic as its author or editor. Modern scholarship, following Tigay's demonstration of how deeply the standard text reworks its predecessors, generally accepts the attribution in substance while correcting its scale: Sîn-lēqi-unninni is best understood not as the poem's inventor but as its decisive editor — the man who gave the epic its final, fixed form — and he is accordingly, as George has observed, among the earliest editors in recorded history whose editorial work can still be examined. His date cannot be fixed from any document of his lifetime; the name's form and the textual evidence place him somewhere in the period c. 1300–1000 BCE, and this article uses that range throughout.

What the revision did can be listed, because Tigay listed it — by comparing the standard text against the Old Babylonian fragments wherever both survive. The editor supplied a new prologue: where the old poem opened on the king surpassing all kings, the new one opens on the traveller who "saw the Deep," who "brought back a message from the antediluvian age" and set his labours on a stele — recasting the whole poem, before it begins, as the record of wisdom bought by suffering. He fitted the poem with its frame: the praise of Uruk's walls that stands in the prologue returns, nearly word for word, as the poem's last speech (§XIII), so that the epic ends where it began, at the city. He interpolated the Flood: Utnapishtim's tale in Tablet XI is adapted from the older Akkadian flood poem Atrahasis — the correspondences, of episode and at points of wording, are set out in this hub's Flood Traditions — and set into the epic as the answer to the hero's question, the one case of granted immortality, explained as unrepeatable. And he regularised the poem's texture: parallel passages were harmonised, repetitions lengthened and fixed, until the text acquired the formal, liturgical steadiness that first-millennium scribes then copied as standard. Whether Sîn-lēqi-unninni himself performed every one of these acts, or completed a revision already under way in the Middle Babylonian centuries — the peripheral fragments of §XV show the poem in motion in exactly those centuries — is a refinement the evidence leaves open; the attribution of the finished form to his hand is the tradition's, and the reworking itself is demonstrable from the tablets.

He who saw the Deep, the country's foundation,
who knew the proper ways, was wise in everything!
— Epic of Gilgamesh, Tablet I, opening lines, in A. R. George's translation, The Epic of Gilgamesh (Penguin, 2003)

The standard version was known by those opening words: Ša naqba īmuru, "He who saw the Deep." Its twelve tablets carry, in the usual numbering, roughly three thousand lines; the copies that preserve them are first-millennium manuscripts, Babylonian and Assyrian, of which the fullest come from Nineveh (§XV). George's critical edition of 2003 — The Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic: Introduction, Critical Edition and Cuneiform Texts (Oxford University Press, two volumes) — is the modern foundation for all work on the poem: it collects every known fragment, including twenty-three published there for the first time, in transliteration, translation, and cuneiform copy, after its editor's personal examination of the surviving pieces. When this article gives a tablet's contents, it gives them as George's edition and the standard translations present them, and where the text is broken at a point that matters, it says so.

VI. Tablet XII — The Appended Tablet

The twelve-tablet form contains a known impostor, and honesty about it is part of telling the epic whole. Tablet XI ends the poem: the hero is home, the walls are praised, the frame is closed (§XIII). Tablet XII then begins again — with Enkidu alive. Its content is a close Akkadian rendering, in a prose-like translation, of the second half of the Sumerian poem Gilgamesh, Enkidu and the Netherworld: the hero loses objects into the land of the dead, Enkidu — here still the servant of the Sumerian telling — descends to retrieve them against instruction, is trapped below, and is allowed up as a shade for one conversation, in which he describes, item by item, how the dead fare according to how they died and how they were buried. The tablet flatly contradicts the epic it is attached to: in Tablets VII and VIII Enkidu dies of a decreed illness and is mourned and buried; in Tablet XII he is alive, descends in body, and returns as a ghost. Both cannot be the same story, and ancient scribes, who copied the twelve tablets as a set, appear to have valued completeness of the Gilgamesh dossier over consistency of its plot.

Why it was appended is debated, and the positions should be attributed. Tigay treated the tablet as an inorganic addition — a piece of the older Gilgamesh material preserved by attachment rather than integrated by revision, its presence explained by the scribal impulse to completeness and perhaps by the attraction of a twelve-tablet set. George prints and translates it with the Sumerian poem it renders, as the dossier's final document. Other readers, ancient and modern, have found in it a grim appropriateness as a last word: after all the hero's travelling, the one report from the dead themselves is the report the poem leaves ringing — that the dead do not return, and that their condition is not to be envied. That is a reading, not a compositional fact, and this article labels it as such. What is fact: the poem Sîn-lēqi-unninni's tradition built ends at Tablet XI, and most modern reading translations either print Tablet XII separately, as an appendix, or flag it in place. This article follows the story through Tablet XI and treats Tablet XII here, in the stratigraphy, where its evidence belongs.

VII. The King and the Wild Man — Tablets I–II

The story, in the standard version, opens with its ending already in view. The prologue (Tablet I) presents a Gilgamesh who has completed his journey: two-thirds divine and one-third human in the poem's reckoning, king of Uruk-the-Sheepfold, builder of its wall, and the man who travelled to the world's end and brought back tidings from before the Flood. The reader is invited up onto that wall in the poem's first movement — to survey the fired brickwork and the foundations the Seven Sages laid — and is shown the tablet-box of cedar in which the hero's own record is said to rest. Only then does the narrative step back to the beginning: to Uruk groaning under its king. Gilgamesh in his strength takes the city's sons for his works and its daughters for his pleasure — the text states the oppression through the people's cry rather than through a charge-sheet, and modern translators differ in how precisely they specify the abuses — until the complaints reach the gods.

The gods' answer is not punishment but balance. The mother-goddess Aruru, who made mankind, is charged to make the king's match: she pinches off clay, casts it into the wilderness, and forms Enkidu — hairy, strong, knowing nothing of people or country, eating grass with the gazelles and drinking at the waterhole with the beasts. A trapper, whose pits and nets the wild man keeps spoiling, is advised to fetch Shamhat, a woman of the temple, and the poem's account of Enkidu's humanisation is one of its most deliberate pieces of construction: after his union with Shamhat the animals flee him — he has become, in the text's terms, like a god in understanding, and weaker in the body — and she clothes him, teaches him bread and beer, the food of the land, and leads him, anointed and dressed, toward Uruk. On the way the shepherds receive him; at the city's gate, in Tablet II, the two heroes meet.

The meeting is a fight, and the fight is the friendship's foundation. Enkidu blocks the door with his foot against the king on an errand of his privilege; they grapple — in the poem's wrestling imagery, like bulls — the doorposts shake, the wall trembles, and Gilgamesh prevails, or is prevailed upon to stop: the versions and translations distribute the last moment differently, and the outcome that matters is not the fall but what follows it. The rage passes; the heroes embrace and make friendship. The king's mother, the goddess Ninsun, will in Tablet III formally adopt Enkidu as her son — a scene George's edition restored in full from a manuscript published in 2003 — making the wild man the king's brother in law as well as in affection. Uruk has its balance: the force that oppressed the city is now doubled and turned outward, and the poem's question becomes what two such men will do with a world too small for them. Gilgamesh's answer, proposed at the end of Tablet II, is the Cedar Forest.

// TWO-THIRDS DIVINE //

The poem's arithmetic of the hero — two-thirds of him god, one-third man — is its own theology, stated in the text and reported here as the text's claim, not as a doctrine about a historical person. It does exact narrative work: Gilgamesh is close enough to the gods to demand their privileges, and human enough, by the decisive third, to be refused them. The whole epic unfolds inside that fraction.

VIII. The Cedar Forest — Tablets III–V

The expedition to the Cedar Forest (Tablets III–V) is the epic's first great deed, and the poem is explicit about its motive, in both the Sumerian forerunner and the Akkadian telling: since a mortal cannot escape death, he can establish a name that death cannot take. The forest stands far away — in the Lebanon or Amanus ranges of the west, as the tradition locates it — and it is guarded by Humbaba (Sumerian Huwawa), a monstrous keeper appointed by the god Enlil, whose roar is a storm, whose mouth is fire, whose breath is death, and whose sevenfold radiance or terrors — the text's melammū, auras of divine dread — protect the cedars of the gods. To kill Humbaba is to do evil in Enlil's sight, and Enkidu, who knew the forest from his wild years, says so. The elders of Uruk say so too, in council, before blessing the venture and entrusting their king to Enkidu's guidance. Ninsun ascends her roof, offers incense to the sun-god Shamash — the expedition's divine patron — and adopts Enkidu. The smiths cast axes and swords of fabulous weight. The heroes set out.

The journey is told as a rhythm of distance and dream: stages of travel compressed into days, halts at which Gilgamesh sleeps in a pit dug for the purpose and wakes with dreams that Enkidu interprets — each dream more violent than the last, each interpretation more determinedly favourable. The Old Babylonian Pennsylvania and Yale tablets preserve much of this movement in its earlier form, and Tigay's comparison shows the standard version heightening the pattern: dream, terror, interpretation, advance. At the forest's edge the poem pauses, unusually for Babylonian narrative, on landscape: the cedar mountain, the trees' shade and scent, the paths Humbaba's tread has worn. That descriptive passage is precisely where the text grew in our own century — the Sulaymaniyah tablet published in 2015 (§XVII) filled a gap in Tablet V with the forest alive around its guardian: cedars dripping resin, and monkeys, cicadas, and birds making the noise that entertained Humbaba in his solitude. The monster of the standard version is met, in the new lines, as a keeper in his own kingdom before he is met as an enemy.

The fight itself (Tablet V) is swift against its long approach: Shamash raises thirteen winds to blind and bind Humbaba, the heroes close, and the guardian — suddenly a suppliant — pleads for his life, offering the forest and its timber. Gilgamesh wavers; Enkidu urges the kill, warning that a spared Humbaba means their own deaths and that Enlil's anger is a price to be faced after, not a reason to falter now. Humbaba curses them both — in the standard text the curse falls prophetically: may the two friends not grow old together; may Enkidu die first — and the heroes strike, felling the guardian and then the forest's greatest cedar, from which Enkidu fashions a door of fabulous size for Enlil's own temple at Nippur, as if the theft could be repaid as a gift. The raft of cedar timber floats the heroes home down the Euphrates. The deed is done, the name is made — and the poem has carefully shown the reader its cost being incurred: a god's appointed keeper killed, a god's forest felled, a curse spoken with a dying breath. Tablets VI and VII are the bill arriving.

IX. Ishtar and the Bull of Heaven — Tablet VI

Tablet VI is the epic's hinge, and its shortest road to catastrophe. Gilgamesh, home from the forest, washes off the journey, dresses in clean garments and his crown — and the goddess Ishtar, goddess of love and war, Uruk's own goddess, sees him and proposes marriage, offering the chariot, the tribute of kings, and the fertility of flocks and fields. The hero's refusal is the poem's most famous speech of insult: a catalogue, lover by lover, of what befell those who accepted her before him — Tammuz condemned to annual weeping, the bird whose wing she broke, the lion she trapped in pits, the horse given the whip, the shepherd turned wolf, the gardener turned frog or mole — each item a small myth in itself, several of them otherwise lost to us. The refusal is not modesty; it is a public reckoning that no mortal, and no former lover, could survive making. Ishtar ascends to her father Anu in tears and demands the Bull of Heaven to loose on Uruk, threatening, if refused, to raise the dead to eat the living. Anu yields.

The Bull's descent is a disaster in three snorts: its first breath opens a pit that swallows a hundred men, its second two hundred, its third reaches Enkidu, who seizes it by horns and tail while Gilgamesh drives his sword into its neck. The heroes kill the Bull of Heaven together, tear out its heart for Shamash, and — in the episode's deliberate final outrage — Enkidu hurls a haunch of the Bull at the goddess herself as she wails on Uruk's wall, telling her he would do the same to her. Ishtar gathers her women to mourn the Bull over its remains; Gilgamesh gathers the craftsmen to admire its horns, has them worked with lapis lazuli, and hangs them in his father's shrine. Uruk feasts. And in the palace, that night, Enkidu dreams.

Read as the poem constructs it, Tablet VI completes the pattern the Cedar Forest began. In the forest the heroes offended Enlil by killing his keeper; at Uruk they offend heaven itself by killing its Bull and insulting its goddess. The deeds are glorious, the fame is real, and the poem does not ask the reader to wish them undone — but it has arranged its theology as a ledger. The gods in council, in the dream that opens Tablet VII, state the account exactly: for the killing of Humbaba and the Bull of Heaven, one of the two must die. The only question the council debates is which.

X. The Death of Enkidu — Tablets VII–VIII

Enkidu's dream (Tablet VII) shows him the council of the great gods: Anu and Enlil decreeing that because the heroes killed Humbaba and the Bull, one must die; Enlil naming Enkidu, against Shamash's protest that the expedition was undertaken at the gods' own prompting. Enkidu wakes to a world already sentenced. He turns first on the cedar door he made — addressing it as if it could have warned him — and then, in the poem's most human sequence of curses, on the trapper who found him and on Shamhat who humanised him, wishing the wild's hardships on them both. Shamash speaks from heaven in mitigation: Shamhat gave him bread, beer, clothing, and Gilgamesh — a friend who will mourn him, lay him on a great bed, and make Uruk weep for him. The curse on Shamhat turns, in Enkidu's own mouth, to a blessing. The illness then takes its course, twelve days of decline that the text counts off day by day, and Enkidu dreams again — of a dark house of dust, the netherworld of the Sumerian poem's inventory, where kings and priests and the hero himself will eat clay. He dies, in the standard version's telling, not in battle but in bed: the death no deed can answer.

Tablet VIII is the lament, and it is among the most celebrated movements in Akkadian poetry. Gilgamesh mourns over the body in set speeches that summon the whole world of the poem to witness: the elders of Uruk, the paths of the Cedar Forest, the beasts of the steppe, the rivers Ulaya and Euphrates, the shepherds, the ale-wives — all are called to weep for Enkidu as the king weeps. He tears his hair, casts off his finery, and commissions a statue of the friend in gold and lapis lazuli; the funeral offerings are listed in a long inventory of treasures laid out for the gods of the dead. And then the poem makes its decisive turn, stated in Gilgamesh's own reasoning: if this is what became of Enkidu, the strong one, the equal — what will become of me? The grief converts, without cooling, into terror. Gilgamesh will not wait for the twelve days in his own bed. He leaves the city, tears off his clothes for skins, and goes looking for the one man who has ever escaped: Utnapishtim, the Flood survivor, made immortal, living at the mouth of the rivers at the end of the world. Tablets IX and X are that road.

// A DEATH BY DECREE, NOT BY WOUND //

Retellings sometimes kill Enkidu in the Bull fight or by Ishtar's direct curse. The standard text is otherwise, and the difference is the poem's point: the gods decree the death in council, for stated offences, and it arrives as illness over twelve days. Gilgamesh's terror in §XI is terror at an ordinary death — the kind that comes for kings in bed — and the poem means it so.

XI. The Wanderings — Tablets IX–X

The second half of the epic is a journey out of the human world, mapped station by station (Tablets IX–X). Gilgamesh wanders the steppe in skins, grieving and afraid, and the poem repeats his refrain to each new encounter: he is seeking Utnapishtim, and he is afraid of death, and he will not rest. At Mount Mashu — the twin mountain where the sun rises and sets — the scorpion-people who guard the sun's gate debate the apparition: flesh of the gods in a man who has crossed deserts no mortal crosses. Their chief lets him pass into the sun's own road, a tunnel of total darkness measured in double-hours, which the hero runs in a race against the sunrise behind him, emerging at the last moment into a garden of jewelled trees at the shore of the sea. There the ale-wife Siduri keeps her tavern at the world's edge.

Siduri's counsel is the poem's wisdom stated plainly, and its textual history is a small stratigraphy of its own. In the Old Babylonian version — the Sippar tablet of §IV — her speech stands at length: the life Gilgamesh seeks he will not find; when the gods created mankind they allotted death to mankind and kept life in their own hands; let him therefore fill his belly, dance and play, wear clean clothes, wash, cherish the child who holds his hand and the wife in his embrace, for that is the portion of man. In the standard version the long speech is cut back — George's edition shows the passage damaged and abbreviated, its full form recoverable only from the older witness — and scholars have debated whether the editor deliberately muted the poem's most direct statement of its moral, the better to let the journey refute or confirm it, or whether the loss is accident of transmission. The debate is reported here as the literature reports it (Tigay; George): the fact is the difference between the versions; the motive is interpretation. Gilgamesh, in every version, does not take the advice. He demands the road onward, and Siduri directs him to Urshanabi, Utnapishtim's boatman.

The crossing (Tablet X) is the hero's last labour. Gilgamesh, arriving in a fury, smashes the Stone Ones — the mysterious stone crew or talismans of Urshanabi's boat — and thereby destroys the very means of crossing the Waters of Death, whose touch is fatal. The repair is work: at Urshanabi's instruction he cuts a forest of punting poles, each sixty cubits long, to push the boat across in a single use per pole, so that his hand never touches the water. The far shore is reached; Utnapishtim, watching, remarks that the stranger arrives with grief in his belly and his face worn like a long traveller's; and the two meet in the poem's great dialogue of Tablet X, in which Gilgamesh tells his story and asks his question, and Utnapishtim answers with the doctrine the whole journey has been approaching: that death is the lot built into mankind's house, that the gods who assemble and decree do not publish the day of any man's death, and that the one exception — his own — was an act of the gods in a vanished age, not a prize to be won. Then, to prove it, he tells the story of that age. It occupies Tablet XI, and it is a story inside the story.

XII. Utnapishtim and the Flood — Tablet XI in Its Place

Tablet XI is the most famous tablet of ancient literature before Homer, and this article deliberately treats it briefly — not from slight regard, but because the tablet has an owner in this archive. The flood telling itself — Utnapishtim of Shuruppak, warned by Ea through the wall of his reed hut; the cube-shaped vessel, sealed with bitumen and launched with his household, his craftsmen, and the seed of all living things; the storm of six days and seven nights; the grounding on Mount Nimush; the birds sent out — dove, swallow, raven; the sacrifice on the mountain and the gods gathering like flies over it; Enlil's rage at a survivor, Ea's defence, and the settlement by which Utnapishtim and his wife are made like gods and set at the mouth of the rivers — is told at full length, with its Sumerian and Atrahasis parallels and its Genesis comparison, in Flood Traditions (§V of that article), and its hero's place among the flood-kings is treated in The Antediluvian King Lists. What belongs here is the tablet's function in the whole.

In the whole, the Flood is an answer, and the answer is no. Gilgamesh has crossed the world to ask how this one man escaped death; Utnapishtim's tale explains that the escape was not achieved but granted, once, in the unique crisis of the Flood, by the assembled gods, to a man who did not seek it — and that the granting will not recur: the divine assembly that made him immortal does not convene for Gilgamesh. The flood story's own antiquity inside the poem is, as §V noted, an editor's act: the tale is adapted from Atrahasis, where it answers a different question — how mankind came to be mortal and regulated — and its transplantation into Gilgamesh, whether by Sîn-lēqi-unninni himself or in the Middle Babylonian revision his edition completed, is the clearest case in the poem of Tigay's thesis that the epic grew by re-thinking older literature into new meaning. A reader who meets the Flood only as an excerpt — a Babylonian Noah, detachable and comparable — misses what the poem does with it: it is the longest way of saying that the door Gilgamesh is knocking on was opened once, from the other side, and is shut.

The tablet does not end with the tale. Utnapishtim sets the seeker a test scaled to his claim: if Gilgamesh would conquer death, let him first conquer sleep — let him stay awake six days and seven nights. Gilgamesh sits down against the wall and sleeps at once. Utnapishtim's wife bakes a loaf each day of his sleep and lays the loaves beside him, from fresh to stale to mouldy, so that when he wakes protesting he has barely closed his eyes, the bread itself gives the count of the days. He is washed, dressed in fresh garments that will not soil until he reaches home, and put on the boat a mortal man again. What follows — the plant, the serpent, and the walls — is the poem's true ending, and it belongs to the next section.

XIII. The Plant, the Serpent, and the Walls

As the boat is leaving, Utnapishtim's wife asks that the traveller not depart empty-handed, and her husband discloses a last secret: a thorny plant that grows on the floor of the sea, whose name, in George's translation, is "The Old Man Becomes a Young Man" — a plant not of immortality but of rejuvenation, a second youth rather than an endless life. Gilgamesh ties stones to his feet, sinks through the deep, seizes the plant though it tears his hands, cuts the stones away, and rises with it. His intention, stated to Urshanabi, is characteristic and should be noticed: he will carry it home to Uruk, test it first on an old man of the city, and then eat of it himself and be young again. The quest that began for himself alone is already turning, in the moment of its success, toward the city.

It does not arrive. At a pool of cool water, on the journey home, Gilgamesh stops to bathe; a serpent smells the plant's fragrance, steals up, and carries it off — shedding its skin as it goes, renewed by the thing it stole, in the poem's single quiet image of where rejuvenation actually lives in the world: with the animals, unasked. Gilgamesh sits down by the pool and weeps, reviewing the whole labour: for whom were his arms worn out, for whom did he give his heart's blood — for a lion of the earth, for a snake. There is no second plant, no appeal, no further road. The poem's honesty at this point is total, and it is the quality that has carried it through four millennia of readers: the hero's failure is not softened, explained, or reversed. He goes home mortal, as he came.

And then the frame closes. Gilgamesh and Urshanabi reach Uruk, and the hero speaks the poem's last speech — the words of the prologue, now earned:

O Ur-shanabi, climb Uruk's wall and walk back and forth!
Survey its foundations, examine the brickwork!
Were its bricks not fired in an oven?
Did the Seven Sages not lay its foundations?
— Epic of Gilgamesh, Tablet XI, closing lines, in A. R. George's translation, The Epic of Gilgamesh (Penguin, 2003)

The repetition is the meaning, and George's reading of it — in his edition's exegesis and in a dedicated study of the frame — is the one this article adopts: the wall the reader was shown in the prologue as the hero's monument is shown at the end by the hero himself, to his companion, as the answer the poem permits. What a mortal builds in common — the city, its wall, its baked brick on foundations older than any king — outlasts the builder in a way his body cannot. Gilgamesh does not receive this as a consolation prize announced by a narrator; he speaks it, having tested every alternative to the edge of the world and back. The poem ends with the survey of the city — one part city, one part date-grove, one part clay-pit, half a part the temple of Ishtar, in the prologue's prose cadastre — and the traveller's story is complete inside its frame. Tablet XII's appendix (§VI) hangs after this ending in the ancient copies; the poem's own ending is here.

XIV. The Themes — Mortality, Fame, and the City

A Tier B reading takes a poem's themes from the poem, and Gilgamesh states its own with unusual explicitness. Three govern the whole, and each is carried by a structural device the preceding sections have shown in place.

Mortality — The Allotted Lot

The poem's doctrine, voiced by Siduri in the Old Babylonian telling and by Utnapishtim in the standard, is that death was allotted to mankind at creation and life kept in the gods' hands. The narrative then tests the doctrine exhaustively rather than merely stating it: the one apparent exception is examined and found unrepeatable (Tablet XI); the lesser test of sleep is failed in a sentence (§XII); the substitute prize of rejuvenation is won and lost in an afternoon (§XIII). Even Enkidu's death is arranged to make the point in its hardest form — not a hero's death in battle, which the fame-economy could repay, but a decreed illness in bed. The epic is not, on this reading, a poem that consoles its hero with an afterlife: the netherworld its tradition describes, in Tablet XII and in Enkidu's dream, is a house of dust where the dead eat clay, and the poem never revises that picture. What it offers instead is the second and third themes.

Fame — The Name That Outlasts

The Cedar Forest expedition is undertaken, in the hero's stated reasoning, to establish an enduring name precisely because life cannot be made enduring. Fame in the poem is thus not vanity but strategy — the only immortality on offer in its first half — and the poem subjects it to the same testing as the rest. The name is won: Humbaba falls, the Bull falls, the horns hang in the shrine. But the fame's price is Enkidu, and by Tablet IX the strategist of the name is wandering in skins, having discovered that the name does not grieve and cannot be grieved for. The poem does not retract the strategy — the epic itself is the name, still being read four thousand years on, a fact the prologue's address to the reader, invited to open the tablet-box and read the hero's labours, seems almost to predict — but it demotes fame below the third theme, to which the frame gives the first and last word.

The City — What Is Built in Common

Uruk is the poem's constant: oppressed by its king in Tablet I, mourning with him in Tablet VIII, receiving him back in Tablet XI. The standard editor's frame makes the city the poem's final object of contemplation, and George's analysis of that frame (§XIII) draws the conclusion the structure supports: the wisdom Gilgamesh brings home is the redirection of a king's energy from the impossible prize to the possible one — the wall, the city, the work that stands because it was never his alone. Read beside this hub's treatment of Uruk itself, in Ubaid and Uruk, the theme gains its archaeological shadow: the real Uruk of the fourth millennium did raise walls and temples that outlasted every name attached to them, and the poem, composed in a world that walked among such ruins, knew exactly what permanence looked like. It looked like brickwork.

The epic has attracted symbolic readings in every age that has read it — psychological, initiatory, and esoteric among them — and this archive's other divisions treat some of those traditions under their own tiers. Here, under Tier B discipline, the themes above are confined to what the text states and its structure performs. A reading that requires the poem to mean what it nowhere says is a telling about the poem, not the poem, and is labelled accordingly wherever this hub meets one.

XV. Transmission — From Uruk to the Ends of the Cuneiform World

Between the standard edition's composition and its modern recovery lies a transmission history that is itself primary evidence — for the poem's standing in its own world. Gilgamesh was a school and library text: copied in scribal training, excerpted for exercises, and collected in the great libraries, and its manuscripts map the whole geography of cuneiform culture in the second and first millennia BCE. In Babylonia and Assyria the copies come from Uruk, Babylon, Nippur, Nineveh, Assur, Sultantepe, and other cities. Beyond Mesopotamia, the Middle Babylonian centuries (§II) carried the poem to the periphery of the cuneiform world, where it was copied in Akkadian and reworked in other tongues — the surest ancient testimony that the epic was already, in its own age, an international classic.

Hattusa — The Poem in Three Languages

The richest peripheral witness is Hattusa, the Hittite capital in Anatolia (modern Boğazköy), whose archives preserve the Gilgamesh tradition in three languages: Akkadian manuscripts of the Babylonian poem; a Hurrian version, surviving in fragments; and a Hittite version — a freer paraphrase rather than a close translation, which compresses and reshapes the story for its new audience. Gary Beckman's edition, The Hittite Gilgamesh (2019), gathers the Hattusa material in all three languages. Beckman's assessment, followed here, is that the tradition was imported to Hattusa for scribal instruction — Gilgamesh as curriculum, part of what a scribe at the Hittite court was expected to know of the Babylonian classics — while allowing that the Hittite paraphrase may also have been read aloud for the court's entertainment. Either way, the evidentiary point stands: by the late second millennium, a scribe could not be fully trained, from the Euphrates to Anatolia, without Gilgamesh.

Ugarit, Emar, Megiddo — The Western Fragments

Down the Euphrates and along the Levantine coast, the fragments continue. At Emar, on the great bend of the Euphrates, a twelfth-century scriptorium produced Gilgamesh copies among its scholarly tablets. At Ugarit (Ras Shamra), on the Syrian coast, excavations in 1994 in a private house — the house of Urtenu — yielded four pieces of the poem, published by Daniel Arnaud and analysed by George (2007): a complete tablet carrying the epic's opening, and fragments of the Humbaba encounter. The Ugarit opening tablet mattered beyond its size: it filled the last gaps in the standard version's prologue, confirming the opening lines quoted in §V, and it showed the standard text — not merely older versions — circulating on the Mediterranean coast in the twelfth century BCE. At Megiddo, in the southern Levant, a single fragment found in a disturbed context carries lines paralleling Tablet VII — Enkidu's death — in a fourteenth-century setting; petrographic analysis has suggested the tablet was made from clay of the Gezer region, a local copy rather than an import. George's conclusion from the western finds, stated in his Ugarit study, is the one this article adopts: copying Gilgamesh was part of scribal learning across the Late Bronze Age West, from Anatolia to Canaan.

Ashurbanipal's Library — The Survival Vehicle

The poem's survival into modernity, however, runs through one collection above all. Ashurbanipal, king of Assyria (reigned 669–c. 631 BCE), assembled at Nineveh the most deliberate library of the ancient world, sending scribes to copy and collect the literature of Babylonia and Assyria for his palace. The library's Gilgamesh manuscripts — seventh-century copies of the standard version, carefully written and collated — are the fullest single body of the poem's text, and the backbone of every modern edition. When Nineveh fell in 612 BCE the palace burned, and the fire that destroyed the library baked its clay tablets hard — the accident of preservation that carried tens of thousands of tablets through twenty-four centuries of burial to their excavation in the 1850s (§XVI). The same pattern of survival-by-catastrophe governs this hub's other great textual subjects, and it deserves its standing caution: what survives of ancient literature is what fire, abandonment, and chance selected, and the Gilgamesh we read is the Gilgamesh of the libraries that burned — a selection, not a totality. For the scribal world that maintained this literature — its omen series, its star-lore, its copying discipline — see Babylonian Sky Science.

XVI. Recovery — 1872 and the Flood Tablet Sensation

The tablets came out of Nineveh in the early 1850s, in the excavations of Austen Henry Layard and, decisively for the library, his assistant Hormuzd Rassam, who in 1853 uncovered the palace collection's main body; the crates went to the British Museum, where the work of reading them fell to a new science — Assyriology — that was inventing itself on the material as it went. Among the museum's assistants was George Smith (1840–1876), a banknote engraver's apprentice who had taught himself cuneiform in the museum's galleries and been taken on to sort and join fragments. In the autumn of 1872, working through Nineveh tablets, Smith recognised on a fragment the outline of a story he knew from Genesis: a ship, a mountain, birds sent out over receding waters. The fragment belonged to Tablet XI. The anecdote later told of that moment — Smith, by his colleague E. A. Wallis Budge's much-later recollection, jumping up and rushing about the room in excitement, even shedding clothing — is reception folklore, repeated here as such; the documented facts are strong enough without it.

On 3 December 1872 Smith read his paper, "The Chaldean Account of the Deluge," to the Society of Biblical Archaeology in London, with the Prime Minister, William Ewart Gladstone, in the audience. The sensation was immediate and is a matter of newspaper record: here was a flood account, in a language older than the Hebrew Bible's composition, on tablets from a library of the seventh century BCE, telling — in its own theology and for its own poem's reasons — of a divinely warned survivor, a vessel, a mountain, and birds. The Daily Telegraph funded Smith to return to Nineveh in 1873 to hunt for the missing pieces, and within days of digging he found a fragment completing a broken passage of the flood account — a piece of luck so neat it was reported as providence. Smith published the wider epic in the years following, in The Chaldaean Account of Genesis (1876) among other works, and died at Aleppo that same year, aged thirty-six, on a third expedition. The epic's modern life begins with him.

This article handles 1872 briefly and factually, as reception, for a reason the hub's rules require stating. The sensation of 1872 was a sensation about the Bible: the Victorian audience heard Tablet XI as a witness for or against Genesis, and the poem entered modern culture as "the Babylonian flood story" — a framing that excerpted one tablet from twelve and set the epic's whole meaning at the service of another book's questions. The century and a half since has been, in part, the slow recovery of the other eleven tablets: the editions of Haupt, Jensen, and Thompson in the decades after Smith; Samuel Noah Kramer's reassembly of the Sumerian poems in the mid-twentieth century; Tigay's stratigraphy in 1982; and George's critical edition in 2003. The comparison between Tablet XI and Genesis is a legitimate and continuing scholarly subject, treated in Flood Traditions under that article's rules. But the poem Smith actually found was larger than the sensation he made, and this article exists, in part, to give the larger poem its proportions back.

XVII. The Text Still Growing — New Tablets, New Lines

Gilgamesh is not a closed text, and its modern history did not end with the great editions. The poem is still being recovered, fragment by fragment, from museum drawers and new acquisitions, and two modern landmarks fix the scale of what "recovery" now means. The first is George's critical edition of 2003 (§V): two volumes, 986 pages, gathering every fragment known to its editor — the known pieces having nearly doubled since the previous critical edition of 1930 — with twenty-three fragments published for the first time, a full introduction to the poem's literary history, and the Sumerian Netherworld poem edited as the dossier's appendix. It is the foundation on which Foster's second edition (2019) and all current work build, and its companion, George's Penguin translation, is the reading text this article quotes. A critical edition of that kind is not a full stop: it is a census, taken at a date, of a body of evidence that keeps arriving.

The second landmark arrived a decade later, from Iraq. In 2011 the Sulaymaniyah Museum, in the Kurdistan Region, acquired a collection of tablets — purchased, under a museum policy of intercepting material from the illicit trade, from a seller who did not know what he had; the circumstances are documented in the publication and are reported here as its authors report them. Among the tablets, Farouk Al-Rawi recognised a large fragment of Tablet V of the standard version. Al-Rawi and George read it together at the museum over five days, and published it in the Journal of Cuneiform Studies in 2014, with the find reaching wide public notice in 2015. The tablet — Neo-Babylonian in script, probably not later than the sixth century BCE on George's palaeographic judgement, its original findspot unknown but probably a Babylonian site — does three things at once: it confirms the order of passages in Tablet V where the older witnesses were disordered or broken; it fills gaps; and it adds some twenty lines new to the epic, chiefly the Cedar Forest description of §VIII — the dripping cedars, the monkeys, cicadas, and birds of Humbaba's jungle, and Enkidu's rueful line after the killing, that the heroes have reduced the forest to a wasteland. A poem standardised by 1000 BCE acquired new lines in 2015 CE. No other work of literature in this archive can say that, and the fact is the fitting close of the transmission story: the dossier is open, and somewhere — in a drawer, in a mound, in a collection not yet read — the next fragment is waiting.

// HOW MUCH OF THE POEM DO WE HAVE? //

About two-thirds of the standard version's lines are recovered, unevenly: some tablets are nearly complete, others — the middle of Tablet V, stretches of VII and VIII — are mosaics. Modern translations differ in how they handle the gaps: George's Penguin marks and bridges them conservatively; Foster's Norton edition supplements from the older versions, with the supplements signalled; Dalley's Oxford translation restores more freely. A reader comparing translations is comparing, in part, three editorial policies toward broken clay — which is why this article names its translation at every quotation.

XVIII. What the Epic Is and Is Not Evidence For

The epic is evidence, of the first order, for the literary and intellectual world of ancient Mesopotamia across more than a thousand years. It shows a civilisation taking a local hero of its remembered past and re-thinking him, in language after language and century after century, into its most searching statement about death: that it is allotted, that fame is a strategy against it and an insufficient one, and that what endures of a person is the work taken up by others — a city, a wall, a story copied by schoolchildren. It is evidence for the mechanics of ancient literature itself: how poems grow by edition and interpolation, how a flood tale can migrate from one poem's theology into another's, how an editor can be an author, and how a text becomes a classic — taught at Hattusa, copied at Ugarit and Emar, made by a local hand at Megiddo, and shelved by a king at Nineveh as part of the deliberate sum of knowledge. And it is evidence for its own reception: that a poem about a king of Uruk could be standardised by 1000 BCE, lost for two millennia, and recovered in 1872 into a sensation — and could then, in 2015, grow by twenty lines.

What it is not evidence for must be stated with the same definiteness. It is not a chronicle of a historical reign: no document of Gilgamesh's supposed lifetime names him, the poems stand centuries after their setting at their very earliest, and the reign the King List assigns him — treated in The Antediluvian King Lists — belongs to that list's own arithmetic of the deep past, not to recoverable political history. It is not evidence for a historical flood: Tablet XI is a literary adaptation of Atrahasis, set in the poem as a story told by an immortal about an unrepeatable age, and its evidentiary home is the history of a tradition, as Flood Traditions argues at length. It is not a biography with the marvels removed: stripping Humbaba and the Bull from the text does not leave a residue of fact, because the residue — the tyranny, the friendship, the journeys — is the same poem's telling, not a second, soberer source underneath it. And its antiquity, great as it is, is the antiquity of its texts: c. 2100 BCE for the first poems, c. 1800 for the epic, c. 1300–1000 for the standard form. Claims that reach behind those dates — to the hero's own age, to events before the Flood — are the poem's claims about itself, honoured here as poetry and recorded as tradition, and printed in this hub under Tier B, where they belong.

Perhaps that is why the poem survives its own discipline so well. Gilgamesh is a text that already knows the difference this article has been enforcing: between the life that cannot be kept and the record that can. Its hero sets his labours on a stele; its prologue invites us to open the tablet-box and read; its scribes copied it for fifteen centuries; its last recovered lines were read in Sulaymaniyah in our own decade. The epic is not evidence that a king of Uruk escaped death. It is the thing he is shown, at the end, pointing to instead — the work that remains when the worker is gone, examined brick by brick, and found, by the poem's own exacting standard, sufficient.
