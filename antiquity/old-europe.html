This is a Tier A article: everything in it rests on excavated, dated, and published archaeological evidence — tells dug in stratigraphic layers, houses recovered by magnetometry, signs catalogued object by object, and slags analysed in laboratories. Where scholars disagree, as they do about the Vinča signs, the burning of the houses, and the population of the mega-sites, the disagreement itself is reported, with its positions attributed. One large interpretive construction is treated in this article at length — Marija Gimbutas's vision of a goddess-centred “Old Europe” — and it is treated as what it is: historiography, the history of an idea about the past, not the voice of this article and not Tier A evidence. It receives its own section (§XIV), fenced off from the excavated record it was built on, so that the reader can see exactly where the record ends and the construction begins.

“Old Europe” itself needs the same discipline, and it gets it here at the start. The phrase is Gimbutas's term for the Neolithic and Copper Age cultures of southeastern Europe before the steppe migrations of the later fourth and third millennia BCE. This article uses it in one sense only: as a geographic-chronological label for a place and a span of time — roughly, the Danube basin, the Balkans, and the forest-steppe between the Carpathians and the Dnieper, from about 5700 to about 3500 BCE. Used that way, the label is convenient and harmless. It carries, in this article, no claim about goddesses, matriarchy, or a lost peaceful civilisation. Those claims are Gimbutas's, they are contested, and §XIV says so in detail.

What the excavated record does show is remarkable enough without them. In the sixth millennium BCE, farming communities of the middle Danube built tell settlements that grew, layer on layer, for eight centuries; they fired some of the finest dark burnished pottery of the Neolithic world, modelled thousands of clay figurines, and incised a repertoire of signs on pots, spindle whorls, and figurines — a sign system that has been claimed as Europe's first writing and that the mainstream of scholarship, for reasons this article sets out in full, does not accept as writing at all. In the fifth millennium, villages of this same world became the setting for the earliest copper smelting yet securely evidenced anywhere — a metallurgical story told in this hub's First Metals article, and treated here only in its social setting. And in the early fourth millennium, far to the northeast, the related Cucuteni-Trypillia communities built planned settlements of up to some 400 hectares, with thousands of houses arranged in concentric rings — among the largest settlements anywhere on earth at that date — without palaces, without royal tombs, and without any writing at all. This article is the hub's account of that world, and its demonstration case for a question the whole division depends on: where, exactly, does a system of marks become writing? The Vinča signs are where the line is drawn, and drawn in public.

I. The Danube World — A Name and a Map

The world of this article is defined by two river systems and the land between them. In the west and centre stand the Balkans and the middle Danube: the plains of modern Serbia, the Banat, southern Hungary, western Romania, and northern Bulgaria, where the Danube and its tributaries — the Sava, the Tisza, the Morava — cut corridors of loess soil through hill country rich in copper ore, salt, and obsidian-bearing volcanic glass. In the east stands the forest-steppe of modern Moldova and Ukraine, between the Carpathian arc and the Dnieper River, where the Prut, Dniester, and Southern Bug run south to the Black Sea across some of the deepest black-earth soils in Europe. The two zones are distinct, and the archaeology keeps their names distinct — Vinča for the Danubian world, Cucuteni-Trypillia for the eastern one — but they were in contact throughout, shared crops, animals, pottery technologies, sign repertoires, and the habit of burning houses, and they rose and transformed on roughly the same clock. Treating them in one article is not a merger. It is a recognition that the questions they pose — about scale without hierarchy, signs without writing, and fire as a way of ending a house — are the same questions.

// THE LABEL //
“Old Europe”

Gimbutas's term, used in this article as a geographic-chronological label only. Her interpretation of the world it names is historiography, treated in §XIV and nowhere else as this article's voice.

The chronological frame matters because this world is contemporary with — not derivative of — the early civilisations treated elsewhere in this hub. While Vinča's tells were rising beside the Danube, the Ubaid world of southern Mesopotamia was building its temple platforms and, in the centuries that followed, Uruk was becoming the first great city (see Ubaid and Uruk). The comparison is instructive precisely because the two worlds diverge. Uruk's growth produced administrators, seals, numerical tablets, and finally writing. The Danube world's growth produced — at its Trypillia extreme — settlements as large as early Uruk, and produced no writing, no sealing bureaucracy, and no visible ruling stratum at all. Scale, it turns out, does not compel hierarchy; and marks, it turns out, do not compel writing. Both halves of that lesson are documented in the ground, and both are central to what this hub exists to record.

A word on names and spellings. The eastern culture carries a double name because it was discovered twice: at Cucuteni in Romania, excavated from 1884, and at Trypillia (Tripolye) near Kyiv, excavated by Vikentiy Khvoyka from the 1890s. Romanian and Moldovan scholarship says Cucuteni; Ukrainian scholarship says Trypillia; the hyphenated form is the standard compromise and is used here. Vinča, with its caron, is the Serbian village beside the type site; the culture's Romanian extension is sometimes called Vinča-Turdaș after the Transylvanian site of Turdaș, where Zsófia Torma excavated inscribed pottery in the 1870s — the first Vinča signs ever recovered, decades before the culture had a name (§VI).

II. Farming Comes Up the Danube — The Package in Its Danubian Form

Farming reached the Danube from the south. The Near Eastern founder package — emmer and einkorn wheat, barley, lentils, peas, chickpeas, bitter vetch, and flax, with sheep, goats, cattle, and pigs — had entered Greece and the southern Balkans by the seventh millennium BCE, and in the centuries around 6200–5700 BCE communities of the Starčevo culture (and its Körös and Criș relatives) carried it into the middle Danube basin and the Carpathian foothills. The Danubian form of the package, as it stabilised in this cooler, wetter, more continental climate, is the economic foundation of everything in this article, and its local adjustments are as informative as its imports. Cereals remained the staple core, grown on the loess terraces above the floodplains; pulses and flax accompanied them; and the animal economy leaned harder on cattle than the Aegean south had done — cattle for meat and, increasingly as the millennia pass, for milk and traction, alongside pig in the riverine woods and sheep and goat on the drier ground. Hunting — red deer, wild boar, aurochs — and Danube fishing continued beside the herds, as they did at Mehrgarh, where the same western grains met a different local world at the other end of the farming expansion.

Longhouses West, Tells East

The package travelled in two architectural forms, and the contrast between them structures the settlement geography of Neolithic Europe. Northwest of the Danube world, up the river and across the loess belt of central Europe, the Linearbandkeramik (LBK) farmers built the famous longhouses: massive timber halls, commonly 20 to 30 metres long and sometimes more, housing an extended household, its stores, and often its byred animals under one roof, grouped in loose villages that shifted within their territories as houses aged. The longhouse village was a settlement of a generation or two, not of centuries. In the Balkans and the Danube basin itself, by contrast, the dominant trajectory ran the other way: toward the tell. Where a community rebuilt on the same plot for centuries — levelling the remains of burned or decayed houses and building on the platform of debris — the settlement rose into an artificial mound. Tells concentrate everything this article studies: deep stratigraphy, dense rebuilding, long local memory, and assemblages rich enough to argue from. The Vinča world is a tell world at its core, ringed by flat, shorter-lived settlements of longhouse scale and plan; the Cucuteni-Trypillia world, which began with smaller villages of the same Danubian stock, would later take settlement aggregation to its extreme in open, planned, unfortified mega-sites (§XI). Both forms grew from the same farming package. What differed was how long a community stayed, and how it chose to gather.

Two further features of the Danubian economy deserve note before the tells themselves, because they explain the tells' wealth. The first is exchange over distance. Obsidian from the Tokaj mountains of the Carpathians, marine shell — above all Spondylus — from the Aegean, salt from Transylvanian and Carpathian sources, high-quality flint, and copper minerals and metal (§IX) all moved along the river corridors, and tell communities sat on the junctions. The second is craft investment in the household: the Danubian Neolithic put extraordinary labour into objects that farming does not require — polished stone axes, burnished and graphite-painted pottery, carved bone and antler, woven textiles attested by loom weights and spindle whorls, and the figurines of §V. This was a world with surplus, skill, and time, organised — so far as the record shows — without anyone visibly in charge of it. Hold that observation; §XIII returns to it with the whole evidence in hand.

// THE PACKAGE, COMPARED //

This hub has now met the western founder package three times: on the Nile margin, where it arrived late and mixed with Saharan pastoralism; at Mehrgarh, where its grains met locally domesticated zebu; and here, in the Danube basin, where it met cattle-rich river plains and copper-bearing hills. In each place the package changed in the meeting. The Danubian changes — cattle emphasis, tell settlement, pyrotechnology — are the ones that matter for this article, because the road from Danubian kilns to Danubian furnaces (§IX) is one of the shortest in prehistory.

III. Vinča-Belo Brdo — The Tell and Its Times

The type site stands on the right bank of the Danube some 14 kilometres southeast of central Belgrade, where a high loess terrace — Belo Brdo, “White Hill” — rises above the river near the mouth of the small Bolečica stream. Miloje Vasić, Serbia's first professional archaeologist, began excavating there in 1908 and returned across three decades; his four-volume Prehistoric Vinča (1932–1936) made the site, and the culture named from it, foundational for Balkan prehistory. The tell's cultural deposits run to roughly nine to ten and a half metres in depth — an accumulation without parallel in the northern Balkans — and the greater part of that depth is Neolithic: beneath later Copper Age, Bronze Age, and medieval traces lies the stacked record of the Vinča settlement itself, founded on a terrace that had already seen a Starčevo occupation.

Stratigraphy — How a Tell Keeps Time

A tell is a clock made of houses. At Vinča-Belo Brdo the excavators distinguished successive building horizons: floors of packed clay, the collapsed and levelled debris of the houses that stood on them — much of it burned daub, fired hard in the fires that ended so many Danubian houses (§XII) — and the floors of the next generation laid over the top. Vasić's deep sounding divided the Neolithic sequence by depth below datum, and the culture's internal phasing still carries his framework, refined by later excavators into the four phases used throughout the literature: Vinča A (c. 5400/5300–5200 BCE), B (c. 5200–5000/4950), C (c. 5000/4950–4850/4800), and D (c. 4850/4800–4600/4500), the first two forming the early, the last two the late Vinča period. Pottery style is the phase-marker in practice: the dark burnished wares, vessel forms, and decorative habits change horizon by horizon in a seriation anchored at this site and recognised, with local variation, at tells across the culture's range — Selevac, Pločnik, Gomolava, Divostin, and dozens more.

The framework was placed on an absolute footing by the Bayesian radiocarbon programme published by Tasić and colleagues in 2015–2016 (“Vinča-Belo Brdo, Serbia: the times of a tell”), which modelled some six hundred radiocarbon determinations from the site and its culture. Its conclusion, now the standard citation, is that the Vinča culture at the type site runs from the first decades of the fifty-third century BCE to the later forty-sixth century — in round terms c. 5300 to c. 4600 BCE for the tell's main occupation, within a culture span of c. 5400/5300–4500 BCE. The same modelling sharpened a second fact: the culture's end, around 4600/4500 BCE, is marked at many sites by a horizon of burned buildings — the tell habit, and much of the world that sustained it, closes in fire (§XII).

John Chapman's syntheses — from The Vinča Culture of South-East Europe (1981) onward — established the reading of the culture that this article follows: Vinča is best understood not as a uniform state-like entity but as a network of tell and flat settlements sharing pottery styles, figurine traditions, exchange goods, and sign repertoires across the central and northern Balkans — an interaction sphere of farming communities, some of them large and long-lived, none of them, on present evidence, capitals. The culture's settlements range from hamlets under a hectare to tells of ten hectares and more; a few flat sites spread far wider. What they share is a material style so consistent that the “culture” label has survived a century of scrutiny — and a social organisation so undemonstrative that the same century has failed to find its rulers.

IV. Houses, Rows, and Daily Life at the Tells

The Vinča house, as recovered at Belo Brdo and its sister tells, is a rectangular, timber-framed building: walls of wattle — woven hazel or willow withies — daubed inside and out with clay, a pitched roof, and a floor of packed clay that was resurfaced as it wore. Houses divide internally into one to three rooms; many contain a built clay oven or hearth, clay benches and bins, and storage vessels set into or standing on the floor. At Belo Brdo the later houses stand in rows, aligned on common axes with narrow lanes between them — a planned regularity, renewed rebuilding by rebuilding, that excavators have reasonably called street-like. Some walls were plastered and painted. The houses were substantial, maintained, and — the point that will matter in §XII — very often burned at the end of their lives, their daub fired to a durable orange-red mass that preserved floor plans, fallen roof timbers as voids and impressions, and the contents of rooms as they were left or placed.

Inside and around those houses, the assemblage of daily life is rich and, by Neolithic standards, remarkably complete. The pottery is the culture's signature: dark burnished wares, black-topped and graphite-painted vessels, carinated bowls, amphorae, and lids, fired in kilns whose control of atmosphere produced the deep black and grey surfaces that no open firing achieves reliably. Ground stone axes and adzes, chipped stone of flint and Carpathian obsidian, bone awls and antler tools, spindle whorls and loom weights for textiles, clay stamps, and — from the later phases — copper ornaments and tools (§IX) fill out the inventory. Food remains track the economy of §II: cereal grains and pulse seeds from storage contexts, animal bone dominated by cattle, pig, sheep, and goat with a persistent wild component, and fish bone and shell from the Danube itself.

Burial, by contrast, is the record's conspicuous gap, here and across most of the Danube world. Vinča tells yield few formal cemeteries; the dead appear sparsely — occasional burials within settlements, scattered human bone — and the great majority of the Vinča dead are archaeologically missing, disposed of in ways that left no recoverable trace. The Cucuteni-Trypillia world shares the pattern almost completely (§XIII). The gap matters methodologically: a civilisation whose graves cannot be read cannot have its hierarchy read from graves either, and claims about its social order — egalitarian or otherwise — must rest on houses, goods, and settlement plans instead. It also matters for §XIV: where the dead are invisible, imagination has room to move in, and it did.

THE HOUSE AS ARCHIVE

Because so many Vinča houses burned, their plans, ovens, bins, and floor assemblages survive as fired-clay snapshots. The tell's stratigraphy is built of these snapshots, stacked eight centuries deep at Belo Brdo.

ROWS, NOT CLUSTERS

Later Vinča houses at Belo Brdo stand in aligned rows with lanes between — order imposed at the scale of the neighbourhood, maintained across rebuildings, without any central monument for the order to radiate from.

THE MISSING DEAD

Formal burials are rare across the Vinča world. Most of its dead left no recoverable trace — a fact that limits every social argument built on graves, in both directions.

KILN-FIRED BLACK WARE

The culture's dark burnished pottery required controlled, reducing kiln atmospheres — the same pyrotechnological mastery that, turned on copper ore, produced the first smelting (§IX).

V. The Figurine Corpus — Described Plainly

No class of object has carried more interpretive weight in this field — or buckled under it more visibly — than the clay figurines. The corpus first, in plain terms. Vinča sites yield anthropomorphic figurines in the thousands when fragments are counted: standing or seated human figures, typically a few centimetres to perhaps twenty centimetres tall, modelled in clay and fired. Many wear the culture's most recognisable face — a triangular or pentagonal “mask-like” visage with incised or applied eyes, a prominent nose, and incised detail marking hair, clothing, belts, and ornaments on the body. Zoomorphic figurines — cattle above all — accompany them, as do clay models of furniture, altars or tables, and houses. The figures are found in houses, in pits, and in settlement debris; most are broken, and complete examples are the minority. Female figures, identified by breasts and other modelled features, predominate among the sexed examples; male and unsexed figures also occur. That is the corpus. Everything beyond it — goddess, ancestress, doll, votive, teaching device, self-representation — is interpretation, and the history of the interpretations is a warning this article intends the reader to receive.

Fragmentation — Chapman's Wager

The most influential modern reading of the corpus is not about what the figurines depict but about what was done to them. In Fragmentation in Archaeology (2000), John Chapman argued that the breakage pattern of Neolithic objects in southeastern Europe — figurines, vessels, and other things — is too patterned to be accident alone: objects were, in many cases, deliberately broken, and their fragments circulated, deposited in different places and contexts. Drawing on anthropological accounts of personhood, Chapman proposed that fragments acted as tokens of relationships — his term is enchained relations: a broken figurine's parts, kept by different people or placed in different houses and pits, materially bound those people and places together, and carried something of the object's and its maker's identity with them. Later work with Bisserka Gaydarska refined the argument object-class by object-class, and contextual studies — for example at Selevac, where complete figurines associate significantly with houses and transversely broken ones with pits — have found patterning of the kind the hypothesis predicts.

The argument is reported here at exactly its standing: it is a serious, evidence-engaged interpretation, widely discussed and not a consensus. Critics have pressed two points that remain live. First, deliberateness is hard to prove object by object: figurines break at their weak points — necks, waists, leg junctions — under trampling and soil pressure as readily as under a deliberate hand, and some re-analyses find breakage patterns compatible with ordinary taphonomy. Second, the step from patterned breakage to enchained personhood is an analogy, borrowed from Melanesian ethnography, and analogies illuminate without demonstrating. What is not in dispute is the underlying observation that made the debate worth having: the figurines were made in quantity, used in domestic space, and broken — somehow — as a normal part of their lives. A Tier A account describes the corpus, reports the wager and its critics, and declines to tell the reader what a Vinča figurine “was.” Nobody knows. The next two sections show the same discipline applied to a harder case, where a wrong answer has been printed in a hundred popular books.

// THE GODDESS SHORTCUT //

The fastest way to misread this corpus is to call every female figurine a goddess and stop thinking. The label forecloses the questions the objects actually raise — who made them, where they were kept, why they broke, what the masks and incised clothing meant — and it imports a theology no Vinča text exists to confirm. Gimbutas's goddess reading is treated as historiography in §XIV. In this section the figurines are clay, context, and breakage: what can be shown.

VI. The Vinča Signs — The Corpus

The signs were found before the culture was. In the 1870s, Zsófia Torma excavated the Transylvanian site of Turdaș and recovered pottery fragments incised with marks she could not read; in 1908 Vasić's first season at Vinča produced more, and as excavation spread across the culture's range, the marks accumulated into a corpus. Its present shape, after a century of collection, is this. Inscribed objects number in the hundreds — the later Danube-wide databases, which extend the net beyond the Vinča culture proper to related sign-using groups, catalogue over nine hundred inscribed artefacts and several thousand individual sign occurrences, but the strictly Vinča corpus is the smaller, hundreds-strong set on which the argument turns. The objects are overwhelmingly pottery: vessel bases, walls, and rims incised or painted with marks. The remainder are ceramic spindle whorls, figurines, and a small scatter of other things — clay tablets, stamps, and weights among them.

The signs themselves form a repertoire of a few hundred distinct types in the broadest catalogues, fewer when variants are grouped: straight and crossed lines, chevrons, triangles, circles and spirals, comb- and brush-like clusters, ladder and net patterns, and a minority of pictorial forms — schematic animals and human-like figures among them. The decisive facts about the corpus, however, are distributional, and they are not in serious dispute. More than 85 percent of all inscriptions consist of a single sign. Groups of two or more signs are the exception; long sequences are vanishingly rare; and where signs do group, their order and reading direction are usually indeterminate — there is no demonstrable syntax, no line structure, no recurring formula of the kind that even the most pictographic true scripts display. Sign use is also partitioned by object: single signs sit almost exclusively on pots, while grouped signs cluster on spindle whorls — a division that already suggests different jobs being done by the same repertoire.

Winn's Catalogue — Care as a Method

The corpus received its scholarly foundation in Shan M. Winn's Pre-Writing in Southeastern Europe: The Sign System of the Vinča Culture ca. 4000 B.C. (1981) — a work whose title is itself the most important sentence ever written about these signs. Winn, a linguist, catalogued the inscribed objects site by site (the catalogue designations, such as the Jela spindle whorls, still anchor discussion), classified the sign forms, and examined their combinations. His conclusion was deliberately modest: the material demonstrated a sign system — conventional, shared, meaningful marks, some of them plausibly functioning as pictograms or ideograms — but the evidence did not warrant calling it writing. He called it pre-writing, and the care of that choice deserves emphasis, because the literature since has split precisely on it: scholars who begin from Winn's catalogue and keep his discipline reach his conclusion; claims that go beyond it characteristically go beyond his evidence as well. Later quantitative work has broadly sustained the catalogue's picture. One much-cited linguistic-statistical analysis sorts the sign occurrences into roughly 59 percent sharing the properties of potters' marks, some 11.5 percent belonging to the asymmetric ornament typical of spindle whorls, and some 29.5 percent that may represent a symbolic — semasiographic — notation: signs that convey meaning without encoding speech. Those proportions are one study's model, not a census of intentions; but every serious analysis of the corpus lands in the same neighbourhood. Most of the signs, most of the time, were doing the ordinary work of marks.

The contrast column is not a rhetorical device; it is the argument. Writing, as this hub defines it in The Origins of Writing, is a system that encodes language — or, at its proto-cuneiform threshold, a system of accounts with fixed sign order, numerical systems, and standardised formats administered by specialists. The Vinča corpus displays none of those diagnostics. It displays something else, interesting in its own right and diminished by being miscalled: a durable, widely shared repertoire of conventional marks, maintained across a culture area for centuries, doing work — marking, counting perhaps, invoking, identifying — that human societies very often do without writing. Before that verdict is sealed in §VIII, however, one group of objects must be faced on its own, because it is the exhibit on which the entire script claim has been made to rest.

VII. Tărtăria — Three Tablets and a Disputed Context

In 1961, at the Neolithic site of Tărtăria in Transylvania, Romania, a team directed by Nicolae Vlassa excavated a pit containing, on the excavators' account, the burned and disarticulated remains of an adult human, a group of some twenty-six clay figurines and other objects, and three small clay tablets — two rectangular, one round, two of them pierced — incised with pictorial and abstract signs. One rectangular tablet carries a horned animal, a schematic figure, and a vegetal motif; the others bear rows of abstract signs. Vlassa interpreted the signs as a kind of primitive writing comparable to early Sumerian pictography, and the find became, at a stroke, the most famous — and the most contested — in Balkan prehistory.

The Dating Problem, Stated Exactly

The tablets' date cannot be read off the tablets, and the reason is a conservation accident with permanent consequences: after excavation, the originally unbaked tablets were baked by museum conservators to preserve them, destroying the possibility of directly radiocarbon-dating their clay. Every date ever offered for them is therefore by association. The famous early date — around c. 5300 BCE, which would make the tablets older than Sumerian proto-writing by well over a millennium — rests on radiocarbon determinations from other materials attributed to the site's relevant layers, extended to the tablets through the excavators' account of the pit's stratigraphy. Both links of that chain are disputed. The stratigraphic circumstances of the discovery were never published to the standard the claim requires, and Vlassa's account has been questioned in detail; scholars have argued that the tablets could be an intrusion from higher, later levels of the site, which would bring them down toward the fourth millennium or later. Gheorghe Lazarovici, the leading Romanian specialist on the Tărtăria material and a defender of the tablets' Neolithic context, has argued the case for the early association at length; other specialists, including contributors to the same debates, regard the context as unproven. The forgery claims that circulate at the debate's fringe — that the signs copy Sumerian forms from popular publications — are themselves unproven, and this article gives them no more than this sentence.

The correct Tier A statement is therefore narrow, and it is worth watching how much of the popular edifice it supports: three incised clay tablets were excavated at Tărtăria in 1961 in a pit assemblage whose exact stratigraphic position is disputed; the tablets cannot be dated directly; dates proposed for them range, on the published arguments, from the later sixth millennium to the fourth millennium BCE and beyond. Even at their earliest proposed date, moreover, the tablets would prove only that someone incised sign-rows on clay — the question of whether those rows are writing is separate, and it is answered by the corpus as a whole, not by three objects. A date is not a decipherment, and an early date for a mark is not evidence that the mark encodes a language. The Tărtăria tablets are genuine objects of Neolithic southeastern Europe, most plausibly of the Vinča-Turdaș world; they are the corpus's most evocative members; and they are, evidentially, its weakest foundation — a claim about the world's first writing built on the one context in the corpus that cannot be checked.

// HANDLE WITH BOTH HANDS //

Popular accounts typically report the c. 5300 BCE date as the tablets' date and the script claim as their meaning. Both steps exceed the evidence: the date is an association through a disputed context, and the meaning is an inference the corpus as a whole does not support (§VIII). The tablets deserve their fame — as the clearest lesson in this hub that an artefact's importance and an artefact's evidentiary strength are different things.

VIII. Script or Sign System? — Where the Line Falls

The claim, in its strongest published form, is Marija Gimbutas's: that the signs constitute an “Old European script” — a sacred writing system of the sixth and fifth millennia BCE, used for some two thousand years, predating Sumerian cuneiform, and readable, in her presentations, as the script of a goddess-centred civilisation. The claim has been carried further by others under names such as the “Danube script,” with proposed decipherments and sign-lists running to several hundred characters. It is an attractive claim. It would give Europe the world's first writing, give the Danube world a literate civilisation to set beside Uruk, and give the figurines of §V a text to be read by. It must nonetheless be reported plainly: the claim is not accepted by the mainstream of archaeology or of linguistics, and the reasons are evidential, not territorial.

The Mainstream Verdict, and Its Grounds

The verdict rests on the corpus facts of §VI, and they can be stated as a short chain. Writing encodes utterances, and utterances are long: a system of writing generates texts, and texts have length, order, and internal structure. The Vinča corpus has, overwhelmingly, single signs; its rare groups show no fixed order, no direction, no recurring formulae, no numerals that anyone has demonstrated, and no signary behaviour — no systematic variation of the kind that phonetic or logographic systems produce. Nothing in the corpus requires a language behind it, and everything in it is accounted for by functions that marks perform in many unwritten societies: potters' marks identifying a maker or a batch; owners' marks on vessels and tools; tallies and counting strokes; ritual and identity signs placed on figurines, whorls, and vessels for purposes — invocation, dedication, affiliation, protection — that the objects suggest and no text survives to specify. The proposed decipherments fail the test every decipherment must pass: none has produced consistent readings of new material, predicted sign behaviour, or won assent from specialists outside its own school. A script that can only be read by its proposer has not been read at all.

Winn's own position — the careful centre of the field — is worth isolating from both extremes, because later polemic has conscripted him in both directions. Winn held that the signs were a real, conventional sign system; that some signs likely functioned pictographically or ideographically; that the system may fairly be called pre-writing or, in the term later scholarship prefers, a form of proto-writing in the weak sense — meaningful notation that does not encode speech. He did not hold that it was a script, and his catalogue's discipline — object by object, site by site, combination by combination — is precisely what the stronger claims dispense with. Where Gimbutas's presentation assembled signs from across two millennia and a dozen cultures into a single “script,” Winn's kept the Vinča corpus in its contexts. The difference is the whole lesson: a signary built by pooling every mark in southeastern Europe will always look richer than any system anyone there actually used.

Why, then, does this hub — which has a whole article on The Origins of Writing — give the Vinča signs a section of their own, and a long one? Because the line between marks and writing has to be drawn somewhere in the record, on evidence, and this is the case where the drawing is most instructive. The token systems of the Neolithic Near East, the numerical tablets, proto-cuneiform: there, the line is crossed, and the crossing can be watched — media dedicated to text, formats standardised, numbers systematised, order fixed. The Vinča signs stand on the near side of that line, closer to it than a potter's thumbprint and further from it than a token envelope: a developed semasiographic repertoire, shared across a culture, doing real communicative work, that never became writing because the society using it never needed accounts more than it needed marks. That is not a failure to be explained away. It is a datum about what writing is for — and the Danube world, which built settlements the size of Uruk's without it, is the proof that the need, not the ingenuity, was what was missing.

// THE LINE, IN ONE SENTENCE //

A sign system tells you something — whose pot, which batch, what power invoked. Writing tells you what was said — in words, in order, recoverable by a reader who was not there. The Vinča corpus, at its strongest, does the first. No inscription in it has been shown to do the second.

IX. A Metal-Using Society — Pločnik, Belovode, and the Hoards

The metallurgy itself — the slags, the furnaces, the analytical proof of smelting — belongs to this hub's First Metals article, and this section does not retell it. What belongs here is the society the metal was made in, because the social setting of the world's earliest secure smelting is itself one of the record's most instructive facts: smelting was invented not in a palace economy, not under a priesthood or a king commissioning arms, but in farming villages of the Vinča world, as one craft among the potters, weavers, and stone-workers of §IV.

Belovode and Pločnik — Villages, Not Capitals

Belovode, in eastern Serbia, is a Vinča settlement of some twenty-five hectares on a plateau above the Mlava valley, occupied across the culture's later centuries. It is there that archaeometallurgical analysis — published by Radivojević and colleagues from 2010 — identified copper slag from contexts dated to about 5000 BCE: the earliest secure evidence for copper smelting yet known anywhere in the world (the full evidentiary argument is in First Metals, §VII). Pločnik, in southern Serbia on the Toplica River, is its counterpart for consumption and craft: a Vinča village that yielded, across old and new excavations, dozens of massive copper implements — axes, adzes, chisels, and hammer-axes, many of them from hoards (see below) — together with evidence of metalworking on site, and a foil of tin bronze dated to about 4650 BCE that stands, on its publishers' analysis, as the earliest tin bronze known, a short-lived experiment centuries ahead of the alloy's age. Neither site is a city. Neither has a citadel, a temple platform, or a ruler's quarter. The first smelters of copper were villagers.

The supply side tells the same story at landscape scale. The copper mine of Rudna Glava in eastern Serbia — shafts following malachite-bearing veins down through the limestone, worked with stone mauls and antler tools — was opened in the Vinča period and is treated in First Metals (§VIII); lead-isotope and compositional studies tie much of the culture's copper to the great ore district around Majdanpek, close by. Malachite and azurite had been collected in this world for pigment and ornament long before anyone smelted them — green and blue minerals are in Balkan graves centuries before metal — and the road from pigment to ore is, on the current evidence, the road the Vinča smiths actually walked: the same manganese-rich black and green copper minerals that colour the dark burnished pottery are implicated, in Radivojević's analyses, in the earliest smelting charges. Kiln and furnace were sister technologies in a pottery-making society; the pyrotechnology came first, and the metal followed from it.

The Hoards — Wealth, Withheld

Pločnik's fame rests on its hoards: groups of large copper tools — in the best-known cases, sets of axes and hammer-axes numbering in the dozens — buried together in or near the settlement, in contexts of the culture's final phase. Hoarding is a social act before it is an economic one, and the Pločnik hoards have been read in both registers: as founders' or smiths' stock, raw material and finished tools held against future work; as community wealth withdrawn from circulation at a moment of danger or abandonment (the hoards belong to the burned final horizon, §XII); and as deliberate deposits — wealth given to the ground, as the fragmented figurines of §V were given to pits and houses. The readings are not exclusive, and the record does not choose among them. What the hoards establish beyond argument is scale: by about 4600–4500 BCE, a Vinča village could assemble, work, and bury copper by the kilogram and the dozen — metal as a store of communal substance, in a society that still shows no individual grave or house in which such wealth was personally displayed. Compare Varna, on the Black Sea coast, where in the same centuries gold was buried with individuals in quantities that announce rank outright: Varna is the exception that maps the rule, and it is treated where it belongs, in First Metals (§X). In the Vinča heartland, wealth pooled in hoards and houses. It did not yet crown anybody.

// READING THIS SECTION WITH FIRST METALS //

The division of labour between the two articles is deliberate. First Metals argues the smelting claim — what counts as slag, why Belovode's evidence is secure, how Rudna Glava was mined, what Varna's gold weighs. This article takes that metallurgy as established and asks the prior question: what kind of society was standing around the furnace? A farming village, hoarding its copper and burning its houses. Claims repeated here from the metallurgical literature are cited to it; the technical case is made there, once, in full.

X. Cucuteni-Trypillia — The Painted-Pottery World

East of the Carpathians, the Danubian farming world took a second great form. The Cucuteni-Trypillia culture — in its full span roughly c. 5050–2950 BCE, with earlier formative (Pre-Cucuteni) phases reaching back toward 5500 BCE — occupied the forest-steppe and forest zone from eastern Romania through Moldova to the Dnieper: a territory of some 250,000 square kilometres at its widest, larger than any other single archaeological culture of Neolithic Europe. Its settlements were, for most of its history, villages of the familiar Danubian kind — wattle-and-daub houses, storage pits, cattle-rich herds, cereal farming on black earth — set on promontories and river terraces, often ditched. What makes the culture instantly recognisable in any museum is its pottery: the most elaborately painted ceramics of the European Neolithic, and the right place in this article to slow down and follow a craft procedurally, step by step, as the potters themselves worked it.

The Pottery — Forming, Pigments, Firing

Forming. Cucuteni-Trypillia vessels were built by hand, without the wheel: long coils of local clay laid in rings to raise first the base and then the walls, the coils pinched and smoothed into a seamless surface, the vessel shaped — pear-shaped jars, bowls, goblets, lids, and the culture's distinctive binocular and anthropomorphic vessels — and finished by scraping and burnishing as it dried to leather-hardness. Clay preparation came first and mattered: local clays, iron- and mica-bearing, were levigated and tempered, and the fine ware's quality begins in that preparation. Painting was done before firing, on the dried surface. The mature Cucuteni palette is trichrome — black, red, and white — laid in sweeping spiral, meander, and geometric designs that run continuously around the vessel. The pigments are mineral, and their analyses are published: red from iron oxide — hematite; white from calcium-rich materials — calcite and calcium silicate slips; black from iron-manganese ores — magnetite and the manganese mineral jacobsite, the best black sourced from a few deposits (Iacobeni in Romania, Nikopol in Ukraine) and circulated as a valued commodity, since a black that survives firing without greying is hard to find in nature. Paint was applied in a fine clay-slip vehicle, and burnishing after painting helped set the design. Firing completed the chain in the culture's most advanced installation: the two-chamber updraft kiln, known from more than a dozen sites, in which fuel burned in a lower chamber and the heat rose through a perforated floor to the stacked pots above. In closed kilns of this type, Cucuteni potters held temperatures in the range of roughly 700–1000 °C (some analyses argue higher) and — the real skill — controlled the kiln's atmosphere: a consistent oxidising firing yields the red and yellow-red bodies of the painted ware, against the reduced, dark surfaces of earlier traditions. A trichrome pot is therefore a small dossier of mastered variables: clay, slip, three mineral pigments each behaving differently in fire, and an atmosphere held steady for hours. The sequence — Pre-Cucuteni incised and white-on-dark wares; Cucuteni A, painted ware triumphant, with more than three-quarters of the ceramic assemblage painted in the Romanian-Moldovan zone; A–B and B, the trichrome at its most fluent — is the culture's clock, as Vinča A–D is the Danubian one.

Settlement Patterns — Villages, Then Something Else

For its first millennium and a half, the culture's settlement pattern is unremarkable in scale and remarkable only in extent: villages of a dozen to a few score houses, founded, occupied for some generations, and abandoned — very frequently by burning (§XII) — as communities moved within their territories. Thousands of sites are known. Then, in the centuries around 4100–3400 BCE, the pattern breaks its own scale: in the Southern Bug–Dnieper interfluve, settlements appear that are not villages at all by any earlier standard, aggregations of hundreds and then thousands of houses over hundreds of hectares. These are the mega-sites, and they receive the next section. But the ordinary pattern is part of their meaning: the mega-sites were built by a culture whose default settlement was a village, whose houses everywhere follow one domestic plan, and whose pottery, figurines, and house-burning habits the mega-sites share without alteration. Whatever the mega-sites were, they were this culture, enlarged — not a new people, and not, so far as any artefact shows, a new social order with rulers at its head.

TRICHROME

Black (magnetite–jacobsite), red (hematite), white (calcite slip): three mineral pigments, painted before firing, each a separate procurement chain — the black ore traded over distance from a handful of sources.

THE TWO-CHAMBER KILN

Fire below, pots above, heat rising through a perforated floor: the updraft kiln gave Cucuteni potters controlled oxidising firings at c. 700–1000 °C — the technical basis of the painted ware.

COIL, NOT WHEEL

Every vessel was hand-built from coils, smoothed, burnished, painted at leather-hardness, and fired once. The ware's refinement is a refinement of hand skill and fire control, not of machinery.

ONE CULTURE, TWO SCALES

Village and mega-site share house plans, pottery, figurines, and the burning habit. The mega-sites are an enlargement of this world, not an intrusion into it.

XI. The Mega-Sites — Talianki, Maidanetske, Nebelivka

The mega-sites were seen before they were understood. In the 1960s the military topographer K. V. Shishkin noticed enormous ring-like patterns on aerial photographs of the Ukrainian forest-steppe; from 1971, Ukrainian teams began testing them with the then-new method of large-scale magnetometry, and the rings resolved into something no one had expected from Neolithic Europe: whole planned towns, preserved as magnetic shadows under the ploughsoil. The modern era of their study opened with the Ukrainian-German and Anglo-Ukrainian projects of 2009 onward — Kiel University's Collaborative Research Centre work with Ukrainian partners, synthesised in Müller, Rassmann & Videiko (eds.), Trypillia Mega-Sites and European Prehistory, 4100–3400 BCE (2016), and the Durham-led project at Nebelivka — which together re-surveyed the great sites to a standard that turned argument about their size into measurement of it.

// TALIANKI //
c. 340–450 ha

The largest known mega-site. Published extents vary by survey and by which outer anomalies are counted; house counts run to c. 2,700. Peak c. 3700–3500 BCE.

// MAIDANETSKE //
c. 200 ha, c. 3,000 houses

About 3,000 structures on the Kiel plan — some 2,300 of them burned, c. 700 unburned — in up to eight concentric rings around an open centre of c. 25 ha.

// NEBELIVKA //
238 ha, 1,445 houses

The only mega-site with a complete modern plan: 1,445 houses within an interrupted ditch, 1,077 of them burned to fired-daub platforms. Peak c. 4000–3900 BCE.

// THE DEFINITION //
>100 ha

“Mega-site” in the literature: a settlement over about 100 hectares, or an order of magnitude larger than the next settlements of its region. The great examples cluster c. 4100–3400 BCE.

How the Plans Were Recovered — Magnetometry as Method

No one will ever excavate a 300-hectare town house by house; at ten houses a year, a single mega-site plan would take three centuries. The plans exist because of a physical accident this article has already met from the other side: the houses were burned. Fired clay is magnetic — its iron oxides, heated past a threshold, retain a measurable magnetic signature — and a burned wattle-and-daub house leaves a rectangular platform of fired daub (the ploshchadka) whose magnetism contrasts sharply with the surrounding soil. A magnetometer walked across the field in close traverses therefore maps, anomaly by anomaly, every burned house, pit, ditch, and kiln, without moving a spadeful of earth. The Kiel surveys refined the method to high-resolution gradiometry over whole sites — Maidanetske's near-3,000 structures plotted in seasons, not centuries — and targeted excavation then tests the map: trenches over selected anomalies confirm house plans, recover the finds that date them, and calibrate what an anomaly means. The method has limits that honest reporting keeps in view. Unburned houses register weakly or not at all, so house counts are minima in one direction and, where phases overlap, may conflate generations in the other; a magnetic plan is a palimpsest of the site's whole life, not a snapshot of one year. Both cautions feed directly into the population problem below.

The Plan — Rings, Quarters, Assembly Houses

What the plans show is order of a specific, repeated kind. Houses stand in concentric rings — at Maidanetske up to eight deep — arranged along ring corridors around a large open central space (some 25 hectares at Maidanetske) that was never built on. Radial streets cut the rings; the rings themselves subdivide into quarters and neighbourhoods, many with their own pottery workshops and kilns. And distributed through the quarters stand buildings of a different scale: the assembly houses — structures far larger than dwellings, read by the Kiel and Durham teams as communal buildings for their neighbourhoods, with, at Maidanetske, a largest example serving the settlement as a whole. The type case is Nebelivka's excavated mega-structure: a building of some 1,200 square metres (about 60 by 20 metres), two-storeyed on the Ukrainian team's reconstruction, with clay altars and placed deposits, standing at a high point of the plan. Its interpretation is a clean specimen of attributed disagreement: Burdo and Videiko, its Ukrainian excavators, reconstruct and name it a temple; Chapman, Gaydarska, and Hale, their British partners in the same trench, read it as an assembly house — a communal hall, not a shrine of a priesthood. The building is agreed; the word is fought over; and the fight is itself informative, because both readings describe a public building in a settlement with no palace. Isotope studies of Maidanetske's animal bone add a social texture to the plan: herds were pastured under two regimes — intensive grazing on good near pasture for valued animals, extensive grazing further out — partitioned among the settlement's groups by negotiation, in the authors' reading, rather than by central allocation. The mega-site, on this evidence, ran on coordination among its quarters. Nobody has yet found the office that coordinated it.

Population — The Number and Its Uncertainty

How many people? The published range is embarrassingly wide, and a Tier A article prints the range, not a favourite. Maximal estimates, which count every mapped house as simultaneously occupied by a full household, reach 14,000–21,000 for Talianki and up to the mid-40,000s in the most generous modelling for Maidanetske. Diachenko's demographic modelling — the standard counterweight, developed with Menotti — builds in house lifespans, rebuilding cycles, and settlement growth and decline, and lands far lower: for Maidanetske, a most plausible figure in the region of 6,000–7,000, within a published span running from a few thousand to the maximal tens of thousands. Chapman and the Nebelivka team press further still, modelling scenarios in which much of the population was seasonal — a permanent core of some thousands, swollen periodically by assembly — on the argument that the houses of a magnetic plan were never all standing at once. The honest summary is threefold. The mega-sites were, at peak, aggregations of thousands — plausibly five figures at the largest, on the maximal reading — and among the largest settlements in the world of c. 4000–3600 BCE, contemporary with or earlier than Uruk's comparable growth. Their populations were not stable: the radiocarbon-modelled lives of the mega-sites run to a few centuries of volatility — growth, peak, dispersal — not millennia of steady urbanism. And whether they are called cities is a question about definitions, on which the literature divides openly: “low-density urbanism” (Chapman and Gaydarska, after Fletcher), “proto-urban” (Videiko), or, in Diachenko and Menotti's later formulation, neither urban nor stably low-density at all. This article calls them mega-sites, states their measured sizes, and leaves the civic title to the reader's definition.

XII. The Burned-House Horizon

Across the whole world of this article — and far beyond it, in a swathe of southeastern and eastern Europe running from Serbia and Croatia through Bulgaria, Romania, Moldova, and Ukraine — Neolithic and Copper Age houses burned. Not occasionally, as houses full of grain and textiles will, but systematically: settlement after settlement, century after century, from as early as 6500 BCE to as late as 2000 BCE, in what Ruth Tringham named the burned house horizon. The Cucuteni-Trypillia culture sits at the horizon's geographic and temporal centre; the Vinča world shares the practice throughout, and its final horizon, about 4600/4500 BCE, is a burned horizon at site after site. The physical evidence is the fired-daub platform itself: masses of daub vitrified and oxidised to bright orange-red, in quantities per house that imply fires of enormous intensity — hot enough, sustained enough, to fire the clay of the walls through, as a kiln fires a pot. Whole settlements of Trypillia houses were burned at intervals their excavators have estimated at some 60–80 years — roughly, at the end of each house-generation of the settlement's life.

Accident, War, or Closure — The Positions

Accident was the default reading of the early literature, and it has not survived the evidence intact. Experimental archaeology is the test: attempts to burn replica Neolithic houses under accidental conditions — a hearth fire escaping, a lightning strike — repeatedly fail to reproduce the archaeological signature. An unassisted house fire typically chars, smoulders, and dies at temperatures far below those that fired the daub masses; to burn a daub house through, experiments show, requires abundant fuel, preparation, and tending — work. Warfare explains some burnings, no doubt, somewhere in four and a half millennia; but as a general explanation it strains against the pattern: burned settlements without massacre deposits, without weapon trauma in the (admittedly sparse) human remains, rebuilt on the same plots by, apparently, the same communities, at intervals that look generational rather than hostile. The reading that has gathered the field's centre of gravity — associated above all with Tringham and with Mirjana Stevanović, whose analyses of burned structures argued they “were destroyed by deliberate burning and most likely for reasons of a symbolic nature” — is deliberate burning as closure: the house, at the end of its life or its household's tenure, was fired intentionally, in what Tringham's school calls domicide — the deliberate killing of a house. On this reading the burning is a social act in the same family as the deliberate fragmentation of §V: a biography ended properly, in fire, its fired remains becoming the platform — at the tells, literally the ground — of the next house. Stevanović's symbolic reading has itself been elaborated and contested in turn: practical motives (sterilising a verminous, decaying structure; hardening daub for reuse; clearing for rebuilding) are urged by some, ritual and mnemonic motives by others, and the Nebelivka project's full-scale experimental house-burning — building a Trypillia house with the village community and firing it, in 2010s work reported by Gaydarska, Chapman, and colleagues — confirmed the labour and spectacle of the act without settling its meaning. Recent speculative proposals, including epidemic control by sterilising fire, circulate at the debate's edge and are noted here as speculation only.

The Tier A position is accordingly layered, and the layers must not be collapsed. That most of these houses burned deliberately is now the majority reading, grounded in experiment and in the uniformity of the fired remains. Why they were burned deliberately — ritual closure, household death, practical clearance, or motives braided from all three — remains genuinely open, with named scholars on each branch. And the phenomenon's scale should not be inflated into uniformity: not every house burned (at Maidanetske, some 700 of 3,000 structures did not), not every burning was an event of the same kind, and accident and enmity each claim their share of four millennia of fire. What the horizon establishes is a civilisation-scale habit: across the Danube world, the normal end of a house was, very often, a fire that somebody set and tended. The past is allowed its rituals. It is not allowed, in this hub, to have them invented for it — and “ritual closure,” it should be noted, is a description of the act's deliberateness, not a recovered theology. No one knows what was said over the flames.

// FIRE AS PRESERVATION //

The burning habit is also the archive's luck. Fired daub preserved Vinča floor plans, fired the signs' pottery, hardened the platforms magnetometry reads at the mega-sites, and fired the very slags and kilns of §IX into permanence. This world's record is legible in large part because its people burned it. Destruction and preservation, in the Danube record, are the same event.

XIII. A World Without Palaces — Social Organisation

Assemble the evidence of the preceding sections and a single pattern dominates it, across two cultures and two thousand years: the near-absence of elite differentiation. Consider the registers one by one. Houses: at the tells and the mega-sites alike, dwellings follow a common plan within a modest size range; the exceptional buildings are communal — assembly houses — not palatial. Goods: copper, fine pottery, and ornaments distribute through ordinary houses; wealth concentrates, where it concentrates, in communal hoards (§IX), not in personal assemblages. Graves: the few formal burials known are modestly furnished, and the one blazing exception of the age and region — Varna's gold-rich cemetery, c. 4600–4200 BCE — lies on the Black Sea periphery of this world and is treated in First Metals; the Vinča and Trypillia heartlands have nothing like it, across millennia in which Varna proves display was possible. Monuments: there are none — no platforms, no ruler iconography, no fortification systems commensurate with the mega-sites' scale (ditches exist; they are interrupted, partial, and variously read). Administration: no seals, no tablets, no standardised ration or measure system has been found — the sign repertoire of §VI is the whole of it. Recent quantitative work sharpens the point: house-size distributions at Nebelivka yield wealth-inequality (Gini) estimates among the lowest recorded for early farming societies — with the authors' own caveat that house size is an imperfect proxy for wealth.

What follows from the pattern must be stated as carefully as the pattern itself, because two opposite over-readings are on sale in the literature. The first over-reading is primitivism: that a society without visible elites was a society without organisation. The mega-sites refute it on their face — concentric planning maintained over centuries, neighbourhood workshops, assembly buildings at two scales, pasture partitioned by negotiation, kilns and smelters run to standard: this is organisation of a high order, achieved, on the evidence, without centralisation — through quarters, neighbourhoods, households, and assemblies, through what the Maidanetske isotope study calls community negotiation. The second over-reading is utopianism: that the absence of palaces proves the presence of equality as an ideology, harmony as a condition. It does not. The record shows an absence of materialised, hereditary-looking rank; it cannot show the absence of influence, seniority, ritual authority, coercion, or conflict — things that leave few traces in any century — and the fortified and burned sites of the record warn against picturing two thousand years without violence. David Anthony's regional synthesis, The Horse, the Wheel, and Language (2007), frames the balance this article adopts: the Danube world was a wealthy, complex, largely unranked farming civilisation whose very success — dense tells, accumulated goods, no military aristocracy — left it exposed when the steppe world to its east transformed in the later fourth millennium. The mega-sites dispersed, the tells ended, and the long experiment in scale without kings closed — not with a conquest horizon anyone has demonstrated at the sites themselves, but with dispersal, contraction, and reorganisation across the centuries after 3500 BCE.

WHAT IS ABSENT

Palaces, royal graves, ruler images, administrative seals, standardised measures, standing fortifications — across c. 2,000 years and hundreds of excavated settlements. Absence this consistent is evidence, of a negative kind.

WHAT IS PRESENT

Planned rings and quarters, assembly houses at neighbourhood and settlement scale, workshops, hoards, kilns, smelters, traded pigments and ores — organisation everywhere, centralisation nowhere found.

THE VARNA EXCEPTION

Varna's gold graves (c. 4600–4200 BCE) prove ranked display existed at this world's edge in these very centuries. Its absence in the heartlands is therefore a fact about those societies, not about the age's possibilities.

THE LIMIT OF THE ARGUMENT

No materialised rank is not no power. Authority that is seasonal, ritual, or persuasive — assembly-house authority — is exactly the kind the record shows and exactly the kind it cannot weigh.

XIV. Gimbutas's “Old Europe” — The Synthesis as Historiography

Everything in this article so far can be checked against a trench, a catalogue, or a laboratory report. This section is about something else: the most influential interpretation ever placed on that record, and the reasons this hub prints it behind glass. Marija Gimbutas (1921–1994), the Lithuanian-American archaeologist whose fieldwork and syntheses — The Goddesses and Gods of Old Europe (1974; revised 1982), The Language of the Goddess (1989), and The Civilization of the Goddess (1991) — defined “Old Europe” for the reading public, was a scholar of genuine and formidable learning: excavator, linguist, and the architect of the steppe (Kurgan) hypothesis of Indo-European origins that ancient DNA has, in its broad outline, since vindicated. Her synthesis of the Neolithic record ran as follows. Old Europe — the farming cultures of southeastern Europe, c. 6500–3500 BCE — was a civilisation centred on the worship of a Great Goddess, evidenced, in her reading, by the thousands of female figurines; it was matrilineal and broadly egalitarian, its art symbolic and its settlements unfortified; its signs were a sacred script (§VIII); and it was overthrown, in successive waves from about 4300 BCE, by patriarchal, horse-riding, sky-god steppe invaders — the catastrophe that ended the goddess's world and began ours.

The Academy's Reservations

The reservations are long-standing, specific, and — it should be said, because Gimbutas's popular reception often omits it — voiced with respect for her learning by the specialists closest to her own material. Ruth Tringham, reviewing the goddess volumes, objected to assertions delivered with a certainty the evidence could not carry: figurines read wholesale as a single goddess, symbols assigned meanings without intervening argument. Peter Ucko's earlier caution stands behind the whole debate: figurines cross-culturally serve as toys, teaching devices, votives, ancestors, and dolls, and femaleness in clay does not diagnose a theology, let alone a matriarchy — a term Gimbutas herself, her defenders note, generally avoided in favour of “matrilineal” and “matrifocal,” though the popular version of her work rarely preserves the distinction. David Anthony, whose own steppe research built on her Kurgan framework, dissented from the peaceful portrait directly: Old Europe had fortified sites, weapons including status weapons, and evidence of hierarchy and violence, and there is no direct evidence, he argued, that women held the central role her synthesis assigns them in either society or religion. The script claim has fared as §VIII records. And the synthesis's method — assembling figurines, signs, and symbols from across two millennia and a dozen distinct cultures into a single civilisation with a single religion — is precisely the pooling move that Winn's contextual discipline refuses. A system built from everywhere is answerable to nowhere.

The historiographic verdict, then, is double, and both halves belong in this hub. As scholarship about the record, the goddess synthesis is not sustained: its central religious claim is underdetermined by the objects, its social portrait is contradicted at points by the fortifications, weapons, and Varna-grade exceptions within its own territory, and its script is not a script. As a force in the culture, it is one of the most consequential archaeological ideas of the twentieth century: it reshaped feminist spirituality, popular prehistory, and the public image of the Neolithic; it sent readers — and funders, and students — to the Danube material in numbers no monograph could have; and its steppe half helped frame the research programme that ancient DNA eventually confirmed. This article's practice follows from the double verdict exactly. “Old Europe” is kept, as a label for a place and a time, because labels are tools. The goddess is returned to her author, as an interpretation, because interpretations have authors. And the figurines go back to being what the trenches found: clay figures, mostly broken, in houses and pits — awaiting readings that their contexts, not our longings, will one day support.

// WHY THIS SECTION EXISTS //

A Tier A hub does not ignore the most famous claim made about its subject; it files the claim where it belongs. Gimbutas's Old Europe is Tier C material in this archive's scheme when it is presented as a recovered ancient religion — a modern construction about the past, however learned its author. It is presented here as historiography: attributed, dated, assessed, and never allowed to speak in the article's own voice. The reader who meets the goddess elsewhere in this hub's pages will meet her under the same rule.

XV. What Old Europe Does and Does Not Show

What the Danube world shows, at Tier A strength, can now be stated in one paragraph. Between about 5700 and 3500 BCE, farming communities of southeastern Europe — heirs of the western founder package, adapted to cattle-rich river plains — built settlements of a scale and durability the Neolithic nowhere else surpassed: tells occupied for eight centuries, villages planned in rows, and finally mega-sites of up to some 400 hectares whose concentric plans are among the largest settlement layouts of the fourth-millennium world. These communities commanded the most advanced pyrotechnology of their age — two-chamber kilns, atmosphere-controlled firing, trichrome painted ware — and in their villages, around 5000 BCE, copper was smelted for the first time on evidence that has survived every laboratory test applied to it. They maintained a shared repertoire of signs for centuries, modelled figurines by the thousand, hoarded copper by the kilogram, coordinated herds, workshops, and assemblies across settlements of thousands — and they did all of it without kings, palaces, administrative writing, or graves that announce rank. Then, over the centuries around and after 3500 BCE, the mega-sites dispersed and the tell world ended, in a transformation whose causes — climate, soil, social volatility, the rising steppe world — remain under active argument.

What it does not show is equally definite. It does not show writing: the Vinča signs are a sign system — marks of ownership and making, tallies, and symbols — and the “Old European script,” with the Tărtăria tablets as its crown witness, fails on the corpus's own statistics and on the tablets' own unprovable context. It does not show a goddess religion, a matriarchy, or a golden age without violence: those are a twentieth-century synthesis, filed in §XIV as historiography, contradicted at points by the record and underdetermined at all of them. It does not show cities in the Uruk sense — no administration, no ration state, no urban-rural divide that anyone has demonstrated — however the definitional debate over the mega-sites is settled. And it does not show its dead: a world that burned its houses and kept its graves from us has set a hard limit on how well it can ever be known, and this article has tried to write up to that limit and not past it.

Perhaps that is the Danube world's deepest instruction for this hub, as Mehrgarh's was for the Indus. The road to civilisation did not run in one direction, and it did not run only through Uruk. Beside the world that invented writing to count its grain, another world — as old, as populous, as skilled — counted its grain without writing, governed its thousands without kings, and ended its houses in deliberate fire, generation after generation, for two thousand years. The record of that world is attested, dated, and debated in the open literature; its signs are catalogued; its plans are mapped to the individual house. It needs no goddess and no script to matter. It matters because it happened, at a scale that still startles, in a form that still instructs: proof, in fired clay, that the things civilisation is supposed to require are not the only things that can hold a world together.
