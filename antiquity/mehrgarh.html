This is a Tier A article: everything in it rests on excavated, dated, and published archaeological evidence, and where scholars disagree — as they do about the domestication of the zebu and the purpose of the drilled teeth — the disagreement itself is reported with its positions attributed. No claim here depends on ancient legend or modern construction, and none is allowed to borrow strength from either.

Mehrgarh is a cluster of settlement mounds on the Kachi plain of Balochistan, in modern Pakistan, at the foot of the Bolan Pass — the great corridor between the Iranian plateau and the Indus plain. Excavated by the French Archaeological Mission from 1974 into the 2000s, it preserves something no other South Asian site of its age can match: a continuous, stratified sequence of occupation running from an aceramic farming village of about 7000 BCE through five thousand years of development to the threshold of the Indus civilisation. In that sequence, excavators found compartmented mud-brick granaries before pottery existed; burials furnished, from the very beginning, with lapis lazuli, turquoise, and marine shell carried over hundreds of kilometres; the earliest cotton fibres known anywhere in the Old World, preserved inside the corrosion of a copper bead; and eleven human teeth, drilled in life with flint-tipped bow drills — the earliest dentistry-like practice known to archaeology.

Mehrgarh also poses, in its sharpest form, the question this hub asks of every farming hearth: how much was imported, and how much was made at home? Its grains are the wheats and barleys of the Near Eastern founder package, and they tie the village westward, to the Zagros and the Fertile Crescent. Its cattle tell the opposite story. On the argument first made by the mission's faunal analyst, Richard Meadow, and broadly supported by later genetic work, the humped zebu of South Asia was domesticated here, or in this region, from the local Indian aurochs — an independent act of animal domestication standing beside the imported seeds. Mehrgarh is therefore not a colony of the western Neolithic and not an isolated invention either. It is a meeting ground, and its five-thousand-year sequence is the deep foundation on which the Indus tradition was built.

I. A Village at the Foot of the Pass — The Place

Mehrgarh lies on the western bank of the Bolan River, near the modern town of Dhadar, in the northern Kachi plain of Balochistan — the broad alluvial apron where the mountains of the plateau's edge give way, eastward, to the Indus floodplain. The site sits at approximately 29°23′ N, 67°37′ E, between the present-day cities of Quetta, Kalat, and Sibi, and a short distance from the mouth of the Bolan Pass. Geography is the first fact of the site's history, and the excavators have never tired of stating it: the Bolan is one of the two classic routes — the other is the Khyber, far to the north — between the Iranian plateau and Central Asia on one side and the Indus valley on the other. Whoever settled at Mehrgarh settled on a road.

// THE CORRIDOR //
Plateau ⇄ Indus plain

The Bolan Pass links the Balochistan highlands and, beyond them, eastern Iran and southern Afghanistan with the Indus lowlands.

The Kachi plain is a demanding but workable farming landscape: hot, dry, and dependent on the seasonal water of the Bolan River and on the runoff from the hills — conditions suited to the winter-sown cereals, wheat and barley, that the first settlers grew, and to grazing on the plain and in the foothills for their herds. The hills behind the site offered stone, including the cherts and flints of the tool industry; the river offered a permanent line of water and a route down to the Indus. The plain also offered neighbours in every direction: eastward, the Indus valley itself; westward and northward, the highland valleys of Balochistan, where a scatter of contemporary and later settlements — Kili Gul Mohammad, Rana Ghundai, and others — forms the regional context in which Mehrgarh must be read.

One caution about scale belongs at the front, because popular accounts inflate it. The archaeological area of Mehrgarh covers more than 200 hectares, but that figure measures five thousand years of accumulated settlement debris spread across a cluster of mounds — the mission's sectors are numbered MR.1 through MR.6 and beyond — not the footprint of any single community. At any one moment, Mehrgarh was a village, later a large village and then a small town. Its importance is not urban size. It is duration: nowhere else in the Greater Indus region can the whole arc from first farming to civilisation be walked, layer by layer, in a single place.

The comparison this hub invites is with the parallel hearths treated elsewhere in this division. At Nabta Playa, Saharan herders built a ceremonial world around cattle without ever farming; at Göbekli Tepe, Anatolian hunter-gatherers raised monumental enclosures on the eve of domestication. Mehrgarh is the third pattern: a settled farming village from its earliest excavated level, growing crops whose ancestors are western and herding cattle whose ancestors, on the prevailing argument, are its own. The three sites are contemporary witnesses that the Neolithic was not one event with one birthplace, but a set of regional experiments that exchanged materials, animals, and ideas along routes like the Bolan.

II. The French Mission — Excavation, 1974–2000

Mehrgarh was identified in 1974 by the French Archaeological Mission to Pakistan, working in collaboration with the Department of Archaeology of Pakistan, and excavation began in December of that year under the direction of Jean-François Jarrige, with Catherine Jarrige as co-director of the mission's work in the region. The first seasons explored mounds of the third and fourth millennia BCE near the river; the discovery, in the mission's third season (1976–1977), of an aceramic Neolithic occupation in the northern sector — sector MR.3 — transformed the project. What had been a Chalcolithic and Bronze Age site became the type-site for the entire early prehistory of northwestern South Asia.

The mission worked at Mehrgarh continuously from 1974 to 1986, then — after a decade given largely to the excavation of nearby Nausharo (§XIV) — returned to the Neolithic sector from 1997 to 2000. Those final seasons mattered out of proportion to their length: excavation of the Period I settlement was extended to some 1,700 m² of exposed buildings, the deepest levels were reached over a smaller area, and the internal stratigraphy of the aceramic sequence was re-established on a footing secure enough that the mission renumbered the Neolithic levels from the bottom up. The site's publication record matches its excavation: the synthetic volume Mehrgarh: Field Reports 1974–1985 — From Neolithic Times to the Indus Civilization (Jarrige, Jarrige, Meadow & Quivron, eds., 1995) remains the standard point of entry, supplemented by a long series of specialist studies — Lorenzo Costantini on the plant remains, Richard Meadow on the fauna, and many others on the beads, the burials, the pottery, and the teeth.

Two consequences follow for the reader. First, Mehrgarh is a specialist-rich site: its famous claims — early cotton, local zebu domestication, drilled teeth — each rest on a dedicated laboratory study published in a peer-reviewed venue, not on field impressions. Second, the interpretation of Mehrgarh has been shaped by a single long-running mission, and its director's framing — Jean-François Jarrige argued, against an older diffusionism, that Mehrgarh had an earlier local background and an originality of its own within a wider cultural continuum stretching to eastern Mesopotamia — is itself a position in a debate, reported as such in §VI and §XV. The strata are the mission's gift; the readings of the strata are scholarship, and scholarship argues.

// THE SECTOR MAP //

The mission's grid divides the mound cluster into sectors cited throughout the literature: MR.3 is the Neolithic sector, with its cemetery of more than 300 graves; MR.4 carries much of the Period II occupation; MR.2 is the great Chalcolithic mound of Period III, with its own cemetery; MR.1 and its neighbours carry the later periods toward the Bronze Age. When a find in this article is tied to a sector — the drilled teeth to MR.3, the Period III potters' ovens to MR.2 — the sector is part of the evidence, because it fixes the find in the sequence.

A note on dates. The chronology used here is the mission's periodisation in its conventional calibrated form, as summarised in the standard syntheses (Jarrige et al. 1995; Possehl 2002). Individual radiocarbon determinations exist for many contexts, but the period boundaries — c. 7000, 5500, 4800, 3500 BCE and so on — are rounded working figures, and different publications round them differently or subdivide periods differently (a Period VIII, at the Sibri cemetery some kilometres away, is sometimes added). Where a specific find carries its own date — the cotton burial to the first half of the sixth millennium BCE, the drilled teeth to a graveyard dated 9,000–7,500 years ago — this article gives the find's date in the form its publishers gave it, and says so.

III. The Sequence — Periods I to VII

The mission divided Mehrgarh's occupation into seven principal periods, defined by stratigraphy, architecture, and material culture, and correlated across the mound cluster. The scheme is the site's backbone: every claim in this article hangs on it. Dates are the conventional calibrated approximations used in the mission's syntheses and in Possehl's The Indus Civilization (2002); they are rounded, and the transitions between periods were processes, not events.

Three features of the sequence deserve emphasis before the detail begins. The first is its continuity: the mission found no sterile break, no destruction layer marking a replacement of one world by another, between the first farmers and the Indus age. Buildings were rebuilt, cemeteries succeeded cemeteries, crafts intensified — but the sequence reads as development in place, which is precisely what makes Mehrgarh the foundation document of the Indus tradition rather than a mere predecessor of it. The second is its shape: the centre of settlement migrates across the mound cluster over time, from the northern Neolithic sector toward the southern mounds, so that "Mehrgarh" names a landscape of successive villages rather than a single tell that simply grew. The third is a warning: continuity of culture is not automatically continuity of population. Dental anthropologists John Lukacs and Brian Hemphill, studying the skeletal series, argued that the Chalcolithic population of Mehrgarh was not simply descended from its Neolithic population — a finding that complicates any straight-line story and is taken up again in §XV.

The periods that follow in this article are weighted as the evidence is weighted. Periods I to III — the Neolithic and the earlier Chalcolithic, c. 7000–3500 BCE — carry the site's most distinctive and most debated evidence, and they receive the fullest treatment. Periods IV to VII are treated more briefly, in §XIV and §XV, because their story is increasingly the story of the whole Greater Indus region, and its proper home is the Indus treatment this hub points toward (§XVI).

IV. Period I — The Aceramic Village: Buildings and Burials

Period I (c. 7000–5500 BCE) is the aceramic Neolithic: a farming village that made no pottery at all. Its absence of ceramics is not a mark of poverty — the same levels yield worked copper ornaments, beads of stones carried from Afghanistan and Iran, and buildings of a distinctive and purposeful plan — but of a technological choice-set in which containers were baskets, skins, stone, and unfired clay. The flint industry produced blades with the gloss of sickle-use; grinding stones processed grain; bone tools, polished stone axes and adzes, and bitumen — used to line baskets and to haft blades — complete the toolkit of a community that was, from its first excavated level, sedentary and agricultural.

The Compartmented Buildings

The signature architecture of Period I is the mud-brick building divided into small square compartments. Excavation in sector MR.3 cleared dozens of structures — forty-six buildings in the area exposed by the 1997–2000 seasons alone — built of hand-formed mud bricks, their interiors subdivided into two, four, or more small cells. Many of the compartmented buildings have no doorways at workable height: they were entered, the excavators argue, from the roof, and their cells were filled and emptied from above. The standard reading, from the mission's first reports onward, is that these are storage buildings — granaries in which the community's harvested grain was kept in bulk, cell by cell. Beside them stood smaller rectangular houses, likewise of mud brick, and open spaces between the buildings that served, as the next paragraphs show, as burial ground.

The granaries matter beyond their function. Bulk storage of a harvest is a social fact as much as an architectural one: it implies a surplus worth guarding, a calendar organised around sowing and reaping, and arrangements — household or communal, the plans alone cannot say — for holding food across the year. The same storage-first pattern appears at the very beginning of the farming story told in this hub's Nile Bridge article for the contemporary Nile margin, and it is one of the quiet continuities of the early Neolithic world: before pottery, before metal in any quantity, before seals or writing, there is the granary. Mehrgarh's version is simply among the earliest and clearest anywhere.

The Burials — Wealth from the Start

The dead of Period I were buried in the open spaces among the buildings, in simple pits and in graves lined or capped with mud brick, the body typically flexed. More than 300 graves were excavated in the MR.3 cemetery ground, which spans the Neolithic occupation. What transformed the site's reputation was the furnishing of these early graves. Burial goods include stone and bone tools, baskets, beads and pendants — and the materials of those ornaments are the point: lapis lazuli, whose nearest ancient source is Badakhshan in northeastern Afghanistan; turquoise, attributed to sources in Iran; carnelian and other hard stones; and marine shell, which can only have come from the Arabian Sea coast, hundreds of kilometres to the south. Some graves contained the remains of young goats, placed with the dead. Unfired clay figurines of women and animals, among the earliest from South Asia, accompany the settlement levels.

Read plainly, the burials say two things at once. First, the aceramic villagers of Mehrgarh were already nodes in exchange networks of continental reach — lapis, turquoise, and shell do not walk to the Bolan Pass by themselves — and those networks are as old as the village itself, present in its earliest levels rather than grafted on later. Second, the community chose to deposit that far-travelled wealth with its dead, in the ground between its granaries. Whatever social distinctions the graves express — and the mission's analysts have noted variation in the richness of furnishing — they express them through ornament: through the same bead-making materials and skills that later produced the drilled teeth of §IX and the lapidary industry of §XI. Mehrgarh's craft tradition and its exchange tradition are one tradition, and both begin in Period I.

V. The Plant Package — Wheats and Barleys from the West

The evidence for Mehrgarh's crops comes by an elegant accident of preservation: the mud bricks themselves. Grain and chaff mixed into the mud as temper left impressions in the fired and unfired brick of the Period I buildings, and the mission's archaeobotanist, Lorenzo Costantini, built the site's plant record substantially on those impressions, supplemented by charred remains. The assemblage he identified is unambiguous in its affinities. The cereals are barleys — two-row hulled barley and six-row forms, including naked (free-threshing) barley — and wheats: emmer, einkorn, and free-threshing bread-type wheats. Wild and gathered plants supplement the package, among them jujube and other fruits of the plain and foothills, but the staple core is cereal farming of a fully developed kind.

That package has a passport, and the passport is western. Emmer and einkorn are two of the founder crops of the Near Eastern Neolithic, domesticated in the Fertile Crescent; the barleys likewise belong to the western founder set. No wild progenitors of emmer or einkorn grow in Balochistan, and the current archaeobotanical consensus — stated, for South Asia, in Dorian Fuller's syntheses of the region's domestication history — is that the wheat-and-barley complex arrived in the Indus borderlands from the west, whether carried by migrating farmers, passed community to community across the Iranian plateau, or some braided combination of the two; the archaeology of a seed cannot distinguish a migrant's sack from a neighbour's gift. What the Mehrgarh sequence shows is the package in place and diversifying by c. 7000 BCE: multiple wheat and barley forms grown together, in a winter-cropping regime fitted to the Kachi plain's seasons, and stored in bulk in the granaries of §IV.

Two qualifications keep the picture honest. The first is that "arrived" does not mean "arrived finished and never changed": the prominence of free-threshing forms and naked barley at Mehrgarh has been read as local selection under local conditions, the package adapting to its new ground within the sequence itself. The second is that the western package was never the whole diet. Hunting remained important in Period I — the earliest faunal assemblages are dominated by wild species, gazelle prominent among them — and gathered wild plants continued alongside the cereals. Mehrgarh's first farmers were also herders and hunters; the Neolithic, here as almost everywhere, was a broadening of the economy, not a switch thrown in a generation.

// A FOUNDER PACKAGE, ADOPTED //

The contrast that structures this whole article is set in the plant record. For the grains, Mehrgarh is a recipient: the founder wheats and barleys are western domesticates, and no local domestication of wheat or barley is claimed for the site in the current literature. For the cattle of the next section, the claim runs the other way. A hearth can be secondary for one domesticate and primary for another — Mehrgarh is the textbook case, which is why the hub calls it a third farming hearth in its own right rather than an outpost of the first.

Cotton belongs to the plant story too, but its evidence is so singular — a few threads inside a bead — that it receives its own section (§VIII). It is enough to register here that if the wheats tie Mehrgarh to the west, the cotton, whatever its domestication status, ties it to its own region: Gossypium is a plant of the South Asian world, and its presence in a sixth-millennium grave at Mehrgarh is the earliest sign anywhere of the fibre that would one day define Indus textiles.

VI. The Zebu Question — Cattle Domesticated on Home Ground

The animal bones of Period I tell a story in two movements. In the earliest levels, wild species dominate: gazelle above all, with wild sheep, wild goat, wild cattle, onager, and pig among the hunted fauna, and domestic or managed stock a minority presence. Through the Period I sequence the balance shifts — sheep, goats, and above all cattle increase steadily as a proportion of the assemblage, until by the later Neolithic the domesticates dominate. The mission's faunal analyst, Richard H. Meadow, devoted his Mehrgarh studies to reading that shift, and from it he drew the site's most consequential argument: that the cattle being brought under management at Mehrgarh were not imported Near Eastern taurines, but the local humped cattle — the zebu, Bos indicus — domesticated here from the Indian aurochs, Bos primigenius namadicus.

Meadow's Argument

The argument is morphometric — built on measurement. Meadow documented, across the Period I stratigraphy, a progressive decrease in the size of the cattle represented in the bone assemblage, together with their rising frequency. Size reduction under human management is the classic osteological signature of domestication in progress: animals bred in herds, culled selectively, and kept from the full growth of their wild kin grow smaller than the wild population from which they are drawn. At Mehrgarh, Meadow argued, the sequence catches that signature in the act — large wild cattle in the early levels giving way, within the site's own strata, to smaller managed animals — and the managed animals are of the zebu kind: figurines of humped bulls and the morphology of the later material mark the herd as Bos indicus, not the humpless Bos taurus of the Near East. The wild progenitor was at hand: the Indian aurochs ranged the subcontinent's plains into historical times. On Meadow's reading, Mehrgarh is therefore a centre of independent animal domestication — for cattle, the most important domesticate in South Asian history — even though its grains came from the west.

Sheep and Goats — The Supporting Herd

The zebu does not stand alone in the faunal record, and the supporting cast complicates the picture usefully. Sheep and goats are present from early in the sequence and grow in importance alongside the cattle; Meadow argued for management of goats at an early date, and the precise geography of caprine domestication — western introduction, local domestication of the region's wild goats, or both braided together — remains debated in the literature, with the wild progenitors of both species native to the Balochistan highlands themselves. The honest summary is that Mehrgarh's herding economy was assembled from more than one source: the village stood where the western domesticates and the subcontinent's own wild herds met, and it drew on both. That mixed origin is not a weakness in the site's claim to importance. It is the claim: the Bolan corridor was a place of combination, and the Indus tradition was born of combination from its first village onward.

The Genetic Verdict — Broad Support, Open Detail

Genetics entered the zebu question a generation after Meadow, and its broad verdict has favoured him. Mitochondrial studies from Loftus and colleagues (1994) onward established that zebu and taurine cattle descend from aurochs populations that had diverged long before domestication — the signature of two separate domestications, not of one herd carried east and humped by later crossing. Chen and colleagues, surveying 844 zebu mitochondrial sequences across Asia in Molecular Biology and Evolution (2010), concluded that domestic zebu are, in their title's words, "an exclusive legacy of the South Asia Neolithic": two maternal haplogroups (I1 and I2), with diversity for the I1 lineage highest in the Indus valley — supporting the Indus region as a primary centre of zebu domestication. Later modelling of autosomal data has added texture rather than reversal: a domestication process in South Asia, probably diffuse, with substantial later interbreeding between managed herds and local wild aurochs.

What genetics cannot do is point at a mound. The genes locate zebu domestication in South Asia, and for one lineage most plausibly in the greater Indus region; they do not and cannot certify that the process happened at Mehrgarh itself rather than across a Balochistan-and-Indus world of which Mehrgarh is simply the best-excavated witness. Nor is the chronology closed: proposals for the span over which zebu domestication unfolded at and around Mehrgarh run broadly across c. 7000–4500 BCE, and the contribution of additional centres elsewhere in the subcontinent remains argued. This article therefore states the standing of the argument exactly: a separate zebu domestication in the Indus region is the prevailing position, supported independently by osteology and genetics; Mehrgarh is its earliest and fullest archaeological witness; the precise locus, tempo, and unity of the process remain open questions in the published literature. That is a strong claim. It is also, at that strength, a defensible one — which is the only kind this tier prints.

// THE MIRROR OF NABTA //

Readers of this hub will recognise the shape of the argument — and should note that its fortunes are the reverse of Nabta Playa's. At Nabta (§VII of that article), an independent African cattle domestication was proposed on ecological grounds and has lost ground to metrical and genetic criticism. At Mehrgarh, an independent zebu domestication was proposed on metrical grounds and has gained ground from genetics. The difference is not the boldness of the excavators; it is the evidence. Independent domestication is a claim that stands or falls site by site, bone by bone, genome by genome — and this archive reports each site at the strength its own evidence has reached.

VII. Period II — The Ceramic Neolithic

Period II (c. 5500–4800 BCE) is defined by a single innovation: pottery. The first ceramics at Mehrgarh — found principally in sector MR.4 — are handmade vessels, coarse at first, some bearing the impressions of the baskets in which or against which they were formed. Through the period the ware improves: finer pastes, better firing, and, toward the period's end, the first wheel-finished and wheel-made pottery — the rotary technology that would come to dominate Period III production. The appearance of pottery in a community that had farmed without it for fifteen centuries is a reminder that the Neolithic package was assembled piece by piece, at different times in different places: Mehrgarh took the grains early and the pots late, exactly reversing the order seen at Nabta, where pottery precedes farming altogether.

The rest of Period II is intensification. Settlement expands; craft production grows in volume and variety; the burial ground at MR.3 continues in use, its graves now accompanied by pottery as well as by the older ornament repertoire. Copper appears in small but telling quantities — a ring, beads, a small ingot are reported from Period II contexts — worked metal still an ornament material, not yet an industry. Figurines continue, in unfired and now fired clay. And two of the site's most famous bodies of evidence belong to this broad horizon and the centuries on either side of it: the cotton threads of the next section, preserved in a burial of the sixth millennium BCE, and the drilled teeth of §IX, from the MR.3 graveyard whose use spans the later Neolithic.

Period II is also when Mehrgarh's regional setting thickens. The mission's syntheses place the period within an expansion of settled farming communities across the Balochistan borderlands — contemporary occupations at Kili Gul Mohammad and a scatter of sites in the highlands and toward the Indus plain — so that by 5000 BCE the Bolan village was one node, albeit the largest and longest-lived, in a settled Neolithic landscape. The farming hearth had become a farming region. That regionalisation, more than any single artefact, is what made the later developments of Period III possible: crafts specialise where there are neighbours to exchange with, and by Period II Mehrgarh had neighbours in every direction.

VIII. Cotton — Threads Preserved in Copper

The earliest cotton known anywhere in the Old World survived by a chemical accident, inside a bead. The context is a Neolithic burial at Mehrgarh dated to the first half of the sixth millennium BCE: beside the left wrist of an adult skeleton lay a set of eight copper beads — small annular beads, 2.2 to 4.8 mm in diameter, each formed by rolling a narrow strip of copper sheet around a rod. The beads had presumably been strung as a bracelet. When the bead set was subjected to metallurgical analysis in Paris, one bead (catalogued MR.84.03.158.01b) was found to be heavily corroded — and within its corrosion, preserved as mineralised casts, were several threads: organic fibres that the corroding copper had impregnated with metallic salts, fossilising their microscopic form as the fibre itself decayed.

Identifying a fibre from its mineralised ghost required a method built for the purpose. Christophe Moulherat and colleagues combined reflected-light microscopy with scanning electron microscopy to read the fibres' diagnostic morphology — the flattened, twisted, ribbon-like convolutions characteristic of cotton — and identified them as cotton, Gossypium sp. The threads, they concluded, were the remains of the string on which the beads had been threaded. The paper — Moulherat, Tengberg, Haquet & Mille, Journal of Archaeological Science 29 (2002): 1393–1401 — states its conclusion in its abstract's own measured terms: the Mehrgarh fibres are the earliest known example of cotton in the Old World, putting the first attested use of the fibre back by more than a millennium.

// THE EVIDENCE, AT ITS EXACT STRENGTH //

What was found: several mineralised threads, preserved within the corrosion of one copper bead from a set of eight, in a burial of the first half of the sixth millennium BCE. What the analysis shows: the fibres are cotton (Gossypium sp.), of varying ripeness, identified by microscopic morphology against modern reference material. What it does not show: the species — the method cannot distinguish the Old World cottons at species level — and therefore the domestication status. The authors state plainly that it cannot be ascertained whether the fibres came from an already domesticated plant. Wild cotton gathered and spun, and cultivated cotton, are both consistent with the threads. What is certain is use: cotton fibre was known, prepared, and spun into thread at Mehrgarh by c. 6000–5500 BCE.

The find's significance is best seen against the later record. Cotton becomes, in the mature Indus civilisation, a signature material — the fibre of Indus textiles traded as far as Mesopotamia — and South Asia is one of the Old World cotton's homelands, with the tree cotton Gossypium arboreum generally treated as a subcontinental domesticate. Moulherat and colleagues, noting the accumulation of early cotton evidence in Pakistani Balochistan and the Indus valley (a fourth-millennium cotton string preserved inside a carnelian bead at Shahi Tump, in the Makran, among it), proposed a South Asian origin, probably in the greater Indus area, for one of the Old World cottons. The Mehrgarh threads do not prove that domestication happened here — the authors are careful, and this article follows them — but they place the exploitation of cotton at the Bolan Pass at the very beginning of the region's farming sequence, millennia before the looms of the Indus cities. A fibre that perishes in every normal context survives, once, inside corroding copper; the whole early history of cotton hangs on accidents of that kind, and the absence of cotton elsewhere at this date is evidence of preservation, not necessarily of absence.

Note, finally, the company the cotton keeps. The threads were found in a copper bead, in a burial, strung as an ornament. Three of Mehrgarh's defining threads — early metal, rich burial furnishing, and craft skill — meet in a single object two millimetres wide. The next section follows the third of those threads to its most startling destination: the same drilling craft that pierced beads at Mehrgarh was, for some fifteen centuries, also applied to human teeth.

IX. The Drilled Teeth — The Earliest Known Dentistry

In April 2006, the journal Nature published a two-page Brief Communication that rewrote the history of dentistry. The authors — Alfredo Coppa and an international team including Luca Bondioli, Catherine and Jean-François Jarrige, and Roberto Macchiarelli — reported on the human dentitions from the Neolithic cemetery at MR.3, a graveyard of more than 300 graves used over some 1,500 years. Among the thousands of teeth examined, they had identified a set unlike anything known from so early a date: permanent molars bearing small, neat, deliberately drilled holes.

Here we describe eleven drilled molar crowns from nine adults discovered in a Neolithic graveyard in Pakistan that dates from 7,500–9,000 years ago.
— Coppa et al., "Early Neolithic tradition of dentistry," Nature 440 (2006): 755–756

The Evidence

The sample is small and exact. Eleven drilled permanent molar crowns, from nine adults — four women, two men, and three individuals of undetermined sex. Every drilled tooth is a first or second molar: four in the upper jaw, seven in the lower. One individual had three drilled teeth; another had one tooth drilled twice. The holes are 0.5 to 3.5 mm deep, conical, cylindrical, or trapezoidal in section, drilled through enamel into dentine, almost all on the chewing surface; one sits on the side of a lower first molar. Under the microscope, the holes' walls carry concentric striations — the regular circular scoring left by a rotating drill — and in at least one case the wall of the cavity shows signs of subsequent deliberate carving. Crucially, the drilled surfaces show wear that postdates the drilling: the margins of the holes are smoothed by continued chewing. These teeth were drilled in living mouths, and their owners went on eating with them. This was not funerary decoration, and it was not done to the dead.

The cemetery's date gives the practice its age: the graveyard spans roughly 9,000 to 7,500 years ago — in calendar terms, broadly the Period I horizon of the mission's sequence, the cemetery ground remaining in use into the period that followed — and the drilled burials are distributed through it, indicating, in the authors' assessment, a tradition sustained for some 1,500 years. Then it stops: the succeeding Chalcolithic cemetery at MR.2, despite continued poor dental health in its population, has yielded no drilled teeth. A craft practised for seventy generations was abandoned, and the record gives no reason why.

The Tool — A Bead-Drill Turned to Teeth

No drill was found in a patient's grave, but the toolkit is not in doubt, because Mehrgarh preserves it in another context. The Neolithic lithic assemblages contain small flint drill heads, found in association with beads of bone, steatite, shell, calcite, turquoise, lapis lazuli, and carnelian — the standard equipment of the bead-maker, used to perforate ornaments of materials far harder than tooth enamel. The Coppa team reconstructed the dental procedure experimentally on the ethnographic model: a flint tip mounted in a bow drill — the bow drawn back and forth to spin the shaft — produced, in their trials, a hole through human enamel in less than a minute. Flint, they concluded, was "surprisingly effective" for the work. The concentric regularity of the ancient holes matches the rotary action of such a drill, and the inference the paper draws is the one this article adopts: the drilling of teeth at Mehrgarh was an application of the bead-maker's craft. The hands that pierced lapis and carnelian for the graves of §IV belonged to specialists whose rotary drilling skill was, on occasion, turned to the molars of the living.

Why? — The Interpretation, at Its Real Strength

The paper's title claims a "tradition of dentistry," and its text is more careful than its title — a distinction this article preserves. The case for a therapeutic purpose: four of the eleven teeth show decay (caries) associated with the hole, and caries is a disease of farming communities, rising with a cereal diet; drilling out a decayed lesion is a rational response to toothache, and the holes' placement deep in the mouth — on molars invisible in any smile — rules out decoration, as the authors note. The case for caution: seven of the eleven teeth show no associated decay; caries occurs in undrilled teeth too; and the perforations exposed sensitive dentine and pulp, which would have required some filling to be tolerable — of which no trace survives, though an organic filling would not be expected to survive. The authors' own conclusion is the correct strength: the intervention "in some cases could have been therapeutic or palliative," and "the motive for the early Neolithic dental procedure described here is unclear."

What is not unclear, and what this article asserts at full strength, is the core finding: at Mehrgarh, between roughly 9,000 and 7,500 years ago, specialists deliberately drilled holes in the molars of living adults, with rotary flint drills, repeatedly, over some fifteen centuries. It is the earliest known evidence anywhere for drilling human teeth in life — earlier by at least a millennium and a half than the isolated later cases known when the paper appeared — and it arises not in a city or a court but in a farming village, as a by-product of its ornament craft. Eleven teeth are not a profession, a clinic, or a medical system, and §XVII will say so again. But eleven teeth, drilled in life with a bead-maker's bow drill, are enough to move the beginning of dentistry-like practice back into the early Neolithic — and to place it, improbably and attestedly, at the foot of the Bolan Pass.

X. Period III — The Chalcolithic Threshold

Period III (c. 4800–3500 BCE) moves the centre of the site south to the great mound of MR.2 and changes its character. The mission classifies it as Chalcolithic — the copper-using age — but the transformation visible in the strata is broader than any single material: this is the period in which Mehrgarh's crafts become industries, produced at a scale and with a specialisation that point beyond household manufacture toward workshops, and in which the settlement's connections to the wider Greater Indus world become dense enough to trace object by object.

The clearest single witness is pottery. Period III production was wheel-made, painted, and conducted at mass scale: excavation on MR.2 exposed a potters' working area where the bases of three ovens stood on an accumulation of some six metres of pottery debris — the wasters, misfires, and breakage of sustained manufacture, generation upon generation in the same place. Firing was done in updraft kilns and large pit kilns, including firings of the big storage jars that continue the site's storage tradition in ceramic form. The vessel repertoire standardises — shapes repeat, painted motifs recur — and standardisation is itself evidence: pots made to a shared template, in quantity, by people who made pots as a specialism rather than as a household chore.

Burial practice changes with the crafts. The Period III cemetery on MR.2 — some ninety-nine individuals in the excavated sample — shows leaner furnishing than the rich Neolithic graves: ornaments still occur, steatite micro-beads prominently among them, but the trend across the Chalcolithic, noted by the mission's analysts, is for finished objects and manufacturing debris to multiply in the settlement while grave goods grow fewer. Wealth, it seems, was increasingly displayed and used in life rather than deposited in death — or displayed in forms that do not survive. Either way, the excavated pattern is the opposite of decline: the community that buried less was making more.

Period III also continues the site's oldest architecture in a new key. Compartmented buildings — the granary plan of Period I — persist among the Period III structures, now in a settlement whose storage jars, kilns, and workshops speak of surplus organised at a larger scale. And it is in Period III that the site's craft repertoire acquires the two elements the next sections treat in detail: a lapidary industry working hard stones and fired steatite at production scale (§XI), and copper worked not only as ornament but melted in crucibles (§XII). Together with the seals and figurines of §XIII, they form the toolkit — material and symbolic — that Mehrgarh hands on to the Indus tradition.

XI. The Lapidary Craft — Beads of Stone and Fire

Bead-making is Mehrgarh's longest craft thread: present in the Period I graves, supplying the drills of the dentistry of §IX, and reaching industrial scale in the Chalcolithic. The Period III evidence, studied by Massimo Vidale and others of the mission's circle, shows local production of beads in soft stones — steatite and limestone above all — alongside the working of imported hard stones: agate, jasper, and carnelian, perforated with specialised stone drills, and the local manufacture of marine-shell ornaments from shell carried up from the coast. Production debris — blanks, unfinished beads, drill heads, waste — attests the work on site, in multiple stages, with a complexity that the literature reads as evidence of more than one specialist hand and more than one workshop tradition.

Steatite — One Stone, Three Finishes

Steatite, a soft talc-rich stone that hardens and changes colour in fire, became the Chalcolithic bead-maker's signature material, worked into the tiny disc micro-beads that occur by the hundred in Period III necklaces and bracelets. The Mehrgarh–Nausharo sequence documents a graded technology of finishes: beads left unfired, in the stone's natural tan or grey-black; beads bleached and fired white; and beads glazed blue-green, the glaze applied to a deliberately roughened surface — among the earliest faience-type glazing traditions in South Asia, requiring controlled high-temperature firing and a prepared frit or glaze. A soft stone, three chemistries of fire: the steatite sequence is a small, complete history of pyrotechnology in itself, and its products — minute, standardised, and made in quantity — are among the most recognisable manufactures of the pre-Indus world.

Hard Stones — Carnelian and the Long Bead

The hard-stone repertoire works against the steatite grain: carnelian, agate, and jasper cannot be cut with a fingernail's ease but must be knapped, ground, and drilled with abrasives and specialised drills — the same rotary drilling technology, scaled up in patience, that pierced the teeth of §IX. Carnelian beads, including elongated forms, were produced at Mehrgarh and across its regional web, and the craft has a famous future: the long carnelian bead, drilled through centimetres of hard stone without breaking, becomes one of the signature luxury products of the mature Indus cities, traded to Mesopotamia. That later mastery did not appear from nowhere. Its apprenticeship is here, in the Chalcolithic workshops of the Bolan corridor, where drilling hard stone was already an old, specialised, and valued skill — old enough, indeed, to have been borrowed for surgery's first experiment two thousand years before Period III began.

STEATITE MICRO-BEADS

Minute disc beads of steatite, mass-produced in the Chalcolithic: unfired, fired white, or glazed blue-green. The glazed finish — faience-type technology — requires prepared glaze and controlled firing.

HARD-STONE DRILLING

Agate, jasper, and carnelian shaped and perforated with specialised stone drills and abrasives; elongated carnelian beads anticipate the long-bead luxury craft of the Indus cities.

SHELL FROM THE COAST

Marine shell, carried hundreds of kilometres from the Arabian Sea, worked locally into ornaments — the coastal arm of an exchange web that also brought lapis and turquoise overland.

PRODUCTION, NOT JUST POSSESSION

Blanks, failures, drills, and waste in the settlement strata show beads being made at Mehrgarh, not merely worn there — the distinction on which every claim of craft specialisation in this article rests.

XII. Copper — Ornament, Crucible, and the Limits of Early Metallurgy

Copper is present at Mehrgarh almost from the beginning — and present, for most of the sequence, in the humblest of roles. Period I levels yield small copper objects and ornaments; the eight rolled-sheet beads of the cotton burial (§VIII) show the early technique exactly: a narrow strip of metal, hammered thin and curled around a rod into a ring-bead. Period II adds a ring, beads, and a small ingot. These are the products of a community that worked copper — hammering, annealing, rolling — as it worked an unusually malleable stone. Whether any of this earliest copper was smelted locally from Balochistan ores, or arrived as native metal or finished stock through the exchange web, the surviving evidence does not settle, and this article does not pretend otherwise. The distinction matters, and the companion article First Metals sets out the thresholds — native-metal use, smelting, alloying — by which such claims must be judged.

The threshold evidence arrives in the Chalcolithic. Terracotta crucibles bearing traces of copper are reported from Period III contexts at Mehrgarh: vessels in which metal was melted — for casting or refining — at temperatures above copper's melting point. Melting in a crucible is a step beyond hammering sheet, and the mission's reports treat it as the beginning of local metallurgy proper. But the honest statement of the evidence is narrower than the word "metallurgy" suggests to a modern ear. A crucible with copper traces proves that copper was melted at Mehrgarh in Period III. It does not by itself prove that ore was smelted there — melted metal can be remelted scrap or imported ingot — and no Mehrgarh furnace-and-slag assemblage of Belovode's evidentiary completeness (§VII of First Metals) has been published for these levels. What the sequence shows is a gradient, legible across three thousand years: ornament-scale working of copper from the Neolithic, ingots by Period II, crucible melting by Period III, and a mature copper and bronze industry only with the Indus age that follows.

That gradient is itself the finding, and it aligns Mehrgarh with the pattern this hub has documented from Anatolia to the Balkans: metal begins as colour and ornament, in graves and on bodies, and becomes infrastructure late and slowly. At Mehrgarh the point can be sharpened. The site's most consequential copper object is not a tool or a weapon but a bead two millimetres across whose corrosion preserved the world's earliest cotton (§VIII); its most consequential metal technology, for fifteen centuries, was arguably not metallurgy at all but the flint drill of §IX. Copper's history at Mehrgarh is a history of small, precious, skilful things — which is to say, of exactly the social world, valuing rarity and craft over bulk, that the Indus workshops would inherit.

// ORE AT THE DOORSTEP //

Balochistan is mineral country: copper ores occur in the hills west and north of the Kachi plain, and the mission's early reports describe the Period I villagers fashioning tools and ornaments with local copper ore among their materials. Proximity to ore, however, is not production evidence. The published Mehrgarh record supports local working of copper from an early date and local melting by Period III; claims beyond that — early smelting at Mehrgarh itself — await an assemblage of slag, furnace, and ore that meets the standard set out in First Metals, and are not made here.

XIII. Figurines, Seals, and Painted Pots — A Symbolic Repertoire

Mehrgarh's material world was never only practical, and its symbolic repertoire grows with its crafts. Figurines begin in Period I as simple unfired-clay figures of women and animals; by the Chalcolithic they are a developed art — hand-modelled terracotta figures of women with applied and painted ornament, elaborate and varied hairstyles, and figures of animals, prominently including humped zebu bulls. Catherine Jarrige's studies of the figurine corpus read the hairstyles and jewellery of the female figures as a mirror of the community's own changing fashions in personal ornament — the same bead necklaces and ornaments the graves preserve — and the diversity of styles has been taken to suggest a community of growing size and internal variety. What the figurines meant — toys, votives, ancestors, goddesses — the evidence does not say, and the frequent modern leap from "female figurine" to "mother goddess cult" is a leap this article does not make: at Mehrgarh, no temple, altar, or cult context has been excavated for them, and their find contexts are the ordinary levels of the settlement.

The seals are a quieter novelty with a louder future. From the Chalcolithic levels come small compartmented button seals of terracotta and bone, stamped or incised with geometric designs — stepped crosses, concentric circles, cross motifs, and combinations of punctated and incised patterning — the same design vocabulary that recurs in the painted pottery. A fragmentary copper or bronze object from a Period III burial, resembling part of a segmented seal, hints at the type in metal as well. Seals imply marking: of property, of bales, of doors, of identity — an administrative gesture, however modest its scale. The line from Mehrgarh's button seals to the carved steatite seals of the Indus cities, with their animals and their still-undeciphered script, is not a straight descent that any single find demonstrates; but the practice — a small carved object that stamps a recognised design — is present at Mehrgarh a millennium and more before the Indus seal-cutters made it famous, in the same region, in the same craft milieu of steatite working (§XI).

Painted pottery binds the repertoire together. The Period III wares carry geometric decoration in a regional Balochistan vocabulary — bands, hatched triangles, stepped and concentric motifs — that matches, motif for motif, the designs of the seals and the inlay work of the settlement. A shared design system across pots, seals, and ornaments is the kind of low-visibility continuity that argues most strongly for cultural transmission: not a monument anyone copied, but a grammar of pattern that potters, seal-cutters, and bead-makers held in common and handed on. When the Early Harappan world assembles its own visual repertoire in the fourth and third millennia, it assembles it substantially from these parts.

XIV. Nausharo and the Regional Web

No site is an island, and Mehrgarh's later history cannot be told without its successor. Six kilometres away, on the same plain, stands the mound of Nausharo, excavated by the French mission under Jean-François Jarrige from 1985 to 1996, in the decade between the mission's two Mehrgarh campaigns. Nausharo's Period I (c. 3000–2600 BCE, in its subdivisions IA–ID) is contemporary with Mehrgarh's Periods VI–VII and identical in character — the same pottery traditions, crafts, and building habits — and its Periods II and III carry the occupation through the mature Indus civilisation and beyond (to c. 1800 BCE). Between about 2600 and 2000 BCE, as the Indus cities rose, Mehrgarh was largely eclipsed: the community's centre of gravity shifted to the larger, eventually fortified town at Nausharo.

The relationship between the two mounds is the regional story in miniature. Mehrgarh was not destroyed, conquered, or abandoned to the desert; it was succeeded — its population and traditions absorbed into a daughter settlement that carried them into the urban age. The mission's regional programme completed the bracket at the other end of time as well: at Pirak, another mound of the Bolan basin, excavation (1968–1974) had already established occupation running down into the second and first millennia BCE. Taken together — Pirak, Mehrgarh, Nausharo — the French mission's Bolan work documents a continuous sequence of settlement in one small plain from c. 7000 BCE into the Iron Age: as Catherine Jarrige summarised it, the first continuous sequence of dwelling-sites in the subcontinent, established in one landscape, by one research programme.

Beyond the plain, the web widens with every period. The highland valleys of Balochistan hold the contemporary farming settlements — Kili Gul Mohammad near Quetta, Rana Ghundai, the sites of the Surab and Nal regions — whose pottery styles and burial customs interlock with Mehrgarh's in a regional Chalcolithic world. Eastward, the borderland settlements toward the Indus plain — among them, in the syntheses' lists, sites such as Sheri Khan Tarakai, Sarai Khola, Jalilpur, and Ghaligai — mark the farming frontier's advance toward and across the river in the fifth and fourth millennia. Westward and northward, the old routes through the Bolan and over the plateau continued to move lapis, turquoise, and ideas. Mehrgarh in its later periods is best pictured not as a lone precursor waiting for history to begin, but as the senior settlement of a populated, connected region that was itself becoming the western province of the Indus world.

XV. The Road to the Indus — Continuity into the Early Harappan

What, exactly, did the Indus civilisation inherit from Mehrgarh? The question must be asked at the level of the evidence — trait by trait — because "precursor" is a word that can smuggle a whole civilisation across a gap in the record. Weighed item by item, the continuities are these:

CROPS

The wheat-and-barley staple complex of the Indus cities is Mehrgarh's complex, in place at the Bolan by c. 7000 BCE and diversified within the sequence. Cotton — exploited at Mehrgarh by the sixth millennium (§VIII) — becomes the Indus world's signature fibre.

CATTLE

The zebu, domesticated in the Indus region on the prevailing argument (§VI), is the Indus civilisation's central animal — in its faunal assemblages, its figurines, and its seal imagery. The humped bull of the Indus seals has its ancestry in the herds and the bull figurines of Mehrgarh.

CRAFT

Steatite working and glazing, hard-stone bead drilling, shell working, wheel-made painted pottery, and crucible metallurgy — the core craft repertoire of the Early Harappan — are all present at Mehrgarh and Nausharo before the cities. Kenoyer's synthesis of the Harappa evidence concludes that the basic technologies of the later site appear already in the Mehrgarh–Nausharo world.

PRACTICE

Compartmented storage architecture, burial within the settlement's orbit, the button seal's marking gesture (§XIII), and a shared geometric design grammar across media all run from Mehrgarh into the Early Harappan repertoire of the fourth and third millennia.

Against this ledger stand the discontinuities, and a Tier A account must enter them in the same hand. The cities themselves — planned streets, baked-brick platforms, drainage systems, standardised weights, the Indus script — have no Mehrgarh precedent; they are creations of the third millennium, assembled in the Early Harappan transformation (c. 3300–2600 BCE) from ingredients that included, but were not limited to, the Balochistan tradition. The population question is open: Lukacs and Hemphill's dental analysis found the Chalcolithic inhabitants of Mehrgarh were not simply the lineal descendants of its Neolithic inhabitants, and they located the Neolithic population's descendants, on dental affinities, to the south and east — a reminder that cultural continuity at a much-visited corridor site can ride on substantial human movement. And the mechanism of transmission is plural by necessity: migration, intermarriage, trade, apprenticeship, and imitation all move crafts and crops, and the record rarely lets us apportion credit among them.

The balanced statement is the one the excavators themselves have defended: the Indus civilisation was not imported into the subcontinent, and it was not invented in a generation at Mohenjo-daro. It grew, over five thousand years, from farming communities of the Indus borderlands — of which Mehrgarh is the earliest, longest, and best-documented — through the regional Chalcolithic world those communities built, into the Early Harappan. Gregory Possehl's standard synthesis titles its treatment accordingly: Mehrgarh stands at the head of the Indus sequence because the sequence, excavated layer by layer, demonstrably begins there. For the parallel question in this hub — how a pastoral world fed a farming valley's civilisation — compare The Nile Bridge, where the Saharan contribution to predynastic Egypt is weighed under the same rules.

XVI. The Indus Prologue — A Bridge This Hub Builds

This article ends, deliberately, where its subject's second act begins. Mehrgarh's sequence runs to c. 2000 BCE, but its last fifteen centuries are no longer a village's story: they are the Early Harappan transformation and the rise of the Indus cities — Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, and their world of seals and script, standardised weights, planned streets, and trade with Mesopotamia. That civilisation is one of the great subjects of ancient history, and it is not the subject of this hub. The Antiquity division of this archive — "Before Kemet" — treats the deep backgrounds: the worlds before and beside the first literate civilisations. Mehrgarh belongs here because its first three and a half thousand years are exactly such a background, recoverable only from the ground, without a single written word to help or to mislead.

The mature Harappan civilisation belongs to a future India hub of this archive, where it can be treated at the scale it deserves — its cities, its undeciphered script, its crafts, its long debate over decline — as the Egyptology division treats Kemet. This article is the bridge this hub builds toward that future treatment, and it is worth stating plainly, in the archive's own voice, what a bridge is: not the far shore, and not a promise about when the far shore will be built, but a span anchored at both ends in evidence. The near anchor is here — a granary village at the Bolan Pass, c. 7000 BCE, with western grains in its bricks and local cattle in its pens. The far anchor is the Indus world of the third millennium, whose foundations §XV has inventoried. Everything between is the sequence this article has walked.

There is a discipline in ending here, and it is the same discipline that governs the hub's treatment of Egypt's own background in Predynastic Egypt: the prologue must not swallow the play. If Mehrgarh is made to contain the whole Indus story in embryo — cities, script, and all, foreordained in a Neolithic granary — then five thousand years of human improvisation are flattened into a destiny, and the evidence is insulted in the act of praising it. The excavated truth is better than the destiny: a village that combined imported seeds with home-grown cattle, that strung Afghan lapis on cotton thread, that drilled beads and, for a while, teeth — and that kept going, adapting, for fifty centuries, until its descendants' world was ready to build cities. Prologues do not determine their stories. They make them possible.

XVII. What Mehrgarh Does and Does Not Show

Mehrgarh's importance does not depend on its superlatives, and it has several genuine ones. Strip away every "earliest" and the excavated remainder is still the foundation document of South Asian prehistory: a farming village founded by c. 7000 BCE at the meeting of the Iranian plateau and the Indus plain, storing its western grains in compartmented granaries, burying its dead with ornaments drawn from half a continent, bringing the local aurochs under management into the zebu herds on which the subcontinent's agriculture would rest, spinning cotton by the sixth millennium, specialising its crafts across three thousand years into workshops of potters, bead-makers, and metal-melters — and handing the whole repertoire, by unbroken local development through Nausharo, to the Early Harappan world from which the Indus cities rose.

What Mehrgarh does not show is equally definite, and this article has tried to keep the negatives as visible as the positives. It does not show a "cradle of civilisation" in isolation: its grains tie it west to the Fertile Crescent, its lapis and turquoise tie it to Afghanistan and Iran, its shell ties it to the coast — the village was a crossroads from its first level, and its originality is the originality of combination, not of solitude. It does not show the domestication of wheat or barley, which are western domesticates here; nor, at the level of proof, the domestication of cotton, whose fibres attest use but not cultivation status. Its zebu domestication, though supported by two independent lines of evidence, is located by genetics in a region, not certified at a single mound. Its dentistry is eleven teeth from nine individuals — a real tradition, sustained for centuries, and not a profession, a clinic, or a medical system; its motive remains, in its publishers' words, unclear. Its copper is ornament and crucible, not an early smelting industry. And it does not show cities, kings, writing, or temples: for fifty centuries Mehrgarh had none of these, and needed none of them to matter.

Perhaps that is the site's deepest instruction for this hub. The road to the Indus did not begin with a monument or a text. It began with mud bricks full of grain impressions, with a bracelet of copper beads on a cotton string, with cattle growing smaller in a herder's pens generation by generation, and with a bead-maker's drill spinning, once in a while, against an aching tooth. Civilisation, when it came to the Indus, came to a world already five thousand years deep in farming, herding, craft, and exchange — and Mehrgarh, attested, dated, and debated in the open literature, is where that depth can still be read, layer by layer, in the ground at the foot of the Bolan Pass.
