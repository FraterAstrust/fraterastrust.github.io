This is a Tier A article: everything in it rests on excavated, dated, and published evidence — clay tablets, clay envelopes, seals, and inscribed labels recovered from named sites and edited in the scholarly literature. Where scholars disagree, as they do sharply over the token theory of writing's origin and over whether Egypt invented its script independently, the disagreement itself is reported, with each position attributed to the scholar who holds it and stated at the strength its holder gives it. No claim here depends on ancient legend or modern construction. The one ancient story this article retells — the Sumerian tale of Enmerkar, in §XI — is labelled for what it is: a composition written down more than a thousand years after the events it imagines, evidence for what later scribes believed about their craft, not for how the craft began.

Writing was invented at least twice in the ancient world this hub covers, and the two inventions happened within a few centuries — perhaps within a few decades — of each other. In southern Iraq, in the great city of Uruk, administrators of the late fourth millennium BCE pressed and drew signs on clay tablets to record grain, livestock, beer, land, and labour: the script now called proto-cuneiform. In Upper Egypt, in a royal tomb at Abydos, officials of a predynastic kingdom incised signs on small labels of bone and ivory and painted them in ink on wine jars, to record goods and the places they came from: the first hieroglyphs. A third system, proto- Elamite, arose in Iran in the same centuries, borrowed the idea and some of the machinery, built a script of its own — and died undeciphered, as it remains today.

Behind the Mesopotamian invention lies a still longer prehistory. For some five thousand years before the first tablet, farming communities across the Near East had kept accounts with small clay counters — tokens — shaped as cones, spheres, discs, and cylinders. Whether those tokens are the direct ancestors of writing, as Denise Schmandt-Besserat argued in Before Writing (1992), or only the ancestors of its numbers, as her critics Robert Englund and Paul Zimansky reply, is the central scholarly argument of this article, and it is given in full on both sides (§§IV–V). What is not in dispute is the destination. By about 3400–3200 BCE the counters, envelopes, seals, and numerical tablets of the old accounting world had been joined — at Uruk, and at first only there — by something new under the sun: a repertoire of more than seven hundred signs that could record not only how many, but what, whose, and from whom. Within a few more centuries those signs would learn to record sounds, then grammar, then — slowly, and last of all — stories. This article follows that sequence at the level of the objects themselves: how an account was physically made, sealed, totalled, and filed, before it asks what the accounts mean.

I. What Counts as Writing — And What Does Not

The argument of this article turns on a definition, and the definition must be fixed before the evidence is weighed. Scholars of early scripts — the usage followed in Stephen Houston's The First Writing (2004) and in Christopher Woods's Visible Language (2010), the two syntheses on which this article most relies — reserve the word writing for a system of visible marks that records language: marks whose values are tied to the words and sounds of a particular tongue, so that a text can, in principle, be read aloud as an utterance. Systems of marks that convey information without tying it to a specific language — tallies, property marks, heraldic emblems, the picture-signs a traveller can read in any country — are classed as proto-writing or notation. The distinction is not a value judgement. A tally can run an economy; an emblem can carry a theology. But only writing can record a name exactly, distinguish "the sheep were delivered" from "the sheep will be delivered," and preserve a sentence its author never spoke in the reader's presence.

The definition matters because the ancient world is full of sign systems that are not writing under it, and two of them stand close to this article's subject. The first is the token system itself (§II): a cone could mean a small measure of grain to anyone who had learned the code, whatever language they spoke, precisely because the cone did not stand for a word. The second is the case this hub treats in Old Europe: the Vinča signs of the Neolithic Balkans (c. 5300–3500 BCE), a repertoire of incised marks — crosses, chevrons, combs, spirals, and abstract forms — found chiefly on pottery, spindle whorls, and figurines across the Vinča culture and its neighbours. The Vinča signs are older than cuneiform by well over a millennium, and they are sometimes popularly called "the first writing." On the excavated evidence they are not, and the reasons are instructive.

More than 85 per cent of Vinča inscriptions consist of a single sign. The longest groups run to only a handful of signs, with no fixed reading order that analysis has established. The signs do not accumulate into archives: there are no Vinča accounts, no lists, no labelled stores, no sign of the administrative machinery that everywhere else accompanies early script. Shan Winn's great catalogue of the signs (1981) — some 210 sign types in his analysis — was published under the deliberately chosen title Pre-Writing in Southeastern Europe: Winn himself concluded that the signs were not texts. Later quantitative analysis has sorted most of the repertoire into potters' marks, ornament, and a residue of perhaps a third that may be symbolic notation of some kind — ownership, identity, cult — without demonstrating any tie to a spoken language. The famous Tărtăria tablets of Romania, three small clay tablets whose signs are the strongest Vinča candidates for a "text," carry their own unresolved problems of find context and dating by association, and no reading of them has won scholarly assent. The Vinča case is therefore this article's control experiment: a farming society, centuries deep, with a rich and long-lived sign repertoire — and no writing. Signs alone do not make a script. What was missing at Vinča, and present at Uruk, was not ingenuity. It was an institution that needed to record, exactly and at volume, who owed what to whom.

One more preliminary belongs here, because it disciplines everything that follows: the earliest scripts sit at the boundary of the definition themselves. Proto-cuneiform, as Woods emphasises, records very little grammar; its sign order does not yet follow spoken word order, and its texts must in a real sense be interpreted from their format rather than read as transcribed speech. Whether the language behind the earliest tablets was even Sumerian is, as §XI shows, a question the specialists themselves leave open — Englund finds no definitive evidence for it, and Woods calls the direct evidence "quite slim" while noting that most scholars assume it. This article therefore uses the field's own careful vocabulary: proto-cuneiform for the Uruk script in its first phases, writing for the achievement toward which it was travelling, and exact dates and corpora wherever the argument depends on them. The boundary is not an embarrassment to the story. It is the story: writing was not found complete, like a stone on a road. It was assembled, device by device, over millennia — and the assembly can be excavated.

II. Before Writing — The Token Systems of the Neolithic Near East

The tokens appear with farming. From about 8000 BCE — the early Neolithic, when wheat, barley, sheep, and goats were first brought under cultivation and herding — small objects of clay begin to occur on sites across the whole arc of the Fertile Crescent and beyond: in Anatolia, Syria, the Levant, Iraq, and Iran. They are among the humblest artefacts in any museum drawer. A typical token is one to three centimetres across, pinched or rolled from a pellet of clay, sun-dried or lightly baked. Schmandt-Besserat's catalogue, assembled over two decades and published in Before Writing (1992), counted more than eight thousand tokens from over a hundred sites, spanning some five thousand years. Whatever else they were, they were not rare, local, or brief: the token is the most widely distributed accounting artefact of the prehistoric world.

Plain Tokens — The First Repertoire

The earliest and commonest forms — Schmandt-Besserat's plain tokens — are geometric solids: cones, spheres, discs, cylinders, ovoids (egg-shapes), tetrahedra (triangular pyramids), and a few other regular shapes, in larger and smaller sizes. They carry no decoration. Their power lay in convention: within a community that shared the code, a shape stood for a commodity in a standard quantity. In Schmandt-Besserat's reconstruction — the readings are hers, argued from the later evidence of §§III–IV, and the reader should hold them at that attribution — the cone, the sphere, and the flat disc stood for small, larger, and largest standard measures of grain; the ovoid for a jar of oil; the cylinder for an animal of the flock; the tetrahedron for a unit of labour, a person-day of work. A storekeeper holding three cones and a cylinder held, in his palm, a record: three small measures of grain and one animal, owed, delivered, or in store. The tokens could be counted, bundled, handed over, and recounted — a ledger that could be audited by touch, by parties who shared no language, because the code was in the shapes and not in any tongue.

The procedure, reconstructed from find contexts, was physical and exact. Tokens representing a delivery were counted out in the presence of the parties; tokens representing a debt were set aside and held by the creditor or the store; tokens were strung, in some cases, on cords through perforations, or kept in groups in bowls, jars, and rooms of the buildings they served. Assemblages of tokens are found in precisely the contexts an accounting reading predicts: in storerooms, in administrative buildings, with seals, and — in the fourth millennium — inside the sealed envelopes of §III. It is equally important to state what the record does not give us. No Neolithic text explains the tokens; their meanings are inferred, backwards, from their shapes, their contexts, and their resemblance to later signs. Some small clay objects catalogued as tokens may have served other purposes entirely — gaming pieces, counters in other games of number, or things whose use is lost. That uncertainty is the seed of the controversy of §V, and it is present from the first drawer of cones.

Complex Tokens — The Repertoire Grows Teeth

For some four thousand years the plain repertoire changed little. Then, in the mid-fourth millennium BCE — the age of the first cities, of Uruk's explosive growth and of the Uruk expansion treated in this hub's Ubaid and Uruk article — a second repertoire appears beside it: Schmandt-Besserat's complex tokens. These keep the old solids but add new shapes modelled on artefacts and animals — vessels, tools, heads of stock — and, above all, they are marked: incised with lines and crosses, punched with dots, built up with applied pellets and coils of clay. Where a plain cone said "a measure of grain," a marked token could in principle say which grain, which product, which grade: the incisions multiply the code's distinctions just as the urban economy multiplied the goods to be distinguished — types of oil, of textile, of beer, of processed food, of animal by age and kind. On Schmandt-Besserat's count the complex repertoire runs to some hundreds of distinguishable types, against the plain repertoire's handful. The timing is her argument's foundation: the token system elaborated itself, suddenly and dramatically, in exactly the generations in which the cities' administrations outgrew what a handful of cones and spheres could say — and in exactly the place where writing then appears.

// ONE-TO-ONE CORRESPONDENCE //

The token system's logic is what Schmandt-Besserat calls concrete counting: there is no token for "three." Three measures of grain are three cone-tokens; three animals are three cylinder-tokens; the number exists only as the pile. Quantity and commodity are fused in the object itself. The long history that follows — envelopes, impressed signs, abstract numerals — is the history of those two ideas, how many and of what, being prised apart until each could be written separately. Whether that history shows a people incapable of abstract number before writing, as the strong form of the token thesis holds, is contested: later critics, Peter Damerow's work among them, treat number as abstract in use long before it is abstract in notation. The piles, at least, are fact.

Tokens did not vanish when writing came. They continued in use alongside tablets for centuries, and sporadically for far longer — a famous envelope from Nuzi, in northern Iraq, of the second millennium BCE, held forty-nine pebbles and bore a cuneiform inscription listing forty-nine animals, a token account still doing its work some two thousand years after the first tablets. The old technology persisted because it was good at what it did: it was cheap, portable, language-blind, and self-verifying. What it could not do was grow. A token account cannot name the debtor, date the debt, qualify it, or total a hundred deliveries on a single surface. The devices of the next section are best understood as attempts — ingenious, and ultimately insufficient — to make the token system do those things.

III. Envelopes, Seals, and Numerical Tablets — The Bridge Devices

The bridge from tokens to tablets was built, in the middle of the fourth millennium BCE, out of three devices, each of which survives in the ground. They are best taken in the order of the problem each one solved.

The Envelope — An Account That Cannot Be Altered

A loose pile of tokens can be added to or raided; a debt recorded in tokens is only as honest as its keeper. The clay envelope — the bulla — solved this. The procedure can be reconstructed object by object. The accountant counted out the tokens for a transaction — a delivery of grain, a consignment of animals — in the presence of the parties. He then took a lump of wet clay, hollowed it into a ball around the tokens, and closed it with the tokens sealed inside. Before the clay dried, he rolled a cylinder seal across the surface: a small stone cylinder, engraved in reverse, whose rolling left a continuous frieze of figures — the signature of the responsible official or institution, pressed into the very skin of the account. The envelope now was the record. It could travel with the goods as a bill of lading. It could be stored. And it could be verified in only one way: by breaking it open and counting the tokens against the delivery. An envelope that arrived intact, seal unbroken, guaranteed that its contents had not been touched since the sealing. Accountability had acquired a tamper- evident seal — in the literal, physical sense.

Envelopes of this kind are known from sites across the Uruk world, with the key stratified sequence at Susa, in Khuzestan (southwestern Iran), where envelopes come principally from level 18 of the Acropole excavations, immediately before the numerical tablets of level 17. Some 130–150 envelopes and fragments have been excavated in total. Most remain unopened to this day — only a handful have been broken in modern times — though computed tomography (CT) scanning of intact examples, including envelopes from Chogha Mish now in Chicago, has begun to read their contents without destroying them, and has confirmed tokens inside where the accounting reading predicts them. It was Pierre Amiet who, in the 1960s, first pointed to the decisive detail on which the next step turns: the surfaces of some envelopes bear impressions that match the tokens sealed inside.

The Impression — The Account Moves to the Surface

An envelope that must be broken to be read is secure but blind. The remedy was obvious once seen: before sealing, press each token into the wet surface of the envelope, so that its shape is recorded on the outside. The surface then declares the contents — three cone-impressions, a cylinder- impression — while the tokens inside remain as the sealed guarantee. Envelopes bearing such token impressions are excavated fact. But the device contains its own abolition: once the surface carries the whole account, the tokens inside are redundant. Drop them, flatten the ball into a slab, keep the impressions and the seal — and the envelope has become a numerical tablet: a solid lump of clay bearing impressed signs for quantities, rolled with the seal of the responsible party, and nothing else. Numerical tablets — quantities without commodities, or with at most the seal to say whose account it was — are found across the Uruk world in the generations immediately before proto-cuneiform: at Uruk itself, at Susa, at Habuba Kabira and Jebel Aruda on the Syrian Euphrates, at Godin Tepe in Iran, and elsewhere in the contact zone. Englund's term for the next and last bridge form is the numero-ideographic tablet: a numerical tablet to which one or two drawn signs have been added naming the commodity itself — butter oil, textiles, small cattle, in the Susa examples he cites. With that addition, every element of the later account tablet exists. What remained was to systematise it.

// THE DEVICES, IN SEQUENCE //

Tokens (from c. 8000 BCE): shaped counters, one per unit of goods; the pile is the number. Envelopes / bullae (mid-4th millennium BCE): tokens sealed inside a hollow clay ball, the surface rolled with a cylinder seal; verified by breaking. Proto-cuneiform tablets (§VII): quantities, commodities, persons, and institutions, in ruled cases, with totals. The sequence is Englund's reconstruction of the Susa–Uruk evidence; its first steps are the least contested part of the whole token debate.

The Seal — The Second Technology

Running through every device in this section is an object that deserves its own sentence of credit: the cylinder seal. Developed in the Uruk period world — Mesopotamia first, with Egypt adopting the form soon after, itself a datum for §XIII — the cylinder seal turned identity into a mechanism. Rolled across wet clay, it marked a tablet, an envelope, a jar sealing, or the clay lump on a storeroom door as the act of a particular office. Sealing is not writing: a seal image identifies without recording language, and seals were used by administrations for three thousand years alongside script. But sealing taught the accounting world the two habits writing would inherit: that a clay surface could carry authority, and that a standardised repertoire of images, learned and recognised across a whole society, could be trusted at a distance. The first tablets are unthinkable without the sealed envelope that preceded them — and the envelope is unthinkable without the seal.

IV. The Token Thesis — Schmandt-Besserat's Argument at Full Strength

Denise Schmandt-Besserat, an archaeologist at the University of Texas at Austin, built the token theory across a series of publications from the 1970s to its full statement in Before Writing (1992, two volumes: the argument, and the site-by-site catalogue of tokens), with a condensed version in How Writing Came About (1996). The theory is the most influential account of writing's origin ever offered, and this article states it first at its full strength, as its author states it, before the critics are heard in §V. A theory that reorganised a field deserves to be met in its best form.

The argument has four movements. First, the tokens were a system: not casual counters but a shared code, stable in its plain forms across five millennia and the whole Near East, in which shape signified commodity and standard quantity — the earliest code, in her phrase, for processing data. Second, the system elaborated under urban pressure: the complex tokens of the mid-fourth millennium, with their incised and applied marks, multiplied the code's distinctions as the redistributive economy of the first cities multiplied the goods to be tracked. Third, the envelope episode converted the three-dimensional code into two-dimensional signs: tokens impressed on envelope surfaces left marks that are, shape for shape, the earliest impressed numerical signs of the tablets — and the marks of complex tokens, drawn rather than impressed, became the drawn, pictographic signs for commodities. Fourth, the solid tablet inherited both families of signs at once, and writing was born as the union of the two: impressed signs for quantities, drawn signs for goods. On this account, specific correspondences can be exhibited token by token and sign by sign: the cone's impression becomes the sign for the small grain measure; the sphere's, the larger; the disc bearing a cross — her token for a sheep — becomes the cross-in-circle sign by which proto-cuneiform writes sheep and goats, a sign whose pictographic origin in any actual sheep is impossible and whose token origin is, she argues, written on its face.

The theory's explanatory reach is its glory. It explains why writing appears where and when it does — in the accounting departments of the first cities, at the climax of a five-thousand-year development of accounting, not in temples as hymn or on walls as royal display. It explains why the earliest tablets are accounts and nothing else. It explains the otherwise baffling duality of the earliest script, in which numbers are impressed with the stylus's ends while commodity signs are drawn with its point — two techniques, two ancestries, fused on one surface. It explains why the numerical signs are the most conservative element of the system, keeping the shapes of counters long after the word-signs stylised beyond recognition. And it grounds a larger claim about cognition that Schmandt- Besserat presses throughout her work: that the token system was an external extension of memory which, by handling ever more data in ever more abstract form — from the thing itself, to a token for the thing, to a mark for the token, to a sign that could at last stand for a sound — trained the abstraction that writing then embodied. On her chronology the phases run: tokens, 8000–3500 BCE; envelopes and impressions, c. 3500 BCE; tablets with impressed and drawn signs, c. 3300–3100 BCE; phonetic signs, introduced to write the names of persons, c. 3100–3000 BCE. Writing, in the famous summary, began as counting — and humanity learned to write because it first needed to keep accounts.

// THE CREDIT WHERE IT IS DUE //

Two elements of the theory were not Schmandt-Besserat's inventions, and she credited them herself: A. Leo Oppenheim had drawn scholarly attention to the tokens as a class of object, and Pierre Amiet had noticed, in the 1960s, that envelope surfaces bear impressions matching the tokens inside. Her achievement was the synthesis: to assemble the Near-East-wide catalogue, to propose the commodity readings of the shapes, and to carry the argument the whole way from the Neolithic cone to the cuneiform sign list. Criticism of the synthesis, in §V, should not be mistaken for a denial that the catalogue itself transformed the evidence available to everyone who argues about it.

V. The Critics — Englund, Zimansky, and the Objections

The token theory has been under expert fire for as long as it has existed, and the fire has burned away parts of it. This section gives the objections at the strength their authors give them. They fall into three groups: objections to the chronology and unity of the token system, objections to the matches between tokens and signs, and an alternative account of what the solid core of the theory actually proves.

Chronology and Unity — Zimansky's Objection

Paul Zimansky's review of Before Writing (1993) remains the most quoted statement of the sceptical case, and Woods's synthesis adopts its central points. The tokens, Zimansky observed, span the ninth millennium to the end of the fourth and a geographical arc from Anatolia to Iran — yet the theory treats them as one system, a single code persisting essentially unchanged for five thousand years across dozens of cultures that shared no state, no priesthood, and no school to maintain it. No other human institution is credited with such stability, and the catalogue's own method manufactures part of the appearance of unity: objects gathered from a hundred sites and fifty centuries are sorted into types by a modern scholar, and the types are then read as the code's signs. The frequency evidence, he argued, cuts against the commodity readings themselves. The token Schmandt-Besserat reads as "sheep" — the disc with an incised cross — occurs, on her own catalogue's figures as Zimansky cited them, only some fifteen times in seven thousand years, while the commonest tokens in the record are forms she reads as "nails" and "work days." A Near East that accounted for nails and labour far more often than for sheep — the foundation animal of its economy, the very commodity the first tablets count obsessively — is, on the critics' reading, a reductio of the identifications: the commonest shapes are probably not commodity tokens at all, and the code dissolves, at its centre, into a miscellany of small clay objects whose uses we do not know.

The Matches — Englund's Objection

Robert K. Englund — of the Berlin Archaische Texte aus Uruk project and the Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative, the editor of more proto-cuneiform material than anyone living — has faulted Schmandt-Besserat, in his own phrase as Woods reports it, for over-interpreting both the systematisation and the iconic differentiation of the tokens. The specific objections are epigraphic. Most of the tokens claimed as forerunners of particular word-signs were never found inside envelopes — the one context that would tie them to the accounting chain. Many of the proposed correspondences between complex tokens and proto-cuneiform signs are, in Woods's summary of the critical consensus, simply not plausible: two systems can share a shape — a cross, a jar outline, a head — without sharing a meaning, and resemblance asserted across five thousand years and a thousand kilometres is not descent demonstrated. Englund's own test case is instructive: a token form that Schmandt- Besserat and others equated with the sign for "precious metal" is, on his analysis of the numerical contexts, more likely to have stood for the fraction one-half. Where the theory needs a picture of a thing, the contexts deliver an arithmetical value. The word-signs, the critics conclude, were in their great majority invented with the script itself — drawn fresh by the Uruk administrators who needed them, from the world of vessels, plants, animals, and body parts around them — not inherited from a Neolithic code. Jean-Jacques Glassner, in The Invention of Cuneiform (2003), presses the point into a counter- thesis: writing was not a slow evolution out of counters at all, but a deliberate, comparatively sudden invention — a system conceived as a whole, by identifiable intellectual effort, to render the Sumerian language; the genuinely pictographic fraction of the earliest sign repertoire is, he argues, far too small to carry an evolution from pictures, and the accounting-first model mistakes the script's first employers for its inventors.

What Survives — The Agreed Core

And yet the critics are not demolitionists, and the present state of the question — the position a Tier A article must report — is a partitioned one. Englund himself calls the role of the simple tokens as forerunners of the impressed numerical signs "core knowledge" among cuneiformists: the chain from plain tokens, through envelope impressions, to the numerical tablets and the numerical signs of proto-cuneiform is accepted by essentially all parties, Englund and Woods included. Woods's formulation is the sharpest: tokens and envelope impressions relate to the numerals — "and these two systems, and only these two, appear to be related" — with the numerical tablets bridging the gap; the word-signs are another matter. A handful of individual signs may indeed descend from tokens — the cross-in-circle for sheep and goats is the standing candidate, accepted even by cautious accounts as apparently borrowed from the older accounting. The dispute is over the systematic claim: that the complex tokens, as a repertoire, became the word-signs, as a repertoire. That claim has not carried the field.

// THE VERDICT, AT ITS EXACT STRENGTH //

Accepted: plain tokens were accounting counters; their impressions on envelopes and numerical tablets are the ancestors of proto-cuneiform's numerical signs; cuneiform therefore began, in one of its two components, with numbers. Rejected or unproven: that the tokens formed one unchanging pan–Near Eastern code for five thousand years; that the complex tokens' commodity readings are secure; that the word-signs as a body descend from complex tokens. Open: a small number of individual sign derivations (sheep-and-goats the chief), and the degree of system in the Neolithic material itself. The first writing was thus born as accounting — that much of the famous thesis the evidence sustains — but it was not born only as accounting's child: its vocabulary of things was, on the prevailing critical view, a fresh invention of the Uruk age itself.

VI. Uruk — The City That Needed Accounts

Writing appears in a city, and the city must be pictured before the tablets can be understood. Uruk — modern Warka, in southern Iraq, east of the Euphrates — grew in the later fourth millennium BCE into something the world had not previously contained: a settlement of some 200 hectares or more, with a population Englund estimates as approaching 40,000, organised around great institutional households and crowned by the monumental temple precinct of Eanna, sacred to the goddess Inana, whose terraced and niched buildings were raised, levelled, and rebuilt in continuous succession. Around it, the alluvial plain of southern Mesopotamia was being settled and irrigated at scale, and the Uruk world reached outward — colonies, outposts, and contact stations up the Euphrates into Syria and across to Iran — in the expansion this hub treats in Ubaid and Uruk. It is not a coincidence that the bridge devices of §III cluster in that contact zone: the accounting technology travelled with the trade and the colonists.

The administrative problem of such a city is easily stated and was, before writing, insoluble. The great households — temple and palace establishments whose exact constitutional form is still debated — received grain, oil, beer, textiles, livestock, and labour from a countryside of farms, estates, and dependent settlements; stored them in bulk; processed them in breweries, bakeries, weaving sheds, and byres; and disbursed them again as rations to hundreds or thousands of dependent workers, as offerings, as seed, as fodder, and as the materials of craft. Every movement was an obligation: a delivery owed, a ration due, a flock entrusted, a field assigned. Human memory, witnessed delivery, tokens, and seals could carry such a system to village scale. At Uruk's scale — thousands of transactions, hundreds of personnel, obligations running across seasons and years — the record itself had to become an institution: standardised, totalled, archivable, and readable by an official who had not been present at the transaction. That requirement, and not any literary ambition, is the documented cradle of the script. Nearly all the earliest tablets come from the Eanna precinct itself — though, as the next section insists, they come from its rubbish, not from its shelves.

Administrative documents dominate; lexical lists (§IX) make up the remainder. Letters, hymns, laws, and royal inscriptions: none. They are centuries in the future.

VII. Proto-Cuneiform — The Tablet Corpus of Uruk IV–III

The script of the earliest tablets is called proto-cuneiform: proto-, because it is the direct ancestor of the wedge-written (cuneiform) script of later Mesopotamia, while its own signs are not yet wedges. Scholars divide the corpus into two phases named for the building levels of the Eanna precinct at Uruk. Uruk IV (conventionally c. 3400–3300 BCE in the chronology this hub's timeline uses, with the corpus's floruit often given as c. 3350–3200) is the phase of invention: its tablets are known, without apparent exception, from Uruk alone. Uruk III (c. 3200–3000 BCE, also called the Jemdet Nasr period after a type site) is the phase of spread: tablets of the same system appear at Jemdet Nasr, Kish, Uqair, Larsa, and other Babylonian sites, with further hundreds reaching museums from illicit digging in recent decades, probably from Umma, Adab, and Kish. The German excavations at Uruk between 1928 and 1976 recovered about five thousand tablets and fragments; Englund's count for the whole corpus is over six thousand pieces containing more than 38,000 lines of text. It is, by any measure, a large corpus for an invention's first two centuries — large enough to be statistics, not anecdotes.

Two cautions about the corpus govern every claim built on it. The first is context: nearly all the Uruk tablets were found in secondary deposits — discarded after use, and employed, with sherds and broken mudbrick, as levelling fill for later construction. A tablet's layer therefore gives only a latest-possible horizon, not the date it was written, and the fine chronology of the script's first steps is argued from the internal development of the signs and formats rather than from stratigraphy. The conventional dates in this article are exactly that — conventions, shared by the literature, resting partly on a small radiocarbon base that Woods describes as troublesome for this period; Englund notes that Dietrich Sürenhagen would place the earliest phase a century or more earlier, in Uruk V. The second caution is survival: the tablets were business records, made to be consulted for a season and thrown away. That six thousand survive is an accident of clay, fill, and occasional ancient fire. What survives is what one precinct discarded — an archive's rubbish, read as an archive, with all the distortion that implies and all the candour that requires.

The tablets themselves are small: most fit in a palm, the primary receipts rarely exceeding about 8 × 8 cm, the great summary accounts two or three times that. Their surfaces are ruled into cases — rectangular boxes, laid out in columns and rows — and each case is a unit of the account: a quantity, a commodity, a person or office. The reverse of a summary tablet carries the totals. The physical format is the syntax: where a later scribe writes "received from the shepherd," the archaic tablet places the flock's number and sign in one case, the shepherd's title in the next, and lets the grid say the rest. Englund distinguishes the two great document classes that later Babylonian bookkeeping also knows: primary documents — receipts, bills, single transfers, small and simply formatted — and secondary documents, the large consolidated accounts into which primaries were gathered, their complex grids doing the work that grammar would later do. Between them, these two classes are the entire literature of the Uruk IV–III world, together with the lists of §IX. There is nothing else. No letter has survived from the invention's first centuries because, on the evidence, no letter was written.

// DRAWN, THEN PRESSED //

The signs change their manufacture between the phases, and the change can be seen under raking light. Uruk IV signs are largely drawn: curved, naturalistic lines — the horns of a beast, the ear of grain — traced with a pointed stylus, or built, as close study shows, from many small straight impressions of the stylus's edge. By Uruk III the lines have straightened and schematised; the signs are increasingly pressed with a stylus of triangular cross-section, their strokes tending toward the wedge-headed impressions from which cuneiform takes its name. Animal heads lose their faces; vessels lose their curves. Abstraction, here, is not a philosophy. It is what happens when a drawn picture must be produced ten thousand times by a working hand.

VIII. Numbers First — The Numerical and Metrological Systems

The decipherment of proto-cuneiform began with its numbers, and the numbers remain its best-understood component — the achievement, above all, of Peter Damerow and Jöran Friberg, carried through in the Berlin project's editions and in Nissen, Damerow, and Englund's Archaic Bookkeeping (1993). The headline finding overturns the modern reader's first assumption. The archaic scribes did not use one number system. They used many — around sixty distinct numerical signs, grouped by the Berlin research into a dozen or so systems — and the system in force on a given tablet was determined by what was being counted. A number in proto-cuneiform is not an abstract quantity with a unit attached. It is a quantity-of-a-kind: the sign sequence itself declares whether it counts animals, rations, or measures of grain, because each kind is counted in its own notation, with its own signs and its own bundling ratios.

The Sexagesimal System — Counting Things

Discrete objects — animals, people, jars, stones, most countable things — were counted in the sexagesimal system (the Berlin project's System S). Its signs are the direct heirs of the token impressions: a small cone- impression for the unit; a small circle for ten; a large cone for sixty (ten units bundled six times: the ratios run 10, 6, 10, 6, 10); a large cone with a circle inside for 600; a large circle for 3,600; a large circle with a small circle inside for 36,000. The alternation of tens and sixes is the seed of the base-60 arithmetic that Mesopotamia would keep for three thousand years and bequeath, through astronomy, to our own hours and degrees. To write 125 sheep, the scribe impressed two large cones (120) and five small cones — in the sheep's own case, beside the sheep sign. The system is additive, not positional: value lies in which signs are present, not in their order.

The Bisexagesimal System — Counting Rations

A second system, the bisexagesimal (System B), shares most of the sexagesimal signs but bundles them differently — its ratios run 10, 6, 2, 10, 6, reaching a largest unit of 7,200 — and its domain is specific: discrete ration and processed goods: grain products, breads, dried fish, cheeses, and similar disbursables. The distinction is administrative, not mathematical. Goods that flowed out of the stores as rations were accounted in the ration system; goods that existed as countable stock were accounted in the sexagesimal. The same clay surface can carry both, each in its own entries, and the trained reader — ancient or modern — knows from the numerical signs alone which kind of transaction a case records before reading the commodity sign at all.

The Grain-Capacity System — Measuring the Staple

Grain, the economy's foundation, was not counted but measured, and it has its own notation: the grain-capacity system (System Š, for the sign ŠE, "grain"). Its smallest sign is a single small cone- impression of a distinctive form, and its bundling ratios run 5, 10, 3, 10 — five small measures to the next unit, ten of those to the next, three to the next, ten to the largest commonly used unit, worth 1,500 of the smallest. The ratios are the fossil of a metrology: they encode the actual vessels and baskets of the granary, the sizes in which grain was in fact scooped, carried, and poured. From accounts that relate grain quantities to field areas and seeding rates, scholars have estimated the base unit at roughly 25 litres and the standard daily barley ration of a dependent worker at roughly 0.8 litres — estimates, offered as such in the literature, but estimates anchored in the tablets' own internal arithmetic. Beside these three great systems stand further specialised notations: a system for area, in which fields are measured in units that will become the standard plots of Babylonian agriculture; a system for time, counting days, months of thirty days, and years in the calendar the administration ran on; and notations for weights and for grain in its processed forms. The plurality is the point. Archaic number was not a single ladder but a toolkit — each tool shaped to the material it measured, each preserving, in its bundling ratios, the shape of the containers and customs of the world it served.

Everything else about the script had still to be invented — and the next section shows the inventors at their second task: teaching it.

IX. The Sign Repertoire and the Lexical Lists — A School in Embryo

Beside the sixty or so numerical signs, proto-cuneiform deployed a repertoire of more than seven hundred non-numerical signs — the count depends on how variants and compound signs are reckoned, and published sign lists, from Green and Nissen's Zeichenliste (1987) onward, differ at the margins; the corpus as a whole attests on the order of 1,500 sign forms across its 6,000 tablets when compounds and rare forms are counted separately. Many signs are recognisable pictures: a head, a hand, a jar, a reed, the head of a bull or a sheep, an ear of grain, a plough, a boat. Many are not pictures of anything in nature — the cross-in-circle for sheep and goats, the reed-bundle emblem of Inana, geometric and emblematic forms drawn from the city's art and cult. And many are compounds: signs built by combining signs, or by inscribing one sign inside another — a vessel containing the sign for a substance is that substance in its vessel; the head combined with the ration- bowl sign gives the notion of eating, of a food disbursement. The repertoire is thus not a picture gallery but a system: productive, combinable, and already partly abstract in its first attested phase. That systematicity is the factual core of Glassner's counter-thesis (§V): whatever the script's debts to counters, its vocabulary presents itself to us as something designed, all at once, by people who understood what they were building.

A repertoire of seven hundred signs cannot be carried in one head by casual apprenticeship. It must be taught — and the teaching materials survive. Roughly a tenth to a sixth of the corpus (the proportion rises from under 1 per cent in Uruk IV to close to a fifth in Uruk III, on Englund's averages) consists not of accounts but of lexical lists: tablets that enumerate, in fixed order, the signs for the members of a category. The lists were published by Englund and Nissen as Die lexikalischen Listen der archaischen Texte aus Uruk (1993). The greatest of them, the Standard Professions List (the list scholars call Lu A), runs to some 120–140 entries: a ranked inventory of officials, priests, and professions, headed by the great officers of the city and descending through overseers, herdsmen, gardeners, and craftsmen — the social order of Uruk, written as a vocabulary. It is attested already on an Uruk IV tablet and in some 185 Uruk III copies and fragments, and it was still being copied, with little change, a thousand years later: the oldest school text in the world had become a classic. Around it stand the other archaic lists: lists of vessels and their contents, of domestic animals, of pigs, of fish, of birds, of trees and wooden objects, of plants, of metal objects, of places — the Archaic Cities list — each fixing the approved signs and their approved order for one department of the administered world.

The lists do three kinds of work at once, and all three matter to this article. Practically, they are the scribal school in embryo: the means by which the sign repertoire was standardised across offices and generations, so that a tablet written in one bureau could be audited in another. Intellectually, they are the first systematic classification of the world that survives anywhere — knowledge organised not by use but by kind, the professions ranked, the animals sorted, the vessels arrayed; Englund and Nissen themselves remark that among the lists we may have the earliest known example of something like literature, the moment the tool of accounting turns, in the schoolroom, into an instrument for ordering thought. Evidentially, they are the modern decipherer's lifeline: because the lists were copied for centuries into periods whose language we can read, their fixed sequences let scholars identify archaic signs whose values the accounts alone would never disclose. The accounts tell us what the script recorded. The lists tell us what its makers thought the world contained.

X. How an Account Was Made — The Procedure, Step by Step

The texts are the residue of a procedure, and the procedure can be reconstructed in unusual detail — from the tablets' own physical testimony (stylus grains and fingerprints are preserved in the clay), from the excavated contexts, and from the unbroken later tradition of Babylonian bookkeeping that descends from it. What follows is the making of an archaic account, step by step, at the level of the hands.

1. The Clay and the Tablet

The scribe began with clay — fine, well-levigated alluvial clay, kneaded free of grit and air, of the same material as the tokens and envelopes before it. He shaped it in his palms into a small, plump, pillow-shaped slab, its face smoothed for writing. Size followed function: a primary receipt might be a few centimetres across; a consolidated account, a slab of fifteen centimetres or more. The tablet was written while the clay was wet and plastic; it was not, in the ordinary course of business, fired. Finished accounts were dried — in the sun or by a hearth — hard enough to file and handle; their survival to our day is owed to the accidents of ancient fires and to the durability of dried clay in fill, not to any archival intention of baking.

2. The Stylus and the Two Techniques

The instrument was a reed stylus — a cut length of reed, its working end shaped to the task — and close study of the impressions can identify the styli themselves from the grain they leave in the clay (Englund). With it the scribe worked in the two techniques whose union §IV described. Numbers were impressed: the stylus held vertically or obliquely, its thick or thin end pressed into the clay, produced the cones and circles of the numerical systems — the direct mechanical descendants of pressing a token into an envelope. Word-signs were drawn: traced with the stylus's point in Uruk IV's curving pictographs, and increasingly, by Uruk III, assembled from short straight impressions of a stylus with a triangular section. The ruled cases came first or as the work proceeded: horizontal and vertical lines dividing the surface into the grid of entries, each case to hold one entry's number, commodity, and responsible party.

3. The Entry, the Total, the Seal

A primary document recorded a single transaction or a small group: so many animals, of such a kind, delivered by or to such an office or official, the entries disposed in their cases across the obverse. At intervals — the month's end, the delivery's completion, the auditor's visit — primaries were consolidated into secondary accounts: the entries gathered, the quantities summed in their proper numerical systems, and the totals written on the reverse, often with the grand total in a case of its own. The arithmetic was done in the systems of §VIII, and the tablets preserve its occasional corrections: erased and rewritten numbers, visible where the scribe smoothed the wet clay and began the case again. Authentication closed the act. The responsible official's cylinder seal was rolled across the tablet — over its face, its edges, sometimes across the writing itself — binding the account to his office as the seal on the envelope had bound the tokens. A sealed tablet, like a sealed envelope, could be challenged only by appeal to the office whose seal it bore.

4. Filing, Auditing, Discarding

Finished tablets were kept — in baskets, in rooms, in the bureau's working archive — for as long as the obligations they recorded remained live: a ration account until the month was audited, a livestock account until the flock was recounted. Then they were discarded, and it is the discarding that excavated them: thrown with the precinct's rubbish into the levelling fill of the next building phase at Eanna. The archaeology of the corpus is thus the mirror of its procedure. We possess the accounts of Uruk because Uruk's accountants threw them away, in thousands, into the foundations of the temples they served — a filing system whose final drawer was the ground itself.

// A RATION ACCOUNT, ANATOMISED //

Take the commonest transaction of the corpus: the issue of food. The obverse cases record, entry by entry, the disbursement — the grain product or beer in its sign, the quantity in the bisexagesimal ration notation (§VIII), the receiving office or work-gang in its title-sign. The sign for the transaction itself is a small picture with a long ancestry: a human head combined with the sign of the ration bowl — the mass-produced bevelled-rim bowl in which rations were actually served — yielding the notion "disbursement," "to eat." The reverse totals the issue. The seal of the issuing office closes the tablet. Nothing in the document is a sentence. Everything in it is an obligation, discharged and witnessed, in a form any trained official in the city could audit at sight.

XI. The Phonetic Turn — Rebus, Names, and Grammar, c. 2900–2600 BCE

Proto-cuneiform could record things and quantities superbly. What it could not do was record speech — and the pressure that forced the change was, in the first instance, a personnel problem. Accounts need names. The shepherd who received the flock, the official who authorised the issue, the woman who headed the weaving gang: a title identifies an office, but obligations attach to persons, and persons have names that no picture can draw. A name is a sound, not a thing. To write it, the script had to learn to write sounds — and the device by which it learned is the rebus principle: use a sign not for the thing it pictures but for the sound of the word that names that thing, and let that sound stand wherever the sound is needed, whatever the meaning.

The textbook examples are Sumerian, and they show the mechanism exactly. The sign picturing an arrow could write the word for "arrow," ti — and then, by rebus, the near-homophonous word for "life," til, which no picture could draw. The sign for "reed," gi, could write the verb "to reimburse, to return," also gi — an abstract obligation captured by borrowing a plant's name for its sound. A sign for "man," lu, and a sign for "mouth," ka, could be set side by side to spell a personal name out of its syllables, the pictures forgotten, the sounds alone at work. Sumerian, rich in short, monosyllabic words, was peculiarly suited to the device: a few hundred signs, each with a known sound-value, could spell anything the language could say. The first clear phonetic usages gather in the centuries after 2900 BCE — sporadic rebus writings and phonetically spelled personal names in the Early Dynastic I–II tablets from Ur and elsewhere — and by the age of the Fara tablets (c. 2600 BCE) the turn is complete: signs carry sound-values as a matter of course, phonetic complements are added to word- signs to fix their reading, grammatical elements — the little words and endings that pictures could never supply — begin to be written, and the sign order starts to follow the order of speech. Writing has become language on clay.

The consequences unfolded over the following centuries, and §XV will trace their slowness. But two immediate ones belong here. First, the system became portable across languages: a script that writes sounds can be adapted to any tongue, and cuneiform was duly adapted — to Akkadian above all, the Semitic language of Mesopotamia's north, whose scribes kept the Sumerian word-signs as logograms while spelling their own grammar phonetically around them. Second, the question of the first tablets' language — pressed by Englund — must be stated plainly, because it qualifies the whole story. Proto-cuneiform writes almost no phonetics and no grammar; its language is therefore not directly attested. Woods counts the positive evidence for Sumerian at some fifteen instances of phonetic writing, plus the continuity of the tradition into the mid-third millennium, when the language is certainly Sumerian; most scholars assume Sumerian on that basis. Englund states the contrary difficulty: there is no definitive evidence identifying the earliest scribes' language, and the many sign sequences that look like personal names ought, if the language were Sumerian, to be analysable as Sumerian names — and largely are not. The favourite candidate remains Sumerian; the candid verdict is that the invention's mother tongue is inferred, not read. It is a fitting uncertainty for a script that began by recording everything except speech.

XII. Egypt — Tomb U-j at Abydos and the First Hieroglyphs

Egypt's first writing was found in a tomb, and the tomb must be introduced before its labels. At Umm el-Qa'ab, the desert cemetery at Abydos in Upper Egypt where the kings of the First Dynasty would later be buried, the German Archaeological Institute's expedition under Günter Dreyer re-excavated, from 1988, the tombs of Cemetery U — the burial ground of the predynastic rulers who preceded them. Tomb U-j proved to be the cemetery's giant: a mud-brick tomb of twelve chambers, dated to the Naqada IIIA1 phase, c. 3250 BCE — a century or more before the unification of Egypt and the First Dynasty. An ivory sceptre among its goods marks its owner as a ruler, a king of the Abydos polity whose name the inscriptions do not preserve. The tomb had been plundered in antiquity, but its stores spoke: hundreds of pottery vessels, among them imported wine jars from the southern Levant — wine for a royal afterlife, traded across deserts — obsidian, ivory, and the earliest body of writing in Egypt. Dreyer published the tomb as Umm el-Qaab I (1998), and the publication more than doubled the corpus of Egyptian inscriptions from before the First Dynasty at a stroke.

The Labels — Bone, Ivory, and a Hole in the Corner

The writing survives in three media, each tied to a kind of container, and the division is itself a lesson in how the system worked. First, the labels: some 160 small tags of bone and ivory published by Dreyer — counts of the full tag material, fragments included, run higher in later summaries — typically a few centimetres across, each pierced with a small hole in one corner. The hole is the function: the label was tied — to a jar, a box, a bundle of cloth, a grave good — and it declared what its object was and where it had come from. The signs were incised with a fine point and the incisions rubbed dark. Most labels carry one to five signs; a large group — some forty-odd of the published labels — carry numerals alone, groupings of strokes and signs that Dreyer read as quantities, plausibly lengths of cloth, the royal store's most portable wealth. The remaining labels combine numerals with pictorial signs: an animal, a plant, a building, a landscape feature. Second, the ink inscriptions: some 125 pottery vessels bear signs painted or written in ink on their shoulders — larger, cursive versions of the same repertoire, marking the vessel's contents or origin directly on the jar. Third, the sealings: some 250 fragments of clay seal impressions, from the sealing of bags, boxes, and doors — Egypt, like Uruk, binding its stores with the rolled cylinder seal it had lately adopted.

What do the labels say? On Dreyer's reading — adopted in its essentials by much of the field, and refined by Ilona Regulski's palaeographic study (§XIII) — the pictorial labels name places: the estates, towns, and regions from which the tomb's goods were drawn. The readings turn on the rebus principle, already at work in Egypt's first attested writing. An elephant standing on rocky ground — elephant upon mountain — writes the place the Egyptians later called Abu, Elephantine, at the Nile's First Cataract: the picture is a puzzle whose solution is a sound. Dreyer identified, in the U-j material, the earliest attested two-consonant phonograms — signs standing for a pair of consonants abstracted from a word: the stork for the consonants bꜣ and a stool-like sign for št, combined in a writing he read as the Delta city of Bubastis. If those readings stand — and they are the excavator's, reported here at his attribution — then Egyptian writing was phonetic from its first surviving page: not a picture-code that later learned sound, but a system born already using the rebus. The goods of a king's tomb, labelled with the names of the places that sent them, from the cataract to the Delta: the labels are a map of a kingdom's reach, written before the kingdom of Egypt itself existed.

The setting deserves a final emphasis, because it differs from Uruk's in a way §XIII will weigh. The Uruk tablets are an administration's working rubbish, discarded in a temple precinct. The U-j labels are grave goods: laboriously incised in bone and ivory, attached to the stores of a king's burial. Egyptian writing, in its first attestation, already serves two masters — the storehouse it labels and the display it performs, the prestige of a court that could command the new technology for its dead. Woods draws the inference cautiously: writing at Abydos may have served display as well as administration from the start. The Egyptian tradition that follows — hieroglyphs carved on monuments for three thousand years, the subject of the Egyptology division's Scribal Arts — is legible in embryo in that double service.

XIII. Independence or Stimulus? — The Egyptian Question, Kept Open

Was Egyptian writing invented independently, or was it prompted — the idea of writing carried from Mesopotamia, the signs invented at home? The question is the oldest in the comparative study of scripts, and this hub's timeline entry for U-j states the discipline this article follows: the question "remains a live scholarly question; its signs, from the start, are its own." The positions, with their holders, are these.

The older orthodoxy was monogenesis. Ignace Gelb's A Study of Writing (1952), the foundational modern work on scripts, taught that writing was invented once, in Mesopotamia, and reached Egypt by stimulus diffusion — the concept travelling without the technique — at the beginning of the third millennium. On the chronology then accepted, Egyptian writing appeared centuries after cuneiform and the priority seemed plain. The U-j discovery destroyed the chronological premise: Egyptian writing is now attested at c. 3250 BCE, within the same band of uncertainty as Uruk IV itself. Dreyer himself argued the stronger conclusion: the U-j signs are, in form and system, wholly Egyptian; the system already deploys phonetic writing of a sophistication that implies development before U-j; and on his reading of the dating evidence Egyptian writing may be as old as Mesopotamia's or older. On this view Egypt is a second, independent invention — full stop.

The cautionary positions begin from contact, which is not in doubt. In the centuries before U-j, Egypt and the Uruk world were in documented communication: cylinder seals appear first in Mesopotamia and then in Egypt (c. 3500–3300 BCE); Mesopotamian artistic motifs and architectural forms — recessed niched brickwork among them — appear in late predynastic Egypt; lapis lazuli, a stone of distant Afghanistan, reached Egypt through the Near Eastern exchange web. A channel existed down which the bare knowledge that marks can record goods and names could have travelled in a generation. Scholars who hold the stimulus view — it remains a minority but a serious one — argue that the near-simultaneity of the two scripts, after millennia in which neither existed, is itself evidence: inventions of this rarity do not usually occur twice in adjacent, communicating regions within a century by chance. Against them, the independence party replies that simultaneity is exactly what parallel state formation predicts: two societies crossing the same administrative threshold at the same time, each driven to the same device by the same need — and that the Egyptian system's particulars (its sign forms, its materials, its phonetic structure, its funerary-display setting) show no Mesopotamian parentage in any single demonstrable trait.

Regulski's palaeography supplies the Egyptian evidence in its most disciplined form. Ilona Regulski's A Palaeographic Study of Early Writing in Egypt (2010) collected and analysed every attested early inscription, sign by sign, from U-j to the reign of Djoser (c. 2700 BCE) — the corpus now continued in her database of more than 4,500 Early Dynastic inscriptions. Its findings frame what can responsibly be said. The U-j system already shows many structural characteristics of later hieroglyphic writing — logograms, numerals, and the two-consonant phonograms of §XII — but it is a limited system beside the mature one: uniconsonantal (single- consonant) phonograms and phonetic complements are still absent. The following five centuries are a history of codification and reform: sign forms standardise, the repertoire is pruned and regularised, and the system that emerges in the Old Kingdom is the product of deliberate scribal housekeeping across the Early Dynastic period. Palaeography cannot settle the independence question — sign forms can prove Egyptian workmanship without proving Egyptian conception — but it fixes the fact both parties must explain: by c. 3250 BCE, Egypt possessed a working, partly phonetic script of entirely Egyptian appearance, in a royal tomb, labelling a kingdom's goods. Andréas Stauder's formulation, in the Visible Language volume, is the one this article adopts as its resting point: the Egyptian development was "roughly simultaneous, but largely different" from the Mesopotamian. Independent invention is the prevailing view in the current literature; stimulus diffusion cannot be excluded on present evidence; and the dating of both scripts is too coarse, by a century either way, for priority to be awarded. The question is kept open here because the evidence keeps it open.

// TWO SETS OF RADIOCARBON //

The chronological argument is sometimes presented as settled by radiocarbon. It is not. As the literature summarised in Regulski's study notes, two sets of radiocarbon determinations bear on the comparison: one places the Abydos material slightly later than the Uruk IV tablets (c. 3200 BCE), the other slightly earlier — and the calibrated date of c. 3320 BCE sometimes quoted for U-j has itself been questioned (Hendrickx 2006, as noted in the Visible Language volume). Late-fourth-millennium radiocarbon cannot separate events a few decades apart, and the tablets' secondary contexts (§VII) deny stratigraphy the deciding vote. Any account that awards the invention to one land by a confident margin of years is going beyond the evidence this tier prints.

XIV. Proto-Elamite — The Parallel Script That Died

The third script of the invention age is the control case that proves the experiment could fail. Proto- Elamite — the script of the Iranian plateau in the centuries c. 3100–2900 BCE, named for Elam, the later civilisation of southwestern Iran — arose at Susa in the immediate wake of the Uruk contact of §III. Susa had used envelopes and numerical tablets in the Uruk manner; as the Uruk expansion receded, its administrators took the final step on their own account, adding commodity and institutional signs to the numbers in a system parallel to Uruk III's — and then, crucially, diverging. The corpus numbers some 1,600–1,750 tablets, the great majority from Susa itself, with further tablets from sites across Iran — Anshan (Tal-e Malyan), Tepe Sialk, Tepe Yahya, and others — attesting a script in use across the plateau, in regions no single state of the age is known to have unified.

What can be read of proto-Elamite is its numbers, and the reading was the work of Friberg and of Damerow and Englund in the 1980s. The script operates several numerical systems in the Mesopotamian manner — sexagesimal for discrete objects, bisexagesimal for rations, a capacity system for grain — but adds one that is its own signature: a decimal system, used above all for counting persons and low-status personnel and certain animals, in which bundling runs in tens throughout. The same tablet can switch systems as it switches categories — a labour account counting its workers decimally and its rations bisexagesimally — so that, as in proto-cuneiform, the notation itself encodes the category of the thing counted. Totals on the tablets' reverses check against their entries: the arithmetic is recoverable, and with it the skeleton of every account — how much, of what numerical class, under what heading sign.

The rest is silence, and the silence is structured. Jacob L. Dahl of Oxford, the script's leading current student — who with Englund began the project of publishing the whole corpus in high-resolution images through the Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative — has catalogued over a thousand non-numerical signs, many occurring once or twice in the entire corpus. Some signs are shared with or adapted from proto-cuneiform — the cross-in-circle for sheep among them — but the great majority are Iran's own: more abstract, on the whole, than their Mesopotamian contemporaries, and including, Dahl notes, essentially no signs depicting body parts, a systematic avoidance he has suggested may reflect a taboo. A subset of perhaps a hundred signs, poorly standardised and occurring where owners' names should stand, may have served as syllabic signs for writing names — proto-Elamite groping, as Uruk had groped, toward phonetics. But the decisive absence is the one Dahl's comparison with Mesopotamia throws into relief: proto-Elamite has no lexical lists. No school tradition fixed its repertoire, generation to generation, in canonical order. Its signs remained the property of individual offices and households, varying from scribe to scribe; its language — presumed, on geographical grounds, to be an early form of Elamite, but nowhere demonstrated — cannot be recovered from accounts that never learned to write a sentence. There is no bilingual text. There is no descendant script to read it by. Around 2900 BCE the tablets cease; writing disappears from Iran for some five centuries, until scripts of Mesopotamian derivation (and the still-undeciphered Linear Elamite) appear in a later age. Proto-Elamite is thus the purest specimen of writing's first age: a complete administrative technology — numbers deciphered, accounts structurally transparent, word-signs mute — invented, spread across a plateau, and abandoned, all within two centuries. It shows that the invention, once made, was not self-sustaining. A script lives only where an institution teaches it.

XV. What Writing Was For — Accounts, Rations, Property, Labour

It remains to say, in one place and at full concreteness, what the first writing was for — because the answer corrects the instinct every modern reader brings to the word "writing." The archaic corpus, Mesopotamian and Elamite alike, divides into a small number of account types, and they exhaust it. Deliveries and receipts: goods entering the stores — grain from the estates, animals from the shepherds, oil, wool, fish, timber, stone — each entry a quantity, a commodity, and the person or office answerable for it. Disbursements: goods leaving the stores, above all the rations of §X — barley and grain products and beer issued to the dependent workforce against the calendar, in quantities the tablets let us estimate at roughly 0.8 litres of barley a day for an ordinary worker, scaled by status, sex, and age. Livestock accounts: flocks and herds entrusted to named herdsmen, their births, deaths, and yields of wool and milk audited against expectation — the cross-in-circle sign's home ground. Field accounts: plots measured in the area system, seed issued in the grain system at fixed seeding rates, yields expected and received — the tablet that lets scholars reconstruct the 25-litre measure records 150 units of barley to sow a field of ten bur, the arithmetic of an agriculture run from an office. Labour accounts: work-crews enumerated by category — men, women, children, each class with its sign — their days totalled in the time system, their rations computed against their days: the management, in Englund's sober analysis, of a dependent labour force whose upkeep and output the great households reckoned to the day and to the litre. And presiding over all of it, property: the seal on every tablet and envelope, declaring whose account it was and whose liability stood behind it. Beer deserves its sentence: it is everywhere in the corpus, counted in jugs under signs distinguishing — on Damerow's analysis — some nine types, from two basic barley ingredients; the first beverage humanity mass-produced is also among the first commodities it wrote down.

What is absent is as definite. There are no letters, no contracts in the later legal sense, no laws, no hymns, no prayers, no royal inscriptions, no histories, no literature. For roughly its first seven hundred years — Woods's figure — writing in Mesopotamia does one job. The turn, when it comes, comes in stages, and each stage can be dated by its first surviving examples. Phonetic writing (§XI) makes names and then grammar writable by c. 2600 BCE. The Fara and Abu Salabikh tablets of that age add the first literary texts — hymns, proverbs, and the Instructions of Shuruppak, wisdom addressed by a father to a son — copied, significantly, in the same scribal schools that copied the lists: literature enters writing through the schoolroom door. Royal inscriptions and votive texts follow in the later Early Dynastic period, as kings discover that the accountants' tool can also proclaim. Letters, the genre the Enmerkar legend imagines first, appear only toward the middle of the third millennium — nearly a millennium after the first tablet. The king lists that would one day reach back into the fabulous depths this hub examines in The Antediluvian King Lists are a still later genre, composed by a civilisation that had long since forgotten writing had ever been anything but natural. In Egypt the arc rhymes: labels and sealings first, royal names and annals next, continuous texts only toward the end of the third millennium's first centuries, and literature later still — the long apprenticeship traced, for the Egyptian hand, in The Scribal Arts. The lesson is uniform across both inventions. Writing was not born as humanity's voice. It was born as humanity's ledger — and it took the better part of a thousand years to learn to sing.

XVI. The Indus Signs — A Later Case, Beyond This Hub

One later case is noted here in a single paragraph, as a forward pointer only, because its full treatment belongs to the future India hub toward which this hub's Mehrgarh article builds its bridge: the Indus signs of the mature Harappan civilisation (c. 2600–1900 BCE) — some 400 principal signs in the standard counts, carved chiefly on small steatite seals, in inscriptions averaging about five signs and never exceeding a few dozen, undeciphered after a century of attempts, with no bilingual text and no agreed identification of the language behind them, and with even their status as language-recording writing debated in the published literature (Farmer, Sproat, and Witzel's non-linguistic thesis of 2004 and its many critics). The Indus case, seven centuries younger than Uruk's and arising in a civilisation in documented trade contact with Mesopotamia, poses the questions of §§I and XIII in their sharpest later form — what counts as writing, and what contact does and does not transmit — and it is left there, deliberately, for the hub that will own it.

XVII. What the Origins of Writing Do and Do Not Show

The evidence assembled in this article supports a definite set of conclusions, and they can be stated without inflation. Writing in the Near East grew out of accounting: the numerical component of the first script demonstrably descends, through envelopes and numerical tablets, from the token counters of the Neolithic, and the first corpus — some six thousand tablets — is, to within a tenth, a corpus of accounts. The invention happened at a particular scale of society: the great institutional households of the first cities, whose obligations in grain, livestock, land, and labour had outgrown memory, witness, and the sealed envelope. It happened, in its full form, at Uruk first, in the centuries around 3400–3200 BCE; it was paralleled, within the same broad moment, by an Egyptian invention at Abydos whose signs are wholly its own; and it was adapted, within two centuries, into an Iranian script that flourished and died. Its makers immediately built the institution that would preserve it — the lexical list, the school — and its completion, the phonetic turn that made writing record language itself, took a further three to five centuries of working use. These are excavated facts, and a Tier A article may rest on them.

What the evidence does not show is equally definite, and this article has tried to keep the negatives in the same light as the positives. It does not show that the whole of writing evolved from tokens: the word-signs, on the prevailing critical view of Englund, Zimansky, Woods, and Glassner, were invented with the script, and the token thesis survives in its numerical core, not in its total claim. It does not show which of the two great inventions came first, or whether the second was prompted by the first: the chronology is too coarse and the contacts too real for either certainty, and the honest position is the one the hub's timeline prints — a live question, signs demonstrably each land's own. It does not show that the first scribes wrote Sumerian: the language of proto- cuneiform is inferred from continuity, not read from phonetics, and Englund's doubt stands in the literature unanswered in kind. It does not show a script invented to glorify kings or gods: no king is named in the first corpus as its author, no god is hymned in it, and the royal and religious uses of writing are later acquisitions, centuries downstream of the ration account. And it does not show an invention that, once made, was safe: proto-Elamite's two centuries and extinction demonstrate that a script without a school is a script on loan from a single generation of offices.

Perhaps the deepest instruction of the record is the order in which the capacities arrive. First, quantity without things: the token, the number, older than farming's first cities by four thousand years. Then things without speech: the tablet, the account, the world sorted into cases and lists. Then, last and hardest, speech itself: the name that no picture can draw, forcing the sign to surrender its meaning and carry a sound. Humanity did not begin by writing what it said. It began by writing what it owed — and discovered, in the margin of the ledger, that the marks could be made to say anything. Everything since — the hymn, the law, the letter, the king list, this archive — is written in that margin.
