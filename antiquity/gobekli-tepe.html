I. A Tier A Site: What Is Attested, and What Is Not

This article is Tier A — Attested. Every substantive claim in it rests on excavated material, radiocarbon measurement, or peer-reviewed and project publication that the reader can check in the Sources section at the end. Where scholars disagree — and at Göbekli Tepe they disagree about a great deal — the disagreement itself is reported, with the positions named and attributed. Where a popular claim goes beyond the evidence, it is labelled as speculation in Section XX, plainly and without mockery. Nothing in this article is presented as a deciphered text, a recovered doctrine, or a lost teaching: the builders left no writing, and no one can quote them.

The attested core is extraordinary enough without embellishment. On a limestone ridge north-east of modern Şanlıurfa, in what archaeologists call Upper Mesopotamia, communities who lived by hunting and gathering built large circular enclosures ringed with monolithic T-shaped pillars, many carved in low relief with wild animals. The earliest enclosures belong to the Pre-Pottery Neolithic A, conventionally placed around c. 9500–9000 BCE in the scheme used throughout this hub, with occupation continuing, in smaller and more rectangular forms, to around c. 8000 BCE. That is roughly six thousand years before the unification of Egypt and more than six millennia before the Great Pyramid.

Three cautions belong at the front, because later sections return to them repeatedly. First, temple is an interpretation, not a find. The excavators themselves now generally prefer the neutral term special buildings for the great enclosures, and recent work has recovered domestic buildings alongside them. Second, the familiar three-layer chronology of the site's first director, Klaus Schmidt, has been revised: the round enclosures and the rectangular buildings overlap in time more than the original scheme allowed. Third, the famous claim that the enclosures were deliberately and ritually buried — a claim Schmidt's team itself made — is now questioned by his successors, who read much of the fill as the product of slope slides. A Tier A article does not hide those revisions. It treats them as the science working as it should.

ATTESTED

Monumental T-pillar enclosures built in the 10th–9th millennia BCE by people with no domesticated plants or animals; extensive wild-game and wild-cereal processing; a regional family of related sites; inscription on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2018.

DEBATED AMONG SPECIALISTS

Whether the enclosures were temples, communal houses, or buildings that were both at once; whether people lived on the hill year-round; how the enclosures filled up; and whether monument-building helped cause, resisted, or merely accompanied domestication.

SPECULATION, LABELLED AS SUCH

Readings of the carvings as a star map, a comet record, or a 365-day calendar; claims of precise stellar alignments as the enclosures' purpose; and appropriations of the site as evidence for a lost advanced civilisation. See Section XX for each claim and the published responses to it.

NOT KNOWN

What the builders called the place, what they believed, what any single carving “means”, and what was said or done inside an enclosure. The iconography plainly carried meaning for its makers; without texts, its specific content is beyond recovery, and honest scholarship says so.

II. The Hill and Its Landscape

Göbekli Tepe — the name is Turkish for “potbelly hill” — is an artificial mound, a tell, rising about 15 metres above a flat limestone plateau in the Germuş mountain range, some 15 kilometres north-east of Şanlıurfa and about 90 kilometres east of the Euphrates. The mound measures roughly 300 metres across and covers about 8–9 hectares. From its top the Harran plain opens to the south; on a clear day the view reaches toward the Syrian border. The plateau itself supplied both of the builders' essential raw materials on the spot: soft limestone bedrock for pillars and walls, and flint for the tools that worked it.

The world of 9500 BCE was not the dry steppe of today. At the end of the last Ice Age and in the early Holocene the region was a more open, better-watered grassland. Pollen, charcoal, and faunal evidence assembled by the project indicate a landscape of wild cereals — wild einkorn, emmer, and barley among them — with pistachio and almond the dominant trees in the charcoal record, and grazing herds of gazelle, wild sheep and goat, aurochs, and equids on the plains below. That abundance matters for everything that follows: Göbekli Tepe was not built in a marginal desert by desperate people. It was built at a rich ecotone, where plant foods could be gathered in quantity and game — above all the seasonally migrating goitered gazelle — could be hunted at scale.

Water was the site's one obvious problem, and for two decades it was used as an argument that nobody can have lived there: there is no spring on the hill. Work reported by Lee Clare and colleagues for 2015–2019 changed that picture. Channels cut in the bedrock, covered in places with limestone slabs, lead toward large rock-cut cisterns; one partially excavated pit in the north-west of the mound measures some 8 metres across and 2.8 metres deep. The project now reads the plateau as a rainwater-harvesting system capable of supporting occupation. The absence-of-water argument against settlement has not survived its own excavation.

// READING THE MOUND //

A tell is not a natural hill. It is the accumulated debris of occupation itself — collapsed walls, discarded tools, ash, bone, and fill, layer upon layer. Göbekli Tepe's mound is therefore part of the evidence: the hill the builders' successors stood on was, in large part, made by the building.

The site also sits inside a named geography that the Sources use consistently. Upper Mesopotamia here means the upland arc between the middle and upper Euphrates and Tigris and the Taurus foothills — modern south-eastern Turkey, northern Syria, and northern Iraq. In the literature on early farming this same arc, where the wild ancestors of the founder crops overlap, has been called the “Golden Triangle” and, by Lev-Yadun and colleagues, a core area — sometimes rendered “cradle” — of agriculture. Göbekli Tepe stands near its centre, within reach of the Karacadağ mountains, where genetic work beginning with Heun and colleagues in 1997 located the wild einkorn population most closely related to domesticated einkorn. Monument and wheat share a landscape. Whether they share a causal story is the subject of Section XVII.

III. Discovery: From a Survey Note to a Research Project

Göbekli Tepe was not lost, exactly. It was recorded, filed, and misunderstood. In 1963 a joint survey by Istanbul University and the University of Chicago visited the hill; Peter Benedict's report described mounds of reddish soil, recognised Neolithic flint on the surface, and took the worked limestone slabs for parts of a much later cemetery. The significance of the flint carpet and the slabs was not grasped, and the site rested in the literature for thirty years. Popular retellings sometimes sharpen this into a story of blindness; the record is duller and more instructive. A survey sees surfaces, and on the surface the tops of T-pillars look like grave markers. What changed the reading was not a better guess but a comparison — with a site that had just been dug.

That comparison came from Nevalı Çori, a Pre-Pottery Neolithic B settlement excavated under Harald Hauptmann between 1983 and 1991 in advance of the Atatürk Dam, which flooded it in 1991–92. Nevalı Çori produced a rectangular communal building unlike its houses: benches around the walls, monumental sculpture, and T-shaped pillars — including a central pair — set into its fabric. A member of that team was Klaus Schmidt (1953–2014). When Schmidt set out in 1994 to survey the Urfa region for further early sites, he carried Nevalı Çori's plan in his head. Guided to the flint-covered hill, he recognised a worked stone in a field-clearance heap as the head of a T-pillar of the Nevalı Çori type. Schmidt's own published account of that October 1994 visit, reproduced in the DAI team's Expedition article listed in the Sources, describes the moment the bare limestone plateau began, in his word, to glitter with worked flint.

Excavation began in 1995 as a joint project of the Şanlıurfa Museum and the German Archaeological Institute (DAI), directed by Schmidt. The first test trench, at the base of the south-eastern slope, struck rectangular buildings of what Schmidt would call Layer II. In the same season a landowner clearing his field uncovered the heads of two huge T-pillars and had begun to break one up with a sledgehammer before he was persuaded to stop; work in that south-eastern depression from 1996 onward revealed the first of the great circular enclosures, and with them the site's fame. Schmidt directed the excavation for twenty seasons, published the synthesis Sie bauten die ersten Tempel (2006; English edition, Göbekli Tepe: A Stone Age Sanctuary in South-Eastern Anatolia, 2012), and died suddenly in July 2014.

Work did not stop with him. The project continued under the Şanlıurfa Museum and the DAI, with Lee Clare of the DAI's Istanbul Department coordinating research, and with Turkish prehistorian Necmi Karul of Istanbul University taking a leading role in the region's archaeology — including, from 2019, the excavation of Karahan Tepe and, from 2021, the coordination of the Taş Tepeler programme (Section XVIII). Two large protective shelters now cover the main excavation areas, and the deep soundings dug for their supports in 2015–2019 produced, incidentally and importantly, some of the evidence that has revised Schmidt's own interpretation. Less than a tenth of the mound has been excavated; geophysical survey indicates at least twenty large enclosures in total, most still underground.

IV. Chronology: Layers, Radiocarbon, and Revision

Schmidt divided the mound into three layers. Layer III, the oldest, contained the great round enclosures and was assigned to the Pre-Pottery Neolithic A (PPNA). Layer II contained smaller rectangular buildings with much reduced T-pillars and was assigned to the early and middle Pre-Pottery Neolithic B (PPNB). Layer I was simply everything above — the plough horizon and later traces. In round numbers, and in the form this hub uses, Layer III belongs to c. 9500–9000 BCE and Layer II runs on to the site's abandonment around c. 8000 BCE. Those dates remain a fair summary of the span. What has changed is how sharply the layers are separated, and how the dates were obtained.

Dating a stone building is indirect work. Radiocarbon measures organic material — charcoal, bone, the organic fraction in mud mortar and wall plaster — not the limestone itself, so every date constrains a context: a fill, a floor make-up, a mortar, a repair. Göbekli Tepe's first dates, published from 1998, came from enclosure fill and clustered in the late 10th and early 9th millennia, younger than a PPNA attribution predicted. Schmidt's team explained the discrepancy through their backfilling theory: the fill, they proposed, was material brought in at abandonment and so post-dated use. Dates on organic material in wall plaster seemed to restore the older chronology. Both props have since been kicked away by the project's own later work: the fill is no longer read as imported (Section XVI), and plaster dates are recognised as vulnerable to the “old wood effect”, in which long-lived or reused timber makes a sample older than its context.

The current position, set out in Clare's 2020 summary of the 2015–2019 research and in the building-archaeology programme of Kinzel and Clare, rests on AMS dates from the mud mortar of the special buildings themselves. Those dates are more homogeneous than the fill dates and they tell a longer story: enclosures A–D were multi-phase buildings, maintained, remodelled, and repaired over centuries, with some final building phases around the middle of the 9th millennium BCE — more than five hundred years after their foundations. On Clare's calibration, the round-oval special buildings (Schmidt's Layer III) span roughly 9500–8700 BCE and the rectangular buildings (Layer II) roughly 8700–8200 BCE, with a clear overlap between them, and the site as a whole is now commonly cited as c. 9500–8000 BCE. Schmidt's neat three-part sequence has been abandoned by the project in favour of a finer, multi-phase history — eight phases in the most recent published synthesis. The enclosures were not a single generation's gesture. They were institutions with biographies.

// A NOTE ON DATES IN THE POPULAR PRESS //

Headline ages for Göbekli Tepe vary — 11,000, 11,600, or 12,000 years — mostly because writers convert differently from BCE or round from different phases. The disagreements that matter are smaller and more specific: which context a sample came from, and whether it dates a building's construction, use, repair, or filling. Any claim that hangs a precise year on a single pillar — including the astronomical claims in Section XX — should be asked which dated context it rests on. Usually, it rests on none.

V. The Pre-Pottery Neolithic Framework

The Pre-Pottery Neolithic (PPN) is the early Neolithic of the Levant and Upper Mesopotamia before the adoption of ceramic vessels: a world of stone, bone, wood, basketry, plaster, and ground-stone technology. Kathleen Kenyon coined the subdivisions PPNA and PPNB from her excavations at Jericho, and the terms stuck for the whole region. In the calibrations used by the Göbekli Tepe project, the PPNA runs approximately 9600–8800 BCE and the PPNB approximately 8800–7000 BCE, with the early PPNB (EPPNB) covering roughly 8700–8200 BCE. Different textbooks shift these boundaries by a century or two; the sequence, not the exact hinge years, is what carries weight.

Across those two millennia and more, the PPN saw, in uneven and locally variable order: the first permanent and semi-permanent villages of round, then increasingly rectangular, houses; the intensive management of wild cereals and pulses; the appearance of morphologically domesticated founder crops — einkorn and emmer wheat, barley, lentil, pea, chickpea, bitter vetch, flax; the domestication of goat, sheep, pig, and cattle, mostly a PPNB story; large communal buildings at many sites; plastered human skulls and other elaborate treatments of the dead; and long-distance movement of valued materials such as Anatolian obsidian. The older textbook image of a single “Neolithic Revolution” — V. Gordon Childe's coinage of 1936 — has given way to a long transition in which different elements arrive at different times in different valleys. Göbekli Tepe's importance inside that framework is precise: its monumental phase falls at the beginning of the sequence, when villages were new, farming did not yet exist, and pottery, metal, and writing were all millennia in the future.

Rectangular, often two-storey houses; domesticated crops widespread and herded animals appearing; large planned villages such as Çayönü and 'Ain Ghazal; plastered skulls; Göbekli Tepe's later, smaller buildings belong to its first half.

AFTER THE PPN

Pottery Neolithic communities spread farming lifeways across Anatolia and into Europe and deepen them in Mesopotamia. Cities, states, and writing are still three to five thousand years away — a gap this hub's Egyptian articles take up from the other end.

One more framework term recurs in the literature and is worth defining once, carefully: the excavators' phrase special buildings. Across the PPN, some buildings differ from houses in scale, in fittings — benches, pillars, terrazzo or plaster floors, niches — in the objects deposited in them, or in their position within a settlement. Archaeologists call these special or communal buildings and infer gatherings, ceremonies, and collective work from them. The term is deliberately non-committal about gods, priests, or doctrine. Göbekli Tepe's enclosures are the largest and earliest famous examples of the class, but they are members of a class — a fact that matters when the site is treated, in popular writing, as if it had no relatives.

VI. Architecture: The Great Enclosures

A Layer III enclosure is a ring, not a roofed hall in any simple sense. A wall of rough, unworked stone set in clay mortar, roughly circular or oval and between about 10 and 30 metres across, encloses a floor that is often the smoothed bedrock itself, sometimes laid with a terrazzo-like burnt-lime surface. Into the inner face of the ring wall stand T-shaped pillars, up to about 4 metres high in the ring, their broad faces turned inward. A low stone bench commonly runs along the wall between them. At the centre stand two much larger T-pillars, parallel to one another, set into pedestals cut from the bedrock — in Enclosure D the pedestals are only about 20 centimetres deep, a socket that has prompted much discussion of how the pillars were stabilised, braced, and possibly roofed.

Whether the enclosures carried roofs is genuinely unresolved. Schmidt generally envisaged open or partly covered structures; Banning argued in 2011, from the span, the pillars' proportions, and ethnographic parallels, that roofing was feasible and likely; the DAI team's building archaeologists now discuss partial roofing, collapse, and repeated repair as parts of the buildings' long lives. The honest summary is that the ground plan is certain and the elevation is reconstruction. Drawings that show a single confident roofline are showing a hypothesis.

The enclosures were also not built once and frozen. Building archaeology — the close reading of wall junctions, inserted pillars, blocked passages, added benches, and repaired damage — shows walls rebuilt inside earlier walls, ring pillars replaced or supplemented, floors renewed, and, in Enclosure C, a sequence of concentric rings. Enclosure C's central pillars were smashed in antiquity; their fragments, found in a pit dug for the purpose, were laser-scanned and virtually reassembled by the project to an original height of about 5 metres. Damage, repair, and remodelling are part of the record, and they are one reason the project's current language speaks of building biographies rather than a single construction event.

Layer II: The Rectangular Turn

The Layer II buildings are smaller, rectangular, and far less monumental: rooms and small complexes in which T-pillars, where present at all, stand under 2 metres and may number only a central pair — the so-called Lion Pillar Building, named for its pair of pillars carved with rearing lions, is the best known — or none. Under Schmidt's scheme this was a later, diminished phase. Under the revised chronology the rectangular form overlaps the enclosures' later lives and also belongs to the site's domestic architecture, which raises a possibility the older literature underplayed: the difference between Layer III and Layer II may be as much functional and social as chronological — great communal buildings and houses changing together — as it is a simple story of decline. The temptation to read shrinking pillars as fading faith should be resisted; it imports a narrative the stones do not supply.

VII. Enclosures A–D, and Beyond

The excavated enclosures are lettered in order of discovery. Four dominate the literature, and each has a distinct character — a fact worth dwelling on, because it suggests the enclosures were not copies of a single template but individual buildings, perhaps belonging to different groups, occasions, or generations.

ENCLOSURE A

Among the first excavated in the south-eastern depression. Its pillars carry snakes prominently, including a net-like relief of serpents on one pillar, and its plan includes a distinctive entrance arrangement. A is a reminder that each enclosure's imagery leans toward particular animals — a pattern whose meaning is debated but whose existence is not.

ENCLOSURE B

A smaller ring whose pillars are dominated by foxes. Its central area produced a floor of terrazzo-like material, and bone samples from B have figured in the site's radiocarbon discussions. B's modest scale, beside D's monumentality, is part of the evidence that the enclosures were built to different briefs.

ENCLOSURE C

The boar enclosure: wild boars dominate its sculpture, including free-standing boar statues and, on Pillar 27, a predator carved in high relief descending the shaft, cut from the same stone as the pillar itself. Its concentric rings and its smashed-and-reassembled central pillars make C the key building for the site's biography approach.

ENCLOSURE D

The largest and best preserved. Two central pillars (18 and 31), about 5.5 metres high and around 8–10 tonnes each, stand in bedrock pedestals inside a ring of eleven excavated wall pillars. Foxes, snakes, cranes and other birds, boar, aurochs, gazelle, and more crowd its reliefs — including Pillar 43, the so-called Vulture Stone, discussed in Sections IX and XX.

Beyond A–D, enclosures E to H and further structures have been identified in the western and north-western parts of the mound. Enclosure E, the so-called rock temple, was largely cleared of its fittings in antiquity and survives as a bedrock-cut platform with pillar pedestals and a relief frieze; Enclosure H, excavated more recently, has added further large pillars and sculpture to the corpus. Geophysics — principally geomagnetic survey and ground-penetrating radar — indicates at least twenty enclosures across the mound. The excavated sample on which all generalisations rest is therefore small, selected by erosion, land use, and the accident of where trenches were opened. Every confident sentence about “Göbekli Tepe” in general should be read with that sampling warning attached.

Two associated finds widen the architectural picture. First, the quarries: on the plateau beside the mound lie unfinished T-pillars still attached to the bedrock, the largest about 7 metres long and estimated at some 50 tonnes — abandoned workpieces that show both the method (a pillar was cut free as a single monolith) and the ambition (the largest pillar ever attempted at the site was never moved). Second, sculpture in the round: statues, animal figures, and a composite “totem pole” recovered in 2009, carved with stacked figures and found in a Layer II context, show that the pillars were one element of a broader sculptural practice, not the whole of it.

VIII. The T-Pillars Are Figures

The T-shape is an abstraction, and the site itself tells us of what. On the central pillars of Enclosure D the narrow side of the shaft carries, in low relief, two arms bent forward, long-fingered hands meeting above the belly, a decorated belt, and a loincloth formed like an animal skin hanging below it. Read with those details, the pillar resolves: the broad T-head is a head in profile — its narrow edge the face, featureless by design — the shaft is a body, and the pillar is not decorated with a person. It is a person, rendered at monumental scale in the most economical possible geometry. The DAI team's synthesis makes this reading explicitly, and the belted, loinclothed central pair of Enclosure D is its standard exhibit.

The excavators have further noted the strongly masculine cast of the imagery: where the sex of a depicted animal can be determined it is male, and comparison with belted male figurines from Nevalı Çori led Schmidt's team to read the Enclosure D pair as male figures, while acknowledging that the loincloths cover the evidence and certainty is impossible. That observation is worth keeping in its exact, limited form. It describes a pattern in the carvings; it does not recover a theology of masculinity, and the literature's more expansive claims in that direction go beyond the reliefs.

Two consequences follow from the anthropomorphic reading. The first is architectural: an enclosure is a gathering of stone persons around a central pair — a ring of presences facing inward — whatever else it also was. The second is comparative, and it has recently sharpened. At Karahan Tepe in 2025 the excavators announced a T-pillar bearing a carved human face — brows, eyes, nose — the first of its kind reported from the Taş Tepeler, found, notably, in a building with domestic equipment. If the T-pillars are stylised human figures, a faced example is the missing explicit instance. The announcement is recent and its full publication is awaited, so this article records it as reported by the excavation director rather than as a settled datum — but it points the same way as the arms, hands, belts, and loincloths have pointed since Enclosure D was cleared.

How a Pillar Was Made

A T-pillar began in the plateau quarries as a single block outlined in the bedrock, freed with flint tools, and shaped where it lay or after a short move; the unfinished 7-metre example shows the process interrupted. Finished pillars were transported — distances on site are short, but a 10-tonne monolith is a 10-tonne monolith — erected into wall or pedestal, and carved, at least in part, in place. No metal, no wheels, no draught animals, and no pulleys are attested anywhere in this world. Flint picks and hammerstones, levers, ropes of plant fibre, timber, water, abrasion, and organised human muscle are the entire available toolkit, and experimental and ethnographic reasoning about megalith transport elsewhere in prehistory shows that toolkit to be sufficient. The achievement is organisational, not technological — which is precisely why it is historically important.

IX. Iconography: A World of Wild Animals

The reliefs cover the broad faces and shafts of the pillars, usually in flat, carefully modelled low relief, occasionally in high relief or in the round. The repertoire is overwhelmingly animal and overwhelmingly wild: foxes, snakes in profusion, scorpions, spiders, wild boar, aurochs, gazelle, wild ass, cranes, storks, ibis, ducks and other birds, vultures, and large predators. Domesticated animals are absent because domesticated animals, in this place and century, did not yet exist. Alongside the fauna stand abstract signs — H-shaped and crescent-and-disc motifs among them — and, rarely, human figures, most famously the headless man on Pillar 43.

Schmidt's team observed early that the animal emphasis differs by enclosure — boars in C, foxes prominent in B and D, snakes in A — and proposed that the enclosures may have belonged to different groups, each with its own emblematic animal, or that the imagery encodes narratives now unrecoverable. Both proposals remain proposals. What the reliefs establish beyond reasonable dispute is narrower and still remarkable: a shared, disciplined visual language, executed by skilled carvers to a consistent standard across multiple buildings and generations, depicting the dangerous and powerful wildlife of the builders' own landscape. Snakes, scorpions, predators, and carrion birds are not a random bestiary; the repertoire leans toward creatures that command caution and respect. Dietrich and Notroff's work on the imagery treats it as a communicative system embedded in the architecture — the pillars do not merely stand in the building, they populate it.

Pillar 43, the “Vulture Stone”

Pillar 43, a ring pillar in Enclosure D, carries the site's most discussed composition. Described neutrally, its broad face shows, in registers: rows of nested V-shaped marks along the top; three bag- or basket-like forms, each surmounted by an animal; a large vulture with one wing raised toward a disc or sphere; a scorpion; further birds and animals; and, at the lower right, a headless human figure. A separate head appears nearby in the composition. Every element in that description is visible on the stone. Every further statement — that the animals are constellations, that the disc is the sun at a particular solstice, that the scene dates a comet — is interpretation, and contested interpretation at that. The pillar gets its own full treatment in Section XX, because the gap between the carving and the claims made for it is the clearest case study this hub has in how Tier A evidence and modern construction come apart.

// HOW TO READ A RELIEF CLAIM //

Ask three questions of any proposed reading of Göbekli Tepe's imagery. Does it account for the whole corpus — more than sixty excavated pillars — or only for one photogenic example? Does it assume the builders saw the same constellations, symbols, or calendar as a much later culture, and if so, on what evidence? And could the same carvings be “read” to a different conclusion by the same method? The excavators' published response to the astronomical readings (Notroff, Dietrich, and colleagues, 2017) presses exactly these points.

X. The Builders: Hunter-Gatherers, Not Farmers

The single most consequential fact about Göbekli Tepe is a negative: in decades of excavation, no domesticated plant and no domesticated animal has been identified from its Neolithic occupation. The archaeobotanical record, though sparse in carbonised remains, yields wild einkorn, wild barley, and possibly wild wheat or rye, with almond and pistachio. The faunal record, by contrast, is enormous. Joris Peters and colleagues' archaeozoological work on more than a hundred thousand bone fragments shows a diet and a debris dominated by wild game: goitered gazelle above all — over 60 per cent in the assemblages Schmidt's team reported — with aurochs, wild boar, wild sheep and goat, red deer, hare, fox, and birds. Seasonal indicators point to large-scale gazelle hunting between midsummer and autumn, consistent with intercepting the herds' migrations past the hill.

The builders were therefore hunter-gatherers — but the phrase misleads if it conjures small, fleeting bands. The PPN world of Upper Mesopotamia was already experimenting with permanence: semi-sedentary and sedentary hunter-gatherer villages precede and accompany Göbekli Tepe across the region, and the old opposition between mobile foragers and settled farmers has dissolved in the evidence. People could live in one place for much of the year, store wild grain, manage stands of wild cereals, build in stone, and still plant no domesticated crop and herd no domesticated animal. Göbekli Tepe's builders stood inside that in-between world, and the site's later fame rests on the demonstration that monumentality belongs to it.

Did they live on the hill itself? Schmidt's answer was no: the early seasons found no houses, hearths, or middens where he expected them, and the site was interpreted as a sanctuary visited periodically by groups from a wide catchment. That answer has been reversed by the project's own later work. The 2015–2019 deep soundings found agglutinated round-oval houses with hearth sequences and bead-working debris in the north-west, further round houses on the western slope, and, for the early PPNB, dense rectangular domestic building — in one case with a sub-floor burial, a hallmark of PPN domestic sites. Lithic assemblages are, in Clare's summary, wholly characteristic of domestic contexts. The current picture is of a settlement that contained special buildings — or, in Banning's sharper formulation discussed in Section XVII, of very large, symbolically charged houses within a settlement. Either way, “a temple with nobody home” is no longer a description the excavation team itself defends.

XI. The Dog: The First Domestication

One domestication does not belong to the PPNB story told in Section V, and it changes the meaning of the negative fact at the heart of Section X. The dog was domesticated in the Pleistocene, by hunter-gatherers, thousands of years before anyone farmed — the first domestic animal of any kind, and the only one domesticated before settled agriculture existed. Göbekli Tepe's builders therefore lived after the first domestication, not before all of them. Yet no domestic dog has been identified at Göbekli Tepe itself: the archaeozoological assemblage is entirely wild, and the canids in the reliefs and the bone record are foxes and other wild animals. The dog belongs here as the deep background of the builders' world: proof that domestication, in its first instance, was not about food production at all.

Bonn-Oberkassel: A Puppy That Was Nursed

The oldest known joint burial of humans and a dog comes not from the Near East but from Germany. In 1914 quarry workers at Oberkassel, now a suburb of Bonn, uncovered a double grave containing a man in his forties, a woman in her twenties, decorated objects of antler, bone, and teeth — and a dog. The burial is dated to about 14,200 years ago (14,223 ± 58 BP in the re-study's reporting). For a century the dog was a footnote to the human skeletons. A re-examination by Luc Janssens and colleagues, published in the Journal of Archaeological Science in 2018, made it the most closely read dog in Palaeolithic archaeology.

The re-study found, first, that the grave held not one dog but two: a mature animal represented by a single upper molar, and a juvenile whose skull and postcranial skeleton survived. The juvenile died at about 27–28 weeks old. Its teeth carry the case. Lines of enamel hypoplasia — growth disruptions laid down in the enamel while the teeth were forming — fall at about 19, 21, and 23 weeks, recording two or three separate episodes of severe illness in the puppy's final two months, alongside severe periodontal disease. The pattern is consistent, in the authors' assessment, with infection by the morbillivirus that causes canine distemper. That diagnosis is stated here at its proper strength: it is a veterinary reading of dental pathology, not a recovered pathogen — no viral material survives, and the authors present distemper as the likely cause, not a demonstrated one.

The inference that follows is the famous one, and it too has grades. Canine distemper in an unvaccinated puppy runs a course of about three weeks with very high mortality. This puppy survived roughly eight weeks beyond its first recorded illness episode, through repeated bouts. Janssens and colleagues argue that survival on that timescale would have been unlikely without intensive human care: the animal kept warm and clean through bouts of diarrhoea and vomiting, given water and probably food, over a period of weeks. During that time the puppy — too young and too sick to have been trained — can have had no utilitarian value, and the authors propose that the care reflects an emotional bond rather than an investment. The bones attest the illness and the survival; the nursing is a strong inference from the two together; the affection is a plausible reading of the nursing, not itself a find. Other specialists have entered the obvious caution, that a juvenile nursed through illness might also have been valued for the working dog it could still become. Even on that cooler reading, the grave stands: at 14,200 BP, people buried their dead alongside a dog they had, on the best available evidence, kept alive through a long sickness.

From Wolf to Dog: Ancestry and Pathways

All dogs descend from the grey wolf — but not from any wolf population alive today. The ancient-genome work discussed below shows that dogs share a common ancestry distinct from present-day wolves, and the specific wolf population that gave rise to them appears to be extinct. When the split happened is estimated, not dated by a find: one influential reconstruction (Perri and colleagues, 2021), correlating the population histories of people and dogs across Siberia, Beringia, and the Americas, proposes domestication in Siberia by about 23,000 years ago, while people and wolves were both isolated by the Last Glacial Maximum. That is a hypothesis built from genetic correlation, and it is labelled as such by its authors; the archaeological record of unambiguous dogs, Bonn-Oberkassel among them, begins later.

How wolves became dogs is contested between two broad mechanisms. The commensal pathway — the classic model, associated with Raymond and Lorna Coppinger and formalised in Melinda Zeder's pathways framework — requires no human intention at the start: the least wary wolves were drawn to scavenge the refuse of human encampments, selection favoured animals that could tolerate people at close range, and a camp-following population gradually diverged from its wild relatives — in effect, wolves domesticated themselves in a human-made niche, and people later took up the partnership that scavenging had begun. The rival human-initiated model gives people the first move: hunter-gatherers took pre-weaned wolf pups from dens, hand-reared them in camp as companions, and bred — deliberately or by tolerance — from the most docile. Its proponents, Mietje Germonpré and colleagues among them, object that mobile Palaeolithic foragers are unlikely to have produced refuse on the scale a self-sustaining scavenging wolf population would need. The two are not mutually exclusive — pups taken from already camp-tolerant wolves would combine them — and the literature has not settled the matter.

One Origin or Two?

The sharpest form of the origins debate is numerical. In 2016, Laurent Frantz and colleagues reported in Science genetic sequences from 59 ancient dogs and a complete genome, at 28-fold coverage, of a roughly 4,800-year-old dog from the Neolithic passage tomb at Newgrange in Ireland. Their analyses revealed a deep split between modern East Asian and Western Eurasian dogs, dated to roughly 14,000–6,400 years ago — at or after the first appearance of dogs at both ends of Eurasia — together with a sharp discontinuity in mitochondrial haplotype frequencies in Europe. They proposed a dual origin: dogs domesticated independently in eastern and western Eurasia from distinct wolf populations, with eastern dogs later carried west with people and partly replacing the European Palaeolithic population.

Four years later the ancient-genome sample grew, and the picture shifted. Anders Bergström and colleagues sequenced 27 ancient dog genomes, up to 10,900 years old, from Europe, the Near East, and Siberia (Science, 2020). They found that all dogs share a common ancestry distinct from present-day wolves, with limited gene flow from wolves into dogs since domestication but substantial gene flow the other way; and that by 11,000 years ago at least five major ancestry lineages had already diversified — a deep Palaeolithic history, compressed into the millennia around the end of the Ice Age. Their summary is precise: the data show no evidence for multiple origins, but an early diversification from shared ancestry. That does not logically extinguish Frantz's second origin — a domestication from a wolf population that left no surviving descendants would be invisible to it — but it removes the need for one. The honest verdict, which is also where Bergström and colleagues begin their paper, is that there is still no consensus on when, where, or how many times dogs were domesticated. Unresolved is the finding, and this article reports it as one.

Dogs in the Builders' World

Whatever happened in the Pleistocene, dogs were present in the Levant before and during the PPNA. At the Natufian settlement of ‘Ain Mallaha (Eynan) in the Hula Valley, Simon Davis and François Valla reported in Nature in 1978 the burial of an elderly human with a puppy of four to five months, the human's hand resting on the small body — among the earliest archaeological evidence for the dog in the region, in a context of about 12,000 years ago. At Hayonim Terrace, humans were buried with two canids. These are the Natufian roots of the partnership: dogs inside settlements, and inside graves, before farming.

The PPNA evidence shows what the partnership was for. At Shubayqa 6, a PPNA settlement in the Black Desert of north-east Jordan occupied around 11,500 years ago, Lisa Yeomans, Tobias Richter, and Louise Martin found the direct bones of dogs to be rare — an articulated forelimb in a midden is the prize specimen — but found their indirect trace everywhere: a large proportion of the animal-bone assemblage bears the unmistakable marks of having passed through a carnivore's digestive tract, on fragments too large to have been swallowed by a human. The dogs, they conclude, lived inside the settlement year-round, roaming freely, feeding on discarded bone, and defecating among the houses. And at the moment dogs appear in the record, the prey changes: hares and other small, fast game rise sharply. The team's explanation is a new hunting method — dogs coursing or driving small prey that netting took only indiscriminately, selecting individual animals instead — a reading supported by later regional rock art showing dogs driving gazelle toward traps. The dog's first documented job in the Neolithic world, on this evidence, was as a hunting partner for exactly the broad-spectrum, small-game economy that the PPNA was developing.

// TELLING A DOG FROM A WOLF //

The earliest dogs were barely distinguishable from the small wolves of south-west Asia, and golden jackals overlap the same size range, so fragmented canid bone from a PPNA midden is often honestly indeterminate — one reason the Shubayqa team's digested-bone proxy matters. Morphology alone under-determines the first dogs; context — burial with humans, residence inside a settlement, a gut's signature on other animals' bones — carries much of the evidence.

At Göbekli Tepe itself, the dog is conspicuous by its absence. The enclosures' world, as the reliefs draw it and the bones confirm it, is a wild one: the fox — hunted for pelt and teeth, carved leaping across the central pillars — is the canid of this site, and the great hunts of the bone record are mass interceptions of migrating gazelle, an undertaking in which no dog assistance has been demonstrated. It would be easy, and wrong, to write dogs into the builders' hunts on the strength of Shubayqa. What the comparison actually shows is finer-grained: within one interconnected threshold world, some communities hunted with dogs and some, so far as the evidence runs, did not — or did not leave the trace. The first domestication frames Göbekli Tepe from outside. The builders' achievement needs no amendment: they raised the enclosures with no domesticated crop, no herd, and — on present evidence — no dog. But the category “domestication” itself does not begin with their successors' farms. It begins with a wolf at the camp edge, and with a sick puppy, fourteen thousand years ago, that somebody chose to keep alive.

XII. Feasting, Porridge, and the Brewing Hypothesis

If people gathered and built at Göbekli Tepe, they ate there, and the evidence for eating is on an industrial scale. The fill and occupation deposits contain tens of thousands of animal bones, many from prime meat-bearing parts, in quantities the excavators read as the residue of feasts rather than daily meals alone. Feasting, in the anthropological literature Dietrich and colleagues draw on, is never only nutrition: competitive and communal feasts create obligations, alliances, marriages, reputations, and the social cohesion that large cooperative projects require. The 2012 Antiquity paper by Oliver Dietrich, Manfred Heun, Jens Notroff, Klaus Schmidt, and Martin Zarnkow — the fullest statement of the project's position — argues that cult and feasting together were the social technology that convened the workforce and held the early Neolithic communities together.

The plant side of the feast has been transformed by Laura Dietrich's programme. Göbekli Tepe has produced more than 7,000 grinding tools — in later counts, over 10,000 pieces — the largest such assemblage known from the northern Mesopotamian Neolithic. Combined use-wear analysis, experiment, and 3D surface modelling (Dietrich and colleagues, 2019, and the subsequent grinding-stone study) shows the tools processed cereals in bulk, and that coarse grinding predominates: the everyday product was broken grain for porridge and gruel, cooked in stone vessels, with fine flour — and something like bread — a minority, perhaps special-occasion, product. Phytolith evidence confirms cereal processing on site. The older image of the site as a meat-feast stage set, with plants as garnish, has been corrected by its own storerooms: vats of porridge fed the builders, and the labour of grinding, largely invisible in the bone record, was one of the site's largest industries.

Beer: Indications, Not Proof

The brewing claim rests on six large limestone troughs, some holding up to about 160 litres, from PPNB contexts, whose inner surfaces returned partly positive results for calcium oxalate — a compound deposited during cereal fermentation — in analyses by Martin Zarnkow, a brewing scientist at Weihenstephan. Dietrich and colleagues published this in 2012 as, in their own careful framing, initial indications for brewing at the site, not proof. That framing has sometimes been lost in retelling, where the troughs become a brewery and the beer becomes the motive for agriculture itself. The evidence supports a more modest and still interesting statement: large-scale preparation of cereal foods is certain; fermentation in these vessels is plausible and chemically hinted; and the social logic by which a taste for beer and feast foods could reward ever-more-intensive cereal management is a coherent hypothesis — one strand of the domestication debate in Section XVII, not a conclusion.

// THE FEAST AS INFRASTRUCTURE //

A feast is a way of paying wages before wages exist. Food and drink, given publicly and remembered socially, can mobilise labour for quarrying, carving, and building across group boundaries. That is why the feasting evidence matters to the architecture: it is the best available answer to the question of who fed the builders while they built.

One further strand belongs here: dancing and music. The Antiquity paper's summary lists dancing alongside carving and feasting among the activities that created cohesion at the site — an inference from the gatherings' scale and from comparative evidence, not a find. No instrument from Göbekli Tepe's enclosures has been published that would let this article say more, and a Tier A treatment leaves the dancing where its authors put it: as a reasonable reconstruction of what communal ritual gatherings involve, flagged as reconstruction.

XIII. Fermentation Beyond Göbekli: Three Grades of Evidence

Section XII closed with a discipline: at Göbekli Tepe, brewing rests on indications, not proof. Two sites beyond the Stone Hills supply the scale on which that judgement can be calibrated, because the same process — turning grain and fruit sugars into alcohol — has been argued at each of them by a different method, with a different strength of result. At Jiahu in China, chemistry names the drink's ingredients in the clay of its jars. At Raqefet Cave in the Levant, use-wear, starch, and experiment reconstruct a brew in stone mortars more than two thousand years before Göbekli Tepe. Set beside the troughs' oxalate traces, the three cases are a worked example of this hub's central skill: grading evidence for an identical claim.

Jiahu: Chemistry That Names the Drink

Jiahu, in Henan province in the Huai River valley, is an early Neolithic village whose pottery has also yielded some of the earliest playable musical instruments and the earliest domesticated rice in northern China. Its occupation is divided into subperiods running from III (c. 7000–6600 BCE) through I (c. 6200–5500 BCE). In 2004 Patrick McGovern and a large Chinese-American team reported in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences the chemical analysis of sherds from sixteen vessels — perforated basins, two-handled narrow-mouthed storage jars, and high-necked jars, forms suited to preparing, storing, and serving liquids. The earliest of them place a fermented beverage in the seventh millennium BCE, roughly two thousand years after Göbekli Tepe's floruit and on the far side of Eurasia. Jiahu's full context is treated in this hub's Neolithic East Asia article; what matters here is the method, because the method is the evidence.

The procedure, in order. (1) Sampling. The team took sherds primarily from vessel bases, because bases absorb the most liquid in use and are where precipitates from a stored drink accumulate; before analysis each sherd was washed gently in water to remove adhering soil. (2) Extraction. The organic compounds absorbed into the porous ceramic over nine thousand years were drawn back out with solvents of varying polarity — methanol, chloroform, and/or hexane — either by sonication or by twice boiling for twenty minutes; the portions were combined, filtered to remove fines, and gently evaporated to dryness, yielding only about 5 to 60 milligrams of solid extract per sherd. Blank extractions were run through the whole process alongside, to expose any contaminant introduced by handling or laboratory materials. (3) Five independent readings of the same extract. Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS) ran microlitre chloroform extracts through fused-silica columns, total-ion scans followed by selected-ion monitoring to catch low-level components, calibrated against paraffin and plant-sterol standards. High-performance liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS) characterised the extracts further, including sterol compounds such as β-amyrin and oleanolic acid. Diffuse-reflectance Fourier-transform infrared spectrometry (FT-IR) read roughly a milligram of extract at a time, its absorption spectrum searched against large commercial databases and an in-house corpus of ancient samples and modern reference compounds — rice and rice wine, grape wines, beeswax, tree resins, honey, and grains among them. Stable carbon and nitrogen isotope analysis tested the plant-versus-animal and C3-versus-C4 character of the residues. And Feigl spot tests, sensitive to micrograms, tested methanol extracts directly for tartaric and oxalic acids: tartaric acid fluoresces dark green under ultraviolet light, while its main cross-interfering compound, malic acid, does not fluoresce. No single method was asked to carry the conclusion; a compound counted as present when independent methods converged on it.

What converged. Thirteen of the sixteen sherds formed a single chemical group — the three outliers were the smallest samples, with correspondingly weaker determinations. Tartaric acid and its tartrate salt were identified by their infrared signature, including the diagnostic hydroxyl bending band at 1435–1445 cm−1, and confirmed by positive Feigl tests in all thirteen: in China the plausible sources are grape — wild grape seeds occur at Jiahu, and China holds more than half the world's wild grape species — or Chinese hawthorn, which carries about four times grape's tartaric acid and whose seeds were identified at Jiahu in 2002. A homologous series of long-chain n-alkanes (C23–C36), with the odd-numbered C23, C25, C27, and C29 compounds characteristic of beeswax, pointed to honey: honey's sugars degrade and vanish, but the wax that crude honey processing never fully filters out survives as this fingerprint. Infrared and HPLC matches identified rice, the only cereal recovered archaeobotanically at the site. The isotope values (averaging δ13C ≈ −25.1‰) fit C3 plants such as rice and grape, not C4 millets, and the very low nitrogen values ruled out an animal product. One step remains inference, and the paper takes it openly: ethanol itself is volatile and microbially destroyed, so no ancient residue can contain the alcohol. Fermentation is inferred because the wine yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae lives in honey and on the skins of sugar-rich fruit, and begins consuming diluted sugars within a day or two. The attested result is therefore exact in its own terms: a mixed fermented beverage of rice, honey, and fruit (hawthorn and/or grape), chemically identified ingredient by ingredient — the strongest grade of evidence early fermentation anywhere has achieved.

Raqefet Cave: Mortars, Starch, and a Reconstructed Brew

Raqefet Cave, on Mount Carmel near modern Haifa, was a Natufian burial place, its graves dated to 13,700–11,700 cal BP — a cemetery of semi-sedentary foragers, in use more than two millennia before Göbekli Tepe. In 2018 Li Liu and colleagues reported in the Journal of Archaeological Science: Reports their analysis of three stone mortars from the cave, combining three methods: contextual examination, use-wear analysis of the mortars' working surfaces, and residue analysis of the starch granules and phytoliths — microscopic plant silica bodies — preserved in them, all checked against an experimental programme in which the team brewed beer by reconstructed Natufian methods and compared the starch damage their process produced, granule by granule, with the ancient material. The residues recorded at least seven plant taxa, including wheat or barley, oat, legumes, and bast fibres such as flax. The two boulder mortars, found beside burials, read as storage: plant foods, including already-malted wheat or barley, packed in fibre-made containers and kept in the stone shafts. The bedrock mortar read as the workplace, used for pounding and cooking plant foods — including, the authors argue, brewing.

The brewing process they reconstruct has three stages, each legible in a different kind of starch damage. First, malting: grain germinated in water, then drained, dried, and stored — germination wakes the enzymes that convert starch to sugar, and leaves its signature in the granules. Second, mashing: the malt crushed, mixed with water, and heated — the team's experiments heated the mash for up to four hours — gelatinising starch and completing the conversion to a sweet wort. Third, fermentation: the mash left to ferment with airborne wild yeast for a day or more. The product would have been a thick, gruel-like, weakly alcoholic brew, closer to a drinkable porridge than to any modern beer. Its setting, the authors propose, was ritual: mortars set among the graves of a burial cave, beer brewed for feasts honouring the dead — which would make brewing, like monument-building after it, a social technology of gathering before it was ever an industry of farming.

The case is strong and it is contested, and both facts belong in a Tier A account. In 2019 David Eitam argued in the same journal that the narrow mortars are better explained as equipment for dehusking and grinding barley into flour for bread or other cereal food — no beer required. Liu and colleagues replied the same year, defending the storage reading of the boulder mortars from the use-wear and arguing that their experimental comparisons do distinguish the starch signatures of malting and brewing from those of other processing. The dispute is not closed. Raqefet's grade is therefore the middle one: a multi-line, experimentally tested reconstruction whose every line is indirect — no chemical biomarker of fermentation has been isolated there — persuasive to many, demonstrative to none.

Ethanol never survives. Every claim for ancient beer or wine — at Raqefet, at Göbekli Tepe, at Jiahu — is an inference from proxies: a biomarker, a damaged starch granule, an oxalate crust, a vessel's shape, a grave-side setting. XIV. Labour, Quarries, and Organisation

How many people does an enclosure take? Any number offered is a model, not a measurement, and published estimates vary with assumptions about work rates, seasons, and how much of the task — quarrying, shaping, hauling, erecting, carving, walling, flooring, roofing, feeding — is counted. What the evidence fixes is the order of the achievement: scores of multi-tonne monoliths quarried and finished; rings of walling and benching; bedrock floors smoothed and pedestals cut; buildings maintained for centuries. Schmidt's team estimated that a single large enclosure would have required the coordinated work of hundreds of people, and that the whole programme implies cooperation among groups beyond any single band or village. Even halving such estimates leaves the conclusion standing: somebody could convene, schedule, provision, and direct labour at a scale previously assumed to require farming surpluses and, later, chiefs or kings.

The organisation need not have been coercive, and there is no evidence at Göbekli Tepe for the apparatus of later hierarchy — no palace, no storage centralisation yet demonstrated, no rich burials of rulers, no fortification. The mechanisms the literature proposes instead are the ones visible in the record: shared ritual commitment, feasting obligations, kinship and alliance networks across a catchment, and craft specialisation. UNESCO's inscription, quoted in the project's own reporting, points to specialised craftspeople in the carving and building. Skilled stonecarving of this consistency is itself evidence of transmitted craft knowledge — masters, apprentices, and standards — inside a society without schools, guilds, or texts.

The unfinished quarry pillar deserves a second mention in this context, because it is the site's clearest lesson in limits. At about 7 metres and an estimated 50 tonnes, it exceeds anything successfully erected at Göbekli Tepe and it never left the quarry. Whether it cracked, proved too heavy to move, or was simply overtaken by changing plans is unknown. Ambition outran capacity at least once, on the record, in stone. A tradition that can fail at that scale is a working building tradition, not a miraculous one — and that ordinariness, at this date, is the marvel.

XV. The Dead: Fragmented Bone and Carved Skulls

For most of the excavation's history, Göbekli Tepe produced human bone but no burials: hundreds of fragments — 691 in the count published in 2017, more since — scattered through building fills and open areas, with skull fragments strikingly over-represented (408 cranial against 283 postcranial fragments in that study). A sub-floor collective burial found in 2017, containing the disturbed remains of at least three individuals beneath a rectangular domestic building, has since shown that formal burial did occur at the settlement, in the ordinary PPN domestic manner. The scattered fragments, however, are not ordinary grave goods or disturbed cemetery material alone, and three of the skulls carry something found nowhere else.

Gresky, Haelm, and Clare's 2017 Science Advances study describes three partially preserved adult skulls bearing deliberate, deep incisions cut with flint tools along the sagittal line — grooves worked by repeated cutting, in one case continuing over the back of the skull and onto the mandible — with, in the best-preserved example, a carefully drilled perforation and traces of ochre. About 40 further skull fragments carry cut marks from defleshing, and two of the seven cervical vertebrae recovered show cut marks consistent with decapitation. The authors interpret the ensemble as a previously undocumented variation of the Neolithic skull cult — the widespread PPN practice of removing, treating, displaying, and eventually depositing human crania — and propose, as possibilities, that the grooves held cords fastening the jaw or suspending the skull for display, or that the carving marked particular individuals after death. Both the interpretation and the possibilities are labelled as such by their authors; what is attested is the modification itself, post-mortem, skilled, and unique in the record so far.

Context matters here, and restraint matters more. Skull treatment is a regional PPN language: plastered and painted skulls are known from Jericho, 'Ain Ghazal, and other Levantine sites, and skull removal occurs across the period. Göbekli Tepe's carved crania are a local dialect of that language, not evidence of a unique death cult, human sacrifice, or a “gathering of the dead” in any sense stronger than the general one — that the treatment of the dead, and probably the presence of ancestors, formed part of what the special buildings were for. Schmidt's team floated stronger mortuary readings of the whole site; the bone evidence, carefully read, supports significance without supporting a script.

XVI. Buried, Filled, or Washed In? The Enclosures' End

Every visitor's account once contained the same haunting sentence: the enclosures were deliberately buried by their builders. Schmidt's team did report the fill as a rapid, intentional backfilling — the mound itself, in the 2012 synthesis, is described as the result of that deliberate act — and the image of a community interring its own temples did more than any other single claim to fix the site in the popular imagination. It is also the claim the current project has most clearly revised.

Clare's 2020 summary states the new position directly. Re-examination of the south-eastern enclosures' fill suggests inundation by displaced deposits: archaeological material — midden, rubble, bone, including mixed PPNA and PPNB material — slid from the higher knolls and slopes of the mound into the lower-lying special buildings in slope-slide events. That reading explains, in one move, both the fill's heterogeneity and the scatter of the old radiocarbon dates taken from it. The revised building histories add human responses to the slides: terrace walls built to stabilise slopes, enclosures repaired after inundation, damaged buildings modified and reused, and eventual abandonment of the worst-affected structures — Enclosure D among them — in the late 9th millennium. Deliberate human dumping and levelling may also have contributed locally; the debate is about proportions and agency, not a clean swap of one certainty for another.

The contrast with Karahan Tepe is instructive and is flagged in Karul's own publication: there, at least for Structure AB, the excavators report a deliberate filling carried out in a sequence of procedures and sealed as a final act (Section XVIII). Building burial is a real Neolithic practice — it is attested across the PPN, from buried houses to the great filled structures at several sites — which is exactly why the Göbekli Tepe case must be argued from its own sediments rather than assumed from the regional pattern. At Göbekli Tepe, on current evidence, the great enclosures were not reverently interred in a single ceremony. They were overwhelmed, patched, struggled with, and finally left — a slower and, to many researchers, more human ending. Popular works published before the revision, and the many that copy them, still state the burial as fact; readers should date any such statement by its sources.

// WHY THIS REVISION MATTERS //

The deliberate-burial claim was load-bearing in two larger structures: Schmidt's reading of the site as a pure sanctuary with a ritual life-cycle, and, in popular appropriations, the idea of a monument hidden or sealed by its makers — a premise the lost-civilisation readings of Section XX lean on heavily. Remove the intentional burial, and both structures lose a support. This is how a sedimentological detail propagates: check the fill, and the interpretation of the whole hill moves.

XVII. Did the Temple Drive the Farm? The Domestication Debate

The oldest model of the Neolithic transition ran in one direction: climate pressure or population growth forced farming; farming produced surplus; surplus fed specialists, priests, and builders; monuments came last. Göbekli Tepe breaks the sequence at its final step — the monuments are first — and Schmidt drew the boldest available conclusion. In his formulation, widely quoted in the form “first the temple, then the city”, organised religion came before farming, and the demands of congregating, building, and feasting at a sanctuary created the pressures — above all, feeding crowds from a hunter-gatherer economy — that pushed communities toward cultivation and, eventually, domestication. The slogan is a paraphrase of Schmidt's thesis as presented across his publications and interviews, not a line this article quotes from a text; the thesis itself, however, is genuinely his, and the DAI's own 2020 summary describes how it catapulted religious motivation to the front rank of proposed triggers for Neolithisation.

The supporting circumstantial case is real. The site stands in the overlap zone of the wild founder crops; the einkorn domestication has a genetic address in the nearby Karacadağ; intensive wild-cereal processing at the site is now documented at scale; and feasting supplies a plausible selective pressure — reliable, abundant cereal supplies reward management, sowing, and eventually the harvesting practices that select for domesticated forms. Jacques Cauvin had argued, before Göbekli Tepe was understood, that a “revolution of symbols” and new religious conceptions preceded and enabled agriculture in the Levant; the DAI team explicitly reads the Upper Mesopotamian evidence as supporting that cultural-cognitive line against purely climatic explanations.

The Critics, Fairly Stated

Chronology and causation. Domestication was a process of millennia, spread across a broad arc from the southern Levant to the Zagros, with cultivation experiments at sites far from Göbekli Tepe and earlier than its floruit. A single site, however spectacular, cannot be the trigger for a continental process, and several of the region's cultivation trajectories show no special connection to it. Correlation in one rich valley is not a cause for the whole Fertile Crescent.

The temple premise. E. B. Banning's 2011 Current Anthropology critique, “So Fair a House”, attacked the identification itself: the sacred/profane distinction Schmidt applied, Banning argued, is a modern ethnocentric grid; ethnography is full of lavishly decorated, ritually central houses, and the Göbekli buildings — plausibly roofed, plausibly residential for large corporate households — may be houses of a “house society” rather than temples of a cult. Dietrich and Notroff replied in defence of special buildings in 2015, and Banning's later reassessment (2023) notes that the subsequent discovery of domestic occupation and roofing arguments has moved the field partly toward his position while the special-building reading also survives. The live consensus, insofar as there is one, is a both/and the original debate lacked: a settlement whose largest buildings concentrated communal and ritual life, in a period that may not have separated those categories at all.

The settlement revision. Clare's recent work cuts Schmidt's thesis in a subtler way. If people lived at Göbekli Tepe year-round, managed water, processed cereals daily, and remodelled their great buildings for half a millennium, then the site is not a pilgrimage spike that forced a subsistence revolution; it is one long-lived community among several, negotiating the transition at its own pace. Clare has further suggested, in presentations and interviews reported in the educational literature, that the imagery's celebration of wild animals could even express attachment to — a kind of resistance to abandoning — the hunting lifeway as farming spread around it. That reading is as much an interpretation as Schmidt's, and it is presented here as one named scholar's current position, not as the project's verdict.

Where the debate stands. Few specialists now defend the simple sequence in either direction. The defensible claims are these: monumental communal building demonstrably precedes farming at Göbekli Tepe; social and ritual organisation was therefore not a product of agricultural surplus; feasting and cereal intensification at such centres plausibly formed one arena in which cultivation was rehearsed; and domestication itself was a slow, multi-regional process no single site explains. “First the temple, then the city” survives as the slogan that broke the old model — which was no small service — rather than as the new model itself.

XVIII. Karahan Tepe and the Taş Tepeler

Göbekli Tepe is not alone, and since 2021 it has not been studied alone. About 35–40 kilometres east-south-east, in the Tektek Mountains, lies Karahan Tepe (also spelled Karahantepe), a site of some 10 hectares — more, counting its quarries — recorded in survey by Bahattin Çelik in 1997, when more than 260 T-pillars were already counted on its surface, and excavated systematically only from 2019 under Necmi Karul of Istanbul University. Its building activity runs broadly parallel to Göbekli Tepe's, from the PPNA (c. 9400–9000 BCE for its main early construction) into the early PPNB, and its occupation reaches back, on wild einkorn evidence reported by the project, toward the end of the Epipalaeolithic. Only a small fraction — around 5 per cent — has been excavated.

Karahan's signature is the opposite of Göbekli's. Where Göbekli's builders quarried pillars and raised them into rings on the surface, Karahan's cut down: a complex of interconnected special buildings (designated AA, AB, AC, and AD) carved partly or wholly into the bedrock on the hill's eastern slopes. Structure AD, the largest, is an elliptical space about 23 by 20 metres whose wall carried T-pillars and benches. Structure AB — the chamber that made the site famous — is a smaller rock-cut room entered from AD through a low, porthole-like opening, containing a row of pillars carved from the living rock in phallic form and, facing them, a human head sculpted emerging from the wall itself, its neck trailing into the stone. Karul's 2021 study of the site's buried buildings reports that the special buildings excavated so far were intentionally filled, with AB's filling carried out in sequential stages and finished by a deliberate sealing — the firmest published case in the region for the building-burial practice that Göbekli Tepe's revision has called into question at Göbekli itself.

Karahan's recent seasons have been prolific: a 2.3-metre statue of a seated naked man, recovered in 2023, ribs incised, hands holding his phallus, a V-shaped collar at the neck recalling the much earlier-found Urfa Man; further pillared halls with central T-pillar pairs echoing Göbekli's enclosures; a quarry with a half-finished T-pillar about 5.8 metres long still in its bed; a field of rock-cut cisterns linked by channels; and, in 2025, the faced T-pillar noted in Section VIII. The two sites plainly belong to one world — the same pillar form, the same animal repertoire, the same building grammar — worked in different materials and moods. Neither is a copy of the other, and the older habit of treating Göbekli Tepe as a lone miracle is no longer available to scholarship.

The Stone Hills as a Programme

Taş Tepeler, “the Stone Hills”, names both the cluster and the research programme launched in 2021 by Turkey's Ministry of Culture and Tourism, coordinated by Karul, that studies the cluster together. The sites usually counted are about a dozen: Göbekli Tepe, Karahan Tepe, Sayburç, Sefertepe, Çakmaktepe, Harbetsuvan, Ayanlar Höyük, Gürcütepe, Kurt Tepesi, Söğüt Tarlası, Yoğunburç, and Yeni Mahalle, with T-pillar finds reported in 2026 at Samsat in neighbouring Adıyaman province suggesting the horizon extends beyond Şanlıurfa. Sayburç has produced a celebrated relief of a human figure with animals, carved on a bench in a communal building among houses — another data point for the settlement-with-special-buildings pattern. The programme's premise is the correct one: whatever happened in these hills happened as a regional society, over centuries, in many places at once. Single-site theories, grand or fringe, are what the Stone Hills exist to replace.

XIX. Regional Context: Jericho, Çayönü, Nevalı Çori

Göbekli Tepe startles less, and means more, when it is set back among its contemporaries. The PPN world was inventive in many valleys at once, and the special building — the large, non-domestic or more-than-domestic structure at a settlement's heart — is one of its shared inventions.

JERICHO (TELL ES-SULTAN)

The southern Levant's type-site, where Kenyon defined the PPNA and PPNB. PPNA Jericho raised a massive stone tower against its wall — communal, monumental construction by early farming-age villagers — and its later PPNB houses yielded plastered human skulls, the classic parallel for Göbekli Tepe's treatment of crania. Jericho shows the same ingredients — monumentality, ancestors, gathering — assembled in a farming village on an oasis, a useful check on any claim that Göbekli's combination was unique.

ÇAYÖNÜ

On the Ergani plain in the Taurus foothills, about 40 km north-west of Diyarbakır, Çayönü was excavated for sixteen seasons between 1964 and 1991, first by Halet Çambel and Robert Braidwood and later by Mehmet and Aslı Erim Özdoğan. Its Aceramic Neolithic sequence runs from the PPNA into the final PPNB and is named for its successive house plans: round buildings (10,200–9400 BP), then grill-plan, channeled, cobble-paved, and cell-plan buildings, and finally large-room buildings (8200–8000 BP) — the region's most complete experiment in village architecture, conducted in one settlement. Its special buildings match the ambition: the Skull Building, rebuilt repeatedly over centuries, received the secondary burials of as many as 400 people — about 120 in its first phase and some 280 in its second — before burial practice shifted back to primary inhumation beneath house floors in the Cell Plan phase; another special building carried a polished terrazzo floor. The faunal record shows pigs, sheep, cattle, and deer exploited in every state from hunted wild to managed and domesticating, making Çayönü the domestication debate's other essential address — the farming village against which Göbekli's hunter-built enclosures stand out.

NEVALI ÇORI

The site that taught Schmidt what a T-pillar was. Nevalı Çori stood on the Kantara stream, a middle-Euphrates tributary in Şanlıurfa province, and was excavated in seven campaigns between 1983 and 1991 by Harald Hauptmann of Heidelberg University with the Şanlıurfa Museum, in advance of the Atatürk Dam. Its PPNB settlement joined large rectangular channeled houses to a cult building at the settlement's north-western end, rebuilt in successive phases: the best-published phase measured 13.90 by 13.50 metres, with benches and T-shaped pillars along the walls, a central pillar pair, and a terrazzo floor; its successor was built inside the earlier walls at 12.10 by 12.80 metres, its terrazzo floor some 15 cm thick. The site yielded some of the earliest known life-size stone sculpture: a more-than-life-size human head, 37 cm high, with a snake coiled on its bald crown, found in a niche in the cult building; human–bird composite figures; a bird statue; relief-carved pillars; a limestone bowl fragment showing two human figures and a tortoise-like creature in a dance-like scene; and several hundred small clay figurines, about 5 cm high, read as votive offerings. Flooded by the Atatürk Dam reservoir in 1991–92, it survives in its publications and in the Şanlıurfa Museum's displays — Göbekli Tepe's grammar at village scale, a generation or more after Göbekli's first enclosures.

THE WIDER CIRCLE

Jerf el Ahmar and Tell 'Abr 3 on the Syrian Euphrates built large circular communal buildings, one famously divided and benched; Hallan Çemi, among the earliest sedentary communities in the east, arranged its houses around a central activity area; Qermez Dere set standing pillars inside its houses. The pattern is consistent: at the Neolithic threshold, communities across the northern Fertile Crescent were building gathering into their settlements. Göbekli Tepe is the pattern's maximum, not its exception.

Two differences still mark Göbekli Tepe out within that circle. Scale: no contemporary site matches the enclosures' size, pillar height, or carving programme. And subsistence: Jericho, Çayönü, and Nevalı Çori were farming or farming-ward settlements for most of their histories, while Göbekli Tepe's monumental phase was built and used without domesticates at all. The comparison thus sharpens rather than dissolves the site's central fact. Monumental communal architecture was a regional invention of the threshold centuries; at Göbekli Tepe, uniquely in the present record, it was achieved first, and at the largest scale, by people who had not yet taken up farming.

XX. Astronomy, Calendars, and Lost Civilisations: Claims Checked

Göbekli Tepe attracts over-claim the way few sites do, because its genuine facts — great age, monumentality before farming, a rich unexplained iconography — leave exactly the gaps that speculation fills. This hub's standard is to state each major claim in its strongest published form, name its author, and then state the published response. Claims are not suppressed here; they are labelled.

1. Stellar Alignments of the Enclosures

The claim. Giulio Magli proposed in 2013 that the central-pillar axes of Enclosures B, C, and D were oriented, in sequence, on the rising of Sirius as precession brought the star back into the southern sky across the 10th–9th millennia BCE. On Magli's reconstruction the mechanism was dramatic: precession had carried Sirius below the horizon at Göbekli Tepe's latitude from about 15,000 BCE, so that around 9300 BCE the brightest star in the sky was returning — a “new” star low in the south — and the three enclosures' differing azimuths track its shifting rising point across the centuries his scheme assigns them, about 9100–8300 BCE. The status. This is an orientation hypothesis published as a preprint and in archaeoastronomy venues; it has not been adopted by the excavation team. Its difficulties are methodological: the enclosures' axes must be defined from remodelled, multi-phase buildings; a bright star can be found near many azimuths over a long enough span; and no independent evidence at the site attests Sirius observation. Critics have added a visibility problem: a star only just returned above the horizon culminates very low, where atmospheric extinction dims even Sirius severely, so the “new star” of the crucial centuries would have been a faint, horizon-hugging object rather than a brilliant beacon. A possible alignment is not a demonstrated purpose. Label: speculation — published, unconfirmed.

2. Pillar 43 as a Comet Record and Date Stamp

The claim. Martin Sweatman and Dimitrios Tsikritsis argued in 2017 (Mediterranean Archaeology and Archaeometry) that the animals on Pillar 43 and other pillars correspond to constellations as later Greek, Egyptian, and Arabian astronomy defined them; that the vulture-and-disc composition encodes, via precession, a date of about 10,950 ± 250 BCE; and that the scene memorialises a cometary encounter connected with the onset of the Younger Dryas cold interval and its proposed impact hypothesis. The response. The excavation team replied in the same journal the same year (Notroff, Dietrich, and colleagues, “More than a vulture”). Their objections: the proposed date is some 700–1,000 years older than the oldest radiocarbon evidence then available for Enclosure D itself; the reading selects a few pillars from a corpus of more than sixty and does not account for the whole iconographic programme; the constellation identifications assume, without evidence, that PPNA hunters in Upper Mesopotamia recognised the same star figures as cultures thousands of years later and far away; and details of the carving itself — including the headless figure's prominent phallus — sit awkwardly with the catastrophe reading. Separately, the Younger Dryas impact hypothesis remains contested in the geological literature, with a major 2023 review by Holliday and colleagues concluding that its proposed markers do not withstand scrutiny — a conclusion its proponents dispute. Label: speculation — published, formally rebutted by the excavators, not accepted.

3. The “Oldest Calendar” Pillar

The claim. In 2024 Sweatman extended the argument in Time and Mind: the V-shaped marks on Pillar 43, counted as days, yield a lunisolar year of twelve lunar months plus eleven extra days — 365 in total — making the pillar the world's oldest calendar; related counts and V-necklaced figures at Göbekli Tepe, Karahan Tepe, and Sayburç were read as time-keeping deities, and the system was again tied to a Younger Dryas comet. The status. The paper is a published interpretive proposal; its correspondences have not become an accepted decipherment. The counting depends on choices — which marks count, where a register begins and ends, which figures are included — that the method does not independently constrain, and the argument inherits every difficulty of the 2017 reading it builds on, including the chronological gap and the borrowed constellations. Specialist responses published since have treated it accordingly, and the excavation teams have not adopted it. That the builders tracked seasons is, by contrast, highly likely — their hunting and gathering calendar demonstrably depended on it — but seasonal knowledge needs no carved 365-day system, and none has been demonstrated. Label: speculation — published, not accepted.

4. The Lost-Civilisation Appropriation

The claim. In popular books and series — Graham Hancock's works and the Netflix series Ancient Apocalypse are the most prominent — Göbekli Tepe is presented as the work, or the inspired legacy, of an advanced Ice Age civilisation destroyed in a catastrophe, its knowledge carried to hunter-gatherers by survivors. The site's sudden sophistication, its scale, and (in older tellings) its deliberate burial are offered as the anomalies requiring that explanation. The status. No artefact, material, technique, or inscription at Göbekli Tepe requires anything beyond the attested toolkit of its own period and region: flint-worked limestone, ground stone, bone, wood, fibre, and organised labour, in a regional tradition — Epipalaeolithic and Natufian antecedents, contemporary special buildings from Jerf el Ahmar to Çayönü, and a dozen sibling Stone Hills — that supplies the supposedly missing precursors. Genetic and archaeobotanical evidence places plant and animal domestication after the Ice Age in identifiable local processes, with no earlier domesticated horizon for a lost civilisation to have produced; no metal, ceramic, or other anachronistic material has ever been recovered from the site's Neolithic contexts; and the deliberate-burial premise has itself been revised by the excavators (Section XVI). Archaeologists' objection is not that the claim is forbidden but that it is unnecessary and evidence-free: it explains a well-documented achievement by undocumented people, and in doing so it takes the achievement away from the hunter-gatherers who actually accomplished it. Label: modern construction — no supporting evidence, contradicted by the regional record.

XXI. UNESCO, Conservation, and Research Today

On 1 July 2018, at its 42nd session in Manama, Bahrain, UNESCO's World Heritage Committee inscribed Göbekli Tepe on the World Heritage List — Turkey's eighteenth inscription — under criteria (i), (ii), and (iv): as a masterpiece of human creative genius; as an early manifestation of monumental architecture whose building techniques and imagery were disseminated to other early Neolithic sites; and as an outstanding example of an architectural ensemble illustrating a significant stage in human history. The Committee's statement of Outstanding Universal Value describes the enclosures as erected by hunter-gatherer groups in the Pre-Pottery Neolithic of the 10th–9th millennia BCE — the scholarly core of this article, in treaty language. Turkey proclaimed 2019 the “Göbekli Tepe Year”, and visitor numbers passed 400,000 in that year alone, a figure the DAI's reporting records with pride and some apprehension.

Conservation at that scale is engineering. The two great shelters over the main excavation areas — whose support soundings produced the domestic evidence of Section X — protect the pillars from rain and temperature cycling while complicating every future excavation beneath them. Stone conservation, drainage, and the management of the slope instability that afflicted the enclosures in prehistory are continuing concerns; the hill's own geology has not retired. The site's presentation, including the Şanlıurfa Museum's displays of its sculpture, now carries the revised interpretation alongside the famous one, and the Taş Tepeler programme's visitor infrastructure extends across the cluster.

Research today runs on several tracks at once: the building-archaeology and mortar-dating programme refining each enclosure's biography; archaeozoology and archaeobotany of the settlement's full span; the Karahan Tepe and Sayburç excavations redrawing the regional map season by season; and the slow publication of the Schmidt-era backlog. The honest expectation set by everyone working there is that the picture will change again. Nine-tenths of Göbekli Tepe is still underground, and Karahan Tepe is less excavated still. A Tier A article is a dated snapshot of a moving science, and this one should be read — and, in this archive's practice, revised — accordingly.

XXII. Why It Matters for “Before Egypt”

This hub exists to answer a deceptively simple question: what was already old when Egypt was new? Göbekli Tepe is its deepest anchor. When the first farming villages of the Nile Valley were coalescing in the centuries treated in this archive's Predynastic article, the enclosures on the Şanlıurfa ridge had already been abandoned for some four thousand years — as far in Predynastic Egyptians' past as the Predynastic is in ours, and then some. The comparison is not a contest. It is a calibration.

What the calibration shows is that the components later ages bundle together as “civilisation” did not arrive as a bundle. At Göbekli Tepe, by c. 9500 BCE, there is already: monumental architecture at the largest scale its millennium anywhere achieved; a disciplined, shared iconography executed by specialist carvers; the convening of labour across group boundaries; feasting as social infrastructure; planned water management; long-lived institutions that remodelled their buildings for centuries; and elaborate, formal treatment of the dead. And there is not yet: farming, herding, pottery, metal, the wheel, writing, cities, kings, or — so far as any evidence shows — priests as a separate class. Organised monumental ritual does not merely predate farming villages, cities, and writing. It predates them by one, three, and six millennia respectively, and on the strongest current reading it developed in conversation with the sedentary, cereal-processing, feast-giving lifeway from which farming eventually emerged.

For the study of Egypt specifically, the lesson is methodological before it is chronological. Egyptian temples, with their pillar halls, axial plans, carved relief programmes, festival calendars, and feasting economies, are not the beginning of the story of sacred architecture; they are a late, literate, extraordinarily documented chapter of a story whose opening chapters are mute. Göbekli Tepe teaches the reader of Egypt how much of a ritual world can exist — and how much can be built — without a single written word, and therefore how much humility is appropriate when the written word finally appears and tempts us to mistake the first recorded form of a thing for its first form. The T-pillars stood in their rings, facing inward, for longer than the Old Kingdom lasted. Egypt's builders were not the first to raise stone persons in a sacred enclosure. They were, so far as we know, about the six-hundredth generation to do so.

The threshold of this article's title is thus double. Göbekli Tepe stands at the threshold of the Neolithic — the moment hunting peoples became, without yet farming, the kind of society that builds forever. And it stands at the threshold of this archive: the earliest site in the hub at which the full apparatus of communal sacred building can be studied in stone. Everything later in these pages — Nabta Playa's cattle and stones, the Predynastic villages, the temples of Kemet — happens downstream of the discovery, made first in these hills, that a community could organise itself around a building. The building came first. On the evidence, that much of Schmidt's famous sentence stands.
