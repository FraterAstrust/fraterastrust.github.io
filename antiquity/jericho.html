This is a Tier A article: everything in it rests on excavated, dated, and published archaeological evidence, and where scholars disagree — as they do, sharply, about the purpose of the Tower and the date of the Bronze Age destruction — the disagreement itself is reported with its positions attributed. One boundary is observed throughout. A famous later textual tradition, the conquest narrative of the Book of Joshua, attaches itself to this mound's Bronze Age walls. That tradition is a text, and its treatment belongs to Tier B elsewhere in this archive. What this article reports is what the excavation record shows, in the order the strata give it; where the record and the tradition are compared, the comparison is stated as a matter of dates and deposits, and nothing more is claimed in either direction.

Jericho's mound — Tell es-Sultan, beside the spring of 'Ain es-Sultan in the Jordan Valley, some 258 metres below sea level — holds one of the longest settlement sequences on earth. Natufian hunter-gatherers camped by the spring before farming existed. By about 9000 BCE a permanent village stood there, and by about 8000 BCE that village — a community of the Pre-Pottery Neolithic A, without pottery, without metal, without writing — had built a stone wall more than three and a half metres high, dug a rock-cut ditch in front of it, and raised inside it a stone tower eight and a half metres tall with a staircase of twenty-two steps sealed within its solid core. A millennium later its Pre-Pottery Neolithic B successors were modelling the faces of their dead in lime plaster over the bone of the skull itself, inlaying the eye sockets with shells — the earliest portraiture, of a kind, that survives anywhere. Cities of the Early, Middle, and Late Bronze Ages followed on the same mound, and occupation of the oasis, in one form or another, has never wholly ceased since.

Jericho is therefore two things at once, and this article keeps them separate. It is a type-site: the place where Kathleen Kenyon defined the Pre-Pottery Neolithic A and B for the southern Levant, and where the stratigraphic method she practised became a standard for the region. And it is a test case for the discipline of this hub: a site so loaded with later meaning that every generation of excavators has had to dig, visibly, against the expectations it brought with it. The stones, read at their own strength, need no help from the fame. A farming village that built monumentally in stone eleven thousand years ago is remarkable enough on the evidence alone.

I. The Place — Tell es-Sultan and the Spring

Tell es-Sultan is an oval mound of accumulated settlement debris, roughly four hectares in extent and rising some twenty metres above the plain, on the northwestern edge of the modern city of Jericho (Ariha) in the Jordan Valley. It stands about ten kilometres north of the Dead Sea and about ten kilometres west of the Jordan River, at approximately 31°52′ N, 35°26′ E. Its elevation — around 258 metres below sea level — makes the oasis the lowest permanently inhabited place on earth, a fact that is not a curiosity but a cause: the depth of the valley gives Jericho a long, warm growing season, frost-free winters, and a climate in which dates, bananas, and winter vegetables ripen while the hills above are cold.

Everything at Jericho begins with the spring. 'Ain es-Sultan — the Sultan's Spring, known in later tradition as Elisha's Spring — rises at the eastern foot of the tell, where water collected in the limestone hills of the Judaean wilderness to the west emerges along the fault line of the valley floor. Its modern measured flow is on the order of a thousand gallons (about 3.8 cubic metres) a minute, enough to irrigate some ten square kilometres of the oasis through a network of channels. In a valley where rainfall is low and summer heat extreme, a flow of that size and reliability is not an amenity. It is the difference between a camping place visited in season and a settlement that can be held all year, every year, for millennia. The UNESCO inscription of 2023 puts the point in its formal language: the property consists of the tell "and the adjacent perennial spring of 'Ain es-Sultan, which for millennia has been an important source of water for the inhabitants of this area." The spring is not beside the site. In every functional sense, the spring is the site.

The setting offered more than water. West of the tell the land rises steeply to Jebel Quruntul — the Mount of Temptation of later tradition — and the Judaean hills; the wadi that drains those hills past the site, the Wadi Qelt system, is also the site's recurring hazard, the source of the flood and silt problem examined in §IV and §V. Eastward the valley floor runs flat to the Jordan and the crossing places of the river, and beyond them to the hills of Moab. Jericho thus sits at a junction: water at its feet, a route westward up the wadi to the hill country and Jerusalem, a route eastward across the river, and the great north–south rift corridor running past it. Settlement persisted here for the same compound reason it persisted at this hub's other long-lived places — water, cultivable land, and a road — but at Jericho the combination is present in its most extreme form, and so, in the event, was the persistence.

A caution about the famous superlative belongs at the front. Jericho is routinely called the oldest continuously inhabited city in the world, and the oasis does have an extraordinary claim: archaeological deposits at the tell reach back, in the UNESCO dossier's dating, to about 10,500 BCE, and habitation at the oasis has recurred in every period since. But "continuously inhabited" smooths over what the strata actually show, which is a sequence with real gaps and thinnings — the tell itself was at times reduced to a small settlement or effectively abandoned (the Late Bronze Age of §XIII is the contested case) while life continued around the spring and in the oasis at large. The accurate statement is stronger for being accurate: people have lived by this spring, in settlement after settlement on or beside this mound, for some eleven thousand years. No charter of unbroken urban continuity is needed, and none is claimed here.

The comparison this hub invites is with the other great spring-and-route settlements of the early Neolithic world treated in this division. At Göbekli Tepe, hunter-gatherers built monumentally on a hilltop with no permanent water and no village at all; at Çatalhöyük, a large farming town grew on an Anatolian river plain without walls, streets, or public buildings anyone can identify. Jericho is the third pattern, and in some ways the most conventional: a watered place, farmed early, defended — or at least enclosed — early, and rebuilt on its own debris until the debris itself became a hill. Its fame is disproportionate to its size at almost every period. Its antiquity is not.

II. First Camp, First Village — Natufian to PPNA

The first people at the spring were not farmers. In the Epipalaeolithic levels at the base of the tell, Kenyon's excavations found the traces of Natufian hunter-gatherers — the culture, spread across the Levant in the closing millennia of the last Ice Age, that first experimented with sedentary life before domestication. The Jericho Natufian left a scattering of the culture's diagnostic crescent-shaped microliths and evidence of structures Kenyon termed proto-Neolithic. During the cold, dry interruption of the Younger Dryas (c. 10,900–9700 BCE), permanent habitation anywhere in the region was precarious; the spring, flowing when the hills were dry, made Jericho the kind of place a mobile group returns to — a fixed point of water in a seasonal round. The Italian-Palestinian expedition's periodisation pushes the first, Mesolithic presence at the site back to about 10,500 BCE (its Sultan Ia phase), a dating the UNESCO dossier adopts.

With the warming that ended the Younger Dryas, the camp became a village. The first permanent settlement at Tell es-Sultan belongs to the Pre-Pottery Neolithic A (PPNA), conventionally c. 9600–8500 BCE in the southern Levant, with Jericho's own PPNA — the phase Kenyon's terminology called Pre-Sultan and Sultanian — running in her sequence from about 9000 BCE. The name states the period's defining absence: these are farming communities that do not yet make pottery. Their containers were baskets, skins, stone vessels, and unfired clay; their architecture, at Jericho, was the round house.

The Round Houses and the Stored Harvest

PPNA Jericho was a village of small circular dwellings built of plano-convex mud bricks — bricks moulded by hand, flat on one face and domed on the other, sometimes called hog-backed bricks — set in mud mortar, each house a single room a few metres across, its floor often sunk slightly below the contemporary ground surface. The dead were buried beneath house floors, a practice that runs through the whole Levantine Neolithic and that matters at Jericho for a specific reason: the treatment of the skull, separated from the body after burial and kept or reburied in groups, begins here, in the PPNA levels, long before the plastering of §VII elaborated it. Kenyon's excavations recovered concentrations of unplastered skulls from PPNA contexts — dozens of individuals in the anthropological counts — the plain first chapter of a mortuary tradition whose decorated second chapter made the site famous.

The village's subsistence was transitional in the strict sense. Its people cultivated emmer wheat, barley, and pulses — the plant remains and the sickle-glossed flint blades attest harvesting — and they hunted, gazelle prominently, on a large scale. Whether the cereals of PPNA Jericho were fully domesticated forms or cultivated wild stands under intensive management is a question the surviving plant evidence answers only in part, and the literature states it with corresponding care: Jericho stands at the threshold of agriculture, with cultivation certain and the domestication status of its crops debated in detail. What is not debated is the sedentism. A community does not bury its dead under its floors, rebuild its houses on the same plots for centuries, and — as the next sections show — quarry, carry, and lay thousands of tons of stone, on the basis of a seasonal camp. By 9000 BCE, on the evidence of its own strata, Jericho was a place people lived.

The walled PPNA settlement covered about four hectares (40,000 m²). Population estimates in the literature diverge by an order of magnitude — from as low as 200–300 to as high as 2,000–3,000 — depending on the assumed density and on how much of the enclosed area is taken to have been built up. The divergence is itself the finding: the archaeology fixes the area and the labour, but not the head-count, and this article uses the range rather than either end of it.

By about 8500 BCE the village entered the phase on which its reputation rests. Within a few centuries, its inhabitants had enclosed the settlement in a stone perimeter, dug a ditch outside it, and raised the great Tower against its inner face — works dated by the radiocarbon series published from Kenyon's samples in 1981 and 1983 to construction around 8300 BCE and use until about 7800 BCE, conventionally rounded, in this article and in the hub's timeline, to c. 8000 BCE. Those works are so far out of scale with everything else known from the ninth millennium BCE that they require, and receive, three sections of their own: the Tower's construction (§III), the debate over its purpose (§IV), and the wall and ditch with which it is associated (§V).

One framing point before the detail. The PPNA is, in the wider region, the age of the first communal architecture: the great enclosures of Göbekli Tepe in the north, the communal buildings of Jerf el Ahmar and Mureybet on the Euphrates, the tower-houses of Tell Qaramel in Syria — the last now known to rival or precede Jericho's Tower in date. Jericho's works belong to that world and are not a solitary miracle. They are, however, its most completely published southern example, and the only one raised by a settled farming village around its own houses — which is why the argument about what they were for has run for seventy years without closing.

III. The Tower — Stone by Stone

The Tower stands on the western side of the PPNA settlement, against the inner face of the perimeter wall, in the deep trench — Kenyon's Trench I — where it was uncovered in the 1950s seasons. It is, on the published measurements, a solid stone structure of slightly conical profile: about nine metres in diameter at the base, narrowing to about seven metres at the surviving top, and preserved to a height of about 8.5 metres. Kenyon's sections showed it was not a single build but a structure with a history — an initial tower, subsequent thickening and heightening, and phases in which parts of it were blocked or remodelled — and that its use-life, on the radiocarbon evidence, ran from about 8300 to about 7800 BCE. It remains among the oldest stone towers known anywhere, and the oldest monumental building in the southern Levant.

The Fabric — Undressed Stone, Laid Dry

The construction material is the local limestone, used as it came from the ground. The Tower is built of undressed stone — unshaped fieldstones and rough quarry blocks, laid in courses without dressed faces and without, in the main mass, any surviving evidence of a mortar technology comparable to the lime plaster the settlement used elsewhere; the stability of the structure comes from its mass, its battered (inward-sloping) profile, and the care of its laying. The walling is about 1.5 metres thick where it is measurable as a shell, but the Tower is not a hollow drum: it is a solid core of stone and rubble fill, through which a single passage — the staircase — is threaded. Bar-Yosef's published estimate put the mass of the structure at about 1,000 tons. There are no rooms inside it, no chambers, no burials: the Tower contains exactly one piece of interior architecture, and that piece is a way up.

The Staircase — Twenty-Two Steps

The staircase enters at the eastern side, from inside the settlement, at ground level, and climbs within the solid core to an outlet at the top — the original outlet, Kenyon's sections showed, before later phases blocked the stair and a new arrangement replaced it. It consists of twenty-two stone steps. Unlike the undressed mass around them, the steps are made of flat, cut slabs of limestone, hammer-dressed smooth — the only deliberately finished stonework in the structure, and among the earliest true staircases known. The passage is steep and narrow, about a metre wide, roofed as it rises by corbelling and slab lintels within the core; a single file of climbers is all it accommodates. At the top, the stair emerged onto the Tower's summit platform — what stood or was done there, no deposit survives to say directly, and every reconstruction of the summit (lookout, platform for display, roofed cap) is an inference from the stair, the mass, and the position, not a find.

The labour accounting has been done more than once, and the figures are worth stating with their authors. Kenyon's own estimate, repeated through the literature, was that the wall and Tower together would have taken a hundred men more than a hundred days to build. Naveh, extending Bar-Yosef's estimates on the basis of half a cubic metre of construction per worker per day, calculated at least 10,400 working days for the complex; the figure of roughly 11,000 working days circulates widely for the Tower alone. The estimates differ in their assumptions, but they converge on the conclusion that matters: this was a communal work, requiring the organised labour of the whole settlement — plausibly of more than the settlement — sustained over a season or repeated seasons, by a community of at most a few thousand and perhaps only a few hundred people (§II). Whoever ordered it, persuaded it, or shamed it into being, the Tower is the material signature of a social fact: by 8000 BCE, Jericho could mobilise labour on a monumental scale, five and a half millennia before the pyramids.

A final construction detail carries interpretive weight, and the excavators noticed it from the first: the Tower was built inside the perimeter. It does not project outward from the wall, as a fighting tower must if its defenders are to shoot or drop missiles along the wall's outer face; it rises behind the wall, within the town, and its staircase is entered from the town side. Whatever it was for, its architecture is not the architecture of a bastion. That single observation, developed by Bar-Yosef, is the hinge on which the whole interpretation debate of the next section turns.

IV. What Was the Tower For? — The Interpretation Debate

Four main readings of the Tower stand in the published literature. They are not equally supported, and this section states each with its author, its evidence, and its standing — because at Jericho the history of the interpretations is part of the evidence for how archaeology handles a monument whose builders left no text.

Kenyon — A Fortress Tower

Kenyon's reading was the first and, for a generation, the standard one: the Tower was part of a system of defences — wall, ditch, and tower together — with which the PPNA town protected itself and its stored wealth (Kenyon, Digging Up Jericho, 1957; Kenyon & Holland, Excavations at Jericho III, 1981). The reading had an obvious strength: the ensemble looks like fortification, and its scale implies something worth defending. It also fitted a mid-twentieth-century expectation that stored agricultural surplus and organised violence arrive together. Its weaknesses were visible in Kenyon's own plans, and it was left to Bar-Yosef to enumerate them.

Bar-Yosef — Flood, Silt, and a Communal Centre

Ofer Bar-Yosef's "The Walls of Jericho: An Alternative Interpretation" (Current Anthropology 27, 1986: 157–162) remains the debate's pivot. His argument proceeds from the sections. The wall, he showed from Kenyon's drawings, was rebuilt and thickened repeatedly at short intervals; the ditch in front of it filled rapidly; and the deposits banked against the wall's western face were not occupation debris but natural alluviation — silt and gravel washed down from the hills. The enemy at Jericho's west wall, on this evidence, was not an army but the wadi flood: flash floods and mudflows descending from Jebel Quruntul after winter rain, in a climate the palaeoenvironmental indicators suggest was wetter than today's, repeatedly burying the settlement's edge in silt. The wall was a flood-defence and silt barrier, heightened as the deposits rose; the ditch was its catchment, cleaned and re-cut until the labour of maintaining it was abandoned and the staircase itself blocked by the rising ground. As for defence against people, Bar-Yosef stated the negative plainly: there is no archaeological evidence — no burned settlements, massacre deposits, or unambiguous fortifications — for organised social aggression in the Levant before the sixth millennium BCE; and a tower built inside its wall, from which defenders cannot cover the wall's outer face, is not a fighting platform in any case. His positive proposal for the Tower was accordingly social and ritual rather than military: a massive communal structure raised above the flood line, associated in his reconstruction with communal storage and serving as a centre of ritual activity — a monument of the community to itself, whose building was itself a social act.

The Critics and the Cosmological Readings

Bar-Yosef's flood hypothesis was itself challenged on hydrological grounds. Ronen and Adler (2001) argued that the water sources around Jericho could not have generated floods of the volume the silt-defence reading requires — the wadis are either too small or too distant — and proposed instead a symbolic defence: a wall and tower built massively toward the west, where the sun sets, as a bulwark against malign forces, shifting the ensemble's purpose from engineering to cosmology. That cosmological direction was then given a precise, testable form by Roy Liran and Ran Barkai of Tel Aviv University ("Midsummer Sunset at Neolithic Jericho," Time and Mind 1, 2008; developed in Antiquity, 2011). Using computer modelling of the ancient landscape, they showed that the axis of the Tower's staircase aligns with the setting sun at the summer solstice behind the highest peak of Jebel Quruntul, and that on solstice evening the mountain's shadow falls first on the Tower and then spreads across the whole town. The Tower's conical profile, they argued, echoes the conical peak it faces. Their reading of its function follows: the Tower was a cosmological marker and an instrument of social power — a monument that linked the town to the hills and the heavens, created awe at the turning of the year, and served those who organised its building as a warrant for the new disciplines of farming and hierarchy. Archaeoastronomers outside the team have counselled the usual caution — alignments can arise by chance in a complex landscape, and intentionality is hard to prove — but the alignment itself is a matter of measurement, and it stands in the literature as the most specific claim ever made for the Tower's design.

Where does the debate rest? The defensive reading in Kenyon's original form — a fortress tower against human enemies — has lost the most ground, on the architectural objection (an internal tower cannot fight) and on the absence of any contemporary evidence for warfare in the region. The flood reading commands the stratigraphy of the wall and ditch, which it explains better than any rival, while its hydrology remains disputed. The astronomical reading accounts for the staircase axis and the Tower's siting in a way neither rival attempts. The readings are not, in fact, mutually exclusive in every part: a silt barrier can carry a communal monument, and a communal monument can be aligned on the solstice. What the literature no longer sustains is the simple picture with which the site entered the textbooks — the world's first fortress, built by the world's first farmers against the world's first army. The stones are older and stranger than that.

V. Wall, Ditch, and the Word "Fortification"

The Tower did not stand alone. On the western side of the settlement, where Kenyon's trenches cut deepest, the PPNA perimeter consisted of three elements in sequence from inside out: the Tower, the wall, and the ditch. The first perimeter wall, as Bar-Yosef summarised Kenyon's sections, stood about 3.6 metres high, 1.8 metres thick at its base and about 1.1 metres at its preserved top — a massive stone construction by any Neolithic standard, of the same undressed limestone as the Tower. In front of it the inhabitants dug a ditch into the bedrock, on the order of eight metres wide and nearly three metres deep in the sections where it is best preserved. Around this first ensemble the ground then rose, inside and out, by the combined accumulation Bar-Yosef documented: natural alluviation against the outer face, occupational build-up within. The response, in successive stages, was a second wall — or a thickening of the first — then a third, each raising the barrier as the silt rose against it, until in the final stages the ditch had filled, the works were abandoned, and the Tower's own staircase was blocked by the deposits it had been built to overlook.

Read as a sequence rather than a snapshot, the western perimeter is therefore not one fortification but a centuries-long negotiation with a rising ground surface. That sequence is the strongest single piece of evidence in the whole debate of §IV, and it is evidence of a particular kind: it is very hard to explain by human attack, since armies do not deposit laminated silt against a wall at a steady rate for centuries, and very easy to explain by water, slope-wash, and a settlement generating its own debris on the inside of its own barrier. Bar-Yosef's reading was built on exactly this asymmetry, and subsequent discussion has not displaced it from the wall and ditch, whatever is concluded about the Tower.

What, then, does "fortification" mean at this date? The word smuggles in a garrison, a siege, and an enemy, and at 8000 BCE none of the three is attested at Jericho or anywhere in the contemporary Levant. The defensible vocabulary is the excavators' more careful one: a perimeter work — a wall, ditch, and tower ensemble that unquestionably enclosed the settlement, controlled its western approach, kept animals in or out, marked a boundary between the town and the world, and, on the stratigraphic evidence, held back water and silt. Enclosure is a fact; fortification, in the military sense, is an interpretation, and at Jericho it is currently the weaker one. This hub's usage follows the evidence: the ensemble is called a perimeter, a wall, a work — and the site is described, as UNESCO's listing describes it, as a permanent settlement with a wall, a ditch, and a tower by the ninth to eighth millennium BCE. The popular formula "the oldest fortified city in the world" compresses three contestable words — fortified, city, and the superlative itself — into a slogan the strata do not quite sign. What they sign is remarkable enough: the earliest known town wall, the earliest known town ditch, and the earliest known stone tower, built together by one small farming community at the dawn of settled life.

THE WALL

First phase c. 3.6 m high, 1.8 m thick at the base, of undressed stone; thickened and succeeded by further walls as deposits rose against it — a sequence of rebuildings, not a single build.

THE DITCH

Rock-cut, on the order of 8 m wide and nearly 3 m deep, in front of the wall on the west. It filled rapidly with silt and debris — the observation from which the flood reading of the whole ensemble begins.

THE TOWER

Inside the wall, not projecting from it; entered from the town; solid but for its stair. Architecturally a communal monument, whatever else it was — not a bastion.

Wall, ditch-filling, heightened wall, blocked stair: the western perimeter records centuries of rising silt and rising occupation, and was finally abandoned to both.

VI. The PPNB Town — Houses, Floors, and Plaster

Between the PPNA village and the town of the plastered skulls there is a break in the sequence — a hiatus in occupation after the PPNA, on which the excavators agree in outline if not in every detail — and then a refoundation. The Pre-Pottery Neolithic B (PPNB) settlement at Jericho, conventionally from the earlier seventh millennium BCE in the site's current periodisation (the Italian-Palestinian scheme runs its PPN broadly c. 8500–6000 BCE, with the classic PPNB town of the skulls in its middle phases, c. 7400–6500 BCE), is a different kind of place from its predecessor. Where the PPNA built round, the PPNB built rectangular: multi-roomed houses of mud brick on stone foundations, laid out room against room in the rectilinear manner that becomes the Levantine standard. The change of plan is one of the period's signatures across the region, and Jericho — where Kenyon first defined the PPNA/PPNB contrast — is its type-site.

The PPNB town's defining material is neither stone nor brick but lime plaster. Its production was an industry: limestone burned at temperatures in the range of 600–900 °C to make quicklime, slaked, and worked into a plaster that could be laid in floors of remarkable quality — thick, burnished, sometimes coloured — spread over walls, and modelled in the round. PPNB Jericho's houses have plaster floors that were renewed again and again, layer upon layer, across a building's life. The same material, in the same centuries, coats the skulls of §VII and builds the statues of §IX: plaster is the medium in which this community expressed everything from domestic finish to the faces of its ancestors, and the fuel and labour its burning demanded — wood in quantity, in a landscape where wood was not free — are part of the period's economy, debated in the environmental literature of the wider PPNB world.

Subsistence, meanwhile, had completed the transition the PPNA began. The PPNB town farmed emmer, barley, lentils, and other pulses intensively — the Italian-Palestinian expedition's work in the oasis has added evidence for the early management of the spring's water in channels for cultivation — and herded sheep and goats under domestication, while hunting, gazelle above all, continued to contribute. Burial practice kept the PPNA pattern and elaborated it: the dead under the floors of houses, in primary burials from which, after an interval, the skull was in selected cases retrieved. It is that retrieval, and what was done with the skull afterwards, that the next two sections follow in procedural detail — because at Jericho the procedure survives completely enough to be reconstructed step by step, and because it produced the most famous objects ever found in a Neolithic context.

// A REGIONAL PHENOMENON //

Jericho did not plaster its skulls alone. Modelled and painted skulls are known from 'Ain Ghazal, Beisamoun, Kfar HaHoresh, Yiftahel, Tell Ramad, Tell Aswad, and Köşk Höyük — some seventy examples across the Levant and Anatolia in the usual counts, about half of them badly deteriorated. Jericho's distinction is priority and preservation: the first found, the largest single-site group, and the best published.

The scale of the PPNB town should be stated plainly, because the skulls' fame inflates it in the imagination. It was a large village or small town of the same few hectares as its PPNA predecessor — one of the substantial settlements of the southern Levantine PPNB, though smaller than the great Transjordanian "mega-sites" such as 'Ain Ghazal at their height. Its importance is not size. It is the completeness of what it preserved: houses, floors, burials, skulls, and statues in stratified association, excavated and published to a standard that still anchors the period's study — and a mortuary art that has no parallel, for naturalism attempted at full scale, anywhere else in the Neolithic world.

VII. The Plastered Skulls — How a Face Was Made

In 1953, in the closing days of the season, Kenyon's team noticed a skull protruding from the section of a trench. Her own rule forbade digging into a standing section; the find was too important, and the rule was broken — the excavators spent days burrowing into the section face to recover what proved to be a cache of seven plastered skulls (registered D110–D116), set in a pit beneath a PPNB house floor. Two more followed in 1956 (D117–D118) and a further example in 1958 (E22), with later counts from the full Kenyon archive bringing the modelled Jericho series to about a dozen specimens, plus two painted only. The seven of 1953 remain the core group: the first plastered skulls ever recognised, and still the most vivid.

What the makers did to a skull can be reconstructed as a procedure, because the finished objects, the unfinished evidence, and — since 2009 — the CT imaging of §VIII preserve its stages. The sequence below follows the published descriptions (Kenyon 1957; Strouhal 1973; the British Museum's technical studies) and states each stage at the strength the objects themselves support.

Stage One — Burial, Waiting, and Retrieval

The process began with an ordinary burial. The dead of PPNB Jericho were buried, typically beneath the floor of a house, and left until the soft tissues had decayed — an interval the regional literature estimates in months rather than weeks. The grave was then reopened and the skull removed, the cranium separated from the rest of the skeleton, which was left in the ground. Not every dead person received this treatment: the modelled skulls number in dozens against a buried population in hundreds, and the selection — elders, founders, particular kin — is inferred from the practice's rarity, not recorded. The unmodelled majority of separated skulls (the PPNA groups of §II, and plain PPNB examples) show that retrieval was the widespread rite; plastering was its rare, elaborate form.

Stage Two — Preparation of the Cranium

The retrieved cranium was cleaned of remaining soft tissue — defleshed — and the mandible was removed: the plastered skulls are crania without lower jaws, the face modelled over the upper jaw and the maxilla alone. The empty cranium was then made structurally ready to carry a modelled face. In the British Museum example, imaging revealed the method exactly: a round piece of bone was cut from the back of the skull, the cranial cavity was packed with soil to support the weight of the plaster and give the object solidity, and the opening was sealed with a ball of fine clay — the cut disc of bone perhaps replaced over it, though in this specimen it has not survived. The skull had become a core: a light, fragile shell converted into a solid head-shaped armature.

Stage Three — Modelling the Face in Plaster

Over the front of the prepared cranium the maker now built a face in lime plaster, applied and modelled by hand while wet. The modelling is anatomical in intent and in sequence: the plaster fills out the cheeks, builds the bridge and flesh of the nose, forms the lips over the toothless upper jaw, and shapes the chin and jaw-line where no jaw exists — the lower face is an invention in plaster, carried on the packed core. Ears are formed in plaster on some examples. The plaster is confined to the face and the front of the cranium: it covers the facial bones and the upper jaw and ends around the eye sockets and the temples, while the cranium's dome is left bare bone. The modelling is individualised — the seven of the 1953 cache differ in face shape, in the set of the features, in expression — to a degree that led Kenyon to call them portraits, a judgement §VIII examines. This is hand-modelling of a high order, done quickly enough to work the plaster before it set, by makers who had plainly done it before.

Stage Four — The Eyes

The eyes are the objects' most arresting feature, and they are inlaid, not modelled. Into the plaster of the eye sockets the maker set shells: in most of the Jericho examples, bivalve shells placed in two segments, the join forming a vertical slit read as the pupil; in one celebrated case, cowrie shells serve instead. The effect — a pale, divided eye, open and gazing, in a modelled face — is deliberate and consistent across the group: these faces were made to be looked at, and to look back. The closed, modelled eye of the 'Ain Ghazal statues (§IX) is the contemporary alternative; Jericho's choice is the open shell eye.

Stage Five — Paint, Hair, and Finish

The finished face was painted. Traces of reddish-pink pigment survive on the plaster and on the bare cranium of several examples — a flesh tone, in Kenyon's reading — and darker, blackish bands across the cranium of at least one skull have been read as a painted headdress or hair. Features such as moustaches and hair were rendered in paint on some Levantine examples. The paint layer is the most fragile stage and the worst preserved; its presence, rather than its full design, is what the record establishes. The completed object was then kept — displayed, handled, curated above ground for a period the wear on some examples suggests was not short — before its final deposition: burial in a group, in a pit under a house floor, as the 1953 cache was found. The object's life thus runs burial → retrieval → manufacture → display → reburial, a full cycle that Garfinkel's "life cycle" analysis of the PPNB skulls formalised and that the Jericho evidence exhibits at every stage.

What the skulls meant is the question every account asks and the evidence answers only in outline. Kenyon's interpretation — that they are portraits of venerated ancestors, the founding dead of the household kept present among the living — remains the most widely held, and the British Museum's current presentation states the consensus in its careful form: initially each skull was a known individual; with time, the modelled dead likely became ancestor figures of the community, and were at last reburied as such. Alternatives in the literature — memorial images without cult, trophies, representations of the collectivity rather than the person — are argued from the same objects. What is not in dispute is the labour and the intent: a community that burned limestone, packed crania with soil, modelled faces by hand, and inlaid shells for eyes was making persons, or the memory of persons, durable. Nine and a half thousand years later, the faces still do the work they were made for, which is the plainest measure of the makers' success.

VIII. Under the Plaster — The Caches, Strouhal, and the CT Study

The Jericho skulls have been studied in three waves, and each wave has changed what the objects can say. The first was the excavation itself: Kenyon's recognition, in 1953, of a class of object no one had seen before, published to the world in Digging Up Jericho (1957) with a fame that has never left them. The second was the physical anthropology. Eugen Strouhal's study of five of the 1953 skulls (Paléorient, 1973) established the anatomical baseline — the crania beneath the portraits, measured and described — and the final anthropological report by Kurth and Röhrer-Ertl in Kenyon's Excavations at Jericho III (1981) revised the sexing of the group against Strouhal's first assessment: where he had read the five as adult males, the final report acknowledged a mixed group, women as well as men among the seven. The revision matters to the interpretation: a rite that models women and men is a rite of ancestors or kin, not of a warrior or priestly caste — one of several places where the Jericho literature has corrected its first, dramatic reading in a quieter direction. Nigro's 2017 synthesis of the full Kenyon archive counts forty-five skulls retrieved from the Pre-Pottery Neolithic layers in all — the modelled dozen being only the decorated tip of a much larger practice of skull separation reaching back into the PPNA.

The Manchester Method in Reverse — The British Museum Skull

The third wave is imaging. One of the 1953 skulls is held by the British Museum, and its curator Alexandra Fletcher brought together a team of osteologists, imaging specialists, and 3D modellers to ask what conventional study could not: who was under this plaster? Ordinary X-rays failed — the soil packed inside the cranium (§VII, stage two) obscured the interior. In 2009 the skull was taken to the Imaging and Analysis Centre of the Natural History Museum, London, for a micro-CT scan, which resolved the interior in three dimensions and allowed the plaster and the soil packing to be removed digitally, leaving the bone alone in the model. The study's findings, published by Fletcher and colleagues in the Museum's reporting of 2014–2017, are the most intimate biography Neolithic archaeology has recovered for any individual:

The skull is that of a man, over forty years old at death. His upper teeth — the mandible, as always in these objects, is absent — were broken and badly decayed, with abscesses that must have caused him chronic pain. His nose had been broken in life and had healed. And his head had been bound in infancy: the varying thickness of the cranial bone shows the tight binding that permanently reshaped his skull, broadening the top and back of the head — artificial cranial deformation, practised deliberately on a child who grew up to be, in death, one of the chosen faces of his community. Several of the other Jericho skulls show the same deformation, in the anthropological reports, which raises the possibility that the bound head was a mark of the group from which the modelled dead were drawn. The scan also confirmed the manufacturing evidence of §VII from the inside: the cut at the back of the cranium, the soil packing, the clay seal.

In 2016 the CT data were used to 3D-print the man's actual skull, and forensic facial-reconstruction specialists — working by the Manchester method, building the face muscle by muscle over the bone, with a lower jaw supplied from comparable examples — reconstructed the face the bone itself implies. The result, exhibited at the British Museum in 2017 in the display Creating an ancestor: the Jericho Skull, is a bearded man in his forties with brown eyes — not the plaster face, but the face beneath it. Fletcher's description of the project has become the standard summary of the whole third wave: the reconstruction followed "the ancient process in reverse" — the Neolithic makers built a face over a skull to keep a person present; the modern team stripped a face from a skull to recover one. The two faces, plaster and printed, now stand as the double portrait of a single man of PPNB Jericho, dead some 9,500 years, whose name, language, and kin the record does not preserve and this article does not invent.

IX. Plaster Statues and the 'Ain Ghazal Parallels

The skulls were not the end of Jericho's plaster art. Garstang's excavations of the 1930s had already recovered, from PPNB levels, caches of plaster statues — human figures modelled in plaster, fragmentary and poorly preserved beside the skulls, but unmistakably the same craft carried to the full figure; Kenyon's seasons added a plaster bust from the same horizon. Nigro's 2017 synthesis reads the sequence as one developing tradition of image-making: skulls separated, skulls modelled, and finally free-standing figures — the community's representation of the human form growing, across the PPNB, from the curated head to the whole body. The Jericho statues survive too incompletely to carry the argument alone. Their vindication came from across the Jordan.

At 'Ain Ghazal, the great PPNB settlement on the outskirts of modern Amman, excavations under Gary Rollefson uncovered in 1983 and 1985 two underground caches of plaster statuary: fifteen statues and fifteen busts in total, figures up to about a metre tall — the earliest large-scale representations of the human form known anywhere. The two caches were buried some two hundred years apart (the first dated to about 6750 BCE, the second to about 6570 BCE, in the excavators' radiocarbon series), beneath the floors of abandoned houses. Their manufacture extends the Jericho procedure from the head to the body, and it is worth stating as a procedure, since it is the fullest account the PPNB gives of its own image-making:

The figure began as an armature of reeds — bundles of the riverside grasses of the Zarqa valley, bound with twine, separate bundles for head, torso, and legs joined into a scarecrow-like skeleton. Over this core the makers applied lime plaster — limestone heated to 600–900 °C, slaked, and mixed to a workable dough, in the technical analyses with marl and vegetal fibre — building the body in layers, turning the figure as each face dried so the coating hardened evenly. The heads received the most labour: multiple plaster layers, modelled features, and great eyes of white chalk or plaster ringed and pupilled with black bitumen — the eyes, unlike Jericho's, closed or downcast in effect, outlined rather than inlaid. Some busts carry two heads on one torso, the tradition's most enigmatic variation. The finished figures were used — displayed, on the evidence of their curation and repair — and then, like the skulls, deliberately buried in pits, in groups, with care.

The parallel disciplines the Jericho material in both directions. It confirms that the skulls belong to a regional PPNB image-culture of real scale and technical ambition, not a local eccentricity; and it shows what that culture could do at full stretch, in figures whose preservation Jericho's own statues, found earlier and kept worse, could not match. The interpretations travel together as well: ancestors, deities, and communal emblems have all been argued for the 'Ain Ghazal figures, by Schmandt-Besserat and others, from the same evidence of manufacture, display, and careful burial that Jericho presents — and the honest position, there as here, is that the objects establish the practice completely and its theology not at all. What a farming town of the seventh millennium BCE demonstrably possessed was a monumental figurative art in a perishable medium, made to be kept for a time and then buried. Stone-Age Jericho and 'Ain Ghazal, between them, hold its two great surviving deposits.

The 'Ain Ghazal statues are held principally by the Jordan Museum in Amman, with examples on loan abroad — one in the Louvre, Paris, parts of others in the British Museum, and a two-headed bust displayed at the Louvre Abu Dhabi. The best-known Jericho skull is in the British Museum (§VIII); others of the Kenyon series are in collections in Jerusalem, Amman, and Britain.

One further parallel, closer to the bone, closes the section. The plastered skull and the plaster statue are, technically, the same object at two scales: a perishable or bony core, built up in burned-lime plaster into a person, given eyes, and in the end buried in a pit in company. The PPNB world of the southern Levant — Jericho west of the river, 'Ain Ghazal east of it, and the smaller sites between — practised a single art of remembrance with two instruments. This hub's Göbekli Tepe article treats the northern, earlier expression of the same broad impulse — the carved and curated skull in the world of the enclosures — and the comparison is instructive: at Göbekli the dead are worked in stone and bone; at Jericho, a millennium and a half later and a world more settled, they are given back their faces in plaster.

X. Digging Jericho — From Warren to Kenyon

Tell es-Sultan has been excavated by four major expeditions, and its archaeology cannot be read apart from their succession, because each generation's Jericho is in part its excavator's method made visible. The first probes were Charles Warren's shafts of 1868, sunk for the Palestine Exploration Fund, which missed the Neolithic Tower by less than a metre and reported the mound uninteresting. The first true excavation was the Austro-German expedition of Ernst Sellin and Carl Watzinger (1907–1909, 1911), which established the Bronze and Iron Age city sequence and its great revetment walls. John Garstang, for the British School, directed the second British expedition of 1930–1936: he uncovered the Neolithic levels in depth, found the plaster statue caches of §IX, and — the act that fixed Jericho in the public mind — dated the fiery destruction of his City IV to about 1400 BCE and identified it with the conquest of the Joshua tradition (§XIII).

Kathleen Kenyon's expedition of 1952–1958, for the British School of Archaeology in Jerusalem, is the excavation on which this article, and the modern study of the site, principally rests. Kenyon brought to Jericho the stratigraphic method she had developed with Mortimer Wheeler — the Wheeler–Kenyon method: excavation in a grid of squares separated by standing baulks, so that every deposit is read against a continuous vertical section; layers removed in reverse order of their deposition; finds recorded against the section, in three dimensions, context by context. At a mound of Jericho's complexity — twenty metres of intercutting pits, floors, walls, and dumps — the method was transformative. It produced the deep, controlled sequences of Trench I and its companions from which the PPNA and PPNB were defined; it recovered the Tower, the walls, and the skulls in their stratigraphic places rather than as isolated wonders; and its sections, published in the five volumes of Excavations at Jericho (1960–1983, the later volumes completed with Thomas Holland after Kenyon's death), remain the primary archive from which all subsequent work — including Bar-Yosef's reinterpretation of the walls, which is built explicitly on her drawings — proceeds.

Kenyon's popular account, Digging Up Jericho (1957), written mid-excavation, did a second kind of work: it carried the Neolithic Jericho — the Tower, the skulls, the town older than farming's supposed consequences — to a general readership, and it stated her Bronze Age conclusions with equal clarity. Those conclusions reversed Garstang's. The walls he had given to the Late Bronze Age she redated, in the main, to the Early Bronze Age, a millennium and more earlier; the destruction of City IV she placed at the end of the Middle Bronze Age, about 1550 BCE, a century and a half before the earliest date the conquest tradition is conventionally assigned; and of a Late Bronze Age walled city she found, in her trenches, effectively nothing. §XIII states the present standing of that argument in full. Here it is enough to mark the methodological point Kenyon herself would have put first: her conclusions were not a reading of a text but of a section — of pottery present and absent in sealed contexts, layer by numbered layer — and they have been tested, refined, and in part revised by exactly the means she used, stratigraphy and the radiocarbon laboratory, never by authority.

XI. Kenyon's Framework and the Radiocarbon Revisions

Kenyon's Jericho was dated, in the first instance, the way all tells of her generation were dated: by pottery seriation against the sequences of better-known sites, and by imported objects — Egyptian scarabs above all — with historical dates attached. Radiocarbon, then a new method, she used as it became available, and the history of Jericho's chronology since her seasons is largely the history of the laboratory revising, confirming, and occasionally overturning the trench's first answers. Three revisions matter most.

The first concerns the Neolithic. Kenyon's own uncalibrated radiocarbon results for the PPNB levels produced dates that, read raw, placed the plastered skulls implausibly late — her published dating of the skull group ran to about 6250–5850 BCE in uncalibrated terms. Calibration of the radiocarbon timescale against tree rings, and the accumulation of further dates, moved the whole PPNB earlier: the skulls' horizon is now placed in the Middle PPNB, broadly c. 7200–6700 BCE in the current preferred reading — the "9,500 years ago" of the British Museum's presentation. The Tower's dates underwent the same discipline in the opposite emotional direction: the series from Kenyon's samples published by Burleigh in 1981 and 1983, calibrated, gave construction about 8300 BCE and abandonment of the stair about 7800 BCE — dates older than the field's first intuition, and the foundation of the conventional c. 8000 BCE used throughout this article. The Neolithic Jericho that calibration produced is thus both older and more precisely placed than the one Kenyon dug: her stratigraphy stood; her calendar was rebuilt under it.

The second revision concerns the Bronze Age destruction, and it is the one on which the argument of §XIII turns. In 1995 Hendrik Bruins and Johannes van der Plicht published high-precision radiocarbon measurements on eighteen samples from Kenyon's excavation, held in museum storage — twelve of charcoal and, decisively, six of charred cereal grains from the City IV destruction layer itself. Short-lived samples matter: a grain dates the harvest of the year it burned, not the growth of a long-lived tree. The six grains gave a weighted average of 3306 ± 7 BP, calibrating to a destruction in the late seventeenth or sixteenth century BCE — the authors' summary figure, c. 1562 ± 38 BCE. Their stated conclusion was a single sentence, and it has anchored the debate ever since: the fortified Bronze Age city at Tell es-Sultan was not destroyed about 1400 BCE, as had been proposed in the revival of Garstang's dating (Wood, 1990). The one earlier radiocarbon date that had seemed to support a c. 1400 destruction — a British Museum charcoal measurement — belonged to a batch the Museum itself retracted and corrected, the corrected value falling in line with Kenyon's chronology. Radiocarbon, invoked against her, had on examination voted with her.

The third revision is the work of the Italian-Palestinian expedition, and it is of a different kind: not a new clock but a reconciliation of all the old ones. Nigro's team re-examined the sections, dumps, and published records of every previous expedition alongside its own excavation, and produced a single periodisation of the tell — its Sultan phases, from Sultan Ia (Mesolithic, c. 10,500 BCE) through the Neolithic (Sultan Ib), the Pottery Neolithic and Chalcolithic (Sultan II), the Early Bronze (Sultan III), the Middle Bronze (Sultan IV), the Late Bronze (Sultan V), and the Iron Age and later (Sultan VI and beyond) — into which Garstang's, Kenyon's, and the new results can all be translated. A site dug four times in four terminologies now has one table. That unglamorous achievement is the precondition of everything the recent seasons have added, from the Early Bronze palaces of §XII to the reopened Late Bronze argument of §XIII.

XII. The Bronze Age City — Walls, Palaces, and Tombs

After the Neolithic town, occupation at Jericho continued through the Pottery Neolithic and the Chalcolithic — phases less completely preserved and less fully published than those before and after — into the age of the first true cities. The Early Bronze Age Jericho of the third millennium BCE was a fortified town of consequence: the Austro-German and British excavations traced its successive city walls, including a double fortification system at the southwestern corner re-examined and restored by the Italian-Palestinian expedition, and Nigro's seasons uncovered on the eastern flank of the Spring Hill a palace of Early Bronze III date (Palace G, c. 2700–2350 BCE) overlooking the spring — the seat, in the expedition's reading, of the city's rulers, set deliberately above the water that made the city. The town was destroyed and rebuilt more than once across the millennium; several of the collapsed walls that later generations of visitors have been shown belong to these Early Bronze destructions, a point of some importance for §XIII.

The Middle Bronze Age city (c. 2000–1550 BCE) was Jericho's Bronze Age height. Its defences were rebuilt on the grand scale: a massive stone revetment — the wall the literature calls Cyclopean, from its great rough boulders — girdled the mound's foot, retaining a steep earthen glacis above which the city's mudbrick wall stood; the Italian-Palestinian work added a Middle Bronze tower (Tower A1) and associated houses at the southern foot, and a further curvilinear fortification structure of Middle Bronze II date. Within the walls stood a substantial town, and outside them, in the cemeteries on the surrounding slopes, its dead were buried in shaft tombs whose grave groups — pottery, weapons, furniture, and in famous cases wooden objects and even food preserved by the oasis's dry conditions — Kenyon's tomb volumes published as one of the richest Middle Bronze funerary records in the Levant. This was the city — City IV in Garstang's numbering, Sultan IV in the current scheme — whose end by fire, with its mudbrick walls fallen outward down the glacis and its storerooms full of charred grain, every excavator has found. The destruction is not in dispute. Its date is the subject of the next section.

The end came, on the chronology established in §XI, in the sixteenth century BCE, at the close of the Middle Bronze Age — in the regional frame, the horizon of the expulsion of the Hyksos from Egypt and the wars that opened the Late Bronze Age, though no evidence at Jericho itself names the destroyer, and this article names none. After the destruction the mound lay largely desolate for a time; what followed on it, in the Late Bronze Age, was of a different order from what had fallen — and it is precisely the modesty of that successor settlement, against the fame of the story later attached to the site, that generated a century of argument.

XIII. The Late Bronze Question — The Walls, Again

This section states the excavation record for Late Bronze Age Jericho as it now stands, the opposed conclusions that have been drawn from it, and the place of the later textual tradition in the argument — in that order, and keeping the three apart, as this hub's tier discipline requires.

What the Record Shows

The undisputed core is small. A walled Middle Bronze city at Jericho was destroyed by fire; its destruction layer contains fallen mudbrick, burned storerooms, and charred grain in quantity. Above and after that destruction, traces of Late Bronze occupation exist but are slight: pottery of Late Bronze I and II date from the tell and, more substantially, from tombs; a structure Garstang called the Middle Building, of fourteenth-century date; and scattered remains badly cut away by erosion, the tell's summit and upper slopes having lost, to wind, rain, and later digging, much of whatever once stood on them. Of a thirteenth-century occupation, and of a Late Bronze destruction layer — a second burned city above the first — the excavations have produced no evidence. Those are the deposits. Everything else is argument about the deposits.

Garstang and Kenyon — Opposite Conclusions

Garstang (1930s) dated the City IV destruction to about 1400 BCE, on the pottery and scarabs as he read them, and identified the fallen walls with the conquest narrated in the Book of Joshua. Kenyon (1950s), excavating with finer stratigraphic control, concluded that the walls Garstang had assigned to the Late Bronze Age were in large part Early Bronze; that City IV fell at the end of the Middle Bronze Age, about 1550 BCE; and that the Late Bronze presence was too slight, and too late layers too eroded, to represent a walled city at any date to which the conquest is conventionally assigned. Her key ceramic observation was negative: the destruction assemblage lacked the imported Cypriot bichrome ware characteristic of the Late Bronze I — an absence she read as a date before that ware's currency.

The argument was then tested from two directions. Piotr Bienkowski's Jericho in the Late Bronze Age (1986) gathered all three expeditions' evidence, including Garstang's unpublished material, and subjected the Late Bronze pottery — much of it from tombs, a methodological point his critics pressed — to full study. His conclusion refined Kenyon's rather than reversing it: Late Bronze Jericho existed, but as a small, unwalled settlement limited largely to the area of the Middle Building, occupied from about 1425 to about 1275 BCE and abandoned early in Late Bronze IIb — "a small and rather simple place, with no signs of wealth or vigorous activity," its few foreign objects a handful of Cypriot pots and Egyptian scarabs. Against it, Bryant Wood (1990) revived Garstang's date, arguing that Kenyon had over-weighted the absent imports against the local pottery, which he read as Late Bronze I, and that the destruction belonged about 1400 BCE. Bienkowski's reply, in the same journal exchange, held the Middle Bronze date; and the radiocarbon programme of Bruins and van der Plicht (§XI) then supplied the independent test, dating the destruction grain to the sixteenth century and concluding explicitly against the c. 1400 proposal. The mainstream standing, as the standard syntheses report it, is accordingly Kenyon's and Bienkowski's: City IV fell at the end of the Middle Bronze Age; the Late Bronze settlement was small and, on the evidence recovered, unwalled; no Late Bronze destruction layer exists.

The most recent work has reopened one corner of that standing, and accuracy requires reporting it. Nigro, synthesising the Italian-Palestinian results (notably in his 2023 overall reconstruction of Late Bronze Tell es-Sultan), argues that the Late Bronze II town reused the Middle Bronze Cyclopean revetment, adding a mudbrick wall about two metres wide on its surviving crest, set back slightly from its front edge — a fortification circuit of some 850 metres enclosing about 4.5 hectares, of which only a few courses of the Late Bronze mudbrick survive. On this reading the Late Bronze town was walled after all, in the economical manner of a small city reusing its predecessor's earthworks. The claim is recent, rests substantially on re-reading the earlier expeditions' field reports together with the new sections, and has not yet displaced the Bienkowski position in the general literature; it is reported here as the current state of an open argument, with its author's name on it. What even this reading does not produce is a Late Bronze destruction: on all current accounts the Late Bronze settlement ends by abandonment in the thirteenth century, not by a burned and fallen city.

The Textual Tradition, in Its Tier

The Book of Joshua narrates the fall of a walled Jericho to an Israelite conquest, its walls collapsing and the city burned. That narrative is a Tier B source — an ancient textual tradition — and it is treated as such in this archive: as evidence for the tradition's own history and meaning, in the divisions that handle texts. Within this Tier A article its only proper appearance is as the reason the Bronze Age dates matter beyond the site, and the comparison can be stated in one factual paragraph. The walled city whose destruction the excavations document fell about 1550 BCE, a century and a half before the earliest conventionally proposed conquest date (c. 1400 BCE) and three centuries before the later one (c. 1250 BCE); at both dates, on the excavation record as it stands, Jericho held at most a small settlement, and no destruction layer of either date has been found. Garstang's identification of City IV with the narrative required his c. 1400 date, which the ceramic restudy and the radiocarbon programme have not sustained; Wood's revival of that date remains a minority position in the published literature. No position is taken here on the tradition itself — its genre, its antiquity, its theological claims are not archaeological questions — and none is needed: the strata say what they say, and the text says what it says, and this hub keeps the record, the tradition, and the telling apart precisely so that each can be read at full strength without borrowing the other's.

// EROSION — THE HONEST COMPLICATION //

Every negative claim about Late Bronze Jericho carries one standing qualification, entered by Kenyon herself: the tell's upper levels are severely eroded, and Late Bronze remains that once stood on the summit may simply be gone. Absence of a Late Bronze wall in the surviving record is therefore weaker evidence than presence would be. The qualification cuts the confidence of the negative; it does not supply the missing wall, the missing destruction, or a different date for the destruction that does survive — which the grain in its storerooms dates to the sixteenth century BCE.

XIV. The Long Afterlife — Iron Age to the Present Park

The mound's story after the Bronze Age is one of recurrence at diminishing scale, and it can be told in outline, as its evidence warrants. An Iron Age settlement occupied parts of the tell — the Italian-Palestinian periodisation's Sultan VI phases, with Iron II houses and installations at the southern foot among the recent finds — and occupation of the oasis continued through the Persian, Hellenistic, Roman, and Byzantine periods, though the centre of settlement shifted repeatedly: to the winter palaces of the Hasmonean and Herodian age in the plain at Tulul Abu el-'Alayiq, to the Roman and Byzantine town near the spring, and in the early Islamic period to the Umayyad palace of Khirbat al-Mafjar — Hisham's Palace, with its famous mosaics — a few kilometres north. The tell itself became what tells become: a ruin-mound and landmark beside the living oasis, its spring still flowing, its name carried by the town that moved around it. Crusader, Ayyubid, Mamluk, and Ottoman Jericho succeeded one another in the plain; the modern city of Ariha, a Palestinian city in the West Bank, is the current holder of the eleven-thousand-year tenancy.

The modern chapter of the tell itself is one of protection catching up with fame. The Italian-Palestinian expedition, from 1997 onward, joined excavation to conservation from the start — restoring the Bronze Age walls and Palace G, re-cutting and presenting Kenyon's Trench I with the Tower, laying out visitor paths and panels — and folded the site into the Jericho Oasis Archaeological Park. On 17 September 2023 the World Heritage Committee inscribed Ancient Jericho/Tell es-Sultan on the UNESCO World Heritage List (criteria iii and iv), as a property of the State of Palestine: the tell together with its spring, managed as a National Archaeological Park by the Palestinian Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities in continuing partnership with Sapienza University of Rome. The inscription cites deposits reaching to about 10,500 BCE and the site's testimony to the Neolithic transition to sedentary life and to early Levantine urban culture — the Tier A substance of this article, in the register of international heritage law.

The visitor's reality, kept brief as it should be, is this. The site is an excavated mound, not a standing city: Kenyon's great trench with the Tower in its depth, stretches of Bronze Age walling and the restored palace on the Spring Hill, the spring house of 'Ain es-Sultan across the road. Interpretation on site has historically been sparse — a guide, human or printed, earns its fee — and the mound is overflown, in the most literal sense, by the Jericho cable car, which carries visitors from beside the tell up Jebel Quruntul to the Monastery of the Temptation on the cliff: the world's longest cable-car ride below sea level, and a fair emblem of the place, where an eleventh-millennium spring, a ninth-millennium tower, a Bronze Age tell, a Byzantine monastery, and a modern resort town occupy a single field of view. The archaeology asks, as it has asked every excavator, to be looked at on its own terms amid the accumulation. This article has tried to supply the terms.

XV. What Jericho Does and Does Not Show

Jericho's importance survives the removal of its superlatives, and it has been assigned more superlatives than any site in this hub. Strip away "oldest city," "first fortress," and "walls that fell," and the excavated remainder is still the type-site of the southern Levantine Neolithic: a spring in the deepest valley on earth, camped by Natufian hunters, settled permanently by about 9000 BCE by cultivators in round mudbrick houses; enclosed, by about 8000 BCE, in a stone perimeter with a rock-cut ditch and a solid stone tower of twenty-two cut steps — communal monumental building by a pre-pottery, pre-metal farming village, among the earliest anywhere; refounded in the PPNB as a rectilinear plaster-floored town that retrieved the skulls of its chosen dead, packed them, modelled their faces in lime plaster, inlaid their eyes with shell, painted them, kept them, and buried them again — the earliest sustained portraiture in the archaeological record — and that carried the same art to free-standing statues; and succeeded, on the same four hectares, by the walled cities of the Early and Middle Bronze Ages, a palace above the spring, and a destruction by fire whose grain the laboratory dates to the sixteenth century BCE. Eleven thousand years at one spring, in strata. That is the attested Jericho, and it needs no adjective.

What Jericho does not show is equally definite. It does not show the world's first war, or any war: no evidence of organised violence attends its famous wall, whose best stratigraphic reading is a defence against water and silt, and whose tower stands inside it, built for a purpose — communal, ritual, astronomical, social — that the literature debates and this article leaves open among its attributed positions. It does not show a "city" in the PPNA by any definition that requires writing, administration, or urban scale: it shows a village that built like one, which is the more interesting fact. It does not show unbroken urban continuity: the tell thins, gaps, and is at times effectively abandoned, whatever the oasis around it sustained. It does not show, in the Late Bronze Age, a walled city destroyed at either date the later conquest tradition is assigned — it shows a small settlement, unwalled on the long-standing synthesis, walled only in the newest and still-contested re-reading, ending by abandonment; and the great destruction it does show belongs to the Middle Bronze Age, a century and a half too early for the correlation once claimed for it. And its plastered skulls, for all their power, do not show their own meaning: ancestor veneration is the prevailing inference from a complete and beautiful procedure, not a caption the makers supplied.

Perhaps that is Jericho's deepest instruction for this hub, and it is the same one Nabta Playa and Göbekli Tepe teach in their different registers: the earliest things are not the simplest. At the very beginning of settled life, in the first centuries of farming, a community of a few hundred or a few thousand people at a desert spring organised the labour to raise stone on a monumental scale, aligned it — perhaps — on the solstice, maintained it against the silt for centuries, and, a millennium later, was modelling the faces of its dead with a naturalism Europe would not attempt again for thousands of years. None of that required kings, priests, scripts, or armies, and none of it is explained by invoking them. The spring flowed; the people stayed; and what they made of staying — tower, wall, plaster, and portrait — is written, layer by layer, in the mound that their staying built. For the road from such beginnings toward the river civilisations of Egypt, compare this hub's Nile Bridge; Jericho is the Levantine witness that the road was walked, in parallel, at more than one spring.
