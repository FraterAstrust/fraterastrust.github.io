This is a Tier A article: everything in it rests on excavated, dated, and published archaeological evidence, and where scholars disagree — as they do about the single-origin model of §V and the dismantled Welsh circle of §X — the disagreement itself is reported with its positions attributed. Archaeoastronomy is audited throughout by the rule this hub applied at Nabta Playa: an alignment demonstrated by survey, or a light effect that can still be watched today, is attested; an elaborate stellar or computational programme reconstructed from the same stones is a claim, and is graded as one. No claim here depends on ancient legend or modern construction, and none is allowed to borrow strength from either.

Between about 4800 and 2000 BCE, the farming peoples of Atlantic and Mediterranean Europe built in large stone on a scale their world had never attempted: passage tombs under cairns the size of hills, temples among the oldest free-standing stone buildings on Earth, rows of standing stones running for kilometres, and stone circles whose lintels were jointed like carpentry. The phenomenon has a conventional inventory figure — some 35,000 megalithic monuments are known across Europe, the count used in Bettina Schulz Paulsson's 2019 synthesis — and a real geography: dense along the Atlantic façade from Iberia to Scandinavia, dense again on the Mediterranean islands, thinner inland. Its builders were Neolithic farmers and herders. They had stone tools, bone and antler, timber, rope, and draught animals late in the story. They had no states, no palaces, no writing, and — for most of the period — no metal. What they had was organisation, and the organisation is the subject of this article as much as the stones.

The article moves as the evidence moves. It begins with scale and with method — how stone was quarried, moved, dressed, and raised, since every later claim about labour and society stands on those procedures — then takes the regions in the order the radiocarbon record presents them: Brittany first, where the earliest monuments stand; the Breton-origin model and its critics; Malta's temple culture; Stonehenge in its phases, with its two great sourcing results and its one great contested claim; Newgrange, where a solstice effect can still be witnessed; and Orkney, where the builders' houses survive with their furniture in place. It closes with the social question the whole phenomenon poses, and with an audit of what the stones do not show.

I. A Continent of Stones — The Phenomenon and Its Scale

"Megalith" is Greek for "large stone," and the word names a building habit, not a culture. Under it, European prehistory groups several distinct kinds of monument. There are tombs: chambers of upright slabs (orthostats) roofed with capstones and buried under mounds or cairns — the simple dolmens, the gallery graves, and the passage tombs whose chambers are reached down a stone corridor. There are standing stones: single menhirs, rows and alignments of them, and the stone circles of Britain, Ireland, and Brittany. There are the temples of Malta, free-standing roofed buildings of dressed megalithic masonry, a type found nowhere else. And there are the earth-and-stone enclosures — henges, cursus monuments, causewayed enclosures — that frame the stone monuments in landscapes like Salisbury Plain and the Boyne Valley. The types overlap in time and region, but they are not one blueprint copied everywhere: a Breton passage tomb, a Maltese temple, and an Orcadian circle are answers in a shared medium to questions each region posed itself. Whether the medium itself had one birthplace is the debate of §V.

The chronology is the first discipline. When radiocarbon dating was young, European megaliths were routinely explained as copies of Near Eastern and Aegean prototypes carried west by missionaries or prospectors — the monuments of the Atlantic were assumed to be too accomplished to be native. Calibrated radiocarbon dating destroyed that scheme in the 1970s: the Breton tombs proved older than the Mycenaean tholos tombs they were supposed to copy, and the Maltese temples older than the pyramids beside which they had been dated by style. Colin Renfrew's generation drew the conclusion that the megaliths were independent regional inventions, and for forty years that was the working consensus. Schulz Paulsson's 2019 model (§V) has reopened the question in a new form — not an eastern origin, but a Breton one, diffused by sea. What no party to the debate disputes is the sequence itself: the earliest megalithic monuments in Europe stand in northwestern France in the middle centuries of the fifth millennium BCE, and the tradition's great regional florescences — Malta from c. 3600, the Boyne passage tombs and Stonehenge's first phase around c. 3300–3000, the Orcadian complex across the same centuries — follow over the next two thousand years.

The second discipline is survival. The 35,000 are the residue. Stone was robbed for roads, walls, and churches for two thousand years; tombs were emptied, circles ploughed flat, menhirs toppled or Christianised with carved crosses. Some of the most famous landscapes are partly modern restorations — at Carnac, many stones were re-erected in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, not always with records of where they had fallen — and every distribution map is therefore a map of survival and recording as well as of building. The phenomenon was larger than its inventory, by an amount that cannot be recovered.

One comparison fixes the hub's frame. At Göbekli Tepe, hunter-gatherers in Anatolia had raised monumental stone enclosures five thousand years before Barnenez — monumentality itself was not invented in Brittany, and this article claims no such thing. What begins in Neolithic Europe is something else: monument-building as the ordinary, repeated practice of farming communities across a whole continent, sustained for nearly three millennia. At Nabta Playa, Saharan herders had already shown that a society without farmers, kings, or cities could quarry, haul, and align large stones. Megalithic Europe is that same lesson written at continental scale, in the imperative mood, by the first farmers.

II. How a Megalith Was Made — Quarry, Sledge, Hammer, Rope

Every social inference in this article — about labour, feasting, and organisation — depends on the physical procedures being feasible with Neolithic means, and on their costs being roughly known. The procedures are reconstructed from three kinds of evidence: the monuments themselves (quarry faces, half-finished stones, stone holes, dressing debris); the tools and materials excavated at building sites; and full-scale experimental archaeology, in which modern teams using only period materials have quarried, moved, and raised multi-tonne stones. The experiments do not prove which method the builders used. They prove which methods work, and what each costs in people and days — which is what the social argument needs.

Quarrying and Dressing

Most megalithic stone was not cut from deep quarries but won from outcrops and surface boulders close to the building site — the sarsens of southern England are glacially scattered surface blocks of silcrete; the flagstones of Orkney split naturally along their bedding planes; the limestones of Malta were cut in shallow open quarries near the temples. Where bedrock was worked, the technique exploited natural joints: a line of the rock was freed by hammering, by wedges driven into cracks — wooden wedges swollen with water are the usual reconstruction, directly evidenced at the Welsh bluestone quarries of §X, where stone wedges and hammerstones were excavated in the working platforms — and by levering with timber. Dressing was done with stone on stone. At Stonehenge, hundreds of hammerstones and heavy mauls of sarsen and flint were found in and around the monument: large mauls for rough shaping, smaller hammerstones for finishing, the surfaces pecked and pounded smooth by hours of percussion. The finest dressing is concentrated where it would be seen — the inner faces of the trilithons and the north-eastern arc of the sarsen circle, the faces that frame the solstitial axis — a distribution that itself records a builder's priorities.

Transport — Sledges, Rollers, Water

On land, the demonstrated vehicle is the timber sledge. A stone lashed to a sledge, hauled by rope teams over a prepared track — of laid timbers, or of ground lubricated with water or fat — can be moved by human muscle at rates the experiments have measured: loads of a tonne by a score of people on the flat, and, by scaling the teams, loads of tens of tonnes. Full-scale trials have repeatedly moved concrete replicas of 10–40 tonnes with teams in the low hundreds. Log rollers are the traditional alternative of the textbooks and are possible under lighter stones, but rollers under the heaviest loads tend to sink, slew, and crush, and most modern reconstructions favour the sledge on a timber trackway, with rollers at most an auxiliary. Where water offered, it was used: the bluestones' journey from Wales (§X) is most plausibly reconstructed as part waterborne — rafts or boats along the coast and up the rivers — because the arithmetic of ton-miles overland for some eighty stones is so punishing, though no Neolithic craft from the journey survives and the route remains inference. The Breton diffusion model of §V is, at bottom, a claim about this same capability: that fifth-millennium communities could move not just stones but an architectural idea, reliably, by sea. Erection — Pits, A-Frames, Cribs, and Levers

An upright was raised into a dug stone hole. The excavated holes at Stonehenge and elsewhere show the standard geometry: one side vertical, the other sloped, so the stone could be slid or lowered base-first down the slope until its foot rested against the vertical face; timber stakes and packing protected the hole's edge; then rope teams, hauling over a timber A-frame or shear legs, pulled the stone upright while levers and counterweights controlled it, and the hole was rammed tight with packing stones and chalk rubble. Lintels and capstones were raised differently: the leading reconstruction, supported by experiment, is the timber crib — a platform of crossed logs built up course by course beside the uprights, the lintel levered up one end at a time, the crib growing under it, until the stone could be slid sideways into place. Earthen ramps are the older textbook suggestion; they leave no trace at Stonehenge and the crib needs none, but at tomb sites a capstone could simply be dragged up the growing mound or cairn itself, and several passage-tomb roofs were almost certainly placed that way, from the back of the monument as it rose. None of this requires any machine beyond rope, timber, lever, and organised muscle. All of it requires the muscle to be organised — fed, timed, directed, and kept safe around loads that kill the careless — which is why the procedures matter to §XVI.

// WHAT EXPERIMENTS PROVE //

An experiment that moves a 40-tonne block with 130 people proves feasibility and cost — that the thing can be done this way, by this many, in this long. It does not prove the builders did it this way. This article cites experimental results only at that strength: as the price list, in labour, against which the social claims of §XVI are checked.

Corbelling — the roofing technique of Malta and the Boyne — is treated where it survives, in §VII and §XIII, because its finest evidence is in the buildings themselves. It belongs to the same procedural family: no arch, no mortar, no metal — only the controlled oversailing of one stone course beyond the one below, held by weight and friction, until the span is small enough to cap. With the methods in hand, the tour can begin where the dates begin.

III. Brittany First — Barnenez and the Early Tombs

The earliest megalithic monuments in Europe stand on the Atlantic coast of Brittany, in the present-day departments of Finistère and Morbihan. The type-site is the great cairn of Barnenez, on the Kernéléhen peninsula near Plouezoc'h, overlooking the bay of Morlaix. Barnenez is a terraced cairn of dry-stone construction about 72 m long, up to 25 m wide and some 8 m high, built in two main phases. Within it are eleven passage graves — stone chambers, some corbel-vaulted, each reached by its own corridor from the cairn's southern face. Radiocarbon dates place the first phase in the range c. 4850–4250 BCE, conventionally summarised as c. 4800 BCE: Barnenez was already old when the first stones of most of this article's monuments were quarried, and it predates the earliest Egyptian pyramid by roughly two thousand years. The cairn's mass has been estimated at some 13,000–14,000 tonnes of stone; the excavators' oft-cited labour figure — quarrying, moving, and building about four worker-days for each cubic metre — gives a sense of a community project measured in years of part-time work rather than in a pharaoh's corvée, a scale that will recur throughout this article.

Barnenez was not an isolated flash. Across the fifth millennium, Brittany and the neighbouring Atlantic regions filled with megalithic tombs: the cairn of Bougon in Poitou, the long mounds and passage graves of the Morbihan, and — crucially for §V — a preceding Breton tradition of monumental earthen graves, large elaborate tombs without stone chambers, from around 5000 BCE. Brittany is the one region of Europe where a local developmental sequence can be walked: earthen long mound, then stone chamber under a cairn, then the great passage tombs of the fourth millennium, among them Gavrinis (c. 3500 BCE), whose passage orthostats carry some of the richest carved decoration of the European Neolithic — axes, arcs, and shield-like motifs pecked across nearly every stone, on slabs that include fragments of an earlier carved menhir reused in the tomb, a palimpsest that shows the tradition already old enough, by 3500 BCE, to be recycling its own monuments. At Locmariaquer, the Er Grah tumulus ran some 140 m long, and beside it stood the stone of §IV.

The builders of these tombs were among Europe's first farmers — communities of the Atlantic Neolithic growing wheat and barley and herding cattle and sheep on the Breton coast — not, as older popular accounts sometimes had it, a mysterious pre-Celtic master race, and not hunter-gatherers: the "hunter-gatherer origin" phrase that attached itself to the 2019 headlines mislabels a farming population that retained strong maritime and hunting traditions, as coastal farmers do. Their tombs were collective: the chambers held the remains of many individuals, deposited over generations, and the grave goods — polished stone axes, callaïs (variscite) beads from Iberian sources, fine pottery — speak of exchange networks already reaching across the sea lanes the diffusion model will need. The tombs faced the sea because the communities lived by it. Whatever else spread from Brittany, it spread from working harbours.

IV. Carnac — Alignments, Tumuli, and the Broken Menhir

South of Barnenez, around the modern town of Carnac in the Morbihan, stands the densest concentration of megaliths on Earth. More than 3,000 standing stones survive there and in the neighbouring communes — the surviving fraction of a far larger Neolithic landscape of tombs, enclosures, and rows, inscribed with the wider Morbihan megalithic landscape on the World Heritage List in 2025. The famous monuments are the alignments: parallel rows of menhirs running across the countryside, in three great groups. At Ménec, 1,099 stones stand in eleven rows over about 1,167 m, the rows ending in stone enclosures at either extremity; at Kermario, some 1,029 stones in ten rows run for about a kilometre; at Kerlescan, 555 stones in thirteen rows form a shorter, squarer system. The stones are undressed local granite, graded in height — tallest at the western ends of the Ménec rows — and set in shallow stone holes packed with wedging stones, the same erection procedure as §II at its simplest: no dressing, no joinery, only selection, haulage, and setting.

The alignments' date and purpose are the two honest difficulties of Carnac, and a Tier A account must state both plainly. Granite cannot be radiocarbon dated, the acid soils destroy bone, and many stones were re-erected during nineteenth- and twentieth-century restorations without complete records — so the rows carry no direct chronology of their own, and their assignment to the Neolithic, broadly the fifth-to-third millennia with the main building usually placed in the fifth and fourth, rests on association with the dated tombs and settlements around them. (A Bayesian programme led by Schulz Paulsson, published in Antiquity in 2025, has since modelled several Carnac-area alignments to c. 4600–4300 BCE on dates from associated contexts — a result that, if it holds, makes the rows nearly as old as Barnenez itself.) As to purpose: the rows have attracted every theory in the archaeoastronomical repertoire, including elaborate lunar observatory claims of the kind audited in §XII, none of which has won general acceptance. Alexander Thom, who surveyed the rows exhaustively with his son, concluded in print that they did not know what the alignments were for. Processional ways, territorial statements, arenas of assembly, and monuments to ancestors have all been proposed; the stones, undated and uninscribed, do not choose among them, and this article follows Thom's example in leaving the question open rather than filling it with astronomy the evidence cannot grade.

The third Carnac monument is a single stone. The Grand Menhir Brisé at Locmariaquer — Er Grah, "the great stone" — now lies in four pieces where it fell or was felled, beside the Er Grah tumulus and the Table des Marchands tomb. Reassembled in the mind, it stood about 20.6 m high and weighed on the order of 280–330 tonnes: the largest stone known to have been transported and erected by Neolithic Europeans, of a granite whose nearest source lies several kilometres away. It appears to have stood at the head of a row of smaller menhirs, and to have fallen — or been pulled down — in later prehistory; earthquake and deliberate destruction have both been argued, and the question is open. Its carved fragments were reused in neighbouring tombs, as at Gavrinis: the Breton tradition, at its height, was already monumental enough to quarry its own past. The Grand Menhir is the calibration point for every transport claim in this article: a community of Breton farmers moved a stone heavier than any single block at Stonehenge or Malta, with stone tools, timber, and rope, in the fifth millennium BCE. The procedures of §II are not conjecture about what such people might manage. They are the minimum the record requires.

V. One Origin by Sea? — The Breton Diffusion Model and Its Critics

In February 2019, Bettina Schulz Paulsson of the University of Gothenburg published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences the most ambitious chronological study the megalithic debate has produced: "Radiocarbon dates and Bayesian modeling support maritime diffusion model for megaliths in Europe" (PNAS 116/9: 3460–3465). She assembled 2,410 radiocarbon dates from megalithic sites, from the "pre-megalithic" monumental graves that precede them, and from contemporary non-megalithic contexts across Europe, filtered them for quality, and modelled the appearance of megalithic architecture region by region. Her conclusions were threefold. First, the earliest megaliths occur in northwest France, in the second half of the fifth millennium BCE — and northwest France is the only region that also has the preceding earthen monumental graves, giving the stone tombs a visible local ancestry there and nowhere else. Second, the subsequent spread falls into three successive phases along the coasts: a first expansion, within some two to three centuries, to southern France, Catalonia, Corsica, Sardinia, and Atlantic Iberia; a second to Britain, Ireland, and the rest of Iberia; a third, in the third millennium, to Scandinavia and northern Europe. Third, the geography of that spread — new tomb types appearing abruptly at coastal sites, specific architectural forms travelling long distances by water while inland regions lag — identifies the vector: the megalithic idea diffused by sea, which in turn implies a level of fifth-millennium seafaring well beyond what the boat evidence alone had established.

The model's reception defines its current standing, and both halves of it belong in this article. The support was immediate and senior: Michael Parker Pearson, the leading Stonehenge archaeologist, told Science's news service that the study demonstrated that Brittany was the origin of the European megalithic phenomenon, and Gail Higginbottom, a specialist in megalithic landscapes, judged the maritime reading plausible. But the qualifications were published alongside the praise, and they are not minor. Parker Pearson himself added that the study did a good job of establishing the Breton priority but did not quite rule out that some later cultures developed megalithic building independently. Karl-Göran Sjögren of Gothenburg — the author's own institution — accepted northwest France as among the first building regions but withheld assent from the single origin: earlier megaliths may yet be uncovered elsewhere, and future redating may push known monuments in other regions earlier. The standing objections, as the debate has settled, are these: the model is only as strong as its date list, and radiocarbon dates date burials and charcoal in and around tombs, not the stones — a region's "first megalith" is a sampling statement as much as a historical one; the three-phase pattern depends on which monument types are counted as megalithic, a classification on which specialists differ; and similarity of tomb form across the sea lanes, while real, does not by itself distinguish an idea carried by voyagers from forms converged upon by similar farming societies facing similar needs — the objection on which Renfrew's independent-invention model had rested for forty years.

// THE MODEL, AT ITS EXACT STRENGTH //

Established by the study: on the largest quality-filtered radiocarbon dataset yet assembled, the earliest megalithic tombs are Breton, the Breton tombs have a local earthen ancestry found nowhere else, and later appearances pattern along the coasts in a sequence consistent with maritime diffusion. Not established: that every regional tradition descends from Brittany — the model cannot exclude independent invention in later phases, and its critics, including sympathetic ones, have said so in print. The Breton origin of the earliest megaliths is now the reference position of the debate; the single origin of all megaliths remains a live claim, reported here as such.

Two further bodies of evidence have entered the debate since 2019, and both cut in instructive directions. Ancient DNA — notably Sánchez-Quinto and colleagues' 2019 sequencing of individuals buried in megalithic tombs in Ireland, Scotland, and Sweden — shows the tomb-builders to have been farming populations of ultimately Anatolian descent mixed with local hunter-gatherers, with kinship structured burials (one Irish tomb held an extended family across generations): the megaliths were built by the ordinary Neolithic population of each region, not by a single wandering master people, which fits diffusion of an idea among related farming communities better than migration of a megalithic tribe. And the sea lanes themselves are independently attested for the period — obsidian, callaïs, and polished axes crossed these waters in quantity — so the model's vector is not an ad hoc invention; what remains unproven is that the architectural idea travelled it as a single package, once. Readers weighing the seafaring claim itself should continue to Early Seafaring, where the direct boat and island-colonisation evidence is audited on its own. Malta, to which the article now turns, is the test case both sides cite: an island temple culture of unmistakably local genius, which any diffusion model must reach by sea — and which resembles nothing in Brittany at all.

VI. Malta — An Island Temple Culture

The Maltese archipelago — Malta, Gozo, and Comino, some 316 km² of limestone in the central Mediterranean, 90 km south of Sicily — holds the most concentrated monumental landscape of the Neolithic world. Farmers reached the islands from Sicily by about 5400–5200 BCE, bringing the full Neolithic package across open water. After two millennia of village life, the islanders entered what Maltese prehistory calls the Temple Period: from the Ġgantija phase (c. 3600–3200 BCE) through the Saflieni phase (c. 3300–3000) to the Tarxien phase (c. 3000–2500 BCE), they built more than thirty stone temples — free-standing, roofed, multi-roomed buildings of megalithic masonry, each organised as a series of semicircular chambers (apses) opening off a central court behind a concave façade and an oval forecourt. UNESCO's inscription of the six principal complexes states their rank without embroidery: the temples "rank amongst the earliest free-standing stone buildings in the world." They are older than Stonehenge's stones and older than the Great Pyramid, and they were built by an island farming population that had no metal, no wheel, no draught animals larger than cattle, and no contact-driven need to impress anyone but themselves.

The oldest and largest complex is Ġgantija on Gozo — the name, "the giantess's tower," preserves the local folklore that a giant built it, the standard folk verdict on megalithic masonry everywhere. Two temples stand side by side inside a single massive boundary wall of coralline limestone: the South Temple (c. 3600–3200 BCE), with five great apses, walls surviving in places to over 6 m, and traces of the wall plaster that once smoothed its interior; and the smaller, later North Temple of four apses. The enclosing wall is itself among the largest megalithic constructions of the islands, built of alternating upright blocks set face-out and edge-out (§VII). On Malta's southern coast, 500 m apart on a ridge above the sea, stand Ħaġar Qim and Mnajdra. Ħaġar Qim, of the softer globigerina limestone, is the most inventive plan of the group — a central passage threading a cluster of apses and independent chambers, with carved altar tables, mushroom-shaped offering tables, and one of the largest single blocks in any Maltese temple in its outer wall. Mnajdra, below it, preserves three temples, of which the Lower Temple carries the alignment evidence audited in §VII's closing paragraphs. Inland, the Tarxien complex (c. 3000–2500 BCE) is the culture's late masterpiece: four linked temples with relief carvings of spirals and of domestic animals — bulls, goats, a sow with piglets — cut into altar blocks, and the remains of its colossal statue (§VIII). Between them, these buildings served their communities for over a thousand years.

What the temples were must be stated at the evidence's strength. They were not houses — no domestic assemblage belongs to them — and not tombs: the culture buried its dead elsewhere, in rock-cut tombs and the Hypogeum of §VIII. Their finds are consistent and strange: animal bones in quantity, including deposits of skulls and horn cores; stone and clay figurines; carved altars and libation holes; evidence of fire; and, at several temples, arrangements of rooms that grade access from the public forecourt through the central court to small inner apses reachable only through porthole slabs — architecture that stages a progression from the many to the few. The conventional label "temple" is an interpretation, but it is the interpretation the buildings' own plan argues: communal ritual buildings, used for ceremonies involving animals, fire, and restricted inner rites, maintained and enlarged over centuries. Who officiated, and what was believed, no evidence says. The Maltese temples are the purest case in this article of monumental architecture as a society's central institution — built by a population of, on most estimates, only some thousands of people, on islands with no stone to spare and no neighbours to overawe.

VII. Malta in Stone — Walling, Corbelling, and the Roofing Problem

Maltese temple building is a complete technology, and it can be described stepwise because its elements survive in combination. The islands offer two limestones, and the builders used each where its properties belonged — the material knowledge UNESCO's assessment singles out. Coralline limestone, hard, grey, and weather-resistant, forms the outer walls and the Ġgantija boundary. Globigerina limestone, soft, pale, and easily cut when fresh, forms the sheltered interiors, the doorways, and every carved element. A temple wall is not a single skin but a built sandwich: an outer face of large coralline blocks, set alternately face-out and edge-out so that headers tie deep into the wall's core; an inner face of upright slabs (orthostats); and between them a packing of earth and rubble that binds the whole into one mass. Façades rise from a bench-like plinth of orthostats; doorways are trilithons — two uprights and a lintel — or porthole slabs pierced with a rectangular opening, dressed with stone hammers, chisels of flint and imported obsidian, and abrasion. Floors are flagstone, or torba — crushed globigerina laid on rubble, wetted and pounded to a hard, polishable surface, a recipe closer to concrete-making than to paving. Interiors were plastered and, in places, painted with red ochre, traces of which survive at Ġgantija and elsewhere.

Above the orthostats, the walls continue in horizontal courses of smaller blocks that step progressively inward — the beginnings of a corbelled vault. The mechanics are those of §XIII's Irish vault in miniature: each course oversails the one below by a few centimetres; the load of the courses above and behind counterweights the overhang; the span narrows course by course until it can be closed. The surviving horizontal masonry at several temples demonstrates that the apses were carried up in this way to a considerable height. Whether any apse was ever closed entirely in stone is the roofing problem, and it is genuinely open. No collapsed stone roof has been excavated in any temple; the spans of the largest apses (over 6 m at Ġgantija) strain what corbelling in this limestone can do; and two pieces of the culture's own evidence point the other way — a small stone temple model from Ta' Ħaġrat, which appears to show a slab roof, and a carved graffito at Mnajdra showing a façade with a pitched or slabbed top. The reconstruction most specialists now work with is mixed: corbelled stone carried as high as the span allowed, closed by timber beams, brushwood, and clay or hide above — UNESCO's summary, "corbelled roofs, probably capped by horizontal beams," states the consensus at its proper tentativeness. What is not in doubt is the walling below the roofline, which survives, and which was sophisticated enough that the roofing question is an argument about the last two metres of buildings whose first six are fact.

Transport on Malta was short-distance — no quarry is far on a small island — but the blocks are not small, and the culture left a candidate tool in the temples themselves: spherical stone balls, found at several temple sites, conventionally interpreted as rollers or ball-bearings over which blocks were manoeuvred into place, the stone-age equivalent of the machine skate. The interpretation is plausible and unproven — the balls may have served other purposes — and is reported here at that strength, as the Maltese counterpart of the sledge-and-trackway reconstruction of §II.

Mnajdra's Sun — The Alignment, Graded

Mnajdra's Lower Temple is the Maltese case for the audit method of this article. The building's main axis is oriented so that on the equinoxes, the rising sun shines directly through the entrance passage and illuminates the central axis of the temple — an effect observed and photographed in modern times, and repeatable by any visitor on the right morning. On the solstices, the sunrise light entering the same passage falls instead on the edges of the great uprights flanking the inner doorway — to the left at the summer solstice, to the right at the winter — a graded pair of effects that archaeoastronomers including Michael Hoskin, who surveyed the Maltese temples' orientations systematically, have documented and that Heritage Malta now presents at the site. Grade the claims in order. The geometry is surveyed fact. The light effects are observed, current, and repeatable — the strongest class of archaeoastronomical evidence there is, short of a text. That the effects were intended is an inference, but a strong one: a single doorway producing equinox centre-light and both solstice edge-lights is a pattern chance assembles reluctantly. What does not follow — and what this article does not claim — is a Maltese observational astronomy, a calendar priesthood, or stellar alignments of the kind popular literature adds to Mnajdra: no stellar programme at the Maltese temples has been demonstrated to the standard the solar effects meet. The temple keeps the sun's year at its door. That, demonstrated, is enough.

VIII. The Hypogeum, the Figurines, and the End of the Temples

The temples' rock-cut counterpart stands — or rather descends — at Paola, near Tarxien. The Ħal Saflieni Hypogeum is an underground complex cut into the globigerina bedrock in three superimposed levels, reaching some 10.6 m below the surface, developed across the same centuries as the temples (c. 4000–2500 BCE, with its main use in the Saflieni and Tarxien phases). Its upper level began as natural caves enlarged by hand; the middle level is the masterpiece — chambers whose carved ceilings imitate, in negative, the architecture of the surface temples, with corbelled and slab-roofed forms cut from the living rock, red-ochre spiral paintings on one ceiling, and a small chamber whose acoustics have attracted modern claims this article notes only to set aside: resonance in a stone room is a property, not a programme. The Hypogeum served as a burial place on a communal scale: the excavators of the early twentieth century reported the remains of some 7,000 individuals from its deposits — a figure that is an early excavator's estimate from commingled bone, to be held at that strength — accompanied by figurines, amulets, and pottery. It is best understood as the temples' other half: above ground, the buildings for the rites of the living; below, a built underworld for the collective dead, decorated to resemble the world above.

From the Hypogeum and the temples comes the culture's famous figurative art, and it requires the discipline this hub applies to female figurines everywhere (compare Mehrgarh, §XIII of that article). The corpus includes small clay and stone figures of ample, full-bodied human forms — the so-called "fat ladies" of the older literature — and, supremely, two works: the Sleeping Lady from the Hypogeum, a clay figure some 12 cm long of a full-bodied woman asleep on a couch, modelled with a naturalism unique in Neolithic art; and the colossal statue from Tarxien, of which the lower half survives — a figure in a pleated skirt whose full height is estimated at 2.5–3 m, which stood in the South Temple's apse. What can be said on the evidence is descriptive: the culture repeatedly represented the well-fed human body, at scales from amulet to monumental; the figures come from temples and tombs; the Tarxien colossus stood where offerings were made. What the evidence does not supply is an identity. "Mother goddess," fertility cult, priestess, ancestor, and deity are all modern labels laid on figures that carry no inscription and no unambiguous attribute; the older literature's confident fertility reading is a projection of its own period, and this archive reports the figures as the culture's central images, meaning unrecovered — which is a finding, not an evasion. A civilisation is allowed to have had a theology we cannot reconstruct.

Around 2500 BCE, the temple culture ends. The temples go out of use — Tarxien's were reused, centuries on, as a Bronze Age cremation cemetery by a different people — and the succeeding Tarxien Cemetery phase shows a new population with new pottery, new burial rite, and metal. No destruction layer, massacre, or invasion horizon has been demonstrated; the explanations in the literature are slower ones: ecological strain on small islands farmed intensively for two millennia, soil exhaustion and erosion, the fragility of an inward-looking ritual economy, and simple demographic replacement across the Sicily channel have all been argued, most fully in the syntheses of Caroline Malone and Simon Stoddart and their colleagues, who stress how complete the cultural break appears against how peaceful the stratigraphy looks. Malta's lesson for this article is double. A few thousand farmers, in total isolation, sustained monumental architecture for eleven centuries — the strongest single proof in Europe that the megalithic impulse needed neither kings nor contact. And then, without catastrophe anyone can find, they stopped, and the temples stood empty for four thousand years until archaeology returned to them. Monumental traditions are mortal. That, too, is attested.

IX. Stonehenge I — Earthwork, Aubrey Holes, and the Cremation Cemetery

Stonehenge, on Salisbury Plain in Wiltshire, is the most excavated, most dated, and most argued-over megalithic monument in the world, and its first lesson is that the famous stones are the middle of the story, not the beginning. The phased sequence used here follows the synthesis of Darvill, Marshall, Parker Pearson, and Wainwright (Antiquity, 2012), which reorganised a century of excavation and radiocarbon dating into five stages running from c. 3000 to c. 1600 BCE; Mike Parker Pearson's Stonehenge: Exploring the Greatest Stone Age Mystery (2012) gives the same sequence its fullest public account. Stage 1 (c. 3000–2920 BCE) is an earthwork: a circular ditch some 110 m across, dug in segments with antler picks — the discarded picks and the bones placed in the ditch bottom date its digging — with banks inside and out, a wide north-eastern entrance and a narrower southern one. Just inside the bank runs the ring of 56 Aubrey Holes, chalk pits about a metre across, named for the seventeenth-century antiquary John Aubrey. What stood in them is contested to this day — timber posts, on the older reading; Parker Pearson and colleagues, re-excavating Aubrey Hole 7 in 2008, argued the holes are too shallow for posts and held the first bluestones (§X), a claim Darvill's 2022 critique disputes on the regional evidence of post-circles preceding pit-circles — and the contest is reported here unresolved, because it is.

What Stage 1 Stonehenge unquestionably was is a cemetery. Cremated human bone was deposited in the Aubrey Holes, in the ditch, and across the interior, over some five centuries: the excavated and re-analysed remains represent on the order of 150 individuals, men, women, and children — the largest known cremation cemetery of Neolithic Britain. Isotope analysis of the cremated bone (Snoeck and colleagues, 2018) added a fact that reframes the monument: a significant fraction of those buried — around ten of the twenty-five individuals sampled in that study — had spent their last years far from Salisbury Plain, in a geology consistent with west Wales. People, as well as stones, travelled to Stonehenge from the bluestone country. Around the earthwork, the plain was already a monumental landscape: the Greater Cursus, a 3 km earthwork avenue of unknown purpose, predates the stones; timber circles stood at Woodhenge and Durrington Walls (§XVI); and some 350 round barrows would eventually crowd the skyline. Stonehenge was never a lone marvel in empty country. It was the stone centre of a ritual landscape that farming communities built, visited, and buried their dead in for fifteen centuries.

To those stones, and to where they came from, the article now turns.

X. Stonehenge II — Bluestones from Preseli and the Waun Mawn Claim

The smaller stones of Stonehenge — the "bluestones," some eighty of them originally, of spotted dolerite, rhyolite, and related rocks, each of roughly 2–5 tonnes — are not English. Geological provenancing, begun by H. H. Thomas in 1923 and refined by Richard Bevins, Rob Ixer, and colleagues to individual outcrops, ties the dolerites to Carn Goedog and the rhyolites to Craig Rhos-y-felin, both in the Preseli Hills of Pembrokeshire, west Wales — some 225 km from Salisbury Plain as the crow flies, and considerably farther by any route a stone can travel. Excavations at both outcrops (Parker Pearson and colleagues, Antiquity 2015 and 2019) found what the quarrying model predicts: recesses where pillars had been prised from the naturally jointed rock, artificial platforms, stone wedges and hammerstones, and a hollow way down which stones could be lowered — dated, at Craig Rhos-y-felin, to the centuries around 3400–3000 BCE, matching Stonehenge's first stage. The quarries are the strongest single evidence that the bluestones were deliberately fetched: the minority alternative — that glacial ice carried Preseli erratics east and the builders merely collected them near Salisbury Plain, argued for decades by Brian John and other geologists — must explain why the stones match specific quarried outcrops with Neolithic working floors, and why no comparable erratic train litters the Plain. The mainstream position, held at that strength here, is human transport: by sledge and, most plausibly for much of the way, by water, down the Welsh coast and up the rivers — a journey whose labour, for stones of a few tonnes each, was well inside the capabilities of §II.

Waun Mawn — The Claim

In 2021 the same research programme raised the stakes. Parker Pearson and colleagues reported in Antiquity (95: 85–110) their excavations at Waun Mawn, a moor on the northern flank of Preseli, where four standing stones survive in an arc. Around them, excavation found a series of empty stone holes which the team fitted to a circle of 110 m diameter — the same diameter as Stonehenge's Stage 1 ditch. One excavated hole (stonehole 91) preserved the imprint of a stone of distinctive pentagonal cross-section, which the team matched to Stone 62 at Stonehenge, one of the few pentagonal bluestones there. Optically stimulated luminescence dating of the hole sediments placed the circle's construction at c. 3400–3200 BCE and its dismantling before 2120 BCE. The proposal: Waun Mawn was a great stone circle — among Britain's largest — built in the Preseli homeland, then deliberately dismantled, its stones carried to Salisbury Plain to become the first Stonehenge, in a migration the authors connected to the isotope evidence (§IX) that people from the Welsh geological zone were cremated at Stonehenge, and to the legend recorded by Geoffrey of Monmouth in the twelfth century CE, in which Merlin moves a stone circle, the Giants' Dance, from the west to Salisbury Plain. Later work in 2021 refined the claim: the circle, the team concluded, had been left unfinished — perhaps 30% built, with at most some seventeen stones erected, of which eight to thirteen were removed.

Waun Mawn — The Rebuttals

The claim was published in a leading journal and announced in a television documentary, and it has been answered in the same currency. Timothy Darvill — himself a senior Stonehenge archaeologist and co-author of the 2012 stage sequence — replied in Antiquity in 2022 ("Mythical rings? Waun Mawn and Stonehenge Stage 1," 96: 1515–1529). His audit found the circle in the evidence wanting: the excavated features, plotted without the assumed 110 m circumference, read as a series of smaller stone settings of types typical of southwest Wales; the trenches had been laid out on the circumference of the circle they were meant to discover; and the Aubrey Holes, on the regional sequence of post-circles succeeded by pit-circles, are better read as a post setting than as bluestone sockets — concluding that a Welsh source-circle for Stonehenge "cannot be excluded" but is "unsupported by the current evidence." Geological papers published in 2022 by Bevins, Ixer, John, and colleagues examined the claimed links between Waun Mawn, the two quarries, and Stonehenge stone by stone and concluded that no such link is demonstrated; Brian John's re-examination of the full Waun Mawn evidence in The Holocene (2024) went further, concluding that the great circle had never existed. Parker Pearson and colleagues answered Darvill in Antiquity's debate pages (2022), reporting a hearth at the putative circle's centre and further circumferential features, and holding their interpretation. The archive's grading follows the state of that exchange: the Preseli origin of the bluestones is demonstrated; the quarries are excavated fact; Waun Mawn as a dismantled 110 m circle, and as Stonehenge's direct source, is a contested claim whose published rebuttals currently stand unanswered in kind — the 2021 season itself produced, by its authors' own report, no new evidence connecting the two sites. It is recorded here in full because it may yet be settled either way, and because the hub's rule is that a famous claim is audited, not omitted.

// THE GEOFFREY PROBLEM //

Geoffrey of Monmouth wrote some 4,000 years after the bluestones moved, placed his source-circle in Ireland rather than Wales, and framed the transfer as Merlin's magic. The Waun Mawn authors cite the legend as a curious echo, not as evidence — and this article grades it likewise: a medieval story can preserve a rumour of stone-moving without preserving a single fact about the third millennium BCE. Legends are Tier B material. They do not testify in Tier A.

XI. Stonehenge III — Sarsens from West Woods: Dressing, Joinery, Erection

Around 2500 BCE, in Stage 2, Stonehenge received the stones the world pictures: the sarsens. Thirty uprights, each about 4.1 m tall and weighing some 25 tonnes, formed a circle 30 m across, capped by a continuous ring of thirty lintels; inside stood five free-standing trilithons — pairs of taller uprights with a single lintel — arranged in a horseshoe open to the north-east, the largest stones of the monument, the Great Trilithon rising some 7.3 m. Sarsen is silcrete, a silica-cemented sandstone of extreme hardness, occurring as surface boulders (greywethers, in the local name, from their resemblance to sheep at rest) across the chalk downs north of the Plain. Where exactly the Stonehenge sarsens came from was debated for four centuries. In 2020 the question was answered geochemically. Nash and colleagues (Science Advances 6: eabc0133) analysed all 52 surviving sarsens non-destructively by portable X-ray fluorescence and found that 50 of the 52 share a single chemical signature — one source area. The match was made through a sliver of luck: a core drilled through Stone 58 during consolidation work in 1958, kept for decades by the drilling engineer Robert Phillips and returned from the United States in 2019, gave the team interior stone to analyse by mass spectrometry against sarsen samples from twenty sites across southern England. The signature matched West Woods, on the Marlborough Downs some 25 km north of Stonehenge — a source area rich in large, high-quality sarsen boulders, in a wooded valley whose archaeology is now being rewritten around the extraction. Two stones — upright 26 and lintel 160 — differ chemically from the fifty and from each other: even at Stonehenge, not every stone came from the main quarry, a detail worth keeping against all tidy stories.

The sarsen phase is where §II's procedures reach their highest European development, and the monument itself is the manual. Dressing: the uprights and lintels were shaped on site by percussion with sarsen mauls — straightening, smoothing, and subtly curving the lintels' inner faces so the ring reads as a true circle — the finest work reserved for the faces on the solstitial axis. Joinery: the builders transferred carpentry into stone, a technique without parallel in European megalithic building: each upright's top was worked into a projecting tenon, each lintel's underside hollowed with matching mortises, so the ring locks together against lateral movement; and the lintels were joined end-to-end by tongue-and-groove, one end grooved, the next ridged, around the whole circumference. Levelling: because the ground slopes, the uprights vary in height so that the lintel ring runs level — level, on modern survey, to within centimetres across 30 m. Erection: the stone holes show the sloped-side geometry of §II; the lintels, weighing some 7 tonnes apiece, were raised most plausibly by the timber-crib method, levered up course by course. Transport: 25 km of chalk downland from West Woods, by sledge on prepared trackways, with teams the experiments scale to the low hundreds per stone — the single greatest haulage campaign of the British Neolithic, repeated some thirty times for the circle alone. A postscript belongs to 2024: mineral-age analysis of the six-tonne Altar Stone, the monument's central slab, matched it to the Orcadian Basin of north-east Scotland, over 700 km away (Clarke and colleagues, Nature, 2024) — the longest attested stone journey of Neolithic Britain, published after the stage sequence above was written and still being absorbed by it. Stonehenge's catchment, it turns out, was the whole island.

XII. Stonehenge's Sky — The Solstitial Axis and the Eclipse Computer, Audited

Stonehenge's astronomy divides, under audit, into one demonstrated orientation, a family of plausible solar and lunar readings, and one famous machine that does not survive its examination. The demonstrated orientation is the solstitial axis. The monument's horseshoe opens north-east; the Avenue — the paired ditch-and-bank processional way that leaves the circle toward the River Avon — runs on the same line; and from the centre, looking along that axis past the outlying Heel Stone, an observer watches the sun rise at midsummer over the Heel Stone's shoulder, while in the opposite direction the midwinter sun sets between the uprights of the Great Trilithon. The alignment was recorded by William Stukeley in the eighteenth century, is a matter of survey, and is accepted across the field, including by the alignment literature's severest critics: the axis is real, and its solstitial reference is the standard reading. A refinement of the last decade adds process to geometry — the first stretch of the Avenue follows natural periglacial ridges in the chalk that happen to run along the solstice line, and Parker Pearson's team has argued the builders monumentalised a line the land itself had drawn, which is why the axis predates the stones. Whether midwinter sunset or midsummer sunrise carried the greater ritual weight is argued — Parker Pearson, from the feasting evidence at Durrington Walls (§XVI), makes the case for midwinter — but the axis itself is not in dispute. That is what a demonstrated alignment looks like: one line, one phenomenon, surveyed, repeatable, and boring to argue with.

The eclipse computer is the counter-case, and its history is the discipline's cautionary tale. In 1963 the astronomer Gerald Hawkins published computer analyses in Nature, expanded in his book Stonehenge Decoded (1965), claiming dozens of solar and lunar alignments among the monument's 165 features, and proposing that the 56 Aubrey Holes were a counting device: by moving markers around the ring on a yearly round, the builders could allegedly track the 18.61-year lunar nodal cycle — 56 being close to three times 18.61 — and predict eclipses. The claim made Stonehenge a "Neolithic computer" in the world's press. The rebuttal came from within archaeology's own house journal: Richard Atkinson's "Moonshine on Stonehenge" (Antiquity, 1966) showed that several of Hawkins's sighting points were natural hollows or features of different periods that could never have been used together, that a tolerance of up to two degrees had been allowed in the alignments, and that with 165 points and a sky full of targets, the probability of finding Hawkins's tally of alignments by chance was about even — not the one-in-a-million his statistics had claimed. Later work deepened the structural objection: the Aubrey Holes held cremations and, on rival readings, posts or stones (§IX) — they were not a clean abacus kept swept for counters — and the eclipse method requires observational records and arithmetic conventions for which no Neolithic evidence exists. Clive Ruggles's authoritative review of the field (Astronomy in Prehistoric Britain and Ireland, 1999) gives the verdict this article adopts: the solstitial axis stands; a broader interest in lunar standstill directions at Stonehenge and other circles remains possible and unproven; the eclipse computer, and the dense web of stellar alignments built around it, are not demonstrated and are no longer defended in the specialist literature. They survive — vigorously — in the popular one, which is precisely why a Tier A article audits them rather than ignoring them.

The audit's point is not that Stonehenge's builders ignored the sky — a monument aligned on the year's turning, built by farmers whose lives ran on the seasons, plainly did not. The point is the difference between an orientation and an observatory, between marking the solstice and computing eclipses. The first is in the ground. The second is in books of the 1960s. Keeping the two apart is the whole of the method, here and at Nabta Playa alike — and it is the standard the next section's Irish evidence, uniquely, meets in its strongest form: an alignment you can still stand inside and watch work.

XIII. Newgrange and Brú na Bóinne — The Passage Tomb and Its Vault

In a great bend of the River Boyne in County Meath, Ireland, three enormous passage tombs — Newgrange, Knowth, and Dowth — and some forty lesser monuments form the Brú na Bóinne complex, a World Heritage Site since 1993 and the richest megalithic landscape of Ireland. Newgrange, the most completely investigated, was excavated and consolidated by Michael J. O'Kelly of University College Cork between 1962 and 1975 and published in his Newgrange: Archaeology, Art and Legend (1982), which remains the site's fundamental report. The mound, built about 3200 BCE on radiocarbon dates from the excavation, is a kidney-shaped cairn of water-rolled stones and earth, some 85 m across and 13.5 m high — its mass conventionally estimated in the hundreds of thousands of tonnes — retained by a kerb of 97 great slabs of greywacke carried from the coast near Clogherhead, and fronted, on O'Kelly's reconstruction, by a wall of white quartz and granite cobbles gathered from the Wicklow Mountains and the Mourne coast. That gleaming façade, the image of Newgrange the world knows, must be labelled honestly: O'Kelly found the quartz and granite collapsed in a spread at the mound's foot and rebuilt it as a near-vertical retaining wall; critics have argued since that the material may originally have formed a sloping revetment or a pavement. The reconstruction is argued, prominent, and contested — the three adjectives belong together whenever it is described.

A passage 19 m long, lined and roofed with great slabs, climbs gently from the entrance into the mound's heart, where it opens into a cruciform chamber: a central space with three recesses, each holding a massive stone basin, in which O'Kelly found the cremated remains of the tomb's dead — unburnt and cremated bone representing at least five individuals, with beads, pendants, and bone pins — a strikingly small company for so great a mound, and itself a datum: Newgrange was not a communal cemetery for a whole people, but a tomb for the few, built by the many. Above the chamber rises the monument's engineering glory, the corbelled vault, and its construction can be followed course by course, as O'Kelly recorded it:

No mortar, no arch, no timber centring that any evidence requires — only oversailing, weight, and patience. The vault has stood, and kept its chamber essentially watertight, for 5,200 years.

The art is the tomb's second glory. Newgrange carries carved ornament on its kerbstones, its passage orthostats, and its chamber stones — spirals, lozenges, arcs, and cupmarks pecked with stone points — and its entrance kerbstone, K1, is the single most famous piece of megalithic art in Europe: a great slab whose face is organised by a vertical groove into fields of spirals, including the celebrated triple spiral, arcs, and lozenge patterns, carved before the stone was set, since the design respects the slab's edges. On the mound's far side, kerbstone K52 answers it with carving of equal ambition — the tomb addresses both the approaching visitor and the valley behind. What the motifs mean is unrecovered: entoptic readings (patterns from altered states), calendrical counts, maps, and pure decoration have all been proposed, none demonstrated, and this article records the carvings as the finest surviving statement of a symbolic system whose key is lost — while noting that Knowth, Newgrange's neighbour, holds roughly a quarter of all known megalithic art in western Europe on its two passages' stones, so the system, whatever it was, was no local doodle but a regional language in stone. Its speakers were the farming communities of the Boyne, and its one certainly-read sentence is the subject of the next section.

XIV. The Roof-Box — Winter Solstice at Newgrange

Above Newgrange's entrance, separate from the doorway and hidden by the mound's face, the builders constructed a feature found at no other passage tomb: a rectangular stone opening, the roof-box, framed by carved slabs, giving onto the passage roof-space above the entrance lintels. Its purpose was unsuspected until O'Kelly's excavation cleared it; local tradition, which he tested rather than assumed, held that the rising sun once lit the chamber's inner stones. The passage's geometry explains why such a device is needed. The passage floor climbs steadily from entrance to chamber, so that light entering at door level, under the lintels, could never reach the chamber floor: the tomb's inner end is higher than its door. The roof-box admits the light above the door, at the height the rising ground requires. It is not an ornament on an alignment. It is the engineering that makes the alignment possible — and its existence is therefore the strongest internal evidence that the effect it produces was designed.

The effect is this. On clear mornings around the winter solstice, a few minutes after sunrise, a narrow beam of sunlight enters the roof-box, strikes the passage floor, and travels up the passage as the sun climbs, widening until it illuminates the chamber floor and the basin in the end recess; the illumination lasts about seventeen minutes before the beam narrows and withdraws. O'Kelly first witnessed it himself on 21 December 1967 — the first recorded modern observation of an event the tomb was built, five thousand years earlier, to stage. It has been observed, photographed, and surveyed repeatedly since; because the sun's rising point barely moves across the solstice standstill, the effect works for several mornings on either side of the shortest day, and modern custodians admit a small lottery-chosen company to the chamber each year to watch it. Grade it by the audit of §XII: the geometry is surveyed; the phenomenon is current, repeatable, and witnessed annually; the design inference rests not on the alignment alone but on a purpose-built aperture whose only demonstrated function is to produce it. Newgrange's winter-solstice illumination is, on present evidence, the most securely attested archaeoastronomical effect in Neolithic Europe — the standard against which the claims at Nabta, Stonehenge, and Mnajdra are each, fairly, measured and found to stand at their own, lesser but still real, grades. One refinement honesty requires: over five millennia the Earth's axial tilt has shifted slightly, so the beam in 3200 BCE fell somewhat differently in detail than it does today — a degree more light, on the usual calculation, reaching deeper into the chamber. The phenomenon the builders designed was, if anything, stronger than the one we watch.

What the illumination meant — the return of the sun at the year's darkest point, enacted inside a tomb, on the floor where the cremated dead lay in their basins — invites the reading every visitor makes, and the reading is plausible: light, renewal, and the dead bound into one staged event. But the archive's discipline holds here as everywhere: the effect is attested; the theology is not. No text, image, or deposition at Newgrange tells us what its builders believed the beam did. It tells us they built a machine of stone and earth to make the shortest day's sunrise enter their tomb once a year, and kept its secret in the dark for five thousand years, and that the machine still works. For a Tier A article, that is the complete sentence, and it needs no adjective.

XV. Orkney — Skara Brae's Furnished Houses and the Ness of Brodgar

At the northern edge of the megalithic world, the Orkney Islands preserve what every other region has lost: the builders' domestic life, in stone, with the furniture in place. The reason is geological. Orkney is nearly treeless, and its Devonian flagstone splits into ready-made slabs; the Orcadian Neolithic therefore built everything in stone — houses, beds, cupboards, tombs, circles — and stone does not rot. The village of Skara Brae, on the Bay of Skaill, was occupied from about 3180 to 2500 BCE, buried under sand for four thousand years, and uncovered by a great storm in 1850. Eight houses survive substantially complete (a ninth is fragmentary), linked by roofed passages, their walls still standing to eaves height because they were built into banks of midden — the community's own refuse, packed around the houses as insulation against the Orcadian winter. Caroline Wickham-Jones's work on Skara Brae and her Orkney: A Historical Guide remain the standard orientation to the village and its landscape; the houses reward a procedural reading, room by room.

Each standard house is a single room of some 36–40 m², entered by a low passage from the lane, its door a stone slab that could be barred from inside — the bar-hole survives in the passage wall. At the room's centre is a square stone hearth of four upright slabs. Facing the door, against the far wall, stands the dresser: two tiers of stone shelving on stone supports, the first thing seen on entering, plainly a display surface as much as a storage one — the house's public face. To right and left of the hearth are the box beds, rectangular stone frames once filled, on the usual reconstruction, with heather or straw bedding and perhaps covered by hide canopies on stone posts; in most houses the right-hand bed is the larger. Set into the floor are small stone tanks, their joints sealed watertight with clay — conventionally read as stores for limpets kept alive in water as fish bait, a reading the limpet shells throughout the village support but do not prove. Beneath the village runs a built drainage system, stone-lined channels passing under the houses and out to the shore; small cells off several houses, connected to the drains, have been read as indoor lavatories — the reading is debated, the drains are not. House 8 breaks the pattern: no beds, no dresser, its floor divided into small working cells, with stone and bone tools in quantity — the village workshop, where chert and whalebone were worked. House 7, the best preserved, kept its dresser, its beds, and, under its floor, two burials in a stone cist — the dead beneath the living, as at Mehrgarh and in the Hypogeum's world alike. Skara Brae's importance to this article is precise: it shows the social unit that built the circles a few miles away — households of farmers and fishers, prosperous, standardised in their comforts, and apparently unranked, house by near-identical house.

Those circles stand at the heart of Mainland Orkney, where the Lochs of Stenness and Harray nearly meet. The Stones of Stenness (from c. 3100 BCE) and the Ring of Brodgar (later third millennium) — a rock-cut ditch 104 m across with originally some sixty stones, twenty-seven still standing — frame the narrow isthmus between the lochs, and on the isthmus itself, excavation from 2004 to 2024 uncovered the Ness of Brodgar: a complex of monumental stone buildings, among them Structure 10, a building some 19 m long with buttressed walls, a paved interior, and a roof once carried on stone piers, repeatedly rebuilt across a thousand years (c. 3300–2200 BCE). The Ness produced decorated and painted stone — pigments applied to walls and slabs, among the earliest evidence for colour on architecture in Britain — and was bounded by great walls across the isthmus. Its end was staged: around 2450 BCE, Structure 10 was decommissioned in an event that deposited the bones of hundreds of cattle — a feasting deposit on a heroic scale — after which the complex was partly dismantled and buried. Nearby stands Maeshowe, the finest chambered tomb of the north, whose passage aligns on the setting sun around midwinter as it drops behind the Barnhouse standing stone — a surveyed alignment of the demonstrated class, the Orcadian counterpart to Newgrange's beam, staged at sunset rather than sunrise. Orkney's whole complex — village, tombs, circles, and the Ness between them — is the most complete Neolithic society the megalithic world has left us, and it is the evidential anchor of the next section: here, unusually, we can see both the monuments and the standard of living of the people who raised them.

XVI. Societies Without Kings — What the Labour Implies

Add up the price list of §II across the monuments of this article — tens of thousands of worker-days for a Barnenez, hundreds of haulers for each great sarsen, eleven centuries of temple maintenance on Malta — and a social fact stands out that no single monument states: this labour was mobilised, repeatedly and across millennia, by societies with no archaeologically visible kings. There are no palaces in Neolithic Europe, no royal tombs of the building centuries (the rich Wessex burials near Stonehenge, like Bush Barrow, belong to the Early Bronze Age, after the sarsens were up), no storehouse administrations, no writing, no sign of a coercive state. The houses at Skara Brae are near-identical; the dead at Newgrange are few and unfurnished with regalia; the Maltese temples grade access ritually but house no throne. Whatever organised the work, it was not pharaoh's apparatus — the contrast with Egypt, where the pyramid age follows hard on the state, is the hub's home comparison, and it runs in Europe's favour as a problem: the harder case is the European one.

The mechanisms the literature proposes are social, not administrative, and several have direct evidence. Feasting: at Durrington Walls, the great timber-circle settlement two miles from Stonehenge, the Stonehenge Riverside Project found the debris of midwinter feasting on a vast scale — pig and cattle bones in the hundreds of thousands, the pigs killed at about nine months, pointing to midwinter slaughter, and isotope analysis showing the animals were driven to Salisbury Plain from catchments across Britain, in later work from as far as Scotland and Wales. People came, from everywhere, bringing animals, at the solstice, and ate together beside the avenue that links the timber circles of the living to the stone circle of the dead: monument-building as the occasion of the largest gatherings the society ever held, and the feast as the wage. The Ness of Brodgar's closing deposit — hundreds of cattle consumed at one decommissioning — is the same mechanism in Orkney. Seasonality: farm labour has a slack season, and the experiments' teams-in-the-hundreds are achievable as seasonal aggregations of kin and neighbours without any permanent workforce. Kinship and obligation: the tomb DNA (§V) shows monuments held by extended families across generations — building as an ancestral duty renewed by each cohort. And competition and emulation between neighbouring communities — the mechanism Renfrew proposed for the Wessex monuments — needs no central power at all, only neighbours watching neighbours raise stones. None of these mechanisms requires a king. All of them require a shared calendar, shared food stores, and shared belief in the work's worth — which is to say, the monuments are evidence less of power than of consensus, sustained at a scale consensus is not supposed to reach.

The hub has met this social shape before, at the other end of its map. At Çatalhöyük, a town of thousands in Anatolia lived for over a millennium in near-identical houses with no public buildings and no visible rulers — the standing proof that early farming societies could be large, dense, and durably egalitarian, and the warning against reading every big work as a big man's order. Megalithic Europe is Çatalhöyük's lesson in the open air: the labour was real, the coordination was real, and the coordinators left no crowns. Leadership there must have been — someone set out the sarsen circle's geometry and someone sequenced the vault courses at Newgrange — but it was leadership that the record shows exercised through feast, calendar, and custom, and rewarded, so far as the graves can tell us, with nothing the archaeologist can weigh. When states finally do appear at the end of this article's period, in the Bronze Age world of rich single burials, the megalithic tradition does not climax. It fades — as if the monuments had belonged, all along, to the stateless world that built them, and could not be inherited by the world of kings.

XVII. What Megalithic Europe Does and Does Not Show

What megalithic Europe shows, at the evidence's full strength, is this. Beginning in Brittany by c. 4800 BCE, the farming communities of Atlantic and Mediterranean Europe developed and sustained, for nearly three thousand years, a monumental stone architecture of tombs, temples, alignments, and circles — some 35,000 known monuments — using only stone, timber, rope, and organised labour. Their procedures are recoverable in detail: quarrying by wedge and hammer, transport by sledge, trackway, and boat, dressing by percussion, erection by pit, A-frame, and crib, roofing by corbelling. Their best work is very good indeed: temples among the world's oldest free-standing stone buildings, a jointed stone ring level to centimetres, a corbelled vault watertight for fifty-two centuries, and a solstice machine in the Boyne that still performs on schedule. Their stones' journeys are now measured facts — Preseli to Salisbury Plain, West Woods to Stonehenge, the Orcadian Basin to the Altar Stone — and their societies, visible whole in Orkney, were prosperous farming communities that mobilised hundreds for a season's building and feasted thousands at the solstice, without kings anyone can find. One regional origin, Brittany, is demonstrated for the earliest monuments; one diffusion model, by sea, is the live reference hypothesis for the rest, with its critics on the record.

What it does not show must be entered in the same hand. It does not show what the monuments meant: not one inscription, image with a recovered key, or deposition decodes the theology of a single tomb, temple, or circle, and the confident modern vocabulary — goddess, observatory, calendar, computer — is ours, not theirs. It does not show a single megalithic people, a master race, or a lost civilisation: the DNA and the settlement record show ordinary regional farmers, region by region, century by century. It does not show precision astronomy: the demonstrated alignments are solar and solstitial, at Mnajdra, Maeshowe, Stonehenge, and supremely Newgrange, and every computational or stellar superstructure raised on them has failed audit or awaits it. It does not show the Waun Mawn circle as Stonehenge's parent — that claim stands published, rebutted, and unproven — nor the Carnac rows' purpose, nor the Maltese temples' gods, nor why, around 2500 BCE, temple and tomb traditions across the continent wound down within a few centuries of one another. And it does not show any connection, of descent or contact, to the monument traditions of Egypt or the Near East beyond the shared Neolithic world: the Breton tombs are older than the pyramids, the Maltese temples independent of them, and parallel invention among early farmers — the lesson of Göbekli and Nabta as much as of Brittany — is the record's own conclusion.

The monuments had a second life, and this hub keeps it where it belongs. From Stukeley's Druids onward, the standing stones of Europe were reimagined — as temples of a Celtic priesthood that never built them, as the work of giants and of Merlin, and in the twentieth century as landing grids, power stations, and relics of Atlantis and the lost continents: a whole Tier C career, in which Stonehenge and the Maltese temples were recruited as evidence for civilisations that left no other trace. Those readings are studied seriously in this archive — as modern constructions, on their own page, Mu and Atlantis Reborn, where the reader who wants the stones' afterlife will find it documented at length. They are kept out of this article for the reason the tiers exist: the Neolithic record, read at its own strength, is stranger and better than the legends laid over it. Five thousand years ago, farmers with stone tools raised a jointed ring on Salisbury Plain, sealed a vault in the Boyne that has never leaked, and set a temple door on Malta to catch the equinox — and then, having no writing, left the stones to say it for them. The stones, on the evidence audited here, have said enough.
