I. Introduction — Three Kinds of Evidence

This hub covers what came before dynastic Egypt — before the unification of the Two Lands around 3100 BCE that opens the Egyptology archive proper. Its subject is therefore enormous: the first pigment workshops of the African Middle Stone Age, the first bread, the first temples, the first metals and the first writing, and the long human story that made Kemet possible. But its subject is also double. Alongside the attested past stand the stories told about that past — by Sumerian scribes, Egyptian priests, Plato, and, much later, by Theosophists, antiquarians and modern esoteric orders. Popular writing habitually blurs those two subjects into one seamless "hidden history." This hub refuses the blur. It separates, plainly and prominently, three kinds of evidence:

TIER A

Attested deep past. Archaeology and the physical sciences: excavated sites, dated objects, laboratory chronologies. Tier A entries can be cited, checked and argued with on evidence. Where specialists debate a date or an interpretation, the debate is stated.

TIER B

Ancient traditions about the deep past. Real texts — king lists, flood epics, philosophical dialogues — studied seriously as texts. A Tier B entry sits on the timeline at its text's real date of composition, and what the text says about remoter ages is always labelled as the text's claim, never narrated as fact.

TIER C

Modern esoteric constructions. Nineteenth- to twenty-first-century creations — Lemuria, Mu, root races, clairvoyant chronologies — presented with their documented origins: who proposed them, when, and from what materials. They are treated respectfully, as a living tradition and a real chapter of intellectual history, and never passed off as attested fact.

// THE HUB'S RULE //

Every timeline entry and every article in this hub wears its tier. Speculation is studied seriously as tradition — and never passed off as attested fact. If you remember nothing else from this page, remember the badge.

The rule cuts both ways. Tier A is not a certificate of final truth: radiocarbon calibrations shift, a "stone circle" may turn out to be a cattle kraal, and this hub says so where scholars say so. Tier B is not a wastebasket: the Sumerian King List is a sophisticated political-theological document, and the Egyptian idea of a First Time shaped three thousand years of temple building whether or not any golden age preceded it. Tier C is not a punchline: the modern lost-continent literature is the native mythology of the modern esoteric revival, and orders that still teach from it — the Inner Garden Foundation among them — are part of the record this hub exists to document fairly.

A word about models and debts. The single chronological spine of this page owes a structural debt to traditional timelines of the kind maintained by modern orders — the Inner Garden Foundation's timeline is the example closest to hand — which set Lemuria, Atlantis, the patriarchs and the pharaohs on one continuous line. That format has a real virtue: it lets a reader see a tradition's whole shape at once. This hub keeps the single spine and changes one thing: every entry on it is tiered, so that a reader can also see, at a glance, which entries rest on excavation, which on ancient testimony, and which on modern construction. The tradition's shape is preserved; its epistemology is made visible. Nothing here copies the text of any order's timeline, and errors found in popular timelines — a Papyrus of Ani misdated by millennia, an Arab conquest of Egypt misdated by centuries — are not inherited: every date below was checked independently for this hub.

The hub's endpoint is deliberate. At c. 3100 BCE the timeline crosses a threshold into the dynastic record, and the story continues in the Egyptology division: see the Predynastic article and the Egyptology timeline. Everything after that threshold on this page — the Tier B texts and the whole of Tier C — is here not because it predates Kemet, but because it talks about what predates Kemet.

// DELIBERATE EXCLUSIONS //

Two absences in this hub are decisions, not oversights. The civilisations of the pre-Columbian Americas are out of scope: the Americas enter this archive's story through the peopling of the continents, not through their later states, which belong to no hub here. And Iron Age Europe — Celts, druids, the Norse world — is likewise excluded; Europe's Neolithic megaliths are in scope as part of the attested deep past, but its Iron Age peoples feed no line this archive follows. Future audits should not re-flag either as a gap.

II. How to Read This Hub

Dates before the historic era are given as BP (years before present, the archaeologist's convention) where the literature uses BP, and as BCE thereafter; "c." marks an approximate date throughout. Tier A dates are calibrated scholarly estimates, not anniversaries — treat the last two digits of any Pleistocene date as weather, not clockwork. Tier B entries are dated by composition or by the date of the surviving copies, and the antiquity the text claims is stated inside the entry, in the entry's own words, as a claim. Tier C entries are dated by publication or by the documented life of the tradition that produced them.

READING TIER A

Ask: what was found, where, and how was it dated? The entries name the site and the method — radiocarbon, uranium-series, stratigraphy — wherever the claim depends on them.

READING TIER B

Ask: who wrote this, when, and for whom? A king list is an argument about legitimacy before it is a chronicle; a dialogue is philosophy before it is reportage. Genre is evidence.

READING TIER C

Ask: what did the author actually have in hand? A zoological puzzle, a misread codex, a claimed clairvoyant record — the documented origin is always more interesting than the rumour of one.

// A NOTE ON RESPECT //

Tiering is a claim about evidence, not about worth, sincerity or spiritual value. Several Tier C chronologies are taught today within living traditions whose members hold them in good faith. This hub documents those teachings in their own terms, corrects only what the documentary record settles (publication dates, misattributed sources, demonstrable misdatings), and leaves questions of inner meaning to the traditions themselves.

III. The Master Timeline — Tier A: The Attested Deep Past

Fifty-six entries run from the first systematic use of pigment to the threshold of the pharaonic state, and a short coda of four later entries — flagged where they stand — follows the threshold marker: sixty in all. This is the spine of fact against which everything else in the hub is measured.

c. 176,500 BP
TIER A
Bruniquel — building in the dark. 336 metres inside Bruniquel Cave in southwestern France, beyond all daylight, early Neanderthals broke more than 400 stalagmites to a standard length and laid them in two rings — the larger 6.7 by 4.5 metres — and four stacks, with traces of fire throughout the chamber. Uranium-series dating of regrowth on the structures and of burnt bone gives a replicated age of 176.5 ± 2.1 thousand years, making these the oldest well-dated constructions attributed to any human population. What the rings were for is unknown; that they required sustained light, several builders, and a shared plan is inference from the structure itself. See The Other Humans — Neanderthal and Denisovan Symbolic Worlds.
c. 164,000 BP
TIER A
Ochre use at Pinnacle Point, South Africa. In cave PP13B on the southern Cape coast, Marean and colleagues documented systematic collection and use of red ochre alongside early exploitation of shellfish, dated to about 164,000 years ago (give or take some 12,000). It is among the earliest widely accepted evidence that early Homo sapiens treated a pigment as worth gathering, grading and keeping. The pigment sequence that begins here is traced in full in Ochre and Symbol.
c. 142,000–100,000 BP
TIER A
Shell beads across Africa and the Levant. Perforated marine shells — at Bizmoune Cave in Morocco possibly as early as c. 142,000 years ago, and at Skhul and Taforalt in the c. 135,000–82,000 BP range — show that personal ornament, and with it a social, symbolic self, long predates any cave painting. The Moroccan dates remain debated; the pattern across sites does not.
c. 130,000–115,000 BP
TIER A
Neanderthal ornament and pigment. In a single level at Krapina, Croatia, dated to about 130,000 years ago, Neanderthals accumulated eight white-tailed eagle talons from at least three birds — cut-marked, polished, and notched as if strung together as jewellery. Some fifteen millennia later, at Cueva de los Aviones in Spain, Neanderthals perforated and pigment-stained marine shells and mixed prepared pigment recipes in shell containers, in a deposit sealed under flowstone uranium-series dated to about 115,000–120,000 years ago. Both finds predate any modern human presence in Europe, closing the argument that such practices were copied from incoming neighbours. See The Other Humans — Neanderthal and Denisovan Symbolic Worlds.
c. 100,000 BP
TIER A
The Blombos ochre-processing workshop. At Blombos Cave, South Africa, Henshilwood's team recovered two abalone shells holding a liquefied red pigment mixture — ochre, bone and charcoal, ground and stirred to a recipe — with the grindstones and hammerstones of the toolkit beside them (Science, 2011). It is the earliest known paint-making kit: chemistry, storage and planning in one assemblage. See Ochre and Symbol.
c. 77,000–75,000 BP
TIER A
Engraved ochre and Nassarius beads at Blombos. Pieces of ochre ground flat and incised with deliberate cross-hatched designs, and perforated Nassarius shell beads worn from stringing, come from the same cave's Still Bay layers. The engravings carry no practical function anyone has identified; they are pattern for pattern's sake, and therefore among the earliest material signs of symbolic thought.
c. 73,000 BP
TIER A
The Blombos silcrete drawing. A flake of silcrete from Blombos bears nine red lines drawn with an ochre crayon — the oldest known drawing, published in Nature in 2018. Modest as it looks, it demonstrates the full chain — pigment prepared, point shaped, lines planned on a surface — tens of millennia before Lascaux.
c. 65,000 BP
TIER A
Madjedbebe, Arnhem Land. At a sandstone shelter on the western edge of the Arnhem Land plateau, in Mirarr Country, excavators piece-plotted some 11,000 artefacts and dated the lowest dense occupation band — edge-ground hatchet fragments, grinding stones, and ground ochre — by single-grain optically stimulated luminescence to about 65,000 years ago (Clarkson et al., Nature 2017): if the association between artefacts and dated sand holds, the earliest occupation of Sahul yet published. The association is contested — O'Connell and colleagues argue that termite bioturbation could have carried younger artefacts into older sediment, and hold an age beyond 50,000 years unlikely; the excavators answer with refits, intact hearths, and trampling experiments, and the debate remains open. The uncontested floor for northern Australia is c. 50,000 BP. See Sahul — The First Crossing and the Long Occupation.
c. 60,000 BP
TIER A
Engraved ostrich eggshell at Diepkloof. Fragments of ostrich eggshell containers from Diepkloof Rock Shelter, South Africa, carry repeated hatched and parallel-line motifs, made over generations to a shared convention. A motif repeated to a standard is a symbol system in embryo: the mark identifies the container, and perhaps its owner.
c. 50,000 BP
TIER A
Denisova Cave — three populations, one sequence. Denisova Cave in Siberia's Altai Mountains is the only site where Neanderthals, Denisovans, and modern humans are all attested in one sequence. Its Early Upper Palaeolithic levels, spanning the period around 50,000 years ago when all three populations used the cave, yielded ostrich-eggshell beads, ivory and tooth pendants, a marble ring, a drilled chloritolite bracelet, and an eyed bone needle among the oldest known. No Denisovan fossil was found in direct association with these objects, and their maker cannot yet be assigned with confidence — a caution the archive prints alongside the finds. See The Other Humans — Neanderthal and Denisovan Symbolic Worlds.
at least c. 45,500 BP
TIER A
Figurative cave art in Sulawesi, Indonesia. A painting of a Sulawesi warty pig at Leang Tedongnge is at least 45,500 years old by uranium-series dating of the calcite over it, and a narrative scene at Leang Karampuang has since been dated to at least 51,200 BP. Figurative art was not a European invention; it was already old on the far side of the world while Europe's painted caves were still in the future.
c. 43,000 BP
TIER A
The Ngwenya hematite mine, Eswatini. The Lion Cave at Ngwenya is the oldest known mine in the world: Middle Stone Age miners dug for hematite and specularite — red pigment ore — some 43,000 years ago. Industrial-scale desire for colour, organised and inherited, is itself a datum about the minds that wanted it.
c. 36,000 BP
TIER A
The Chauvet Cave paintings, France. The Ardèche cave's lions, rhinoceroses and horses, drawn in charcoal and ochre in two main phases around 37,000–33,500 BP, display shading, wall preparation and composition of a maturity that overturned the idea of art evolving from crude to skilled. Chauvet is the European benchmark — and, after Sulawesi, no longer the world's oldest.
c. 34,000 BP
TIER A
Early textiles — the Dzudzuana fibres, Georgia. In the clay deposits of Dzudzuana Cave, in the foothills of the Caucasus in Georgia, microscopic bast fibres survive from Upper Palaeolithic layers radiocarbon dated to 32,000–26,000 years BP, about 36,000–31,000 calendar years ago. Identified by Kvavadze and colleagues (Science, 2009) as wild flax — an identification formally disputed since, in favour of bast fibre generally — the fibres number in the hundreds in the oldest unit alone. Some are spun, some twisted, one thread knotted, and dozens are dyed, in black, grey, turquoise, and other colours: worked thread and cordage in routine use more than thirty millennia before farming. The full treatment is given in Ochre and Symbol.
c. 23,000–21,000 BP
TIER A
Human footprints at White Sands, New Mexico. On the shore of palaeolake Otero in the Tularosa Basin, trackways of barefoot people — mostly adolescents and children, among mammoth and giant sloth tracks — survive on multiple buried surfaces at WHSA Locality 2. Bennett et al. (Science, 2021) dated them by Ruppia seed layers to the Last Glacial Maximum; a reservoir-effect critique (Oviatt et al., 2022) was answered by independent pollen radiocarbon and quartz OSL ages (Pigati et al., Science, 2023) and lakebed-mud dates (Holliday et al., 2025) converging on the same span. If the age stands, people were south of the ice sheets during the glacial maximum itself. The full argument, both rounds, is set out in The Peopling of the Americas.
c. 20,000 BP
TIER A
Pottery at Xianrendong Cave, China. Sherds from Xianrendong, Jiangxi, dated to about 20,000–19,000 BP (Science, 2012), are the earliest known pottery vessels — made by Ice Age hunter-gatherers some ten thousand years before farming. Fired clay begins as a forager's technology, a fact that reorders the usual story in which pots follow ploughs.
from c. 14,000 BCE
TIER A
The Jōmon period of Japan begins. Around 14,000 BCE, hunter-fisher-gatherers in Japan begin making some of the earliest pottery in the world — cord-marked vessels by people who would sustain a sedentary, pottery-using way of life for more than thirteen thousand years without staple agriculture. Pit-house villages, shell middens, storage pits, lacquer working, and clay dogū figurines elaborate across the sequence, reaching their greatest scale at settlements such as Sannai-Maruyama (c. 3900–2200 BCE), where hundreds of people lived in a planned village on fish, game, and managed chestnut forest. The Jōmon are the standing proof that sedentism and complexity do not require farming. The full treatment is at Neolithic East Asia — Jōmon, Yangshao, and Jiahu.
c. 15,000–11,500 BP
TIER A
The Natufian culture of the Levant. The Natufians were the first broadly sedentary hunter-gatherers of the region: stone-built settlements, burials with ornaments, sickles and grinding stones for intensive wild-cereal use. They stand at the hinge between foraging and farming — settled life demonstrably came first, agriculture second.
c. 14,800 BP
TIER A
The African Humid Period begins. Around 14,800 years ago, as the last ice age loosened, the strengthening monsoon began turning the Sahara green: lakes rose, rivers ran, and over the following millennia the largest desert on earth became savanna crossed by fishers, hunters, and then herders. The onset date rests on lake-core and dust records read together; the florescence it opened is entered below at c. 7500–5500 BCE. The green Sahara and its image-world are surveyed in The Green Sahara and Its Image-World.
c. 14,600 BP
TIER A
Meltwater Pulse 1A and the drowning of Sundaland. As the Bølling warming began, global sea level rose 14–18 m in under 350 years (Tahiti coral record; Deschamps et al. 2012) — on the near-flat Sunda Shelf, a shoreline retreat of tens of kilometres within generations. Sundaland, the continent joining mainland Southeast Asia to Sumatra, Java, Borneo, and Bali, lost roughly half its area between the glacial maximum and the mid-Holocene; genetics (Hill et al. 2007; Kim et al. 2023) reads the modern map of the region's peoples as, in part, the signature of that displacement. See Drowned Landscapes.
c. 14,500 BP
TIER A
Monte Verde, Chile — a settlement older than Clovis. On Chinchihuapi Creek in southern Chile, a peat bog preserved a community of some twenty to thirty people: a tent-like structure of about a dozen rooms, each with its own hearth, wooden tools, cordage, mastodon bone, dozens of food and medicinal plants, and nine species of seaweed carried from the Pacific coast (Dillehay excavations, 1977–1985). Its age — about 12,500 radiocarbon years, c. 14,500 calibrated years BP, a millennium before Clovis — was resisted for twenty years, then confirmed unanimously by a visiting panel of specialists in 1997, ending the Clovis-first consensus. Monte Verde remains the benchmark pre-Clovis site. See The Peopling of the Americas.
c. 14,400 BP
TIER A
Bread at Shubayqa, Jordan. Charred remains of flatbread baked by Natufian hunter-gatherers at Shubayqa 1, from wild grains, tubers and mustard, predate the domestication of wheat by some four thousand years (PNAS, 2018). Baking — and the labour chain behind it — was a forager invention that farming later inherited, not the other way round.
c. 14,200 BP
TIER A
The Bonn-Oberkassel dog (Germany). In a double human burial at Oberkassel, near Bonn, a man and a woman were laid to rest with grave goods and two dogs — the oldest known joint burial of humans and a dog. Re-examination (Janssens et al., 2018) showed that the younger dog, dead at about 27–28 weeks, had survived repeated bouts of a distemper-like illness between 19 and 23 weeks of age, a survival the authors argue was possible only with weeks of intensive human care for an animal of no working use. The grave is the earliest durable evidence that the first domestication was also a companionship. The full treatment is in Göbekli Tepe.
c. 13,000 BP
TIER A
Melian obsidian reaches Franchthi Cave. Pieces of obsidian in the Upper Palaeolithic levels of Franchthi Cave, on the shore of the Argolic Gulf, have been assigned by characterisation analysis to the quarries of Melos — an island never joined to the Greek mainland — and direct hydration dating of Melian exploitation spans the 13th to late 10th millennium BP. The glass crossed some 120 km of Aegean water in the hands of hunter-gatherers, millennia before farming: the earliest well-documented sea transport of a raw material anywhere. The full evidence, and the laboratory method that proves it, is set out in Early Seafaring and the Obsidian Trade.
c. 9500 BCE
TIER A
Göbekli Tepe begun, southeastern Anatolia. On a hill above the Harran plain, communities still living largely by hunting and gathering raised circular enclosures of T-shaped limestone pillars, carved with animals and abstract signs, in the earliest phase dated to around 9500 BCE. Its excavator Klaus Schmidt argued the sanctuary came before the city; current research complicates that slogan — there is evidence of settlement too — but not the site's primacy among monumental ritual architecture. The site and its debates are examined in Göbekli Tepe.
c. 11,000 BP
TIER A
The flooding of Beringia. The rising sea overtopped the c. 53 m sill of the Bering Strait and drowned the plain that had joined Siberia to Alaska for some twenty thousand years. A Herald Canyon core (Jakobsson et al. 2017) dates the Pacific's breakthrough to c. 11,000 cal BP; modelling (Pico et al. 2020) argues a two-phase flooding from c. 13,000 BP. Either way, the homeland in which the ancestors of the First Peoples had lived out the glacial maximum — the Beringian standstill — ceased to exist. See Drowned Landscapes and The Peopling of the Americas.
c. 9000 BCE
TIER A
Karahan Tepe and the Taş Tepeler. Some 35 km from Göbekli Tepe, Karahan Tepe — occupied in the same Pre-Pottery Neolithic world, broadly c. 9400–8000 BCE — adds its own T-pillars, a bedrock-cut chamber and a hall of phallic pillars watched by a carved human head. Göbekli was not an anomaly but the best-known member of a whole ritual landscape, the "Stone Hills" now under excavation across the Şanlıurfa region. See Göbekli Tepe.
c. 9000 BCE
TIER A
Jericho settled. Tell es-Sultan, Jericho, shows occupation by Natufian hunter-gatherers and then one of the earliest sustained farming settlements on earth, from around 9000 BCE. Its springs made it a place people returned to for eleven thousand years; its fame in later texts rests on this very real antiquity. The full sequence — Natufian camp, first farmers, tower and wall — is set out in Jericho — Eleven Thousand Years at the Spring.
c. 8500 BCE
TIER A
Domestication of wheat and barley in the Fertile Crescent. Across the arc from the Levant to the Zagros foothills, communities brought einkorn, emmer and barley under cultivation in a process spread over centuries around 8500 BCE and after. There was no single "invention of agriculture": the archaeobotanical record shows a long, regional mosaic of pre-domestication cultivation becoming dependence.
c. 8500–8000 BCE
TIER A
Domestication of sheep, goat and cattle. Herd animals followed crops within the same broad horizon: goats and sheep under human management in the Zagros and Anatolia, cattle in the upper Euphrates region. The managed herd — wealth that walks, breeds and can be raided — remade economy, diet and social competition across the Near East and, in time, Africa.
c. 8000 BCE
TIER A
The Tower of Jericho. Around 8000 BCE Jericho's inhabitants built a stone tower some 8.5 metres high with an internal stair of 22 steps, attached to a massive wall. Defence, flood control and ritual display have all been argued; what is not in doubt is the communal labour — among the earliest monumental building by a farming town anywhere. The tower, the wall, and the arguments over both are examined in Jericho — Eleven Thousand Years at the Spring.
c. 7500–5700 BCE
TIER A
Çatalhöyük, Anatolia. For over a thousand years this dense town of mudbrick houses — entered through the roof, its dead buried beneath the floors, its walls painted and set with bulls' horns — housed thousands without streets, palaces or temples anyone can identify. Çatalhöyük is the standing proof that early urban life did not require kings. The town's houses, burials, paintings, and the argument over its supposed goddess are treated in Çatalhöyük — The Town Without Streets.
c. 7500 BCE
TIER A
Native copper worked in Anatolia. At sites such as Çayönü, naturally occurring native copper was cold-hammered and annealed into beads, pins and awls by the mid-8th millennium BCE — metal treated as a rare, malleable stone. This is metallurgy's first chapter: working metal found as metal, long before anyone learned to win it from ore by fire. See First Metals.
c. 7500–5500 BCE
TIER A
The African Humid Period — the Green Sahara florescence. Strengthened monsoons turned the Sahara into savanna and lake country. Pastoral peoples spread across what is now desert, leaving rock art of cattle, swimming figures and ceremony from the Tassili to the Ennedi. The "desert" of later Egyptian imagination was, within human memory of the region's peoples, a watered homeland — climate context no account of early Egypt can omit. The period's image-world — its periods, its dating problems, and its cattle — is surveyed in The Green Sahara and Its Image-World.
c. 8000–6000 BCE
TIER A
Cattle pastoralism at Nabta Playa, Egypt's Western Desert. Cattle bones occur in Nabta's earliest levels, and the site's excavators read them as evidence of an independent African domestication — herders keeping cattle around the seasonal lake and digging wells deep enough to hold a settlement through the dry season. Metrical re-examination (Smith; Grigson) places the early animals within the range of wild aurochs, genetics favours a Near Eastern origin for African domestic cattle, and domestic herds reach the region shortly before 6000 BCE. The claim and its audit are both part of this entry's record, and Nabta's pastoral tradition fed directly into the prehistory of the Nile Valley. The pastoral world behind this entry is set out in Nabta Playa.
from c. 7000 BCE
TIER A
Mehrgarh — farming begins at the Bolan Pass. At the foot of the Bolan Pass in Balochistan, a settlement sequence excavated by the French mission under Jean-François and Catherine Jarrige runs unbroken from an aceramic Neolithic (c. 7000 BCE) toward the Indus world. Its grains are the Near Eastern founder package; its zebu cattle, on Meadow's argument, were domesticated locally from the Indian aurochs — a farming hearth half borrowed, half its own. Its graveyards hold the earliest known drilled teeth (Coppa et al., Nature 2006), dentistry's first chapter, written by bead-drillers. The sequence is set out in Mehrgarh and the Road to the Indus.
c. 7000–5700 BCE
TIER A
Jiahu — music, signs, and the first fermented drink. Jiahu, a sedentary village in the upper Huai valley of Henan, was occupied from about 7000 to 5700 BCE and packs an extraordinary record into its excavated fraction: early rice in use and very probably under cultivation at the plant's northern limit; crane-bone flutes that are the oldest complete, playable, dated musical instruments known, one of them acoustically tested and shown to carry a coherent scale; tortoise shells bearing eleven certainly incised signs — deliberate sign use, claimed by some as a first step toward writing and treated by critics, and by this hub, as not yet writing; and, in its earliest jars, the chemically attested mixed fermented beverage of rice, honey, and fruit, the earliest known anywhere. The site is treated in full at Neolithic East Asia — Jōmon, Yangshao, and Jiahu.
c. 6500 BCE
TIER A
The Ubaid period begins in southern Mesopotamia. The Ubaid expansion carried a shared repertoire — painted pottery, tripartite houses, early temple platforms — from the southern alluvium across Mesopotamia. It laid the institutional groundwork, temple households included, on which the Uruk world and then the Sumerian cities would be built. The temple sequence that anchors the period — eighteen building levels on one footprint at Eridu, from a 2.8-metre shrine to a tripartite platform temple — and the interaction sphere that carried the Ubaid repertoire from the Gulf to north Syria are set out in Ubaid and Uruk — The First Cities.
c. 6200 BCE
TIER A
The Storegga tsunami and the end of inhabited Doggerland. A submarine landslide off Norway — the Storegga Slide, displacing some 2,400–3,500 km³ of sediment — sent a tsunami across the northern North Sea: deposits in Shetland record runup above 20 m over the sea of that day, and the layer is dated to c. 6225–6170 BCE. What the wave did to the Mesolithic remnant of Doggerland is debated: catastrophic final flooding (Weninger et al. 2008), a lesser inundation within a retreat already driven by sea-level rise (Hill et al.), or a real but indecisive blow (Walker, Gaffney et al. 2020). The Dogger Bank island drowned later, c. 5000 BCE. See Drowned Landscapes.
c. 5400 BCE
TIER A
The first shrine at Eridu. On virgin soil at the marsh-edge town of Eridu, a community raised the first building in the longest temple sequence known from Mesopotamia: a single mud-brick cella of about 2.8 × 2.8 metres, with a niche for a cult image and a pedestal for offerings (Temple Sounding, Level XVII, above the sleeper walls of Level XVIII). Rebuilt on the same footprint through eighteen levels — platformed by Level XI, tripartite by Level VIII, its final Ubaid form (Level VI) receiving offerings of fish — the shrine's ground became, by accumulation, the temple platform from which the ziggurat form descends. The sequence is walked level by level in Ubaid and Uruk — The First Cities.
c. 4800 BCE
TIER A
The Nabta Playa stone circle and alignments. Nabta serves as a regional ceremonial centre from the Middle Neolithic (c. 6050–5550 BCE); the small stone circle belongs to its Late Neolithic phase, a hearth beside it dated to about 4800 BCE, and the alignments of slabs follow in the Final Neolithic, the main quarrying between about 4500 and 4200 BCE. Claims that the stones form a precise astronomical observatory are debated and are flagged as such in this hub's Nabta Playa article; the deliberate, ceremonial arrangement of stone by a pastoral society is not debated.
c. 5400–4500 BCE
TIER A
The Vinča culture of the central Balkans. Across Serbia and neighbouring regions, farming communities built tell settlements — Vinča-Belo Brdo beside the Danube among them — occupied and rebuilt for centuries, producing dark burnished pottery, thousands of clay figurines, a shared repertoire of incised signs, and, at villages such as Belovode and Pločnik, the social setting of the world's earliest secure copper smelting. Bayesian radiocarbon modelling at the type site places the culture between about 5400/5300 and 4500 BCE, closing in a horizon of burned houses. Its tells, signs, figurines, and hoards are treated in full in Old Europe — Vinča and the Cucuteni-Trypillia World.
c. 5000 BCE
TIER A
Copper smelting at Belovode, Serbia. Slag, furnace remains and finished metal from the Vinča-culture site of Belovode give the world's earliest securely dated evidence of high-temperature copper smelting, around 5000 BCE (Radivojević and colleagues). Smelting — ore, fire and air combined to birth metal — appears in the Balkans at least as early as anywhere in the Near East. See First Metals.
c. 5000 BCE
TIER A
The Rudna Glava copper mine. In eastern Serbia, Vinča miners followed malachite veins down some twenty shafts with stone hammers and antler picks around 5000 BCE. Rudna Glava and Belovode together show a complete early industry — mine, smelter, smith — in Copper Age Europe.
c. 5000 BCE
TIER A
The Fayum and Merimde Neolithic — farmers on the Nile. By about 5000 BCE, farming communities on the Fayum lake shore and at Merimde, on the western edge of the Delta, were growing emmer wheat and barley and herding sheep, goat and cattle — the first fully Neolithic horizon in Lower Egypt, and the bridge between the Mesolithic Nile and the predynastic cultures that followed. See The Nile Bridge.
c. 5000–3000 BCE
TIER A
The Yangshao culture of the middle Yellow River. The Yangshao is the mature millet-farming Neolithic of north China. Its villages follow a shared plan — semi-subterranean houses around communal open space inside a ring ditch, with kilns and cemeteries outside — documented whole at Jiangzhai, where some 120 houses stood in five residential groups around a 4,000 m² plaza, and at the type site of Banpo near Xi'an. Its painted pottery, black mineral pigment on a red ground fired in updraft kilns, develops from the naturalistic fish of the Banpo phase to the pure geometry of Miaodigou and the swirling wares of Majiayao. The older textbook claim that these villages were matriarchal is now treated as historiography, not evidence. See Neolithic East Asia — Jōmon, Yangshao, and Jiahu.
c. 4600–4200 BCE
TIER A
The Varna gold cemetery, Bulgaria. Graves at Varna, radiocarbon-dated to about 4569–4340 BCE, held more than 3,000 gold objects — among the oldest worked gold in the world — concentrated in a few burials, some of them cenotaphs with gold-mask faces. Varna is the earliest dramatic evidence that metal had already become a language of rank and inherited inequality. See First Metals.
c. 4400–4000 BCE
TIER A
The Badarian culture of Upper Egypt. Around el-Badari, farming villages produced the finest pottery of predynastic Egypt — black-topped redware of eggshell thinness — together with early copper objects, glazed steatite and carved slate. The Badarians are the first Nile Valley culture whose cemeteries show craft specialisation and graded wealth: Egypt's long social story starts here, in the ground.
c. 4000–3100 BCE
TIER A
The Uruk period in Mesopotamia. Uruk grew into the world's first true city, and the Uruk expansion carried its material signature — bevel-rim bowls, cylinder seals, temple precincts — across the Near East, with outposts and influence reaching Egypt's doorstep. Urbanism, bureaucracy and mass production enter the record together, and none of them ever leaves it again. Uruk's scale is an estimate with stated error bars — c. 250 hectares, 25,000–50,000 people by the area-and-density method — and its precincts, ration bowls, sealings, and northern outposts, with the debate between Algaze's world-system model and Stein's distance-parity alternative, are treated in Ubaid and Uruk — The First Cities.
c. 4000–3600 BCE
TIER A
The Trypillia mega-sites of the Ukrainian forest-steppe. In the centuries around 4000–3600 BCE, Cucuteni-Trypillia communities aggregated into planned settlements without precedent in Europe: Talianki at up to some 400 hectares, Maidanetske at about 200 hectares with some 3,000 houses, and Nebelivka at 238 hectares with 1,445 houses on its complete magnetometry plan — concentric rings of houses around open central spaces, with neighbourhood workshops and large assembly houses, and populations estimated, with wide uncertainty, from a few thousand to five figures. They had no palaces, no royal graves, and no writing. Their plans, populations, and burned houses are treated in full in Old Europe — Vinča and the Cucuteni-Trypillia World.
c. 3800 BCE
TIER A
The Naqada culture expands. From its Upper Egyptian heartland, the Naqada culture — heirs of the Badarians — spread northward through the Nile Valley in the centuries after c. 3800 BCE, standardising pottery, iconography and burial practice as it went. The cultural unification of Egypt preceded its political unification by centuries; the state, when it came, ratified a merger already underway.
c. 3600–3200 BCE
TIER A
Malta's temple culture begins — Ġgantija raised on Gozo. On two small limestone islands south of Sicily, farming communities begin building the Ġgantija temples: two multi-apsed stone temples inside a single massive coralline boundary wall, among the earliest free-standing stone buildings in the world. Raised without metal, the wheel, or writing, they open a tradition that will run for over a thousand years — Ħaġar Qim, Mnajdra, and Tarxien among its monuments — sustained by an island population of only a few thousand people. The temples' plans, two-limestone walling, and corbelled construction are set out in Megalithic Europe.
c. 3400 BCE
TIER A
Proto-cuneiform writing at Uruk. Clay tablets of the Uruk IV–III levels record goods, rations and labour in hundreds of pictographic and numerical signs — the earliest generally accepted writing system. It was born as accounting: the first thing humanity wrote down was not a hymn or a king's boast but a tally of beer, grain and sheep. The road from tokens and bullae to that tablet — and the debate over how directly it ran — is traced in The Origins of Writing.
c. 3300 BCE
TIER A
Ötzi — a Copper Age man in the ice. In September 1991, two hikers found a mummified man emerging from the ice of the Tisenjoch pass, on the Austrian–Italian border, at 3,210 metres: radiocarbon dates him to about 3300 BCE. His copper axe — a nearly pure copper blade in a yew haft — is among the earliest copper tools in Europe, and his kit (bow, quiver, flint dagger, birch-bark containers) and his last meals are preserved with him. He died violently, an arrowhead lodged in his shoulder. The man, the axe, and what the metal says about his world are treated in First Metals.
c. 3250 BCE
TIER A
Earliest hieroglyphic writing — Abydos, tomb U-j. Some 160 small bone and ivory labels from the great predynastic tomb U-j at Abydos (Umm el-Qa'ab) bear incised signs identifying goods and their origins — the earliest attested Egyptian writing, dated to Naqada IIIA1, about 3250 BCE. Whether Egyptian script was invented independently or in awareness of Mesopotamian practice remains a live scholarly question; its signs, from the start, are its own. The Egyptian beginning, and whether it was an independent invention, are weighed in The Origins of Writing.
c. 3200 BCE
TIER A
Newgrange — a passage tomb built around the winter solstice. In the bend of the River Boyne in Ireland, farming communities raise a cairn some 85 metres across over a cruciform chamber roofed by a corbelled vault that still keeps the rain out. Above the entrance they set a feature found at no other tomb, a roof-box: for a few mornings around the winter solstice the rising sun sends a beam down the 19-metre passage to light the chamber floor for some seventeen minutes — an effect first witnessed in modern times in 1967 and still performed every year. The tomb, its vault, and its art are treated in Megalithic Europe.

c. 3000 BCE
TIER A
Stonehenge begun — an earthwork cemetery on Salisbury Plain. Around 3000 BCE the communities of Wessex dig a circular ditch and bank some 110 metres across and set the 56 Aubrey Holes within it. For five centuries the enclosure serves as a cremation cemetery for on the order of 150 people — the largest known in Neolithic Britain — some of whom, isotope analysis shows, had lived as far away as west Wales. No stones stand yet; the sarsen circle and trilithons are five hundred years in the future. The monument's full phased sequence is set out in Megalithic Europe.
c. 17th century BCE (observations; preserved in 1st-millennium copies)
TIER A
The Venus observations of Ammisaduqa. Preserved as Tablet 63 of the omen series Enuma Anu Enlil, this tablet records the first and last visibilities of Venus, with the lengths of its invisibility, for twenty-one years of the reign of Ammisaduqa, fourth king after Hammurabi — the oldest datable planetary observations surviving from anywhere. No seventeenth-century original survives; the text reaches us in first-millennium copies. Because Venus's visibilities nearly repeat every eight years, the same data support a ladder of absolute datings — High, Middle, Middle-Low, Low, Ultra-Low — and the chronology of the whole age remains unresolved. The tablet and the debate are set out in Babylonian Sky Science.
compiled c. 1100 BCE
TIER A
MUL.APIN compiled — the sky put in order. Around 1100 BCE, from older star lists, Babylonian scholars compiled the two-tablet compendium named for its first constellation, the Plough: a catalogue of the stars arranged in the three paths of Enlil, Anu, and Ea; the heliacal rising dates of the stars against an ideal 360-day year; the constellations of the Moon's path; rules for intercalating the calendar by observation; and schemes for the gnomon's shadow and the water clock. Copied for seven centuries, it was the working handbook of the Babylonian watch. Its contents are treated in full in Babylonian Sky Science.
5th century BCE
TIER A
The zodiac invented — twelve equal signs. In the course of the fifth century BCE — attested by 475 BCE and in routine use by 410 BCE — Babylonian scholars divided the ecliptic into twelve equal signs of thirty degrees, named for the constellations but independent of them: the sky's first true coordinate system, sidereal and star-fixed. Positions could now be stated as numbers, carried in tables, and computed for dates no one had watched; within a generation the same tablets record the first horoscopes, cast for private births. The invention and its consequences are traced in Babylonian Sky Science.

IV. The Master Timeline — Tier B: Ancient Traditions About the Deep Past

Sixteen entries. Each sits at its text's real date; the deep antiquity inside the text is stated as that text's claim. Read in order, they show the ancient world doing with its past exactly what this hub does with all pasts: arranging it into an argument.

attested from c. 2350 BCE onward
TIER B
Zep Tepi — the Egyptian "First Time." Egyptian texts from the Pyramid Texts onward invoke sp tpy, the First Time: the age of the gods' rule and of creation's ordering, to which temples claimed their plans and rites descended. As a claim, it asserts a perfected origin age before human kingship. As attested history, it is a theological idea with three millennia of documented influence — the distinction this hub exists to keep. The First Time and its texts are treated in Divine Dynasties.
composed early 2nd millennium BCE
TIER B
The Sumerian King List. Surviving chiefly in Old Babylonian copies — the Weld-Blundell Prism (c. 1800 BCE) is the fullest — the List claims that before the Flood, eight kings in five cities ruled a total of 241,200 years, after which "the flood swept over" and kingship descended again. Jacobsen's classic edition remains the reference point: the antediluvian figures are schematic, built from sexagesimal round numbers, and the List's real subject is the legitimacy of kingship passing from city to city. The List and its numbers are read in full in Antediluvian King Lists.
Old Babylonian copies, c. 18th–17th c. BCE
TIER B
The Eridu Genesis — the Ziusudra flood tradition. The fragmentary Sumerian flood story, preserved on a tablet from Nippur, tells of the creation of mankind, the founding of the first cities, and the flood survived by Ziusudra, king and priest, who is granted eternal life. It is the earliest surviving form of the Mesopotamian flood narrative that Atrahasis and Gilgamesh would carry forward. See Flood Traditions.
c. 17th century BCE
TIER B
The Atrahasis epic. Composed in Akkadian by about the 17th century BCE, Atrahasis runs from the creation of humans — made to relieve the lesser gods of labour — through plagues and failed divine attempts to curb human noise and numbers, to the flood survived by its hero on Enki's warning. Its claim is theological anthropology, not chronology: mankind exists, in this telling, as a workforce that outgrew its makers' patience. See Flood Traditions.
c. 1800–1000 BCE
TIER B
The Epic of Gilgamesh — the whole poem. Five Sumerian poems about the king of Uruk were reworked in Akkadian across the second millennium BCE into the epic of the king who would not accept death: the Cedar Forest, Enkidu made and lost, the flood hero Utanapishtim, the plant of rejuvenation taken by a snake. Late in the second millennium the poem reached its Standard Babylonian form — the version whose Tablet XI is entered below — and it kept being copied, at Ugarit and Hattusa as well as in Babylonia and Assyria, into the first millennium. The whole poem, its layers, its manuscripts (including the Sulaymaniyah Tablet V, published in 2015), and its close at the walls of Uruk are treated in Gilgamesh — The Whole Epic.
compiled c. 13th century BCE
TIER B
The Turin Royal Canon. This Ramesside hieratic papyrus (reign of Ramesses II) lists Egypt's rulers with reign lengths — and opens, before Menes, with dynasties of gods and of the "Spirits of the Dead" (the Followers of Horus), credited with reigns of thousands of years. The Canon is a genuine archival document whose earliest sections record what Egyptian tradition claimed, not what modern chronology can verify; both facts belong in the entry. The Canon's divine and spirit dynasties are treated in Divine Dynasties.
compiled c. 1100–700 BCE
TIER B
The omen and medical series compiled. Between the eleventh and seventh centuries BCE, Babylonian and Assyrian scholars edited the great scribal series into the received forms in which they survive: Esagil-kīn-apli, chief scholar to King Adad-apla-iddina, ordered the forty tablets of the Diagnostic Handbook Sakikkû, while the celestial omens of Enūma Anu Enlil, the terrestrial omens of Šumma ālu, the birth omens of Šumma izbu, the extispicy corpus, and the incantation series Maqlû and Šurpu reached their canonical shapes in the same centuries. Their claim was descent from Ea through the antediluvian sages; their reality was the library and the school. See Omens and Medicine.

In the Standard Babylonian Gilgamesh (associated with the scholar Sîn-lēqi-unninni, and known from 7th-century BCE copies in Ashurbanipal's library), the flood hero Utanapishtim tells how he built his vessel, survived the storm and was made immortal. The Genesis flood narrative. Genesis 6–9, in the form received in the 1st millennium BCE, tells of Noah, the ark and the covenant after the waters. Set beside Atrahasis and Tablet XI, it is one branch of a shared ancient Near Eastern flood-tradition family; set in its own canon, it makes the flood a moral judgement and a new beginning for all flesh. This hub records it as Tier B: an ancient text making ancient claims, in conversation with its neighbours. See Flood Traditions.
c. 360 BCE
TIER B
Plato, Timaeus and Critias — Atlantis. Plato's dialogues present Atlantis as a great island power beyond the Pillars of Heracles that warred with primeval Athens and sank "in a single day and night of misfortune," placed — as the claim runs, through Solon from Egyptian priests at Sais — 9,000 years before Solon. No Egyptian source for the tale has ever been identified, and the dialogues' philosophical purpose is explicit in the text; the claim is recorded here as Plato made it, neither inflated nor dismissed. See Plato's Atlantis.
composed 3rd c. BCE – 1st c. CE
TIER B
The Book of Enoch and the Watchers. The Enochic corpus — the Book of the Watchers foremost, composed in the centuries before the Common Era — is set in the antediluvian age and claims that fallen angels, the Watchers, taught humanity metalwork, cosmetics, astrology and other arts before the Flood judged them. As a claim about the origin of technology in a corrupted gift, it shaped Jewish and Christian angelology for two thousand years. The corpus, its manuscripts, and its afterlife are treated in The Book of Enoch and the Watchers.
c. 290 BCE
TIER B
Berossus, Babyloniaca. The Babylonian priest Berossus, writing in Greek for Antiochus I, gave ten antediluvian kings ruling 432,000 years (120 saroi of 3,600 years) before the flood of Xisouthros. His work survives only in fragments quoted by later authors — which is itself the lesson: Tier B texts reach us through chains of excerptors, each with an agenda. Berossus's ten kings are read alongside the List in Antediluvian King Lists.
3rd century BCE
TIER B
Manetho, Aegyptiaca. The Egyptian priest Manetho, writing in Greek under the early Ptolemies, organised Egypt's kings into the thirty dynasties Egyptologists still use — and, as the fragments transmitted by Africanus and Eusebius report the scheme, placed divine and semi-divine dynasties before the human kings. The original is lost; what survives is epitome and quotation, and the divine-dynasty totals vary between transmitters. See Divine Dynasties.
composed 1st–3rd century CE
TIER B
The Hermetica's antediluvian framing. The Corpus Hermeticum — Greek philosophical-religious treatises of the 1st–3rd centuries CE, set in an Egypt of deep antiquity and voiced by Hermes Trismegistus — presents its teaching as the pristine wisdom of the world's dawn. The framing is a claim the texts make about themselves; their real date, language and Alexandrian milieu are documented, and the Egyptology division treats the corpus in full in its own right.
systematised c. 2nd c. BCE – 5th c. CE
TIER B
The yuga cycles — India's ages of the world. The Sanskrit tradition orders time in four declining ages — Kṛta, Tretā, Dvāpara, Kali — of 4,800, 3,600, 2,400 and 1,200 divine years: a mahāyuga of 4,320,000 human years, nested in manvantaras and in the 4.32-billion-year day of Brahmā. The scheme reaches systematic form in the Manusmṣti and the Purāṇas, and its present age is anchored by the tradition's own epoch: Kali Yuga from 3102 BCE, the reckoning Āryabhaṭa uses in 499 CE. That epoch is the tradition's claim about 3102 BCE, not an event of it. The doctrine, its arithmetic and its texts are treated in The Yuga Cycles.
compiled 9th century CE
TIER B
The Bundahishn — Iran's bounded twelve thousand years. The Zoroastrian Bundahishn, compiled in the ninth century CE from older Pahlavi material, orders creation inside a bounded scheme of twelve thousand years: three thousand of spiritual creation, three of material creation unassailed, then the long mixture of good and evil through history to the final renovation. It is the Iranian member of this hub's family of bounded deep-time schemes — set beside the yuga arithmetic above and the king lists' numbered reigns, as a text making its own claim about the shape of time. Its scheme is read in Antediluvian King Lists.

V. The Master Timeline — Tier C: Modern Constructions

Twenty-four entries, each at its real publication or tradition date. Tier C begins, fittingly, inside respectable science — and the hub follows it, without mockery and without credulity, into the living traditions that still teach it.

In "The Mammals of Madagascar" (Quarterly Journal of Science, 1864), zoologist Philip Lutley Sclater proposed a sunken Indian Ocean continent to explain why lemur relatives occur in Madagascar and India but not Africa or Arabia. // WHAT THE THREE TIMELINES TEACH TOGETHER //

Read down the tiers in parallel and a pattern emerges that no single tier shows alone. Tier A gives a deep past that is real but mute — pigment, bread, pillars, gold — with no names in it. Tier B gives the ancient world's own names for that muteness: kings before the flood, gods before the kings, a First Time before the temples. Tier C gives modern names, coined the moment science withdrew its own. The human need the three tiers answer is the same need. Only the evidence differs — and the evidence is the whole of the law here.
