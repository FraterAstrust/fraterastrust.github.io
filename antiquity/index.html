I. Introduction — Three Kinds of Evidence

This hub covers what came before dynastic Egypt — before the unification of the Two Lands around 3100 BCE that opens the Egyptology archive proper. Its subject is therefore enormous: the first pigment workshops of the African Middle Stone Age, the first bread, the first temples, the first metals and the first writing, and the long human story that made Kemet possible. But its subject is also double. Alongside the attested past stand the stories told about that past — by Sumerian scribes, Egyptian priests, Plato, and, much later, by Theosophists, antiquarians and modern esoteric orders. Popular writing habitually blurs those two subjects into one seamless "hidden history." This hub refuses the blur. It separates, plainly and prominently, three kinds of evidence:

TIER A

Attested deep past. Archaeology and the physical sciences: excavated sites, dated objects, laboratory chronologies. Tier A entries can be cited, checked and argued with on evidence. Where specialists debate a date or an interpretation, the debate is stated.

TIER B

Ancient traditions about the deep past. Real texts — king lists, flood epics, philosophical dialogues — studied seriously as texts. A Tier B entry sits on the timeline at its text's real date of composition, and what the text says about remoter ages is always labelled as the text's claim, never narrated as fact.

TIER C

Modern esoteric constructions. Nineteenth- to twenty-first-century creations — Lemuria, Mu, root races, clairvoyant chronologies — presented with their documented origins: who proposed them, when, and from what materials. They are treated respectfully, as a living tradition and a real chapter of intellectual history, and never passed off as attested fact.

// THE HUB'S RULE //

Every timeline entry and every article in this hub wears its tier. Speculation is studied seriously as tradition — and never passed off as attested fact. If you remember nothing else from this page, remember the badge.

The rule cuts both ways. Tier A is not a certificate of final truth: radiocarbon calibrations shift, a "stone circle" may turn out to be a cattle kraal, and this hub says so where scholars say so. Tier B is not a wastebasket: the Sumerian King List is a sophisticated political-theological document, and the Egyptian idea of a First Time shaped three thousand years of temple building whether or not any golden age preceded it. Tier C is not a punchline: the modern lost-continent literature is the native mythology of the modern esoteric revival, and orders that still teach from it — the Inner Garden Foundation among them — are part of the record this hub exists to document fairly.

A word about models and debts. The single chronological spine of this page owes a structural debt to traditional timelines of the kind maintained by modern orders — the Inner Garden Foundation's timeline is the example closest to hand — which set Lemuria, Atlantis, the patriarchs and the pharaohs on one continuous line. That format has a real virtue: it lets a reader see a tradition's whole shape at once. This hub keeps the single spine and changes one thing: every entry on it is tiered, so that a reader can also see, at a glance, which entries rest on excavation, which on ancient testimony, and which on modern construction. The tradition's shape is preserved; its epistemology is made visible. Nothing here copies the text of any order's timeline, and errors found in popular timelines — a Papyrus of Ani misdated by millennia, an Arab conquest of Egypt misdated by centuries — are not inherited: every date below was checked independently for this hub.

The hub's endpoint is deliberate. At c. 3100 BCE the timeline crosses a threshold into the dynastic record, and the story continues in the Egyptology division: see the Predynastic article and the Egyptology timeline. Everything after that threshold on this page — the Tier B texts and the whole of Tier C — is here not because it predates Kemet, but because it talks about what predates Kemet.

II. How to Read This Hub

Dates before the historic era are given as BP (years before present, the archaeologist's convention) where the literature uses BP, and as BCE thereafter; "c." marks an approximate date throughout. Tier A dates are calibrated scholarly estimates, not anniversaries — treat the last two digits of any Pleistocene date as weather, not clockwork. Tier B entries are dated by composition or by the date of the surviving copies, and the antiquity the text claims is stated inside the entry, in the entry's own words, as a claim. Tier C entries are dated by publication or by the documented life of the tradition that produced them.

READING TIER A

Ask: what was found, where, and how was it dated? The entries name the site and the method — radiocarbon, uranium-series, stratigraphy — wherever the claim depends on them.

READING TIER B

Ask: who wrote this, when, and for whom? A king list is an argument about legitimacy before it is a chronicle; a dialogue is philosophy before it is reportage. Genre is evidence.

READING TIER C

Ask: what did the author actually have in hand? A zoological puzzle, a misread codex, a claimed clairvoyant record — the documented origin is always more interesting than the rumour of one.

// A NOTE ON RESPECT //

Tiering is a claim about evidence, not about worth, sincerity or spiritual value. Several Tier C chronologies are taught today within living traditions whose members hold them in good faith. This hub documents those teachings in their own terms, corrects only what the documentary record settles (publication dates, misattributed sources, demonstrable misdatings), and leaves questions of inner meaning to the traditions themselves.

III. The Master Timeline — Tier A: The Attested Deep Past

Thirty-three entries, from the first systematic use of pigment to the threshold of the pharaonic state. This is the spine of fact against which everything else in the hub is measured.

c. 164,000 BP
TIER A
Ochre use at Pinnacle Point, South Africa. In cave PP13B on the southern Cape coast, Marean and colleagues documented systematic collection and use of red ochre alongside early exploitation of shellfish, dated to about 164,000 years ago (give or take some 12,000). It is among the earliest widely accepted evidence that early Homo sapiens treated a pigment as worth gathering, grading and keeping. The pigment sequence that begins here is traced in full in Ochre and Symbol.
c. 142,000–100,000 BP
TIER A
Shell beads across Africa and the Levant. Perforated marine shells — at Bizmoune Cave in Morocco possibly as early as c. 142,000 years ago, and at Skhul and Taforalt in the c. 135,000–82,000 BP range — show that personal ornament, and with it a social, symbolic self, long predates any cave painting. The Moroccan dates remain debated; the pattern across sites does not.
c. 100,000 BP
TIER A
The Blombos ochre-processing workshop. At Blombos Cave, South Africa, Henshilwood's team recovered two abalone shells holding a liquefied red pigment mixture — ochre, bone and charcoal, ground and stirred to a recipe — with the grindstones and hammerstones of the toolkit beside them (Science, 2011). It is the earliest known paint-making kit: chemistry, storage and planning in one assemblage. See Ochre and Symbol.
c. 77,000–75,000 BP
TIER A
Engraved ochre and Nassarius beads at Blombos. Pieces of ochre ground flat and incised with deliberate cross-hatched designs, and perforated Nassarius shell beads worn from stringing, come from the same cave's Still Bay layers. The engravings carry no practical function anyone has identified; they are pattern for pattern's sake, and therefore among the earliest material signs of symbolic thought.
c. 73,000 BP
TIER A
The Blombos silcrete drawing. A flake of silcrete from Blombos bears nine red lines drawn with an ochre crayon — the oldest known drawing, published in Nature in 2018. Modest as it looks, it demonstrates the full chain — pigment prepared, point shaped, lines planned on a surface — tens of millennia before Lascaux.
c. 60,000 BP
TIER A
Engraved ostrich eggshell at Diepkloof. Fragments of ostrich eggshell containers from Diepkloof Rock Shelter, South Africa, carry repeated hatched and parallel-line motifs, made over generations to a shared convention. A motif repeated to a standard is a symbol system in embryo: the mark identifies the container, and perhaps its owner.
at least c. 45,500 BP
TIER A
Figurative cave art in Sulawesi, Indonesia. A painting of a Sulawesi warty pig at Leang Tedongnge is at least 45,500 years old by uranium-series dating of the calcite over it, and a narrative scene at Leang Karampuang has since been dated to at least 51,200 BP. Figurative art was not a European invention; it was already old on the far side of the world while Europe's painted caves were still in the future.
c. 43,000 BP
TIER A
The Ngwenya hematite mine, Eswatini. The Lion Cave at Ngwenya is the oldest known mine in the world: Middle Stone Age miners dug for hematite and specularite — red pigment ore — some 43,000 years ago. Industrial-scale desire for colour, organised and inherited, is itself a datum about the minds that wanted it.
c. 36,000 BP
TIER A
The Chauvet Cave paintings, France. The Ardèche cave's lions, rhinoceroses and horses, drawn in charcoal and ochre in two main phases around 37,000–33,500 BP, display shading, wall preparation and composition of a maturity that overturned the idea of art evolving from crude to skilled. Chauvet is the European benchmark — and, after Sulawesi, no longer the world's oldest.
c. 20,000 BP
TIER A
Pottery at Xianrendong Cave, China. Sherds from Xianrendong, Jiangxi, dated to about 20,000–19,000 BP (Science, 2012), are the earliest known pottery vessels — made by Ice Age hunter-gatherers some ten thousand years before farming. Fired clay begins as a forager's technology, a fact that reorders the usual story in which pots follow ploughs.
c. 15,000–11,500 BP
TIER A
The Natufian culture of the Levant. The Natufians were the first broadly sedentary hunter-gatherers of the region: stone-built settlements, burials with ornaments, sickles and grinding stones for intensive wild-cereal use. They stand at the hinge between foraging and farming — settled life demonstrably came first, agriculture second.
c. 14,400 BP
TIER A
Bread at Shubayqa, Jordan. Charred remains of flatbread baked by Natufian hunter-gatherers at Shubayqa 1, from wild grains, tubers and mustard, predate the domestication of wheat by some four thousand years (PNAS, 2018). Baking — and the labour chain behind it — was a forager invention that farming later inherited, not the other way round.
c. 9500 BCE
TIER A
Göbekli Tepe begun, southeastern Anatolia. On a hill above the Harran plain, communities still living largely by hunting and gathering raised circular enclosures of T-shaped limestone pillars, carved with animals and abstract signs, in the earliest phase dated to around 9500 BCE. Its excavator Klaus Schmidt argued the sanctuary came before the city; current research complicates that slogan — there is evidence of settlement too — but not the site's primacy among monumental ritual architecture. The site and its debates are examined in Göbekli Tepe.
c. 9000 BCE
TIER A
Karahan Tepe and the Taş Tepeler. Some 35 km from Göbekli Tepe, Karahan Tepe — occupied in the same Pre-Pottery Neolithic world, broadly c. 9400–8000 BCE — adds its own T-pillars, a bedrock-cut chamber and a hall of phallic pillars watched by a carved human head. Göbekli was not an anomaly but the best-known member of a whole ritual landscape, the "Stone Hills" now under excavation across the Şanlıurfa region. See Göbekli Tepe.
c. 9000 BCE
TIER A
Jericho settled. Tell es-Sultan, Jericho, shows occupation by Natufian hunter-gatherers and then one of the earliest sustained farming settlements on earth, from around 9000 BCE. Its springs made it a place people returned to for eleven thousand years; its fame in later texts rests on this very real antiquity.
c. 8500 BCE
TIER A
Domestication of wheat and barley in the Fertile Crescent. Across the arc from the Levant to the Zagros foothills, communities brought einkorn, emmer and barley under cultivation in a process spread over centuries around 8500 BCE and after. There was no single "invention of agriculture": the archaeobotanical record shows a long, regional mosaic of pre-domestication cultivation becoming dependence.
c. 8500–8000 BCE
TIER A
Domestication of sheep, goat and cattle. Herd animals followed crops within the same broad horizon: goats and sheep under human management in the Zagros and Anatolia, cattle in the upper Euphrates region. The managed herd — wealth that walks, breeds and can be raided — remade economy, diet and social competition across the Near East and, in time, Africa.
c. 8000 BCE
TIER A
The Tower of Jericho. Around 8000 BCE Jericho's inhabitants built a stone tower some 8.5 metres high with an internal stair of 22 steps, attached to a massive wall. Defence, flood control and ritual display have all been argued; what is not in doubt is the communal labour — among the earliest monumental building by a farming town anywhere.
c. 7500–5700 BCE
TIER A
Çatalhöyük, Anatolia. For over a thousand years this dense town of mudbrick houses — entered through the roof, its dead buried beneath the floors, its walls painted and set with bulls' horns — housed thousands without streets, palaces or temples anyone can identify. Çatalhöyük is the standing proof that early urban life did not require kings.
c. 7500 BCE
TIER A
Native copper worked in Anatolia. At sites such as Çayönü, naturally occurring native copper was cold-hammered and annealed into beads, pins and awls by the mid-8th millennium BCE — metal treated as a rare, malleable stone. This is metallurgy's first chapter: working metal found as metal, long before anyone learned to win it from ore by fire. See First Metals.
c. 7500–5500 BCE
TIER A
The African Humid Period — the Green Sahara florescence. Strengthened monsoons turned the Sahara into savanna and lake country. Pastoral peoples spread across what is now desert, leaving rock art of cattle, swimming figures and ceremony from the Tassili to the Ennedi. The "desert" of later Egyptian imagination was, within human memory of the region's peoples, a watered homeland — climate context no account of early Egypt can omit.
c. 8000–6000 BCE
TIER A
Cattle pastoralism at Nabta Playa, Egypt's Western Desert. Cattle bones occur in Nabta's earliest levels, and the site's excavators read them as evidence of an independent African domestication — herders keeping cattle around the seasonal lake and digging wells deep enough to hold a settlement through the dry season. Metrical re-examination (Smith; Grigson) places the early animals within the range of wild aurochs, genetics favours a Near Eastern origin for African domestic cattle, and domestic herds reach the region shortly before 6000 BCE. The claim and its audit are both part of this entry's record, and Nabta's pastoral tradition fed directly into the prehistory of the Nile Valley. The pastoral world behind this entry is set out in Nabta Playa.
c. 6500 BCE
TIER A
The Ubaid period begins in southern Mesopotamia. The Ubaid expansion carried a shared repertoire — painted pottery, tripartite houses, early temple platforms — from the southern alluvium across Mesopotamia. It laid the institutional groundwork, temple households included, on which the Uruk world and then the Sumerian cities would be built.
c. 4800 BCE
TIER A
The Nabta Playa stone circle and alignments. Nabta serves as a regional ceremonial centre from the Middle Neolithic (c. 6050–5550 BCE); the small stone circle belongs to its Late Neolithic phase, a hearth beside it dated to about 4800 BCE, and the alignments of slabs follow in the Final Neolithic, the main quarrying between about 4500 and 4200 BCE. Claims that the stones form a precise astronomical observatory are debated and are flagged as such in this hub's Nabta Playa article; the deliberate, ceremonial arrangement of stone by a pastoral society is not debated.
c. 5000 BCE
TIER A
Copper smelting at Belovode, Serbia. Slag, furnace remains and finished metal from the Vinča-culture site of Belovode give the world's earliest securely dated evidence of high-temperature copper smelting, around 5000 BCE (Radivojević and colleagues). Smelting — ore, fire and air combined to birth metal — appears in the Balkans at least as early as anywhere in the Near East. See First Metals.
c. 5000 BCE
TIER A
The Rudna Glava copper mine. In eastern Serbia, Vinča miners followed malachite veins down some twenty shafts with stone hammers and antler picks around 5000 BCE. Rudna Glava and Belovode together show a complete early industry — mine, smelter, smith — in Copper Age Europe.
c. 5000 BCE
TIER A
The Fayum and Merimde Neolithic — farmers on the Nile. By about 5000 BCE, farming communities on the Fayum lake shore and at Merimde, on the western edge of the Delta, were growing emmer wheat and barley and herding sheep, goat and cattle — the first fully Neolithic horizon in Lower Egypt, and the bridge between the Mesolithic Nile and the predynastic cultures that followed. See The Nile Bridge.
c. 4600–4200 BCE
TIER A
The Varna gold cemetery, Bulgaria. Graves at Varna, radiocarbon-dated to about 4569–4340 BCE, held more than 3,000 gold objects — among the oldest worked gold in the world — concentrated in a few burials, some of them cenotaphs with gold-mask faces. Varna is the earliest dramatic evidence that metal had already become a language of rank and inherited inequality. See First Metals.
c. 4400–4000 BCE
TIER A
The Badarian culture of Upper Egypt. Around el-Badari, farming villages produced the finest pottery of predynastic Egypt — black-topped redware of eggshell thinness — together with early copper objects, glazed steatite and carved slate. The Badarians are the first Nile Valley culture whose cemeteries show craft specialisation and graded wealth: Egypt's long social story starts here, in the ground.
c. 4000–3100 BCE
TIER A
The Uruk period in Mesopotamia. Uruk grew into the world's first true city, and the Uruk expansion carried its material signature — bevel-rim bowls, cylinder seals, temple precincts — across the Near East, with outposts and influence reaching Egypt's doorstep. Urbanism, bureaucracy and mass production enter the record together, and none of them ever leaves it again.
c. 3800 BCE
TIER A
The Naqada culture expands. From its Upper Egyptian heartland, the Naqada culture — heirs of the Badarians — spread northward through the Nile Valley in the centuries after c. 3800 BCE, standardising pottery, iconography and burial practice as it went. The cultural unification of Egypt preceded its political unification by centuries; the state, when it came, ratified a merger already underway.
c. 3400 BCE
TIER A
Proto-cuneiform writing at Uruk. Clay tablets of the Uruk IV–III levels record goods, rations and labour in hundreds of pictographic and numerical signs — the earliest generally accepted writing system. It was born as accounting: the first thing humanity wrote down was not a hymn or a king's boast but a tally of beer, grain and sheep.
c. 3250 BCE
TIER A
Earliest hieroglyphic writing — Abydos, tomb U-j. Some 160 small bone and ivory labels from the great predynastic tomb U-j at Abydos (Umm el-Qa'ab) bear incised signs identifying goods and their origins — the earliest attested Egyptian writing, dated to Naqada IIIA1, about 3250 BCE. Whether Egyptian script was invented independently or in awareness of Mesopotamian practice remains a live scholarly question; its signs, from the start, are its own.

IV. The Master Timeline — Tier B: Ancient Traditions About the Deep Past

Twelve entries. Each sits at its text's real date; the deep antiquity inside the text is stated as that text's claim. Read in order, they show the ancient world doing with its past exactly what this hub does with all pasts: arranging it into an argument.

attested from c. 2350 BCE onward
TIER B
Zep Tepi — the Egyptian "First Time." Egyptian texts from the Pyramid Texts onward invoke sp tpy, the First Time: the age of the gods' rule and of creation's ordering, to which temples claimed their plans and rites descended. As a claim, it asserts a perfected origin age before human kingship. As attested history, it is a theological idea with three millennia of documented influence — the distinction this hub exists to keep. The First Time and its texts are treated in Divine Dynasties.
composed early 2nd millennium BCE
TIER B
The Sumerian King List. Surviving chiefly in Old Babylonian copies — the Weld-Blundell Prism (c. 1800 BCE) is the fullest — the List claims that before the Flood, eight kings in five cities ruled a total of 241,200 years, after which "the flood swept over" and kingship descended again. Jacobsen's classic edition remains the reference point: the antediluvian figures are schematic, built from sexagesimal round numbers, and the List's real subject is the legitimacy of kingship passing from city to city. The List and its numbers are read in full in Antediluvian King Lists.
Old Babylonian copies, c. 18th–17th c. BCE
TIER B
The Eridu Genesis — the Ziusudra flood tradition. The fragmentary Sumerian flood story, preserved on a tablet from Nippur, tells of the creation of mankind, the founding of the first cities, and the flood survived by Ziusudra, king and priest, who is granted eternal life. It is the earliest surviving form of the Mesopotamian flood narrative that Atrahasis and Gilgamesh would carry forward. See Flood Traditions.
c. 17th century BCE
TIER B
The Atrahasis epic. Composed in Akkadian by about the 17th century BCE, Atrahasis runs from the creation of humans — made to relieve the lesser gods of labour — through plagues and failed divine attempts to curb human noise and numbers, to the flood survived by its hero on Enki's warning. Its claim is theological anthropology, not chronology: mankind exists, in this telling, as a workforce that outgrew its makers' patience. See Flood Traditions.
compiled c. 13th century BCE
TIER B
The Turin Royal Canon. This Ramesside hieratic papyrus (reign of Ramesses II) lists Egypt's rulers with reign lengths — and opens, before Menes, with dynasties of gods and of the "Spirits of the Dead" (the Followers of Horus), credited with reigns of thousands of years. The Canon is a genuine archival document whose earliest sections record what Egyptian tradition claimed, not what modern chronology can verify; both facts belong in the entry. The Canon's divine and spirit dynasties are treated in Divine Dynasties.
Standard Babylonian version, late 2nd–1st millennium BCE
TIER B
Gilgamesh, Tablet XI — Utanapishtim. In the Standard Babylonian Gilgamesh (associated with the scholar Sîn-lēqi-unninni, and known from 7th-century BCE copies in Ashurbanipal's library), the flood hero Utanapishtim tells how he built his vessel, survived the storm and was made immortal. George Smith's 1872 reading of this tablet announced the Babylonian flood to the modern world — an event in Tier C's prehistory as much as Tier B's text history. See Flood Traditions.
received form, 1st millennium BCE
TIER B
The Genesis flood narrative. Genesis 6–9, in the form received in the 1st millennium BCE, tells of Noah, the ark and the covenant after the waters. Set beside Atrahasis and Tablet XI, it is one branch of a shared ancient Near Eastern flood-tradition family; set in its own canon, it makes the flood a moral judgement and a new beginning for all flesh. This hub records it as Tier B: an ancient text making ancient claims, in conversation with its neighbours. See Flood Traditions.
c. 360 BCE
TIER B
Plato, Timaeus and Critias — Atlantis. Plato's dialogues present Atlantis as a great island power beyond the Pillars of Heracles that warred with primeval Athens and sank "in a single day and night of misfortune," placed — as the claim runs, through Solon from Egyptian priests at Sais — 9,000 years before Solon. No Egyptian source for the tale has ever been identified, and the dialogues' philosophical purpose is explicit in the text; the claim is recorded here as Plato made it, neither inflated nor dismissed. See Plato's Atlantis.
composed 3rd c. BCE – 1st c. CE
TIER B
The Book of Enoch and the Watchers. The Enochic corpus — the Book of the Watchers foremost, composed in the centuries before the Common Era — is set in the antediluvian age and claims that fallen angels, the Watchers, taught humanity metalwork, cosmetics, astrology and other arts before the Flood judged them. As a claim about the origin of technology in a corrupted gift, it shaped Jewish and Christian angelology for two thousand years.
c. 290 BCE
TIER B
Berossus, Babyloniaca. The Babylonian priest Berossus, writing in Greek for Antiochus I, gave ten antediluvian kings ruling 432,000 years (120 saroi of 3,600 years) before the flood of Xisouthros. His work survives only in fragments quoted by later authors — which is itself the lesson: Tier B texts reach us through chains of excerptors, each with an agenda. Berossus's ten kings are read alongside the List in Antediluvian King Lists.
3rd century BCE
TIER B
Manetho, Aegyptiaca. The Egyptian priest Manetho, writing in Greek under the early Ptolemies, organised Egypt's kings into the thirty dynasties Egyptologists still use — and, as the fragments transmitted by Africanus and Eusebius report the scheme, placed divine and semi-divine dynasties before the human kings. The original is lost; what survives is epitome and quotation, and the divine-dynasty totals vary between transmitters. See Divine Dynasties.
composed 1st–3rd century CE
TIER B
The Hermetica's antediluvian framing. The Corpus Hermeticum — Greek philosophical-religious treatises of the 1st–3rd centuries CE, set in an Egypt of deep antiquity and voiced by Hermes Trismegistus — presents its teaching as the pristine wisdom of the world's dawn. The framing is a claim the texts make about themselves; their real date, language and Alexandrian milieu are documented, and the Egyptology division treats the corpus in full in its own right.

V. The Master Timeline — Tier C: Modern Constructions

Twenty-three entries, each at its real publication or tradition date. Tier C begins, fittingly, inside respectable science — and the hub follows it, without mockery and without credulity, into the living traditions that still teach it.

1864
TIER C
Sclater coins "Lemuria." In "The Mammals of Madagascar" (Quarterly Journal of Science, 1864), zoologist Philip Lutley Sclater proposed a sunken Indian Ocean continent to explain why lemur relatives occur in Madagascar and India but not Africa or Arabia. It was ordinary, falsifiable land-bridge science for its day — and the name outlived the science by a century and a half. See Lemuria.
1864–1870
TIER C
Brasseur de Bourbourg and the birth of "Mu." The French antiquarian Charles-Étienne Brasseur de Bourbourg, working from a misreading of the Troano Codex (part of the Madrid Codex), rendered two glyphs as "Mu" and told of a land sunk by earthquake and flood. The syllable was a mistranslation; the continent built on it became one of the most durable constructions in Tier C. The Mu construction that grew from the syllable is traced in Mu and Atlantis Reborn.
1868 onward
TIER C
Haeckel makes Lemuria the cradle of mankind. Ernst Haeckel, Darwin's great German champion, adopted Sclater's continent and placed the origin and dispersal of humanity upon it — in the English History of Creation (1876) its map of human origins labels Lemuria, in so many words, "Paradise." A zoological hypothesis had become an Eden, two decades before the Theosophists took it up. See Lemuria.
1875
TIER C
The Theosophical Society founded. Helena Blavatsky, Henry Steel Olcott and William Quan Judge founded the Society in New York in 1875. It became the institutional vessel into which Lemuria, Atlantis and a dozen other currents were poured and systematised — the single most important fact for understanding why Tier C took the shape it did.
1870s–1880s
TIER C
Le Plongeon in Yucatán. Augustus Le Plongeon, excavating and photographing Maya ruins, developed Brasseur's hint into a full diffusionist epic: Queen Moo, Prince Coh, and a Mu-Atlantis motherland whose refugees, in his telling, founded both Maya civilisation and Egypt. His photographs remain valuable; his translations and chronology were rejected by the Maya scholarship of his own day. His epic is retold and audited in Mu and Atlantis Reborn.
1882
TIER C
Donnelly, Atlantis: The Antediluvian World. Ignatius Donnelly, a former U.S. congressman, argued with formidable energy that Plato's Atlantis was literal history and the source of civilisation on both Atlantic shores. A bestseller that founded the modern Atlantis genre, it set the template — Plato read as reportage, parallels read as descent — that Tier C writing still follows. The genre Donnelly founded is traced in Mu and Atlantis Reborn.
1888
TIER C
Blavatsky, The Secret Doctrine. Blavatsky's vast synthesis recast Lemuria and Atlantis as the homelands of humanity's third and fourth "root races," within a cosmic scheme of spiritual evolution drawn, she claimed, from the archaic Book of Dzyan. Whatever its sources, its influence is documentary fact: nearly every later lost-continent teaching descends from this book or reacts against it. The book's chronology is examined in full in The Theosophical Chronology.
1896
TIER C
Scott-Elliot, The Story of Atlantis. William Scott-Elliot, a London banker and Theosophist, published a detailed Atlantis — seven sub-races, airships, staged submergences — with four maps, sourced, he stated, from clairvoyant investigation of the "Akashic records" by unnamed seers. It became the standard Theosophical Atlantis and a quarry for a century of imitators. See Mu and Atlantis Reborn.
1904
TIER C
Scott-Elliot, The Lost Lemuria. The companion volume gave Lemuria the same treatment: maps, a third-root-race ethnography of egg-born giants with a third eye, and a staged sinking. The two books, combined in 1925 as The Story of Atlantis and the Lost Lemuria, fixed the Theosophical deep past in its canonical form. See Lemuria.
1904–1908
TIER C
Steiner reads the Akashic Chronicle. Rudolf Steiner's Lucifer-Gnosis articles (collected in English as Atlantis and Lemuria, 1911) retold both continents from claimed spiritual-scientific reading of the Akashic record, with Atlanteans of prodigious memory and direct command of life-forces in place of Scott-Elliot's machines. Anthroposophy carried the scheme into education and agriculture, where its cultural footprint remains visible.
early 20th century
TIER C
Lemuria becomes Kumari Kandam. Tamil writers and revivalists adopted the sunken continent as Kumari Kandam, the lost Tamil homeland south of India, seat of the first Sangams. A hypothesis born in a London zoology journal thus entered a living language politics — a reminder that Tier C constructions acquire real communities, and real stakes, far from their origins. The Tamil afterlife of Lemuria is treated in Lemuria.
1912
CONTEXT
Wegener proposes continental drift. Alfred Wegener's 1912 papers and his Origin of Continents and Oceans (1915) argued that continents move — the idea that, matured into plate tectonics by the 1960s, dissolved the problem Lemuria was invented to solve: Madagascar and India were once joined in Gondwana, no sunken bridge required. This entry is honest context, placed in Tier C's timeline because it is the scientific event that ended Lemuria's scientific life while leaving its esoteric life untouched.
1913
TIER C
Besant & Leadbeater, Man: Whence, How and Whither. Annie Besant and C. W. Leadbeater published their clairvoyant history of humanity's deep past, revising the Theosophical chronology Blavatsky had set out in The Secret Doctrine: root races, sub-races and the early history of Atlantis and Lemuria were re-dated and re-narrated from claimed direct investigation of the Akashic record. It is the canonical clairvoyant revision of the Theosophical scheme, examined in full in The Theosophical Chronology.
1924
TIER C
Spence, The Problem of Atlantis. Lewis Spence attempted to return Atlantis to Donnelly's "purely historical" footing, against the Theosophical version, marshalling geology and folklore for a real Atlantic civilisation destroyed in stages. His quarrel with the clairvoyant school marks Tier C's internal fault line — literalist versus occult Atlantis — which runs through everything after him.
1926
TIER C
Churchward, The Lost Continent of Mu. James Churchward, a British-American engineer, relocated Mu to the Pacific and claimed to base his account on "Naacal tablets" shown to him in India decades earlier — tablets no one else has ever seen. His Mu, a 64-million-strong empire sunk some 12,000 years ago, became the Pacific standard, elaborated across four sequels. See Mu and Atlantis Reborn.
1920s–1940s
TIER C
The Cayce Atlantis readings. The American clairvoyant Edgar Cayce gave hundreds of "life readings" describing Atlantis — its crystals, its schisms, its staged destruction, and refugees to Egypt and Yucatán — and predicted that Atlantean remains would rise near Bimini in 1968–69. The Association for Research and Enlightenment preserves the stenographic record, making Cayce the best-documented seer in Tier C. The readings are handled as a document in The Cayce Readings.
1954
TIER C
De Camp, Lost Continents. L. Sprague de Camp's history of the Atlantis theme is Tier C's first great work of self-examination: a sympathetic but unsparing audit of Donnelly, Blavatsky, Churchward and the rest, tracing each claim to its source. Much of the source-tracing method this hub uses for Tier C was pioneered, in popular form, by de Camp.
1968
TIER C
The "Bimini Road" found. Divers off North Bimini, Bahamas, reported a regular formation of large submerged stones, promptly promoted — in the very years of Cayce's prophecy — as an Atlantean road or harbour work. Geological investigation identifies it as natural beachrock fractured in place. The episode is Tier C's cleanest modern case study: prophecy, discovery, promotion and testing, all on the record. The paperback Atlantis boom of 1969 onward carried the story worldwide. See The Cayce Readings.
1991
TIER C
Schoch redates the Sphinx. Geologist Robert Schoch argued (presented 1991; published in KMT, 1992) that water-erosion features on the Great Sphinx and its enclosure imply a substantially earlier carving date than the conventional c. 2500 BCE. The claim is labelled here as what it is: a fringe geological argument, rejected by most Egyptologists and many geologists, which nonetheless re-energised the lost-civilisation synthesis of the 1990s. Schoch's argument and its reception are kept distinct from the readings themselves in The Cayce Readings.
1995
TIER C
Hancock, Fingerprints of the Gods. Graham Hancock's bestseller synthesised Schoch, positional astronomy and world flood traditions into a single thesis: an advanced Ice Age civilisation, destroyed by cataclysm, whose survivors seeded Egypt, Mesopotamia and Mesoamerica. It is the most influential lost-civilisation book since Donnelly, and the point at which Tier C acquired its contemporary form. See Mu and Atlantis Reborn.
2010
TIER C
The Inner Garden Foundation founded. The Inner Garden Foundation, a modern Paracelsian order, dates its founding to 2010. Its published traditional timeline — a Lemurian golden age placed some 125,000 years ago, Atlantis falling in stages, history turning on equinoctial cycles — is treated in this hub as a living Tier C document: a primary source for what a contemporary tradition teaches, documented in Traditional Chronologies in its own terms, with its datings labelled as the tradition's claims.
2015
TIER C
Hancock, Magicians of the Gods. The sequel recast the lost civilisation around the Younger Dryas impact hypothesis (itself a contested scientific proposal, c. 12,800 BP) and around Göbekli Tepe, read as a survivor culture's warning and memorial. Tier A's most spectacular site thus became Tier C's Exhibit A — which is precisely why this hub keeps the tiers on separate badges even when they share a hillside. See Mu and Atlantis Reborn.
20th–21st century usage
TIER C
The equinoctial-cycle chronology scheme. Modern orders, the Inner Garden Foundation among them, commonly organise deep history by the precession of the equinoxes — a Great Year of roughly 25,700–26,000 years, divided into ages of about 2,150 years each — and key golden ages, falls and renewals to its turning points. The astronomy is real (precession is Tier A fact); the historical scheme built on it is a modern construction, described fairly and in detail in Traditional Chronologies.
// WHAT THE THREE TIMELINES TEACH TOGETHER //

Read down the tiers in parallel and a pattern emerges that no single tier shows alone. Tier A gives a deep past that is real but mute — pigment, bread, pillars, gold — with no names in it. Tier B gives the ancient world's own names for that muteness: kings before the flood, gods before the kings, a First Time before the temples. Tier C gives modern names, coined the moment science withdrew its own. The human need the three tiers answer is the same need. Only the evidence differs — and the evidence is the whole of the law here.

// A STANDING CORRECTION //

Popular timelines in this field repeat two datings this hub has checked and does not repeat: the Papyrus of Ani (the famous Book of the Dead) belongs to c. 1250 BCE, not to any antediluvian age; and the Arab conquest of Egypt was completed in 641 CE. Kingdom date-ranges throughout follow Shaw's conventional chronology, as in the Egyptology division.

Dates in this hub will be revised as the literature moves — Sulawesi's minimum ages have already moved once during the hub's own preparation. That is not a defect of Tier A; it is what Tier A is for.
