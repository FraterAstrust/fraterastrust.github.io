This is a Tier A article: its subject is a documented practice — observations written on clay, in dated tablets, by named professions, in excavated archives — and every load-bearing claim in it can be walked back to a published tablet, a standard edition, or a named scholar's argument. One element of the material does not belong to Tier A, and this article flags it wherever it appears: the Babylonians' own account of where their knowledge came from. The series present their contents as wisdom shown by the gods to the sages of old. That is the texts' claim about themselves — a Tier B framing, of exactly the kind this hub records in Antediluvian King Lists — and it is labelled as such in §II. The watching, the recording, and the computing are attested. The revelation is reported. The two are never allowed to borrow each other's strength.

The scale of the practice is the first fact. Between the middle of the eighth century BCE and the first century BCE, scholars in Babylon and its sister cities kept a nightly record of the sky — the Moon's place among the stars, the planets' appearances and disappearances, eclipses expected and eclipses seen, the weather, the height of the river, the price of barley — that runs, with gaps in survival but not in intent, for some seven hundred years. It is the longest continuous observational programme of the ancient world, and very probably the longest deliberate scientific record-keeping enterprise anywhere before the modern era. Out of that archive, and only out of it, Babylonian scholars extracted the periods of the Moon and planets, learned to predict eclipses, invented the zodiac of twelve equal signs, cast the first horoscopes, and built a mathematical astronomy — arithmetic without geometry, tables without a physical model — accurate enough that Greek astronomers took its numbers over wholesale, and precise enough that its eclipse records are still used today to measure the slowing rotation of the Earth.

This article follows the practice in the order the practice itself developed: first the watch (§I) and the omen library that gave the watch its purpose (§II); then the great compendium that ordered the visible sky (§III) and the oldest datable observations it absorbed (§IV); then the Diaries, the archive itself (§V), and the two predictive methods built directly on the archive — goal-year reckoning (§VI) and the Saros (§VII); then the zodiac (§VIII) and the horoscopes it made possible (§IX); then the full mathematical astronomy of Systems A and B (§§X–XI), with the computation of a lunar ephemeris walked through step by step; and finally the transmission of the whole apparatus to Greece (§XII). The cities of the story are Babylon above all, with Nineveh, Uruk, Borsippa, Sippar, and Nippur in supporting roles; its deep background — the urban world of temple households and scribal schools from which the watchers came — is set out in this hub's Ubaid and Uruk article.

I. The Observational Programme — Scribes, Temples, and the Nightly Watch

Babylonian sky science was not the hobby of gifted amateurs. It was a profession, with a title, a training, an employer, and a duty roster. The title was ṭupšar Enūma Anu Enlil — "scribe of (the series) Enuma Anu Enlil" — naming the man by the omen library he had mastered. The ṭupšarru (the word is plural in sense: a corps, not a person) were scholars attached to temples and to royal courts, heirs to a scribal education that ran from sign lists through the great canonical series to the commentaries that explained them. Francesca Rochberg's The Heavenly Writing (2004) remains the standard account of this world: a closed, hereditary-leaning guild of experts whose authority rested on demonstrable mastery of a written tradition, and whose daily work joined observation, textual scholarship, and counsel to power in a single job description.

The employer's interest was not curiosity. In the world the ṭupšarru served, celestial phenomena were signs addressed to the king and the land — the premise examined in §II — and a sign missed was a warning unheard. Hence the watch. The Akkadian term behind the later Diaries is naṣāru ša ginê, "the regular watching," and regularity is the operative word: the sky was observed not when something interesting was expected but every night, in the three watches into which the Babylonian night was divided, because the sign might be a halo, a conjunction, a planet's first appearance in the dawn, or an eclipse that no one had predicted. Observation was a standing duty of the state, maintained the way a garrison is maintained — continuously, at public expense, whether or not anything happened.

Watching, in Practice

What did a watcher actually do? The Diaries (§V) let the procedure be reconstructed in detail, because they are its product. Working from a temple precinct — in Babylon, the great temple complex of Esagila, Marduk's house, is the institutional home the later texts presuppose — the observer tracked a fixed checklist of phenomena. The Moon was the primary object: its position each night was recorded by its distance, in cubits (KUŠ, roughly two and a half degrees) and fingers (one thirtieth of a cubit), from one of a standard set of some thirty reference stars near the ecliptic — the Normal Stars, as modern scholarship calls them — with the direction specified: so many cubits "in front of," "behind," "above," or "below" the star. The planets were logged by the same star-relative method, and by their characteristic events: first appearance after invisibility, the two stationary points, acronychal (evening) rising, and last appearance. Around new and full moon the watchers measured, with the water clock, the six standard time intervals between the risings and settings of Sun and Moon that scholarship calls the Lunar Six (§V) — the raw material from which the Moon's behaviour would eventually be computed rather than watched.

Two features of this procedure deserve emphasis, because they are what make the archive science rather than mere chronicle. The first is standardisation: the same reference stars, the same units, the same formulaic phrasing, night after night, city after city, century after century — so that an observation made in 700 BCE and one made in 100 BCE are directly comparable numbers, not two men's impressions. The second is the honest negative. The Diaries record, in their set phrase, NU PAP — "not watched" — and DIR NU PAP, "cloudy, not watched," when the sky failed or the watch did. A predicted eclipse that cloud hid is entered as predicted, not as seen; an observed eclipse is marked with a different word order from a merely expected one. The distinction between observed, predicted, and not observed is maintained inside the record itself. That discipline — not any instrument — is the Babylonians' first invention, and everything else in this article is built on it.

Reporting to the King

The watch had a second product besides the archive: the report. From the Neo-Assyrian court of the eighth and seventh centuries BCE — the reigns above all of Esarhaddon (680–669 BCE) and Ashurbanipal (668–c. 627 BCE) — there survive more than five hundred astrological reports and a large correspondence of scholars' letters, preserved in the royal archives at Nineveh and published in the State Archives of Assyria series (the reports edited by Hermann Hunger as volume 8, the letters by Simo Parpola as volume 10). Scholars stationed in Nineveh itself and in Babylon, Borsippa, Uruk, and other cities wrote to the palace, sometimes daily, in a set form: the phenomenon observed; the omen or omens from Enuma Anu Enlil that it matched, quoted verbatim; and, where needed, a brief comment on what the omen did and did not portend for the king.

The letters show the system working under pressure, and they are the best evidence for how observation and interpretation actually interacted. Scholars argue with the evidence and with each other: a report is corrected because the observation was made through cloud; a colleague's reading is disputed on textual grounds; the king is reassured that a threatening configuration "does not concern" him, or advised that a ritual response is required. The gravest case was the lunar eclipse whose omen pointed at the king's death. The court's answer — documented step by step in the letters — was the substitute king ritual: a man of no account was enthroned, robed, anointed, and fed as king, the written omens were recited over him and bound into the hem of his garment, and the real king withdrew under the title "the farmer" until the danger period passed — whereupon the substitute, having absorbed the fate, was put to death. The ritual belongs to the history of religion; but its paperwork belongs here, because it shows what the observational programme was for in its own time: not the advancement of knowledge in the abstract, but the timely protection of the king and the state. That the same records later yielded mathematical astronomy is the great irony — and the great fact — of Babylonian sky science.

The institutional setting shifted with the empires — Assyrian, Babylonian, Persian, Seleucid — and the watch shifted with it, from a service of the Assyrian court at Nineveh to a function of the temples of Babylon and Uruk under foreign kings who had no personal stake in Babylonian omens at all. That the programme survived every conquest, and outlasted the belief system that had created it by centuries, is a datum to hold in mind through what follows: by the Hellenistic period the watchers were keeping the record because keeping the record was what their profession did, and the computations of §§X–XI, which need no omens at all, grew directly out of the archive the omen service had accumulated. The library that housed the series at Nineveh — Ashurbanipal's — is also the library that preserved the flood tablet of Gilgamesh; the archive habit was one habit, applied to every kind of text the scribes valued.

II. The Omen Series — Enuma Anu Enlil and the Framing Claim

The ṭupšarru took their title from a book. Enūma Anu Enlil — "When (the gods) Anu and Enlil…," from its opening words — is the great canonical series of celestial omens: some seventy tablets (sixty-eight to seventy, depending on the recension) holding an estimated 6,500 to 7,000 omens. Its materials reach back to the Old Babylonian period; its canonical form was assembled by the later second millennium BCE; its surviving copies, from Nineveh, Babylon, Uruk, and elsewhere, run from the eighth century BCE into the second century BCE. For more than a thousand years it was the reference work of Mesopotamian sky-watching: the text the watchers quoted to the king, commented on in an extensive commentary literature, and organised their observations around. Hunger and Pingree's Astral Sciences in Mesopotamia and Egypt (1999) is the standard modern survey of the series and its world.

Every omen has the same grammatical skeleton: a protasis, "If X is seen…," describing a celestial or atmospheric configuration, and an apodosis, "…then Y will happen," naming a consequence for the king, the army, the harvest, the land, or its neighbours. The series is organised by subject in four great blocks. Tablets 1–14 treat the Moon: its first appearance in the month, its shape, haloes, and conjunctions — closing with Tablet 14, which is not omens at all but a mathematical scheme for the Moon's visibility, the series' own acknowledgement that some of its subject is computable. Tablets 15–22 treat lunar eclipses, encoding date, watch of the night, and the eclipsed quadrant of the Moon's disc — each quadrant standing for a land — into predictions for regions and kings. Tablets 23–39 treat the Sun: its appearance, colour, and markings, and solar eclipses. Tablets 40–49 treat weather — thunder above all — and earthquakes. The final block, Tablets 50–70, treats the stars and planets, Venus first among them: it is in this block, as Tablet 63, that the Venus observations of §IV are preserved.

The Framing Claim — Knowledge Shown by the Gods

What did the series claim about itself? The question matters to this hub, because the answer is a claim of exactly the kind its tier system exists to label. The Mesopotamian scholarly tradition understood the sky as a text: the stars were šiṭir šamê, "the writing of heaven," a phrase Rochberg takes as the title and organising metaphor of her study — the gods wrote their intentions in celestial configurations, and the ṭupšarru's art was literacy in that script. The series themselves were presented, in colophons and in the scholarly tradition that surrounded them, as revealed knowledge: the lore of the apkallu, the antediluvian sages, transmitted from the gods through the sages to the scribal families who guarded it as a "secret of the great gods," not to be shown to the uninitiated. Learned catalogues assign series to divine or semi-divine authorship; the knowledge is old because it was given old.

The claim that Babylonian sky knowledge was shown by the gods is the texts' claim about themselves. In this hub's terms it is Tier B framing inside a Tier A practice : the practice — the watching, recording, and computing — is attested by the tablets themselves; the revelation is what the tablets say , and it is reported here as their saying, never endorsed as this archive's finding. The same discipline governs the parallel material wherever it occurs in this archive: the medical series likewise present their lore as handed down from the gods and the sages, and are treated under the same rule in Omens and Medicine . A reader who keeps the two apart loses nothing of the Babylonian achievement. The achievement is greater, not smaller, when it is credited to the watchers rather than to the revelation they believed in: men, not gods, kept the seven-hundred-year record.

Two consequences of the framing are historical facts in their own right, whatever one makes of the claim itself. First, the omen form shaped the observations: because the series asked whether the Moon appeared on the expected day, whether an eclipse began in a given watch and quadrant, whether Venus was present or absent for a stated interval, the watchers recorded dates, times, positions, and durations — precisely the quantities from which periods and predictions can later be extracted. Divination's checklist became astronomy's dataset. Second, the framing made the knowledge conservative and cumulative: a revealed corpus is copied exactly, collated against old exemplars, and commented on rather than discarded — which is why observations made in the seventeenth century BCE (§IV) were still being copied, accurately enough to argue over, in the seventh. The archive exists because the tradition believed the texts must not be lost. The historian may give the credit to the belief's effects without sharing the belief.

III. MUL.APIN — The Compendium That Ordered the Sky

If Enuma Anu Enlil is the watchers' omen library, MUL.APIN is their handbook of the sky itself. The title is the work's first word: mulAPIN, "the Plough," the constellation with which its star list opens. The compendium runs to two tablets (a third, auxiliary tablet of commentary is sometimes attached in the manuscripts), and it survives in dozens of copies and fragments from Assyrian and Babylonian libraries — the earliest dated copy was written in 687 BCE, and copies were still being made around 300 BCE. The standard edition is Hunger and Pingree's MUL.APIN: An Astronomical Compendium in Cuneiform (1989), superseded in presentation by Hunger and John Steele's Routledge edition, The Babylonian Astronomical Compendium MUL.APIN (2019).

When was it compiled? The task of this article states the conventional answer, and the literature supports it with a nuance that must be kept visible. The compilation is generally placed around c. 1100 BCE, assembled from older materials — the so-called "Three Stars Each" lists (the astrolabe texts, assigning three stars to each month) are its direct ancestors — and Hunger and Pingree argue for a date around 1000 BCE for the compendium in something like its final form. Independent checks on the data point older: computations by Bradley Schaefer and by Teije de Jong of the heliacal rising dates embedded in the text find them fitting the latitude of Assur best for an epoch somewhere in the range c. 1400–1100 BCE. The honest summary is the one this article uses throughout: MUL.APIN was compiled around 1100 BCE, give or take a century, from observational material some of which is older still — a handbook whose contents, like the Venus Tablet's (§IV), are a stratified deposit, not a single season's work.

Tablet I — The Static Sky

The first tablet is an inventory and a map. Its opening list catalogues the stars and constellations — seventy-one entries in the usual counting of the opening list — and assigns every one of them to one of three great bands across the sky, the paths of three gods, defined by celestial latitude: the northern path of Enlil, with thirty-three stars and constellations; the equatorial path of Anu, a belt roughly seventeen degrees to either side of the celestial equator, with twenty-three; and the southern path of Ea, with fifteen. The scheme is the first known attempt to organise the entire visible sky under a single geographical system: to know a star's path is to know, at once, roughly where it rises, how high it climbs, and in what company it travels. Many of the Babylonian constellation figures in these lists — the Bull of Heaven, the Great Twins, the Crab, the Lion, the Scorpion, the Goat-Fish — are the direct ancestors, name and figure alike, of the zodiacal constellations the Greek world would inherit (§XII).

The lists that follow turn the inventory into a clock and a calendar. One list gives the heliacal rising dates of thirty-four stars and constellations — the date of each one's first visible rising in the dawn after its period of invisibility in the Sun's glare — plotted against an ideal year of twelve thirty-day months, 360 days, in which the equinoxes fall on the 15th of the first and seventh months and the solstices on the 15th of the fourth and tenth. Further lists give the stars that rise and set simultaneously, the numbers of days between successive heliacal risings, and the ziqpu stars — stars whose culmination (passage across the meridian) at nightfall or through the night served as the watcher's clock, marking the hours against which the water clock was checked. The tablet closes with the constellations that stand in the path of the Moon: the band of sky along the ecliptic through which the Moon, and with it the Sun and planets, actually travel — the working sky of all later Babylonian astronomy, and the raw material from which the zodiac of §VIII would be cut.

Tablet II — The Moving Sky, the Calendar, and Time

The second tablet treats everything that changes. It names the Sun and the five planets and states that they travel the Moon's path; it gives schematic periods of visibility and invisibility for each planet — how many days, in the idealised scheme, each is a morning star, absent, an evening star, absent again; it fixes the cardinal points of the year by observation — which stars rise, and where the full moon stands, at solstice and equinox; and it preserves the compendium's most practically consequential material, its intercalation rules.

The problem those rules solve is the calendar's permanent defect. Twelve lunar months total about 354 days, eleven days short of the solar year; left uncorrected, the first month, Nisanu, would drift through the seasons, and the spring festivals and the barley harvest with it. The correction is intercalation: in some years a thirteenth month — a second Addaru, or a second Ululu — is inserted. MUL.APIN's rules decide the question by observation. The best known is the Pleiades rule: observe the Moon's position against the Pleiades at the opening of Nisanu — in the formulation preserved in the compendium and its tradition, if the Moon stands in the prescribed relation to the Pleiades on the evening appointed at the month's head, the year is normal; if the conjunction falls late (on the third day rather than the first), the year is intercalary. Companion rules use the heliacal risings of the Pleiades, of Sirius (the Arrow), and of ŠU.PA (the stars around Arcturus), and the Moon's relation to them. The procedure, step by step, is: (1) watch at the appointed dawns and evenings; (2) compare the observed configuration with the rule's stated norm; (3) if the sky is behind its schedule, proclaim the extra month. For centuries the proclamation was the king's, made on scholarly advice — the letters of §I include calendar business — and only in the fifth century BCE was the decision itself reduced to arithmetic, when the fixed nineteen-year cycle (235 lunar months ≈ 19 solar years; seven intercalary months in nineteen years, in a set order) passed from near-regular practice to official rule, fully standardised by 380 BCE on the evidence assembled by Parker and Dubberstein and summarised by Rochberg. MUL.APIN stands at the head of that development: its rules are the observational form of a problem its successors would solve by computation.

Tablet II also carries the compendium's timekeeping schemes: a table of the gnomon's shadow lengths at stated hours on the solstices and equinoxes, and a water-clock scheme in which the length of day and night through the year is given by the weight of water — in minas — to be measured out: at the summer solstice, four minas for the day against two for the night in the schematic version, three and three at the equinoxes, the values varying month by month in a simple arithmetic progression. The day:night ratio of 2:1 built into that scheme is schematically wrong for Babylon's latitude (the true extreme ratio is nearer 3:2, which later texts adopt), and the error is itself instructive: MUL.APIN's numbers are a model of the year — tidy, computable, and only approximately true — set down beside the observations it was the watchers' duty to keep making. Babylonian sky science would live in that tension, between the scheme and the sky, for the rest of its history; its greatest achievements (§§X–XI) are what happened when the schemes were forced, column by column, to fit the archive.

The compendium ends, characteristically, with omens. The placement is the document's own statement of purpose: the star lists, the rising dates, the intercalation rules, and the clocks exist so that the signs of Enuma Anu Enlil can be caught, timed, and named correctly. MUL.APIN is not a rival to the omen series but its instrument panel — the ordered sky against which every sign must be located before it can be read.

// READING MUL.APIN'S DATES //

The dates in MUL.APIN are dates in the ideal 360-day year, not entries from a diary. "ŠU.PA rises on the 15th of Ululu" states a position in the scheme — a norm against which a given year's actual rising, early or late by days, could be judged and the calendar corrected. Confusing the scheme with a record is the commonest beginner's error with this text, and the editions of Hunger and Pingree and of Hunger and Steele both warn against it explicitly.

IV. The Venus Tablet of Ammisaduqa — Old Observations, Open Chronology

The oldest body of planetary observations to survive from anywhere is preserved as the sixty-third tablet of the omen series itself. The Venus Tablet of Ammisaduqa — Enuma Anu Enlil Tablet 63, edited in the standard modern treatment by Erica Reiner and David Pingree, Babylonian Planetary Omens, Part One: The Venus Tablet of Ammisaduqa (1975) — records, for twenty-one years, the first and last visibilities of Venus: the dates on which the planet appeared and disappeared as morning and evening star, and the lengths of its intervals of invisibility between disappearance and reappearance. The observations are attributed to the reign of Ammisaduqa, the fourth king after Hammurabi in the First Dynasty of Babylon — that is, on the conventional Middle Chronology, to 1646–1626 BCE. No tablet of the seventeenth century BCE survives: the text reaches us in some twenty copies and fragments, all of the first millennium BCE, the Nineveh copies of the seventh century BCE prominent among them — a transmission of a thousand years, through the copying discipline described in §II, between the observation and the oldest manuscript we can hold.

The form of the entries shows the double nature of the whole tradition in miniature. Each observation is stated in the calendar — month, day, direction of appearance or disappearance, days of absence — and each is followed by its omen: the apodosis for the land, of the type "the harvest of the land will prosper" or rain, flood, or the king's fortune, attached to that configuration of Venus. To the scribe who copied it, the tablet was a chapter of the omen series that happened to be built from real dated observations. To the modern historian, it is the reverse: a chapter of observational astronomy that happens to carry its omens with it. Both readings are correct about the same object, and the tablet is the cleanest single proof that in Babylonia the two were one enterprise.

As observation, the data are good enough to argue with — which is precisely the problem. Venus's appearances repeat on a near-exact cycle: five synodic periods of the planet equal eight years, less about four days, so that the pattern of visibilities in any twenty-one-year run closely resembles the pattern eight, sixteen, and twenty-four years earlier or later, and resembles it again, with accumulating drift, at intervals governed by that near period — the candidate absolute datings for the same tablet fall in families separated by fifty-six or sixty-four years. The relative chronology of the First Dynasty is secure: king lists and year names fix who reigned, in what order, for how long, and Ammisaduqa's distance from Hammurabi and from the sack of Babylon that ended the dynasty is not in dispute. What the Venus data are asked to supply is the absolute placement of that whole floating sequence — and the tablet, because of Venus's obliging periodicity, supplies not one answer but a ladder of them.

This archive does not adopt a chronology for the second millennium BCE, and no date in this article that depends on the Venus Tablet should be read as settled. The Middle Chronology (Hammurabi 1792–1750 BCE) remains the conventional default of textbooks and museum labels, and dates quoted from the literature in this article — including Ammisaduqa's reign above — are given on it and labelled as such. But the contest is live and the positions are held on evidence: Peter Huber's statistical studies of the tablet's month-lengths and Venus data have repeatedly favoured the High chronology; Teije de Jong and collaborators, modelling the atmospheric visibility of Venus at Babylon, found the fits for the rival chronologies statistically indistinguishable — and argued that a cluster of anomalously poor visibilities in Ammisaduqa's twelfth and thirteenth years might register the atmospheric effects of the Thera (Santorini) eruption, which would tie the choice to the radiocarbon dating of that event; dendrochronological and radiocarbon arguments have been deployed against the Low chronology in particular; and a minority of scholars question whether the transmitted Venus data, copied for a millennium and partly schematic in places, can bear the weight placed on them at all. The spread between the extreme candidates exceeds a century and a half. Where this hub's other articles need second-millennium dates — the king-list material of Antediluvian King Lists among them — the same rule applies: the chronology in use is named, and the debate is not silently resolved.

What the tablet establishes beyond the chronological quarrel is worth stating plainly, because it is the foundation fact of this whole article. By the seventeenth century BCE — on any of the rival chronologies — Babylonian observers were already recording a planet's first and last visibilities to the day, in a standard calendar format, as part of a state scholarly enterprise; and their successors valued the record enough to copy it, with its omens, for a thousand years. The observational programme of §I did not begin with the Diaries. The Diaries are what happened when a habit already a millennium old was intensified into a system.

V. The Astronomical Diaries — From Nabonassar to the Last Tablet

The Astronomical Diaries are the archive the watch produced: cuneiform tablets, each normally covering six months of a Babylonian year (occasionally seven), in which the phenomena of every night were entered in a fixed order and a fixed phraseology. The edition of record is Astronomical Diaries and Related Texts from Babylonia, begun by Abraham Sachs and completed by Hermann Hunger (volumes I–III, the Diaries proper, 1988–1996, with the related text genres — goal-year texts, almanacs — in the volumes that followed). More than a thousand diary tablets and fragments survive, the great majority in the British Museum; most come from Babylon itself, with a smaller body from Uruk.

When do they begin? The tradition of the discipline, ancient and modern, gives the same answer, and the manuscripts give a more complicated one — both must be stated. The systematic keeping of the record is associated with the reign of Nabonassar (Nabû-nāṣir, 747–734 BCE), the Babylonian king whose accession the Greek astronomer Ptolemy, nine centuries later, took as the epoch of his own chronological system — the Era of Nabonassar, beginning 26 February 747 BCE in proleptic Julian reckoning — precisely because, as Ptolemy says in the Almagest, ancient observations were preserved complete from that reign onward. The oldest Babylonian eclipse records Ptolemy actually cites belong to 721 and 720 BCE, within a generation of the era's start. The surviving diary tablets, however, begin later: the oldest preserved diary fragment is dated to 652 BCE, the oldest preserved diary in the full standard form to 568 BCE, and the record grows dense and near-continuous only from the fifth century BCE. John Steele's study of the Diaries' early history (in Keeping Watch in Babylon, 2019) adds the necessary caution that the "Era of Nabonassar" as a deliberate foundation — a king ordering systematic astronomy into being — is a modern construction laid over a real change: what the evidence shows is a marked increase in the quality, frequency, and preservation of observations from the mid-eighth century onward, from which the later compilations demonstrably drew. The last known diary is dated to 61 BCE. Between those bounds the programme ran for some seven centuries — through the Neo-Assyrian, Neo- Babylonian, Persian, and Seleucid empires and into the Parthian period — and the related almanac tradition carried Babylonian astronomical publication a further century and more beyond it.

What a Diary Contains

A diary's month is a dossier. Its astronomical core is the nightly log: the Moon's passage by the Normal Stars, entered in the form Rochberg's Heavenly Writing quotes from the diary for 331 BCE — the Moon so many cubits below one reference star, having passed a fraction of a cubit behind another — with the laconic honesty of the working record preserved in the same lines: an equinox entered, and against another night, "I did not watch"; against another, "clouds were in the sky." The Lunar Six — The Watch's Precision Instrument

The most consequential measurements in the Diaries are the least spectacular. The Lunar Six — the term is Sachs's — are six time intervals, measured in time-degrees (UŠ; sixty to the double-hour, four of our minutes to the degree), between the crossings of the horizon by Sun and Moon at the month's two critical moments. At the month's opening stands NA: on the first evening, the interval from sunset to the setting of the newly visible crescent — the measurement that certified the month had begun. Around full moon come four: ŠÚ, moonset to sunrise, when the Moon sets last before sunrise; NA again (a second, distinct interval), sunrise to moonset, when it sets first after; ME, moonrise to sunset, when it rises last before sunset; and GE₆, sunset to moonrise, when it rises first after. At the month's end stands KUR: moonrise to sunrise on the morning of the Moon's last visibility. None of the six requires an instrument beyond a water clock and a clear horizon; together they pin the Moon's motion, month by month, with a precision no positional estimate could match, because they time the Moon against the Sun at exactly the configurations — conjunction and opposition — that lunar theory must predict. When Babylonian astronomy turned mathematical (§§X–XI), it was the Lunar Six, accumulated over centuries, that the new computations were built to reproduce. The procedure text tradition (§XI) contains whole methods whose sole purpose is to predict next month's NA from values one Saros, and one Saros plus six months, earlier — the archive feeding the algorithm, in the archive's own terms.

The compilation procedure is itself recoverable. The Diaries as we have them are fair copies: nightly observations, first noted as they were made, were gathered into the month's sections, the month closed with its summary — the Lunar Six, the month's length, the prices, the river — and six months bound the tablet. Derivative genres then mined the archive for use: goal-year texts (§VI) excerpted the phenomena due to repeat; almanacs and Normal Star almanacs published the coming year's predictions, computed from the archive, for working astrologers; and the mathematical ephemerides of §§X–XI replaced excerpting with calculation altogether. The Diary is thus not one text type among others. It is the database of Babylonian astronomy, and every other genre in this article is a query run against it.

VI. Goal-Year Texts — Prediction by Looking Back

The first predictive astronomy built on the archive required no mathematics at all — only the discovery, which the archive alone made possible, that celestial phenomena repeat after characteristic periods. A planet's first appearance, its stations, its last appearance: after a certain number of years the whole sequence recurs on nearly the same calendar dates and at nearly the same places among the stars. Find the period, and prediction becomes retrieval: to know what Jupiter will do next year, copy out what Jupiter did one period ago, and apply the small known correction. The texts that embody the method are the Goal-Year Texts (edited by Hunger as volume VI of the Astronomical Diaries and Related Texts, 2006): compilations made for a stated future year — the "goal year" of their modern name — assembling, section by section, the observations from exactly one period earlier for each body. The method was in use by about 600 BCE and remained a working tool for the rest of the tradition's life, alongside the mathematical astronomy that eventually supplemented it.

The Procedure, Step by Step

A goal-year text for, say, the coming year Y was compiled as follows — the structure is standard, and Hunger's edition lets it be stated as a recipe. Step one: take down from the archive the diary for year Y − 71, and copy Jupiter's synodic phenomena — first appearance, first station, acronychal rising, second station, last appearance — into the new text's first section. Step two: from year Y − 83, copy Jupiter's passages by the Normal Stars into the second section; the longer period governs the passages because it restores the planet to the same star, not merely to the same date. Step three: from year Y − 8, copy Venus's visibilities and passages, applying the standing correction the procedure texts state outright — in the rendering John Steele quotes from the goal-year procedure tablet BM 45728: appearances of Venus are found by going back eight years and subtracting four days; "four days you subtract, and you see it." Step four: repeat the retrieval for Mercury (Y − 46), Saturn (Y − 59), and Mars (Y − 79 for its phenomena, Y − 47 for its passages). Step five: for the Moon, copy the eclipse reports of Y − 18 and the Lunar Six of the year before the goal year at the eighteen- and nineteen-year removes the lunar section prescribes. Step six: adjust every copied date for the calendar's own drift — intercalary months in the interval shift a phenomenon's month, and the small standing corrections (Venus's four days are the documented type) shift its day. The result is not a table of predictions in the modern sense but something more transparent: last time's record, re-dated — a forecast whose entire evidence is exhibited on its face, and whose errors, when the goal year arrived and the watch compared, could be measured against the same archive that produced it.

The periods deserve a moment's respect as discoveries. None is obvious; each represents the moment when a pattern in a multi-generational archive crossed the threshold from noise into knowledge. Venus's eight years could be found within one scholar's lifetime of watching. Saturn's fifty-nine, Jupiter's seventy-one and eighty-three, Mars's seventy-nine could not: they are periods longer than a career, extractable only because the Diaries outlived their makers and were kept in a form — standardised, dated, retrievable — that let a later scribe ask the archive questions its first compilers never posed. And the periods were known to be approximate: the mathematical texts work with the long refinements in the table's last column (427 years for Jupiter, and so on), built by combining goal-year periods, and Ptolemy, centuries later, would take over the goal-year periods themselves, through Hipparchus, as the foundation of his planetary mean motions (§XII) — the surest external certificate of what the method was worth.

// WHY "GOAL YEAR"? //

The name is modern — Sachs's coinage, from the texts' own heading, which Hunger renders "First days, appearances, passings and eclipses which are established for year X." The texts do not call themselves predictions. They are dossiers assembled for a year, from which the working astrologer — casting a nativity (§IX), advising a client, watching for an omen — read off what the sky of that year would do. Prediction by retrieval is still prediction; the goal-year texts are where Babylonian astronomy first becomes a forward-looking science.

VII. The Saros — Eclipse Prediction as Procedure

Of all the periods in the archive, one mattered more than any other, because the phenomenon it governed was the most feared sign in the sky. A lunar eclipse read against the king could set the substitute-king machinery of §I in motion; a predicted eclipse was therefore not an intellectual ornament but a piece of state intelligence, wanted in advance, in writing, with its date and its hour. The period that supplied it is the Saros: 223 synodic months — 6,585⅓ days, eighteen years and about eleven days — after which the geometry of Sun, Moon, and node very nearly repeats. The near-exactness is a triple coincidence the Babylonians exploited without needing to state it in modern form: 223 returns of the Moon to the Sun (synodic months) ≈ 239 returns to the same point of its anomalistic cycle (fastest/slowest) ≈ 242 returns to the same node (draconic months). Because all three cycles come round together, an eclipse is followed, one Saros later, by an eclipse of similar character — similar magnitude, similar duration, similar place in the Moon's cycle — displaced by the leftover third of a day, roughly eight hours, which shifts its visibility some 120 degrees of longitude westward. The Saros was known and in use by the seventh century BCE at the latest; Lis Brack-Bernsen and Steele have shown that the scheme for the length of the Saros — modelling its excess over whole days by a zigzag function varying between about six and eleven hours — was in place by the mid-sixth century BCE.

From Period to Calendar of Possibilities

The Babylonian achievement was to turn the period into a scheme — a standing grid of eclipse possibilities that could be run forward indefinitely. The logic is elementary and is set out in the modern literature (Steele; Brack-Bernsen and Steele, Centaurus 2005) exactly as follows. Eclipses can occur only at syzygies that fall near a node, and such "eclipse possibilities" recur at intervals of six months, with an occasional interval of five. Within one Saros of 223 months, let a possibilities stand six months apart and b stand five months apart: then 6a + 5b = 223, whose solution in whole numbers is a = 33, b = 5 — thirty-eight eclipse possibilities per Saros. Distribute the five short intervals as evenly as the arithmetic allows, and the possibilities fall into five groups of eight, seven, eight, seven, and eight. Lay the groups out as the columns of a table — the "Saros canon" form in which such schemes survive — anchor the table to the observed record by matching its possibilities against the eclipses the Diaries actually report, and the grid thereafter generates itself: every cell is a date on which an eclipse is possible, repeating Saros by Saros, for as many cycles as the scribe cares to copy forward.

// THE ECLIPSE PROCEDURE, STEP BY STEP //

1. Locate the possibility. From the Saros scheme, read off the month — and, within it, the syzygy (new moon for a solar, full moon for a lunar eclipse) — at which the next possibility falls. 2. Take the predecessor. Find the corresponding eclipse one Saros (223 months) earlier in the Diaries or in the scheme's own record: its date, its observed time, its magnitude. 3. Carry the date forward. The new possibility falls 223 months after the old, on the calendar date the scheme assigns; adjust for any intercalary months in the interval. 4. Carry the time forward. Add to the predecessor's time the Saros excess — the amount by which 223 months exceeds a whole number of days, taken not as a constant but from the scheme's zigzag function for that point in the cycle (between roughly six and eleven hours) — to obtain the predicted time of day or night. 5. Judge visibility and magnitude. From the shifted time, decide whether the eclipse can be seen at Babylon at all (the eight-hour displacement means a lunar eclipse well seen one Saros ago may fall in daylight this time); from the Moon's distance from the node, carried in the scheme, estimate whether the eclipse will occur and how great it will be. 6. Enter it as a prediction. The coming eclipse is written into the Diary and the almanacs as predicted — the record's own marking (§I) keeps the forecast distinct from the observation that will confirm, correct, or (through cloud) fail to test it. A prediction, in this system, is a possibility with a date and an hour , not a certainty — and for solar eclipses, whose visibility is local and whose Babylonian prediction remained the weaker art, the texts' caution is visible in the record itself.

Two clarifications protect the achievement from its popular retellings. First, the Babylonians did not predict eclipses by possessing a theory of shadows and orbits in the Greek manner; they predicted them the way the goal-year texts predict Venus — by period and retrieval, extended by the Saros scheme into a self-perpetuating grid — and the method works without any model of why eclipses happen. Second, the modern word "Saros" itself is a later label, fastened onto the 223-month period by a chain of transmission through Greek and Byzantine sources (the name comes from a Byzantine lexicon's entry, applied to this period by Edmond Halley in 1691); the Babylonian texts know the period and the scheme, not the name. Neither clarification diminishes the fact: by the sixth century BCE, Babylonian scholars could tell their king, months or years ahead, on which nights an eclipse might darken the Moon and at about what hour to watch for it — a capability no earlier society is known to have possessed, and the direct ancestor of every eclipse prediction since.

VIII. The Zodiac — Twelve Equal Signs

The single most consequential invention in the history of astronomy after the archive itself was made in Babylon in the fifth century BCE, and it was an invention of pure abstraction. The watchers had always located the Moon and planets among the constellations of the Moon's path (§III) — real star figures, of unequal size, unevenly spaced, excellent as landmarks and useless as a coordinate system: a position "in the Lion" cannot be added to a period, subtracted from a date, or carried in a table. The invention replaced the figures with a grid. The ecliptic — the Sun's annual road, and the Moon's and planets' highway — was divided into twelve equal signs of thirty degrees each, 360 degrees in all, each sign bearing the name of a constellation that lay in or near it, and every position in the sky of a wandering body thereafter stated as a number: so many degrees within such a sign. The signs are not the constellations, and the Babylonians, who kept both systems in simultaneous use — constellations for the nightly log's star passages, signs for computation — never confused them. The zodiac is a measuring instrument laid over the sky, and it is the instrument that turned Babylonian astronomy from record-keeping into mathematics.

The dating of the invention is a matter of attestation, and this article states it at the strength the tablets support. The twelve-sign zodiac is first attested in texts of the fifth century BCE — a tablet dated to 475 BCE is among the earliest witnesses commonly cited — and it is in universal Babylonian use by the century's end: the earliest horoscopes (§IX), dated 410 BCE, already presuppose it as a matter of routine. Behind it lies a visible preparation: the seventeen or eighteen constellations of the Moon's path in the older lists, progressively regularised; the schematic 360-degree division of the circle, cousin to the 360-day ideal year of MUL.APIN; and the practice, growing through the fifth century, of stating planetary positions in degrees within a constellation's span — the coordinate habit waiting for its grid. The Babylonian zodiac is sidereal: its zero point is fixed against the stars (by the Normal Star frame), not against the equinox. The two mathematical systems of §X register the equinox's position within that star-fixed frame as a constant — at 10° of Aries in System A, at 8° of Aries in System B — a detail worth pausing on, because it shows the system's self-awareness: the Babylonians knew where the seasonal point fell among the stars and treated it as a measured quantity, not as the definition of the scale. The Greek redefinition of the zodiac as tropical — zero point at the equinox itself — came later, with Hipparchus, and is one of the few structural changes the Greeks made to what they received (§XII).

Why twelve signs of thirty degrees? Because the grid was built to mesh with everything else the tradition already used: twelve months in the ideal year, thirty days in the ideal month, 360 UŠ in the circle as in the day. A planet's mean motion could now be stated as a whole number of signs per period; a position could be advanced by a period with pencil-and-clay arithmetic; and the goal-year retrievals of §VI acquired a positional precision the constellation language could never give — a passage "two cubits below" a star for the diary, a longitude in sign and degree for the table. The zodiac's invention is therefore best understood not as an astrological event but as a metrological one: the moment Babylonian sky science acquired the coordinate system in which mathematical astronomy — and, not incidentally, every horoscope ever cast since — is necessarily conducted.

IX. The First Horoscopes — The Sky at a Birth

Within a generation of the zodiac's attestation, the archive produced its most famous application. The Babylonian horoscopes — the name is Sachs's, given to the genre in 1952; Francesca Rochberg's Babylonian Horoscopes (1998) is the edition and study of record — are small tablets, some thirty of them known, each recording the state of the sky at the birth of a named (or nameable) individual child. The earliest two are dated to 410 BCE — births in January and April of that year; the latest known example belongs to 69 BCE. Between those dates the genre runs quietly alongside the Diaries and the ephemerides, sharing their personnel, their archive, and their arithmetic. It is the first personal astrology in the world that can be documented tablet by tablet — the moment the sky, read for a thousand years as a correspondence addressed to kings, began to be read for private persons.

A Babylonian horoscope's contents are austere beside its Greek descendants. The tablet gives the date and the time of the birth (in the night's watches or in time-degrees from sunset or sunrise); the positions of the Moon, the Sun, and the five planets in the zodiac, by sign and degree; a selection of calendar and lunar data — the length of the birth month, Lunar Six values, eclipse information where relevant; and, in many examples, a short set of predictions for the child's life, drawn from the positions by the logic of the nativity omens, the branch of the omen tradition that had long read a birth's circumstances as signs. One of the 410 BCE texts, in Hunger's summary of the corpus, closes its positional record with the flat colophon of the whole enterprise: at that time — the Moon in its sign, the Sun in its, Jupiter, Venus, Mars, and Saturn in theirs — "the child was born." The predictions that accompany such records are terse and conventional: length of life, prosperity or its opposite, the character of the child's fortune in the omen tradition's stock phrases. Nothing in the tablets suggests the elaborate chart interpretation of later astrology; the Babylonian document is a record with a verdict attached, closer in spirit to a diary entry for a single life than to a Greek genethlialogical treatise.

// "HOROSCOPE" WITHOUT THE HOROSKOPOS //

The genre's modern name must not be allowed to smuggle Greek contents into Babylonian tablets. The Babylonian texts contain no ascendant — no computation of the zodiacal point rising at the birth moment, the horoskopos from which the word "horoscope" derives — and no system of houses or places built upon one. That apparatus is a Hellenistic addition, assembled in Egypt from Babylonian positions, Egyptian timekeeping, and Greek geometry, and it is treated in this archive where it belongs: in the Greco-Roman hub's Greek Astrology as a System , which takes the present article as its upstream source. What Babylon contributed to the horoscope was its indispensable foundation: the zodiac itself, the planetary positions stated in it, the periods and methods by which those positions could be computed for a date when no observer had watched — and the idea, new in the fifth century, that the sky at a birth was a text about the person born. Rochberg's term for the shift is exact: a democratisation of celestial divination, from the king to the client.

The horoscopes are also a proof of concept for the predictive machinery of §§VI–VII, and it is in that capacity that they matter most to this article. A birth record's positions are rarely observations. Many horoscopes were cast retrospectively — for a child already born, sometimes years before — and even a contemporary casting could not wait on a clear night for every planet. The positions on the tablets were computed: taken from the Diaries where available, filled out from goal-year dossiers and, increasingly, from the almanacs and ephemerides of §§X–XI. Their accuracy can be checked against modern computation, and it is the accuracy of the methods behind them — good to within a degree or so for the slow planets, cruder for Mercury — not of any night's watching. The horoscope is therefore the archive's certificate of maturity: by 410 BCE the system could state the sky for a date it had not seen, in coordinates it had just invented, for a purpose its founders had not imagined. Every subsequent horoscope in history — Greek, Indian, Persian, Arabic, Latin — is a descendant of these thirty clay tablets and of the seven-hundred-year watch that made them computable.

X. Mathematical Astronomy — Systems A and B

The final Babylonian transformation replaced retrieval with computation. In the corpus modern scholarship calls the ACT texts — from Otto Neugebauer's Astronomical Cuneiform Texts (1955), the edition that first presented them whole — some hundreds of tablets from Babylon and Uruk, dating overwhelmingly to the Seleucid and early Parthian periods (c. 300–50 BCE), contain ephemerides: tables whose rows are successive months (for the Moon) or successive characteristic events (for a planet), and whose columns are the computed quantities — position in the zodiac, velocity, latitude, time of the event, length of daylight — from which the phenomenon could be known without watching at all. Beside the ephemerides stand the procedure texts, which state, in words and worked examples, the rules by which the tables are generated: Mathieu Ossendrijver's Babylonian Mathematical Astronomy: Procedure Texts (2012) is their standard edition. The two genres together are a complete computational discipline — algorithm and output, nothing else: no diagrams, no physical model, no statement anywhere of what the Moon or a planet is or why it moves. The tables simply work, and working was the criterion.

Two Ways to Make a Quantity Vary

Every varying quantity in the system — the Sun's monthly progress, the Moon's velocity, the length of a lunation, the duration of daylight — is generated by one of two arithmetical devices, and the two devices define the two great systems, which coexisted for two centuries, in both cities, sometimes on the same shelf.

System A, the older — John Britton's studies place its lunar theory's invention at Babylon shortly after the opening of the fourth century BCE — models variation by the step function: a quantity holds one constant value across one arc of the zodiac and a second constant value across the complementary arc, switching at fixed boundaries. The System A Sun is the type case: across a fast arc of 194° the Sun advances 30° per month; across the remaining slow arc of 166° it advances 28;7,30° per month. The device is crude as physics and elegant as arithmetic: to advance the Sun, the scribe needs only to know which arc he is in, add the arc's constant, and watch for the boundary crossing, at which the excess is carried into the next arc at the other rate by a fixed rule. System A's parameters are chosen for internal consistency — its periods close exactly — and Britton's analysis shows its lunar theory to have been constructed as a unified design: the effects of the Moon's own varying speed (its anomaly) modelled first, the Sun's contribution separated and fitted after.

System B, developed roughly a century later (Uruk is its traditional association, though the evidence places it in both cities), models variation by the linear zigzag function: a quantity increases by a constant difference, month by month, to a fixed maximum; then decreases by the same difference to a fixed minimum; then increases again — a triangle wave in numbers, reflecting at its bounds. Where System A asks where the body is (which arc?), System B asks only which line of the table one is on. Its parameters are the more accurate of the two systems': its mean synodic month, to take the famous case, is the value 29;31,50,8,20 days — in the sexagesimal notation explained in §XI, 29 days plus 31/60 plus 50/3600 plus 8/216000 plus 20/12960000 of a day — which differs from the modern mean value by less than half a second per month, and which Ptolemy, in Almagest IV.2, transmits as the value Hipparchus had taken over (§XII). System B's lunar velocity zigzag runs on a period of 251 synodic months (in which the Moon makes 269 anomalistic returns) — the same relation Ptolemy quotes. Neither system is a draft of the other; they are two complete, rival arithmetics for the same sky, and the tablets preserve both without embarrassment, as a modern bureau might keep two ephemeris programs.

The Jupiter Trapezoids — Geometry in an Abstract Space

One group of procedure texts shows the discipline reaching beyond its own arithmetical habits. In five tablets dated between 350 and 50 BCE, analysed by Ossendrijver in Science in 2016, the scribes compute Jupiter's displacement over the first sixty days after its morning appearance by a geometrical construction: Jupiter's daily velocity decreases linearly across the interval, so the scribes form the figure that velocity-against-time describes — a trapezoid whose parallel sides are the velocities on the first and sixtieth days — and compute its area, obtaining the total displacement, 10;45° in the worked case. They then divide the trapezoid into two smaller trapezoids of equal area to find the day on which Jupiter covered half that distance. The figure exists in no physical space: it is a graph, velocity plotted against time, and its area is what a modern student would call an integral — the same construction the Oxford calculators of the fourteenth century CE would formalise as the mean speed theorem, anticipated here by some fifteen hundred years. The tablets contain no drawing; the trapezoid lives in the procedure's words. It is the exception that illuminates the rule: Babylonian astronomy was arithmetical by choice and by triumph, not by incapacity — when a problem wanted geometry, its practitioners could invent the necessary geometry, in an abstract space of their own.

XI. Computing a Lunar Ephemeris — The Procedure, Step by Step

This section does what the hub's method requires of a repeatable process: it walks the computation through, in order, at the level of the scribe's actual operations. The walkthrough follows the structure of the lunar ephemerides as Neugebauer's ACT edition and Ossendrijver's procedure texts jointly establish it. Column letters are the modern editorial labels of the ACT corpus, retained because the literature uses them; the worked numbers in the illustration box are a deliberately stripped-down demonstration of the zigzag rule, labelled as such, not a transcription of any tablet.

First, the Notation

All computation is sexagesimal and positional: base sixty, with place value, exactly as decimal notation is base ten with place value. A modern semicolon marks the boundary between units and sixtieths, and commas separate the fractional places: 29;31,50,8,20 days is 29 days and a fraction whose successive terms divide by 60, 3,600, 216,000, and 12,960,000. Sixty is a calculator's base — divisible by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, and 30 — so the common fractions terminate, and multiplication and division run on standard tables (of reciprocals, squares, and multiples) that the scribal schools had maintained for a millennium. Positions are stated in UŠ — degrees — within a sign; times in UŠ of time (360 to the day); and the month is handled in tithis, thirtieths of a lunation, when the computation needs a unit finer than the day and independent of the calendar's 29- and 30-day months. The scribe computes on clay or a erasable board; the ephemeris tablet is the fair copy of finished work.

The Computation Itself, in Order

Step one — set the epoch. The scribe begins from a syzygy he trusts: a conjunction or opposition whose date and zodiacal longitude stand in the Diaries or in the preceding tablet of the series, together with the current values of the varying functions at that line. An ephemeris is a continuation, not a fresh start; its first row is inherited. Step two — advance the longitude. For the next syzygy, add one step of solar motion. In System A the addition is the constant of the arc the Sun currently occupies (30° or 28;7,30° per month, doubled appropriately for a full lunation's two syzygies as the tablet's scheme prescribes); if the sum crosses the arc boundary, the excess is converted at the other arc's rate by the boundary rule before being entered. In System B the addition is the current term of the solar zigzag, and the zigzag is then advanced one line. The result is entered as sign and degrees: the new syzygy's longitude. Step three — update the velocity functions. Advance the lunar velocity function by its rule: in System B, add or subtract its constant difference, reflecting at the maximum or minimum if the bound is crossed — the reflection rule being: overshoot is folded back by the same amount, and the direction of travel reverses. (The illustration below works this rule in miniature.) In System A the corresponding correction is carried in column Φ, whose values are generated from the longitude itself by the system's step scheme — the design Britton showed to be prior in the theory's construction. Step four — form the interval. Combine the mean syzygy interval with the corrections: the lunar-anomaly correction, read from the velocity function (a fast Moon reaches the next syzygy sooner), and the solar-anomaly correction, which enters as a separate additive or subtractive term whose sign reverses half-yearly. The sum is the predicted interval, in days and sexagesimal fractions, to the next syzygy. Step five — accumulate the date and time. Add the interval to the running total from the epoch, in tithis; convert to calendar days and months (allowing for the calendar's own intercalations); the fractional remainder, set against the daylight column, yields the time of the syzygy in UŠ from sunrise or sunset. Step six — compute the latitude. Advance the nodal function to the new syzygy and enter the Moon's latitude. If the syzygy falls within the eclipse limits of the node, mark the eclipse possibility, with magnitude estimated from the latitude's size — the mathematical counterpart of §VII's Saros grid, and in the late period the more precise of the two. Step seven — predict the month's opening. From the conjunction's computed time, work forward by the procedure texts' rules to the first evening's NA — the sunset-to-moonset interval of the new crescent. If NA meets the visibility threshold, the crescent will be seen that evening and the old month closes at 29 days; if not, the month runs to 30 and the crescent belongs to the next evening. The calendar's most basic fact — how long this month is — has been produced by arithmetic, in advance, without the sky being consulted. Step eight — copy forward. The completed row becomes the basis of the next: every function now stands at its new value, and steps two through seven repeat, row by row, for as many years as the tablet is meant to cover — ephemerides commonly run to a decade or more of syzygies, several hundred rows of interlocking arithmetic, each row checkable against the last and, in due course, against the watch.

// THE ZIGZAG RULE, WORKED IN MINIATURE (ILLUSTRATION, NOT A TABLET) //

Let a quantity vary between a minimum m = 10 and a maximum M = 22, by a constant difference d = 4 per line, starting at 14 and rising. The lines run: 14, 18, 22 — the maximum is reached, so the motion reflects: 18, 14, 10 — the minimum is reached, so it reflects again: 14, 18, 22… The period is 2( M − m )/ d = 6 lines. Had a step overshot a bound — from 20, adding 4 toward a maximum of 22 — the entry would be 22 − (24 − 22) = 20, and the direction would reverse from that line. Every zigzag column in System B, whatever its astronomical meaning, is this rule with real parameters: a difference, two bounds, a starting value, and the fold at the bounds. The whole of System B is that fold, applied with superbly chosen numbers.

Planetary ephemerides run the same logic in a sparser form: the rows are the planet's successive synodic phenomena (first appearances, stations, and so on, in their fixed sequence around the synodic cycle), and the columns advance the date and the zodiacal longitude of each phenomenon by increments that vary by step or zigzag rule according to where in the zodiac — System A — or in the count — System B — the phenomenon falls. The procedure texts give the rules phenomenon by phenomenon and planet by planet, with worked examples; Ossendrijver's edition runs to hundreds of such rules, and their cumulative effect is a complete predictive astronomy of the visible planets, accurate to the order of the observations themselves. That accuracy is the final vindication of the archive: the tables could only be as good as the seven hundred years of dated, standardised, honestly-kept records from which every parameter in them had been extracted.

XII. Transmission to Greece — Periods, Signs, and Data

Babylonian sky science is the only ancient science whose transmission to its successor culture can be audited line by line, because the receivers kept the numbers. What the Greek world took from Babylon can be listed with unusual precision, and the list is long: the zodiac of twelve equal signs and the 360-degree circle; sexagesimal computation, which Greek astronomy adopted entire and which survives in every degree, minute, and second of arc and time in use today; the period relations of the Moon and planets, including the Saros relations (223 synodic = 239 anomalistic = 242 draconic months) and the goal-year periods of §VI; the mean synodic month 29;31,50,8,20 days; the observational archive itself, eclipse records reaching back to the era of Nabonassar; and the practice of personal astrology founded on computed birth positions (§IX). What the Greeks added was equally definite, and is not Babylonian: geometrical models of circles and epicycles, the tropical redefinition of the zodiac, the ascendant and the house system of the developed horoscope, and the ideal of a physical demonstration of why the heavens move as they do. The Greco-Roman hub's Greek Astrology as a System treats the resulting synthesis in full and takes this article as its upstream source; the present section confines itself to the channels and the receipts.

The Receipts — Hipparchus and Ptolemy

The audit trail runs through two names. Hipparchus of Nicaea (fl. c. 150–125 BCE), whose own works are lost, is known through Ptolemy's reports of him — and Ptolemy reports, in Almagest IX.3, the period relations Hipparchus used for the planets: in 59 years Saturn completes 57 returns in anomaly; in 71 years Jupiter 65; in 79 years Mars 37; in 8 years Venus 5; in 46 years Mercury 145. These are, number for number, the Babylonian goal-year periods of §VI — the working periods of the goal-year texts, taken over as the foundation of Greek planetary mean motion, and refined by Hipparchus and Ptolemy over longer intervals exactly as the Babylonian mathematical texts had refined them (the 265-, 427-, 284-, 1151-, and 480-year periods of §VI's table). In Almagest IV.2 the same pattern holds for the Moon: Hipparchus's great period of 4,267 synodic months in 126,007 days and one hour divides down to the Babylonian System B relations — 251 synodic months to 269 anomalistic returns — and to the mean month 29;31,50,8,20, which Ptolemy transmits explicitly. And the data beneath the periods are Babylonian too: Ptolemy's lunar theory is calibrated on eclipse records that begin with the Babylonian eclipses of 721/720 BCE, dated by him in the Era of Nabonassar, and his star catalogue's and solar theory's Babylonian debts, traced by modern scholarship (Aaboe; Swerdlow) through parameters and observations alike, are of the same kind. Greek mathematical astronomy, in its quantitative skeleton, is Babylonian arithmetic wearing Greek geometry — Neugebauer's verdict, and a half-century of subsequent scholarship has refined its details without displacing it.

The Bridge Figure — Berossus

One transmitter has a name, a book, and a biography, and he stands in this hub's Tier B timeline in his other capacity already. Berossus — Bēl-reʾūšu, a priest of Bel (Marduk) at Babylon — wrote his Babyloniaca, a history of Babylon in Greek, for the Seleucid king Antiochus I around 290–278 BCE. The work survives only in fragments quoted by later authors (Alexander Polyhistor, Josephus, and through them Eusebius), and its astronomical content in the fragments is thin: its fame in this hub is for the antediluvian king list — ten kings, 432,000 years — read in Antediluvian King Lists. But the ancient tradition about Berossus the man is specifically astronomical: Vitruvius reports that he settled on the island of Kos and opened a school there, teaching Babylonian astronomy and astrology to Greek students; Pliny adds that the Athenians honoured him with a statue with a gilded tongue, for his predictions. Modern scholarship treats the Kos school as an ancient report to be weighed, not a documented institution — the fragments do not confirm it, and the technical transmission demonstrably ran through texts as much as through teachers — but Berossus's historical function is not in doubt: he is the first named Babylonian scholar to present his tradition's learning in Greek, to Greeks, on purpose, within a generation of Alexander's conquest of Babylon (331 BCE). Greek sources name other Babylonian astronomers in the same role — Kidinnu (Kidenas), Naburimannu, Sudines, cited by Strabo, Pliny, and Vettius Valens as authorities of the "Chaldaeans" — and while the attributions of particular discoveries to particular men (the mean month to Kidinnu is the famous case) cannot now be verified, the names themselves are evidence that the transmission had human faces its receivers remembered.

The textual channel, finally, is documented by the most unglamorous and most decisive evidence of all: Greek papyri from Egypt containing Babylonian lunar theory — System B syzygy tables, zigzag columns and all, transcribed into Greek letters and used by Greek-writing astrologers of the Roman period (Alexander Jones's editions of the Colker papyrus and its Cairo counterpart are the modern treatments). A technique does not cross cultures more completely than by being copied, column by column, into the successor's notebooks. By the time Ptolemy wrote, the Babylonian inheritance was not a foreign import within Greek astronomy. It was Greek astronomy's foundation — its data, its periods, its coordinates, and its arithmetic — and the centuries of the watch on the temple roofs of Babylon had become, by transmission, the permanent possession of every astronomy that followed.

XIII. What Babylonian Sky Science Does and Does Not Show

What it shows, at full strength, is the invention of predictive astronomy — and the demonstration, unique in the ancient world, of how much of science is infrastructure. A profession with a title and a duty roster; a standardised record, kept nightly for some seven hundred years, honest about its own gaps; periods extracted from the record by retrieval before they were ever modelled by arithmetic; a coordinate system — the zodiac — invented to make positions computable; two complete mathematical systems that predict the Moon's and planets' phenomena to the accuracy of the observations themselves, without geometry, without a physical model, without a single claim about what the heavens are made of; and a transmission so faithful that the successor culture's greatest astronomer built on Babylonian numbers he could name the source of. The data have outlived every empire that housed them: the Diaries' eclipse records, collated by F. Richard Stephenson and others, remain a primary source for measuring the secular slowing of the Earth's rotation — Babylonian watchers are, in the most literal sense, still on duty in modern geophysics. No part of that paragraph requires the omens to be true, the revelation of §II to be credited, or the chronology of §IV to be settled. It is what the tablets, as tablets, establish.

What it does not show must be entered in the same hand. It does not show a physical theory: no Babylonian text proposes a mechanism, a cosmos of spheres, or a cause for the motions its tables predict so well — the absence is total, and Rochberg's work warns against reading it as a failure rather than as a different answer to the question of what knowledge of the sky is for. It does not show instrumental astronomy beyond the water clock, the gnomon, and the trained eye: every position in the archive is an estimate against reference stars, and the system's precision is the precision of averaging over centuries, not of sights and scales. It does not show that the omens were efficacious, or that the horoscopes' verdicts were sound: this article has treated divination as the programme's historical engine and social purpose, and takes no position — as an archive of Tier A — on the truth of its apodoses. It does not show a single moment of invention: the zodiac, the Saros scheme, the two Systems each condensed out of generations of practice, and the named discoverers of Greek testimony (Kidinnu and his colleagues) stand for schools and successions we cannot now reconstruct. And it does not settle the second millennium: the Venus Tablet that opens the observational story still floats, with the whole chronology of Hammurabi's age, across a range of a century and a half that the current evidence does not close (§IV).

The deepest instruction of the material may be the one the watchers would have found strangest. They kept the record to protect a king from the anger of gods whose writing they believed the sky to be; the record outlasted the king, the gods' temples, the language it was written in, and the empire that finally inherited it — and what it turned out to be for, in the long run, was everything its keepers had not intended: the periods, the zodiac, the computable sky, and the proof, still legible in clay, that prediction is possible wherever observation is honest, standardised, and kept. The first astronomy was a watch kept for the wrong reasons, perfectly. This archive knows no better definition of how sciences begin.
