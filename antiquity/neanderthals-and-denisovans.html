This is a Tier A article: everything in it rests on excavated, dated, sequenced, and published evidence, and where scholars disagree — as they do, sharply, about cave art, about flowers in a grave, and about what counts as a burial at all — the disagreement itself is reported with its positions attributed. No claim here depends on ancient legend or modern construction, and none is allowed to borrow strength from either. In this field that discipline matters more than usual, because the central question is not only what Neanderthals and Denisovans did. It is what standard of proof we require before we say they did it.

For most of the twentieth century the answer, in practice, was asymmetric. A perforated shell in a modern human context was an ornament and evidence of symbolism; a perforated shell in a Neanderthal context was, first, a problem to be explained away — by intrusion, by misdating, by a carnivore, by a rodent. That asymmetry is now itself an object of study (§X), and the last two decades of work have made it harder to sustain: stalagmite constructions built 336 metres inside a French cave at about 176,500 years ago; eagle talons cut, polished, and apparently strung in Croatia at about 130,000 years ago; perforated and pigmented shells in Spain at about 115,000 years ago; and, from Siberia, an entire population — the Denisovans — recognised from DNA in 2010 before any diagnostic skeleton for it existed. None of those findings, on its own, settles what these humans thought. Together they settle something narrower and firmer: the old picture of two populations without ornament, without construction, and without care for their dead cannot be held in its simple form any longer.

This article is the full treatment promised by the single Neanderthal paragraph in this hub's Ochre and Symbol article. It proceeds from the strongest evidence to the most contested, in that order on purpose: the dated construction first, the dated ornaments next, the cave-art claim and its published audit after that, and the burials — where the evidence is oldest, most famous, and most argued over — with the criteria debate stated in full. The Denisovan half of the story is shorter, because the Denisovan record is shorter: a handful of bones and teeth, one mandible from a high cave on the Tibetan Plateau, one first-generation hybrid, and a genetic legacy out of all proportion to the fossils. That disproportion is itself one of the findings. A population can be almost invisible in stone and unmistakable in genomes.

I. The Cast — Two Populations, Two Kinds of Evidence

The Neanderthals are the archaeologically visible population of the two. Named for the Neander Valley in Germany, where the type specimen was recognised in 1856, they are known from hundreds of individuals and thousands of sites across Europe and western Asia — from Iberia and Wales in the west to the Altai Mountains of Siberia in the east, and south into the Levant, where at caves such as Kebara and Amud they lived, at times, within the same landscape as early modern humans. Their classic range of dates runs, in round terms, from about 400,000 years ago for their antecedents to about 40,000 years ago for their last well-dated populations, though both ends of that range are argued over site by site. Their material signature for most of that span is the Mousterian: prepared-core stone technology, hearths, hunted large game, and — the subject of this article — a sparse, scattered, and fiercely debated trail of pigment, ornament, construction, and burial.

The Denisovans are the opposite case: a population known first, and still primarily, from molecules. In 2008 Russian excavators working in Denisova Cave, in the Altai Mountains of southern Siberia, recovered a small distal finger bone — the tip of a juvenile's fifth finger — from a layer dated broadly to 50,000–30,000 years ago. The bone looked like nothing in particular. Its mitochondrial DNA, published by Johannes Krause and colleagues in 2010, looked like nothing known: it diverged from the lineage leading to both modern human and Neanderthal mitochondrial genomes roughly twice as deeply as those two diverge from each other. The nuclear genome, published the same year by David Reich and colleagues in Nature, resolved the relationship differently and more precisely: the finger-bone individual belonged to a group that shared a common origin with Neanderthals — a sister group, not a distant outlier — which had not taken part in the gene flow from Neanderthals into Eurasians, but which had contributed some 4–6 per cent of its genetic material to the genomes of present-day Melanesians. Reich and colleagues gave the group its name in that paper: Denisovans, after the cave, exactly as Neanderthals were named after their valley.

Neanderthals: a dense archaeological record, genomes later. Denisovans: genomes first, a sparse fossil record still being assembled around them.

The sequence of discovery deserves emphasis, because it reversed the normal order of palaeoanthropology. Every other named human population began as a skeleton that was measured, compared, and classified; its genome, if one was ever recovered, came a century later. The Denisovans began as a genome in search of a skeleton. A large molar from the same cave carried a closely similar mitochondrial genome and shared no derived features with either Neanderthals or modern humans, but teeth and a finger tip do not make an anatomy. For nearly a decade after 2010, researchers could say what Denisovans were related to, roughly where their genes survived (§IX), and almost nothing about what they looked like. The Xiahe mandible of §VIII — identified as Denisovan-related by ancient protein, not DNA, in 2019 — was the first substantial piece of Denisovan anatomy found outside Denisova Cave itself, and it came from 3,280 metres up on the Tibetan Plateau. The cast, in short, is still being assembled, and this article marks, at each step, whether a Denisovan claim rests on a bone, a protein, a genome, or an inference from living people's DNA. Those are four different kinds of evidence, and they do not carry the same weight.

Species, Subspecies, Populations — A Note on Names

This article calls Neanderthals and Denisovans populations and groups, and the choice is deliberate. Whether Neanderthals are a separate species, Homo neanderthalensis, or a subspecies of our own, Homo sapiens neanderthalensis, remains unsettled in the literature, and the Denisovans have never received a formal Linnaean name at all — a genome is not a type specimen in the traditional sense. Chris Stringer and Julia Galway-Witham, reviewing the species questions for the Natural History Museum, London, have set out the difficulty plainly in their recent syntheses: the fossil record of the Middle Pleistocene does not sort itself into clean species boxes, interbreeding between the groups is now a documented fact (§§VIII–IX), and any taxonomy has to accommodate populations that were distinct for hundreds of thousands of years and yet not reproductively isolated. Readers should therefore take every species label in the sources cited here as a working label, not a verdict. What is not in dispute is the underlying pattern: by the later Middle Pleistocene, Eurasia held several distinguishable human populations, of which ours was one, and they met, and at least sometimes they had children together.

One further caution governs everything that follows. To show that a population made ornaments is not to show what the ornaments meant; to show that a body was deliberately buried is not to show that it was buried with ritual, belief, or grief as we would recognise them. The sources in this article are at their best when they keep those steps apart, and at their weakest — on both sides of every debate — when they run them together. This article follows the sources at their best. Where a claim of symbolism is made, it says on what object, at what date, by what method, and who has answered it.

II. Bruniquel — Building in the Dark, c. 176,500 BP

Bruniquel Cave, in southwestern France near Montauban, was sealed by a natural collapse during the Pleistocene and re-entered only in 1990, when cavers dug through the blocked entrance. Deep inside it — 336 metres from the entrance, well beyond any daylight — they found something no one had a category for: structures built from broken stalagmites. The archaeologist François Rouzaud recognised their importance, but his early death and the cave's restricted access stalled study for two decades. In 2013 Jacques Jaubert and colleagues returned to date and record the structures, and published the results in Nature in 2016 (Jaubert et al., Nature 534: 111–114).

The structures are six in number, all in the cave's largest chamber, and all made exclusively of stalagmites and stalagmite fragments — pieces the paper calls speleofacts. Two are annular, ring-shaped. The larger is an ellipse of about 6.7 by 4.5 metres; the smaller, about 2.2 by 2.1 metres. Each ring is built of one to four aligned layers of stalagmite pieces, with smaller fragments wedged in as supports. The other four structures are accumulations — stacks and piles — two of them inside the large ring. In total the builders used more than 400 pieces. The pieces are strikingly standardised: an average length of about 34.4 cm in the large ring and about 29.5 cm in the small one. Stalagmites were broken at a consistent size, carried to the chamber, and laid in courses. Several traces of fire accompany the structures, including reddened and cracked stalagmite and a fragment of burnt bone, and heated zones on and near the rings.

The date is the finding's foundation, and the paper builds it carefully. Uranium-series dating was applied not to one sample but to a system: to the tips of stalagmites built into the structures, which must predate their breaking; to the calcite regrowth that formed over the broken pieces after they were laid, which must postdate construction; and to the burnt bone. The results converge on a replicated age of 176.5 thousand years, plus or minus 2.1 thousand years. At that date, in that place, the attribution is not really in question: early Neanderthals were the only humans in western Europe. No modern human presence anywhere near the region is claimed for another 130,000 years. If the structures are human-made — and their geometry, standardisation, layering, and fire traces are the paper's argument that they are — then Neanderthals made them. Chris Stringer, commenting at publication, accepted the attribution on those grounds.

What Building Them Required — Inference, Labelled as Such

The paper itself is restrained about purpose, and this article follows it: what the rings were for is unknown. No occupation debris, no tool assemblage, and no further trace of activity in the chamber has been found to explain them. What can be stated is not purpose but requirement — the minimum capacities the construction itself demonstrates, as inference from the structure. The builders worked 336 metres from daylight, so they carried a reliable, sustained source of light — the fire traces are consistent with torches or hearths maintained in the chamber, not a single brief visit. They selected, broke, and standardised hundreds of heavy calcite pieces, which is work for several people over an extended period, not an individual's afternoon. They laid the pieces in a shared plan — two rings and four stacks in one chamber — which requires that the plan existed before and during the building, was communicated, and was followed. Jaubert and colleagues summarise the implication in general terms: the constructions show that humans of this period had mastered the underground environment, a step they describe as major. Beyond that summary, every richer reading — shelter, storage, ritual enclosure, social space — is speculation, and is labelled as such wherever it appears in the secondary literature.

One alternative was aired at publication and should be recorded, because this hub records alternatives: could cave bears, which hibernate in such chambers and shift material as they dig their sleeping hollows, have made the structures by accident? The paper's answer, which has carried the field, is that bear hollows are smaller than the large ring, that bears push material aside rather than stacking it in aligned courses of standardised pieces, and that bears do not account for the fire traces or the burnt bone. The bear suggestion survives in popular retellings more than in the specialist literature. The standing position, stated at its proper strength, is this: the Bruniquel structures are the oldest well-dated constructions attributed to humans anywhere, they are Neanderthal work, and their function is unknown. That combination — certainty about the building, honesty about the purpose — is the model this article asks the reader to hold for every claim that follows.

// WHY 176,500 YEARS MATTERS //

The date falls in Marine Isotope Stage 6, a glacial period — yet calcite was actively depositing on the rings at the time, indicating a locally warm, humid interval within the cold stage. More importantly, the date is roughly three times older than any Neanderthal evidence in §§III–VI. Whatever Neanderthals were doing with shells and pigment at 130,000–115,000 years ago, their ancestors had already organised collective construction in total darkness some fifty millennia earlier. Bruniquel does not prove symbolism. It removes the premise that organised, planned, cooperative work was beyond them.

Bruniquel also reframes the geography of the Neanderthal world. Cave use by early humans is usually a story of entrances and rock shelters — the lit, sheltered margin where daylight, hearths, and debris accumulate. The deep cave, beyond the twilight zone, was long treated as a modern human territory, entered for art and ritual in the Upper Palaeolithic. Bruniquel puts Neanderthals in the deep cave, building, at a date earlier than any dated modern human cave art anywhere. It does not follow that they entered for the same reasons. It does follow that darkness itself was not a barrier they failed to cross.

III. Pigment and Ornament — Shells, Talons, and Colour

If Bruniquel is a single extraordinary site, pigment and ornament are a pattern — thinner, wider, and individually weaker, but repeated across the Neanderthal range and across tens of thousands of years. The pattern has three strands: mineral pigments collected, ground, and sometimes mixed; shells perforated and stained; and raptor talons and feathers selected and modified. Each strand has a functional reading as well as a symbolic one, and this section gives both. What it will not do is what the weaker literature does on both sides: call every lump of ochre a crayon of ritual, or every perforated shell a accident of the sea.

Cueva de los Aviones — Shells and Pigment Mixtures, c. 115,000 BP

Cueva de los Aviones is a sea cave near Cartagena, in southeastern Spain. Excavated in the 1980s and re-studied by João Zilhão and colleagues, its Neanderthal-associated Middle Palaeolithic deposit yielded a small, dense assemblage unlike ordinary food debris: marine shells of cockle and mussel-like bivalves, several of them perforated; shells stained with red pigment; and, most tellingly, shells used as containers, holding residues of prepared pigment mixtures. One Spondylus shell held a compound of red lepidocrocite with ground inclusions of haematite and pyrite. Another context produced a lump of natrojarosite, a yellow mineral pigment, and red and yellow colourants more generally. The perforations and the haematite residue on the inner face of one shell were read by the excavators as the traces of suspension and wear: shells hung, probably as ornaments, and pigment mixed and kept in shells, as paint or cosmetic is mixed and kept in a pot.

The assemblage was first published in 2010 with a radiocarbon age of roughly 45,000–50,000 years on associated food shells — already early, already before any secure modern human presence in the region, but close enough to the contact period to invite the objection that Neanderthals might have learned the practice from incoming modern humans. In 2018 Dirk Hoffmann and colleagues closed that door with a different clock. Uranium-series dating of the flowstone that caps the Aviones deposit — carbonate laid down over the archaeological layer, so that everything beneath it must be older — gave an age of about 115,000 to 120,000 years for the capping flowstone, and therefore a minimum age in that range for the shells and pigments beneath it (Hoffmann et al., Science Advances 4 (2018): eaar5255). At 115,000 years ago there were no modern humans in Europe to learn from. The authors' conclusion, stated at its exact strength, is that the Aviones finds predate the earliest comparable evidence associated with modern humans — the shell beads and pigment work of the African Middle Stone Age — by some 20,000 to 40,000 years, and that the roots of symbolic material culture may therefore lie in the common ancestor of Neanderthals and modern humans, more than half a million years ago. The second half of that sentence is an inference, and a large one. The first half is a date, a context, and an assemblage, and it has not been overturned.

Krapina — Eight Eagle Talons, c. 130,000 BP

The Krapina rock shelter in northwestern Croatia was excavated between 1899 and 1905 under Dragutin Gorjanović-Kramberger, and produced one of the largest Neanderthal fossil collections in Europe. More than a century later, Davorka Radovčić and colleagues re-examined the site's faunal collection and identified, from a single level dated by ESR and uranium-series to about 130,000 years ago — the last interglacial, MIS 5e — eight white-tailed eagle talons, mostly complete, together with an associated foot bone (Radovčić et al., PLoS ONE 10 (2015): e0119802). Eight talons from one level is more than the entire rest of the European Mousterian has produced. Duplication of one talon position shows the eight come from at least three different eagles: they were accumulated, not taken from a single carcass.

The modifications are the argument. Four talons bear multiple cut marks, their edges smoothed — the signature of cuts made in life-fresh tissue and then worn. All eight show polishing facets or abrasion. Three of the largest bear small notches at roughly the same position on the underside, interrupting the margin of the talon in a way the authors read as the wear of a cord. Taken together — cutting to remove the talons, smoothing, polishing where adjacent hard objects rub, notching where a binding would sit — the pattern matches what happens to talons mounted together in a strung assemblage, a necklace or bracelet, and matches nothing in ordinary butchery, where a raptor foot has no meat worth this attention. A later surface study of one talon (Radovčić et al., Scientific Reports 2020) added a fibre preserved within a wide cut mark, identified as degraded collagen consistent with a leather or sinew binding, and spots of two ochres and charcoal on the same surface. That study is supportive, not decisive — a single fibre on a museum specimen handled for a century must be read with care — and this article rests the Krapina case where its authors rested it in 2015: on the number, the selection, and the modifications of the talons themselves. White-tailed eagles are large, uncommon, and dangerous birds. Acquiring the talons of three of them, and keeping eight together, was a project.

The Pigment Record Generally — and Its Functional Shadow

Beyond Aviones, ochre and other mineral pigments — red haematite, yellow goethite and natrojarosite, black manganese oxides — occur at Neanderthal sites across Europe and the Levant, in some cases from well before 100,000 years ago, occasionally ground, faceted from use, or scored. Shell beads and perforated shells are reported from a small number of other Middle Palaeolithic contexts; cut-marked raptor wing bones and talons, suggesting the deliberate removal of feathers and claws, recur at several French and Italian sites in the later Mousterian. The full inventory is maintained in the specialist literature and in this hub's Ochre and Symbol article, which treats the pigment record from its earliest African occurrences onward.

The functional shadow must be stated, because the critics state it well. Ochre has uses that are not symbolic: it is an ingredient in hafting mastics, a hide-processing agent, an insect repellent, and a sunscreen, and each of those uses is experimentally supported. A lump of ochre, alone, therefore proves collection and use, not symbolism. The symbolic reading gains strength only where the context excludes or outruns the functional ones: pigment mixed to a recipe and stored in a shell container, as at Aviones; pigment applied to an object with no practical surface to protect, as on the Krapina talon; a perforation placed and worn in the way ornaments are worn. That is the standard this article applies throughout, and it is the standard the strongest papers on both sides now claim to apply as well. Where the two readings remain genuinely open — as they do for much of the plain ochre record — §XI will say so, and say that pigment use is attested while pigment symbolism, at those sites, is not proven.

IV. The Cave-Art Claim and Its Audit — c. 65,000 BP

On 22 February 2018, Science published the most consequential — and most contested — claim in this article. Dirk Hoffmann and colleagues reported uranium-thorium dates on carbonate crusts overlying pigment in three Spanish caves (Hoffmann et al., Science 359 (2018): 912–915). At La Pasiega, in Cantabria, a red ladder-like sign — horizontal and vertical bars, a scalariform — lay beneath carbonate dated to more than 64,800 years ago. At Maltravieso, in Extremadura, a hand stencil lay beneath carbonate of comparable antiquity. At Ardales, in Andalusia, red pigment on stalagmite formations was bracketed by carbonate dates reaching back beyond 65,000 years. The logic is the logic of Aviones, applied to walls: carbonate that formed over pigment must be younger than the pigment, so its date is a minimum age for the painting beneath it. A minimum age of 65,000 years, for art in Iberia, precedes the earliest secure modern human presence in the region by roughly twenty millennia. The paper's conclusion followed directly: the paintings were made by Neanderthals, and Iberian cave art — the first parietal art in Europe, on these dates — is a Neanderthal creation.

This section gives the claim, then its audit, at full strength on both sides, and ends where the literature ends: unresolved. Readers who meet this claim elsewhere stated as settled fact, in either direction, are meeting advocacy, not the published record.

The Case for the Dates

The Hoffmann team's case rests on method as much as on numbers. Uranium-thorium dating of cave carbonate is an established technique with a range reaching back some 500,000 years; the team analysed more than sixty samples, each of a few milligrams, taken with surgical care from crusts directly overlying the pigment. Crucially, they sampled sequentially through the crusts: a series of subsamples from the outer surface inward, toward the pigment, should grow older in stratigraphic order if the carbonate has behaved as a closed system since it formed. The team published multiple such sequences, from all three caves, in the expected order, and presented that ordering as the test for the disturbance their critics fear. They also dated crusts at the same sites that returned very young ages — around a thousand years — which they argue is incompatible with the idea that the cave drip waters carry enough inherited thorium to fake great antiquity: water loaded with old thorium could not produce a thousand-year date. Finally, the team points to pattern: the oldest minimum ages come from three caves, hundreds of kilometres apart, for three different motifs. A single aberrant sample can be an accident. A regional pattern of minimum ages beyond 64,000 years, they argue, is a phenomenon that requires an explanation, and Neanderthal authorship is the explanation that fits the chronology of human presence in Iberia.

The Audit — Slimak, White, and the Open System

The responses came in the same journals, within the year, and they attack the method at its foundation. Ludovic Slimak and colleagues, in a Technical Comment in Science (2018), raised three objections. First, open-system behaviour: cave carbonates are not sealed clocks — uranium can be leached by percolating water, and loss of uranium makes a sample calculate older than it is; the critics argue that ordered subsample sequences do not exclude every form of that disturbance. Second, the corrections applied for detrital thorium — thorium that was in the carbonate from the start, not produced by decay within it — depend on assumed initial values, and different defensible assumptions move the oldest dates substantially. Third, an archaeological objection: if Neanderthals were painting caves at 65,000 years ago, the near-absence of comparable dated art for the following twenty-odd millennia, until the modern human Upper Palaeolithic, is a strange silence that the claim does not explain. Randall White and colleagues then extended the critique in a full-length paper whose title states its verdict — "Still no archaeological evidence that Neanderthals created Iberian cave art" (Journal of Human Evolution 144 (2020): 102640). White's team argued that uranium leaching in thin calcite crusts can account for the anomalous ages; that the association between some dated crusts and the pigment beneath them is less direct than the claim requires — at Ardales, in particular, whether the red colouration of the stalagmites is human-applied pigment at all, rather than natural iron staining, has been questioned; and that a claim overturning a century of archaeological context should be carried by archaeological evidence — tools, occupation, a Neanderthal presence demonstrated at those caves at those dates — and not by physicochemical measurements alone. Maxime Aubert, and David Pearce and Adelphine Bonneau, published parallel methodological cautions in the same period.

The Hoffmann team answered in kind, in a formal Response in Science (2018). On the supposed hiatus: a minimum age cannot create a gap — the same caves carry younger minimum ages and bracketing date pairs that place painting episodes squarely inside the alleged silent interval, so the silence is an artefact of reading minimum ages as exact ages. On open-system behaviour: the sequential sequences stand, and the critics, they argue, propose no mechanism that disturbs the system while preserving perfect stratigraphic order across multiple sequences in three caves. On the Ardales pigment: its anthropogenic character, they reply, is supported by more than a century of study of the cave, including technical features of its application. On the isochron re-analysis offered by the critics for one key sample: three points cannot define an isochron, and the exercise assumes, without evidence, that the crust formed in a single short episode. The exchange, at the time of writing, rests there: claim, comment, response, and a long-form critique unanswered in equally long form.

// THE VERDICT THIS ARTICLE PRINTS //

Unresolved. The U-Th minimum ages are real measurements, published with their methods, and no critic has shown a specific sample to be wrong by a specific amount. The critics' central point is also real: uranium leaching biases this clock in one direction only — toward ages that are too old — and the burden on a claim of this size is to exclude that bias sample by sample, not in general. Neanderthal authorship of the La Pasiega, Maltravieso, and Ardales marks is therefore a live, well-supported, and genuinely contested hypothesis. It is not an attested fact, and it does not appear in this article's assessment (§XI) as one. Note what does not depend on it: Aviones and Krapina (§III) rest on different materials, different contexts, and — in the Aviones case — a capping flowstone, not a thin crust over pigment.

There is a wider lesson in the exchange, and the hub's readers will recognise it from the zebu argument at Mehrgarh and the cattle argument at Nabta Playa: a dating method is not a verdict. It is an instrument with known failure modes, and its readings become history only where the failure modes have been tested in the specific material at hand. Hoffmann's team tested them one way; Slimak's and White's teams argue the tests are insufficient; both positions are in the peer-reviewed record, attributed, and open. This article's tier discipline requires exactly that presentation — and it requires the same discipline in reverse: the critics have not disproven the dates either. "Not yet proven" and "proven false" are different findings, and only the first is available here.

V. Burials I — Shanidar and the Flowers

Shanidar Cave, in the Zagros Mountains of Iraqi Kurdistan, at about 750 metres in the foothills of the Baradost range, is where the Neanderthal burial debate acquired its most famous exhibit and its most famous cautionary tale. Ralph Solecki, of Columbia University, excavated the cave from 1951 to 1960 and recovered the remains of ten Neanderthals — seven adults and three infants, in the usual count — from its Mousterian levels. Solecki argued that some of the dead had been killed by rockfalls from the cave roof, and that others had been deliberately buried. One burial carried the argument into the culture at large. Shanidar IV, an adult male, was found with soil samples around the body that contained, on analysis by the palynologist Arlette Leroi-Gourhan, strikingly concentrated pollen — in a cave whose sediments were otherwise pollen-poor. The pollen occurred in clumps, which Leroi-Gourhan read as the signature of whole flowers deposited at once, and the species identified included yarrow, groundsel, grape hyacinth, St Barnaby's thistle, and hollyhock-type pollen, among twenty-eight taxa in total. Solecki's conclusion, published in his 1971 book Shanidar: The First Flower People and in the journal literature in 1975, was that the dead man had been laid on, or covered with, flowering plants gathered in their late-spring blooming season — a flower burial, and with it, evidence of ritual, care, and something like mourning among Neanderthals.

The Taphonomic Critique — Jirds, Bees, and Wind

The flower burial was doubted early and dismantled in stages. Robert Gargett, in a wide-ranging critique of Middle Palaeolithic burial claims (1989; extended 1999), argued that natural agencies — wind, animals, sediment movement — had not been excluded at Shanidar or anywhere else, and that wishful reading had done the rest. Jeffrey Sommer then supplied the specific mechanism the critique had lacked (Cambridge Archaeological Journal 9 (1999): 127–129). The Persian jird, Meriones persicus, is a burrowing rodent native to the Shanidar region, living in colonies, digging burrow systems metres deep in soft cave-front soils — and storing large quantities of seeds and flower heads in its burrows. Excavated jird burrows contained many of the same flower species as the Shanidar IV samples. A jird colony working the sediments around a skeleton after its burial could introduce concentrated, clumped flower pollen exactly where Solecki found it, without any Neanderthal ever gathering a flower. Later work added further natural vectors: pollen surveys of the cave in 2015 and after showed pollen moving through its sediments by wind and by bees, and the cave's deposits are now known to be riddled with burrows. The standing position in the literature — and the one this article prints — is that the flower burial, as evidence of ritual, does not stand: the pollen is real, its deposition by mourners is not demonstrated, and a sufficient natural explanation exists. It remains in this article, at length, because its career is the single best lesson in this field about the distance between a beautiful finding and a proven one — and because Shanidar itself, remarkably, was not finished.

Shanidar Z — The Burial Beneath the Argument (2020)

Excavation resumed at Shanidar in 2015, under Graeme Barker's team, with the limited original aim of locating and dating Solecki's surviving sections. In 2018 the team uncovered, close to the flower-burial location and in sediments Solecki's excavation had left in place, the articulated upper body of an adult Neanderthal — crushed skull, upper thorax, and both hands, in anatomical connection — published by Emma Pomeroy and colleagues in Antiquity (94 (2020): 11–26) as Shanidar Z. The individual, of undetermined sex, perhaps in their forties or fifties, dates to about 70,000–60,000 years ago. The claim for deliberate burial this time was built not on pollen but on sediment: the body lay at the base of a shallow, scooped depression; the sediment immediately beneath it showed disturbance consistent with digging; the fill around the body differed from the natural deposits below; and the bones' articulation — fragile connections of hand and ribcage still in place — argued that the body was covered promptly, before scavengers or sediment movement could disperse it. Two stacked stones near the head were noted as a possible marker, at the strength of a possibility only. Pomeroy and colleagues further argued that Shanidar Z belongs with three previously excavated individuals from the same small area of the cave, and that the cluster represents repeated, deliberate deposition of the dead in one place — a burial area, used more than once.

Two features of the Shanidar Z paper deserve the reader's attention. The first is its tone: the authors state that the sediments around Shanidar Z contain ancient pollen and mineralised plant remains, that the earlier flower-burial reading now seems to them more plausible than the critiques had left it — and that the analyses which could test that are still underway, with rodent introduction still to be excluded. A lead author describing herself as a former sceptic coming round, while withholding the conclusion until the tests are done, is the field's standard of proof working as it should. The second is what Shanidar Z does and does not rescue. It strengthens, considerably, the case that the Shanidar Neanderthals deliberately buried at least some of their dead. It does not retrospectively prove that Shanidar IV was buried with flowers: each deposit must carry its own evidence, and the jird critique of the 1975 pollen samples stands unanswered for those samples. Both halves of that sentence are the finding.

// CARE IN LIFE, AT SHANIDAR //

Shanidar I, an older adult male from Solecki's excavations, survived for years with severe injuries — including a withered arm, apparently amputated or lost in life, and healed fractures — that would have limited his ability to provide for himself. His survival is widely read as evidence that his group provisioned and protected an injured member over a long period. The reading is an inference, and alternatives (that he retained useful skills and status) are discussed in the literature; but the fact beneath it — long survival with disabling, healed trauma — is not in dispute.

VI. Burials II — La Chapelle, Kebara, and the Criteria

Shanidar is the famous case. The strong cases are in France and the Levant, and they were built, a century after their discovery, by going back into the ground.

La Chapelle-aux-Saints — The Old Man, Re-excavated

In 1908, in the small cave called the bouffia Bonneval at La Chapelle-aux-Saints in southwestern France, excavators recovered a nearly complete Neanderthal skeleton — an older adult male, soon famous as the Old Man of La Chapelle — lying in a depression they described as a dug grave. The claim of deliberate burial was made at once, and doubted for just as long: the excavation standards of 1908 could not exclude a natural hollow, and the skeleton itself, reconstructed in a stooped posture by Marcellin Boule, did more to fix the brutish image of Neanderthals than any other single specimen. Between 1999 and 2012, William Rendu, Bruno Maureille, and colleagues re-excavated the bouffia Bonneval in a twelve-year field project designed for one purpose: to test the burial claim with modern geoarchaeology and taphonomy (Rendu et al., PNAS 111 (2014): 81–86). Their results established, first, that the depression is anthropogenic — it cuts into and modifies an earlier cryoturbated deposit, removing accumulated debris in a way natural processes at the site do not explain — and second, that the body was buried rapidly: the Neanderthal bones are exceptionally preserved, with few cracks and no sign of the weathering, smoothing, or animal disturbance that mark the reindeer and bison bones from the same cave, which lay exposed far longer. Two different taphonomic histories in one small cave: the animals lay open; the man was covered at once. The project's conclusion is that the La Chapelle Neanderthal was placed in a pit prepared or modified by his group and protected by rapid covering — an intentional burial. The same re-excavation recovered further Neanderthal remains — elements of the original individual, two children, and a second adult — complicating the cave's history without weakening the central result.

The audit, again, is part of the record. Harold Dibble and colleagues, applying a published criteria list to the Rendu evidence in the Journal of Archaeological Science (2015), concluded that the La Chapelle evidence still fails several criteria for unequivocal intentional interment and that the case remains, in their assessment, ambiguous. Readers should weigh the asymmetry of the two papers fairly: Rendu's team produced new primary evidence — a demonstrated anthropogenic pit and a demonstrated preservation contrast — while the critique assesses that evidence against a checklist and finds it short of a standard the checklist's authors set. That is a legitimate and continuing argument about where the bar sits. It is not a counter-finding. The standing position this article prints is that La Chapelle is, on the new primary evidence, a deliberate burial — and that the criteria debate described below is unresolved as a general matter, which is precisely why single sites must be argued from their sediments and not from slogans.

Kebara — The Grave That Cut Through Hearths

Kebara Cave, on Mount Carmel in the Levant, was re-excavated between 1982 and 1990 by an international team including Ofer Bar-Yosef and Bernard Vandermeersch. In 1983, in a Mousterian layer dated to about 60,000 years ago, the team found Kebara 2 — the most complete postcranial Neanderthal skeleton known, an adult male lacking only, conspicuously, the cranium. The burial evidence is stratigraphic and, in Paul Pettitt's assessment in his review of Neanderthal mortuary practice, beyond doubt: the grave's cut was directly observable, slicing obliquely through two hearths in the level below; its limits were visible on two sides; its fill — a yellow-brown sediment — stood out against the black unit it was dug into; and the skeleton lay on its back in full anatomical connection, down to the hyoid bone, within the body's original volume, in a position indicating it was laid in the grave rapidly, before rigor mortis passed. The body, the excavators concluded, decomposed inside a filled grave. The missing cranium generated its own literature: the position of the atlas bone between the branches of the jaw suggests the head was present at burial and was removed later, after the connecting ligaments had decayed — a secondary treatment of the dead that, if the reading is right, extends the Kebara mortuary behaviour beyond simple disposal. That reading is an interpretation, and is labelled as such in the sources. The grave itself is not: Kebara 2 is the benchmark against which other claimed burials are measured, and notably, its pit contained no convincing grave goods — the tools and bones in its fill read, in the excavators' own judgement, as ordinary material from the artefact-rich layers the grave was dug through. The strongest burial in the record is also one of the plainest. That plainness is evidence too, about what burial did and did not necessarily involve.

The Criteria Debate — What Counts as a Burial?

Beneath every site argument sits a definitional one, and this article states it openly because the literature does. At least four distinct claims are habitually confused under the word "burial." The first is deposition: a body came to rest where it is found. The second is deliberate deposition: the body was placed there by others, not by rockfall, flood, or carnivores. The third is interment: a grave was dug or modified and the body covered promptly. The fourth is funerary ritual: the interment was accompanied by goods, offerings, or ceremony expressing belief. Each step requires its own evidence, and evidence for a lower step is not evidence for a higher one. Gargett's critiques (1989, 1999) worked by holding every claimed burial to the highest steps at once and finding the evidence short; Sandgathe and colleagues formalised criteria — a demonstrable grave cut, articulation inconsistent with natural accumulation, preservation inconsistent with exposure, exclusion of natural hollows and carnivore action — and applied them stringently across the classic French sites, Roc de Marsal among them, concluding that several famous cases fail. Pettitt, Rendu, Maureille, and others answer that the criteria, applied as a checklist, demand of Pleistocene sediments a perfection that ethnographic burial itself would often fail, and that the proper test is the balance of site-specific evidence: cut, fill, articulation, preservation, and context together. The debate is unresolved as a general standard. Its practical outcome, however, is visible in the newest work: Shanidar Z was published, from the start, in the language of the criteria — cut, fill, articulation, disturbance — because the critics had taught the field what a burial claim must show. Scepticism, here, did not stop the science. It specified it.

// THE BURIAL LEDGER, AT ITS EXACT STRENGTH //

Deliberate interment, strongly supported: Kebara 2 (observed grave cut, contrasting fill, complete articulation); La Chapelle-aux-Saints 1 (anthropogenic pit, rapid covering shown by preservation contrast). Deliberate interment, supported by new primary evidence: Shanidar Z and its cluster (scooped depression, disturbed substrate, articulated upper body). Ritual accompaniment: not demonstrated at any of these sites. The Shanidar IV flowers fail the taphonomic test; Kebara's fill contains no convincing grave goods; La Chapelle's pit is plain. Burial, in this record, is a fact about the treatment of the body. Ceremony remains, for now, beyond the evidence.

VII. Denisova Cave — Ornaments, a Needle, and a Crowded Cave

Denisova Cave, in the Anui valley of the Altai, is a large karst cave with a long, stratified occupation sequence spanning the Middle and Upper Palaeolithic. It is the only place on earth where Neanderthals, Denisovans, and modern humans are all attested in a single stratigraphic sequence — the Altai Neanderthals from its layers, the Denisovans from the finger bone and molars of §I, and modern human presence in its Upper Palaeolithic levels. That crowded sequence is the cave's glory and its interpretive trap, and the trap must be named before the ornaments: in a cave used by three populations, an ornament found in a layer is not automatically an ornament made by the population whose bones occur in that layer. Attribution at Denisova is argued object by object, from dating and stratigraphy, and some of its most famous objects carry that argument unresolved.

The Early Upper Palaeolithic levels of the cave — layer 11 of the East Gallery above all — produced one of the richest early ornament assemblages in northern Asia: pendants of animal tooth and mammoth ivory, beads of ostrich eggshell, bone tubes, a marble ring, and a fragment of a bracelet in dark green chloritolite, ground, polished, and drilled. The bracelet was published by Anatoly Derevianko, Mikhail Shunkov, and Pavel Volkov (Archaeology, Ethnology and Anthropology of Eurasia 34/2 (2008): 13–25), whose use-wear and technological analysis documented grinding on abrasives, polishing with skin, rasping, and a drilled hole made, they argued, with a fast, stable, two-sided drilling technique they described as without parallel in the Palaeolithic and more familiar from much later periods. From the same broad horizon comes a bone needle, about 7.6 cm long, with a drilled eye — among the oldest eyed needles known anywhere, and evidence, whoever made it, of sewn clothing in the Altai at a very early date. The deposits of layer 11 were initially dated to around 30,000 years ago for their upper part, with the Denisovan occupation of the cave placed broadly between about 100,000 and 40,000 years ago; subsequent dating work has shown the layer's history to be complex, with material of different ages in close association.

// ATTRIBUTION AT DENISOVA — READ WITH CARE //

The popular version of this assemblage — "Denisovans made jewellery 50,000 years ago" — runs ahead of the published evidence in two ways. First, the ornaments come from Early Upper Palaeolithic levels of a kind associated across Siberia with modern humans, and no Denisovan fossil was found in direct association with the bracelet or the needle. Second, layer 11's dating has been revised and complicated since 2008, and direct dates on individual ornaments have not assigned them to a maker. The defensible statement is narrower: Denisova Cave preserves an early, sophisticated ornament and bone-tool assemblage in levels spanning the period when Denisovans, Neanderthals, and modern humans all used the cave, and its maker or makers cannot yet be assigned with confidence. This article prints the narrower statement. The assemblage is included here because any honest account of the cave must include it — and because the unresolved attribution is itself a first-class example of this article's subject: the standard of proof.

What Denisova Cave does attribute, beyond argument, is presence and mixture. Genetic work on its sediments and bones has shown the cave alternating between populations over time — Neanderthal and Denisovan occupations succeeding each other in the sequence — and has produced the single most startling individual in this article, to whom §VIII now turns. The cave is best pictured not as a home of one population but as a crossroads used, across a hundred thousand years, by all three. Crossroads are where meetings happen, and at Denisova, uniquely, we can prove that they did.

VIII. The Plateau and the Hybrid — Xiahe and Denny

Xiahe — A Jaw at 3,280 Metres, at Least 160,000 Years Old

In 1980 a Buddhist monk found half a mandible — the right side, with two large molars attached — in Baishiya Karst Cave, at Xiahe on the northeastern edge of the Tibetan Plateau, 3,280 metres above sea level. The fossil passed to Lanzhou University and waited nearly four decades for a method that could identify it. No DNA survived in it. What survived was protein: collagen preserved in the dentine of a molar, whose sequence, analysed by Frido Welker and colleagues, placed the Xiahe individual with the Denisovans of Denisova Cave at high statistical confidence (Chen et al., Nature 569 (2019): 409–412). Uranium-series dating of the carbonate crust adhering to the jaw gave a minimum age of about 160,000 years. The mandible is robust, primitive in form, with very large teeth — the first substantial Denisovan anatomy, and the first Denisovan fossil identified outside Denisova Cave.

Its implications run in two directions. Geographically, Denisovans were no longer a single cave in Siberia: they were on the high plateau, in thin air, at least 160,000 years ago, in the penultimate glacial period — long before modern humans reached the region. Physiologically, the paper's authors drew the connection the genome had already suggested (§IX): the Denisovan-related population of the plateau is the plausible local source of the EPAS1 variant that helps modern Tibetans live at altitude, inherited, on this reading, from neighbours who had solved the altitude problem a hundred millennia earlier. Later excavation at Baishiya itself recovered Denisovan DNA from the cave's sediments, confirming occupation of the cave by Denisovans across a long span. The Xiahe jaw thus converted the Denisovans from a genetic abstraction into a population with a range — Siberia to the roof of the world — and a demonstrated capacity to live where our species, arriving much later, would need borrowed genes to thrive.

Denny — A First-Generation Hybrid, c. 90,000 Years Ago

Among more than two thousand unidentifiable bone fragments from Denisova Cave, screened by collagen fingerprinting to find the human ones, one fragment — a piece of long bone, catalogued as Denisova 11 — told a story no fossil had told before. Its mitochondrial DNA was Neanderthal. Its nuclear genome, sequenced by Viviane Slon and colleagues and published in Nature in 2018 (Slon et al., Nature 561: 113–116), was half Neanderthal and half Denisovan — not a mixture accumulated over generations, but an even, chromosome-by-chromosome split: a first-generation offspring. The individual, nicknamed Denny, was female, at least thirteen years old at death from the thickness of her bone's cortex, and lived about 90,000 years ago. Her mother was a Neanderthal — and, unexpectedly, a Neanderthal more closely related to the later Neanderthals of Vindija Cave in Croatia, thousands of kilometres west, than to the earlier Neanderthal known from Denisova Cave itself, implying movement of Neanderthal populations between western and eastern Eurasia after about 120,000 years ago. Her father was a Denisovan, related to the later Denisovans of the cave — and he carried, himself, a small trace of Neanderthal ancestry from some three to six hundred generations before his own time. Mixing, in Denny's family, had happened before.

The authors' inference from Denny is statistical and is stated as such: among the very small number of archaic individuals whose genomes have been sequenced, finding even one first-generation hybrid suggests that when these groups met, they mixed commonly — that such offspring were not vanishing rarities. A single individual cannot carry a rate, and the paper does not pretend otherwise; but Denny ended, permanently, any model in which Neanderthals and Denisovans were sealed lineages that never touched. They diverged more than 390,000 years ago. They remained, demonstrably, one family in the only sense biology finally recognises: their children were born, and lived, and at least one of them grew up in a cave in the Altai and left a fragment of bone for a laboratory to find.

IX. The Legacy in Living Genomes

The Neanderthals and Denisovans are extinct as populations. They are not extinct as ancestry. The draft Neanderthal genome of 2010 (Green et al., Science 2010) established the founding result: present-day people outside Africa carry Neanderthal-derived DNA — in current refined estimates, roughly 1.5–2.5 per cent of the genome of a typical non-African individual, with somewhat higher proportions in East Asian than in European genomes — inherited from interbreeding that occurred, in the main episode, roughly 50,000–60,000 years ago as modern humans spread out of Africa into Neanderthal country. Small amounts of Neanderthal ancestry are also now detected in African genomes, carried back by later migrations. No living person is a Neanderthal, and no population is more or less human for its percentage; the variation among populations is a matter of which migration routes their ancestors happened to walk, and this article states the figures as population history and nothing else.

Denisovan ancestry is distributed very differently, and the distribution is a map of a vanished range. It is highest — around 4–6 per cent in the original estimates, around 5 per cent in later refined ones — in Indigenous peoples of Melanesia, in Aboriginal Australians, and at notable levels in some Philippine populations, including the Ayta Magbukon, in whom the highest Denisovan proportions yet measured have been reported. It is present at low levels, well under one per cent, across mainland East and South Asia, and effectively absent from Europe. Genetic modelling indicates more than one Denisovan population contributed to this pattern — the Denisovans who mixed with the ancestors of Oceanians were related to, but distinct from, the Denisova Cave individuals, and at least one further, more distantly related Denisovan group appears to have mixed with East Asian ancestors. A population known from a handful of bones in one Siberian cave turns out, from its genes, to have ranged across Asia and diversified there. The genome, again, is the larger fossil.

Adaptive Introgression — Borrowed Solutions

Most archaic ancestry in modern genomes is neutral or slightly harmful, and natural selection has slowly purged much of it — archaic segments are conspicuously scarce near important genes and on the X chromosome. But a small number of inherited archaic variants rose in frequency because they solved problems. The textbook case is EPAS1, a gene involved in the body's response to low oxygen. The variant common in modern Tibetans — carried by the great majority of Tibetan individuals and rare everywhere else — matches the Denisovan genome, and was shown by Emilia Huerta-Sánchez, Rasmus Nielsen, and colleagues (Nature 2014) to have entered the Tibetan population by interbreeding with Denisovan-related people: adaptive introgression, in which an incoming population acquires, in a single generation of mixing, an adaptation the resident population spent millennia evolving. The Xiahe mandible (§VIII) gives that transaction a time and a place: Denisovan-related people were on the plateau by 160,000 years ago. Other introgressed regions with evidence of positive selection involve the immune system — including HLA variants — and skin, hair, and fat metabolism, a pattern consistent with archaic populations handing their incoming relatives a working knowledge, written in DNA, of Eurasian pathogens and climates. David Reich's synthesis, Who We Are and How We Got Here (2018), remains the standard general account of this revolution in the evidence, and this article follows its framing: ancient DNA did not merely add detail to the human story; it replaced a tree with a braided stream, in which mixture is the normal condition and isolation the temporary exception.

One consequence belongs in this article's ledger. The survival of Neanderthal and Denisovan DNA in living people is sometimes used, in popular writing, to soften the fact of their extinction — as if the populations did not really disappear because their genes persist. The geneticists' own framing is more exact. A few per cent of ancestry, filtered by fifty thousand years of selection, is a legacy, not a survival: the languages, crafts, and worlds of §§II–VII are gone entire, and what remains of their makers in us is a residue of useful molecules. Both halves matter. The other humans are our kin, demonstrably, in the flesh of people alive today — and they are gone, demonstrably, as peoples. This article holds both, because the evidence holds both.

X. The Standards Question — Revolution, Package, Double Standard

Every debate in this article is a local skirmish in one long war over a model. The model, in its classic form, is the human revolution: the proposal, developed by Paul Mellars, Richard Klein, and others from the European record, that modern human behaviour — art, ornament, blade technology, worked bone, long-distance exchange, ritual burial — appeared suddenly, as a package, around 50,000–40,000 years ago, in association with the arrival of anatomically modern humans in Europe and the replacement of the Neanderthals. In Klein's influential version, the package reflected a biological change, a neural reorganisation in African modern humans around 50,000 years ago, which Neanderthals, lacking it, could not match. On this model, apparent Neanderthal symbolism should be rare, late, and derivative — copied from modern human neighbours in the brief contact period — and each new claim for it properly faces the strongest scrutiny. The Châtelperronian ornaments of France, made by late Neanderthals in the contact period, became the model's test case: genuine Neanderthal work, on the excavators' reading, but work that might itself be imitation.

The counter-model was built first from Africa. Sally McBrearty and Alison Brooks, in their landmark review "The revolution that wasn't" (Journal of Human Evolution 39 (2000): 453–563), showed that the traits of the supposed package appear in the African Middle Stone Age separately, sporadically, and tens or hundreds of thousands of years apart — blades, bone tools, pigment, beads, and specialised hunting assembling gradually across 300,000 years, not arriving in a single revolutionary moment. Francesco d'Errico extended the argument into a multispecies frame: if the capacities assembled gradually in Africa, their roots lie in the shared ancestry of modern humans and Neanderthals, and sporadic symbolic expression should be expected in Neanderthal populations too — which is what Aviones, Krapina, and Bruniquel, all far too early for modern human contact, appear to show. On this reading the difference between the populations is not a cognitive wall but a difference of degree, demography, and accumulation: symbolism expressed intermittently by small, scattered populations leaves a faint, patchy record, and the patchiness is a fact about preservation and population size as much as about minds.

The Double-Standard Charge, Named and Attributed

Between the models sits a methodological charge that this article was commissioned to name, and it names it with its proponents and its limits. The charge — pressed by João Zilhão, by d'Errico, and in systematic form in reviews of the Mousterian symbolic record — is that identical evidence is read by different standards depending on its maker: a perforated shell among modern human remains is published as an ornament; a perforated shell among Neanderthal remains is published as a problem, with intrusion, misdating, and animal action investigated first and the ornament reading admitted last, if at all. The charge points to real asymmetries in the older literature, and the flower burial's career shows the mechanism in reverse: there, a romantic reading was admitted early, on weak taphonomy, because it flattered a narrative — proof that the standard bends toward stories in both directions.

The answer to the charge, from the sceptical side, is equally entitled to its full strength, and this article gives it. White, Slimak, Gargett, and Sandgathe, in their different debates, argue a principle that is not bias but method: a claim that contradicts a large, coherent body of prior evidence carries a heavier burden than a claim that extends it, and the burden is discharged by excluding the known failure modes — leaching, intrusion, burrowing, natural staining — in the specific case, not by appeal to fairness. On this view, the Neanderthal claims are not held to a different standard; they are held to the standard that any field applies to its most surprising results, and the proof that the standard is fair is that claims meeting it — Bruniquel's replicated dating, Kebara's observed grave cut, Aviones's sealed context — are in fact now widely accepted, including by many former sceptics. Both positions are live in the literature. What has changed, measurably, across the period this article covers is the location of the argument: in 1989 the debate was whether Neanderthals buried their dead at all; in 2020 it was which criteria a demonstrated burial must meet. The ground moved because specific claims met specific tests. That is the standards question's real answer, and it is procedural, not rhetorical: the way to end a double standard, if one exists, is the way Bruniquel and Kebara ended their local versions of it — with evidence built to survive the audit.

// A NOTE ON THE SPECIES QUESTION //

Stringer and Galway-Witham's reviews of the Middle Pleistocene record make a parallel point about taxonomy: the proliferation of species names for fragmentary fossils can outrun the evidence for reproductive boundaries between the populations named. Denny's genome (§VIII) is the sharpest evidence in that debate — two populations, separated for over 390,000 years, producing a healthy first-generation child. Whether that makes them one species or two is a question about the species concept, not about the child. This article's use of "populations" throughout is its way of keeping the evidence ahead of the label.

XI. What the Other Humans Do and Do Not Show

What the record shows, at Tier A strength, can now be stated in a single ledger. Neanderthals built: at Bruniquel, about 176,500 years ago, they broke, carried, standardised, and laid hundreds of stalagmite pieces into rings and stacks, with fire, 336 metres inside a cave — the oldest well-dated construction attributed to any human population, of unknown purpose. Neanderthals wore and made: at Krapina, about 130,000 years ago, they collected the talons of at least three white-tailed eagles and modified them in the pattern of a strung ornament; at Cueva de los Aviones, about 115,000 years ago, they perforated and pigmented marine shells and mixed pigment recipes in shell containers, in contexts sealed before any modern human reached Europe. Neanderthals buried: at Kebara about 60,000 years ago, in a grave whose cut was observed slicing through hearths; at La Chapelle, in a prepared pit with rapid covering demonstrated by a preservation contrast; at Shanidar, again, in the 2020 excavation of Shanidar Z. Denisovans ranged: from the Altai to the Tibetan Plateau by 160,000 years ago, adapted to altitude long before our species arrived there. The two populations met and mixed, repeatedly, and left a first-generation daughter in a Siberian cave. And both persist, in filtered fragments amounting to a few per cent of many living genomes, including one variant — EPAS1 — that still helps people breathe at the altitude where Xiahe's owner lived.

What the record does not show is equally definite, and this article has kept the negatives in the same type as the positives. It does not show Neanderthal cave art: the Iberian U-Th dates of c. 65,000 BP are published, defended, and genuinely contested on methodological grounds that have not been closed, and the verdict printed here is unresolved. It does not show funerary ritual: the Shanidar flowers fail their taphonomic audit, the strongest graves are plain, and no grave goods survive scrutiny at Kebara or La Chapelle. It does not show what the Bruniquel rings were for, what the Aviones pigments depicted or adorned, or what an eagle talon meant to the person who wore it — meaning is the one thing this record, so far, does not preserve. It does not show Denisovan symbolism at all: the Denisova ornaments and needle are unattributed, and honesty requires leaving them so. And it does not show equality, in any simple sense, between the symbolic worlds of these populations and those of the modern humans who replaced them: the Neanderthal record of ornament and pigment is real but sparse and intermittent across two hundred millennia, where the later modern human record is dense and cumulative — a difference the gradualist model attributes to demography and accumulation, the revolution model to cognition, and this article, following the evidence, leaves attributed to the debate in §X.

Perhaps the deepest finding is the one no single site owns. A century ago the other humans were a problem of bones: stooped reconstructions, small brains inferred from posture, a dead end. The bones have not changed. What changed is the instruments — uranium-series clocks read through a cave crust a few milligrams at a time, collagen fingerprints sorting a girl's bone from two thousand fragments, a genome recovered from a fingertip, a protein coaxed from a molar at 3,280 metres — and, with the instruments, the questions the evidence could be made to answer. The other humans built in the dark, wore eagles, mixed their paints in shells, dug graves that cut through their own hearths, climbed to the roof of the world, and had children with each other and, later, with us. None of that makes them modern humans in other bodies, and this article has not claimed it. It makes them what the archive exists to record: peoples, with worlds of their own, whose traces survive at exactly the strength the evidence has reached — no stronger, and, after the audits of the last decade, no weaker either.
