This is a Tier A article: everything in it rests on published geological, archaeological, and genetic evidence — sea-level curves built from dated corals, landscapes mapped from seismic data, artefacts with recorded findspots, and sites excavated by divers. Where scholars disagree, as they do sharply over the Storegga tsunami's human toll and more quietly over the flooding of the Bering Strait, the disagreement is reported with its positions attributed. Nothing here depends on legend, and nothing is borrowed from the hub's other tiers.

One plain statement belongs at the front, because this article is the attested counterpart of a famous fantasy. Where popular writing reaches for Atlantis — a rich civilisation swallowed in a day, as this hub's Tier C treatment of Mu and Atlantis Reborn documents in detail — the geological record offers something both less theatrical and far larger: whole inhabited landscapes, the size of countries and small continents, drowned not in a day but across some twelve thousand years, as the melting of the last ice sheets raised the world's oceans by about 120 metres. There were no cities on those plains and no kings to lose. There were hunting camps and fishing villages, river valleys and grazing herds, and — in Beringia — arguably a homeland in which a people's distinctive ancestry was formed. The loss was not of a civilisation. It was of most of the stage on which the late Palaeolithic and Mesolithic world had lived.

That loss is this article's real subject, and it is methodological before it is romantic. Human beings of the last glacial period were, like human beings now, disproportionately coastal and riverine: the richest hunting, fowling, fishing, and gathering grounds lay exactly where the rising sea struck first and deepest. The archaeological record on dry land is therefore not a fair sample of the human past. It is the upland residue of a mostly lowland world — a survival bias built into every map, every distribution plot, and every claim of "earliest" or "first" in this hub's Tier A timeline. Three drowned landscapes carry the argument. Doggerland, the Mesolithic plain beneath the North Sea, is the best mapped. Sundaland, the drowned half of Southeast Asia, is the largest. Beringia, the plain between Siberia and Alaska, is the most consequential, because a continent's peopling was staged there. Each is treated in turn; the working methods that recover them, and the famous claims that fail the same tests, close the article.

I. The Low Ground Went First — The Scale of the Loss

Begin with the arithmetic of exposure. At the Last Glacial Maximum, roughly 26,000 to 19,000 years ago, so much of the world's water stood on land as ice — in the Laurentide sheet over North America, the Fennoscandian sheet over northern Europe, and their fellows — that global sea level lay about 120 metres below its present stand (published estimates cluster between about 120 and 130 metres). Every continental shelf on Earth stood dry. The exposed land was not marginal fringe. For Europe alone, the SPLASHCOS synthesis (Bailey et al. 2020) puts the additional land at lowest sea level at about 2.5 million square kilometres — some 40 per cent added to the continent's present area — much of it, in the editors' assessment, well-watered and well-stocked lowland, often more attractive to hunter-gatherers than the colder, higher hinterland behind it. Comparable shelves ring every inhabited continent: the Sunda Shelf in Southeast Asia, the Sahul Shelf between Australia and New Guinea, the Bering and Chukchi shelves, the broad plains off eastern North and South America, the floor of the Persian Gulf.

Now run the water forward. Between about 19,000 and 6,000 years ago the ice sheets wasted and the sea took the shelves back, strip by strip, generation by generation. What drowned was not a random cross-section of the inhabited world. It was, systematically, its lowest and richest fraction: the coastal plains where marine and terrestrial foods meet, the lower river valleys, the estuaries and lagoons, the lakeshores of basins like the southern North Sea's. The record that survives on dry land is correspondingly skewed toward the places people used least — uplands, cave country, the interiors — and toward the periods after the flooding stopped. Archaeologists have a name for the resulting distortion in other contexts, and it applies here with unusual force: this is survival bias, and it is not a footnote to the deep past. It is a property of the evidence itself.

Three consequences follow, and they structure everything below. First, absence is cheap: a missing site, a missing migration route, a missing millennium on the shelf is what the flooding predicts, and it cannot be argued from as though the shelf had been searched and found empty — for most of human prehistory it has barely been searched at all. Second, coastal routes are under-evidenced by design: any dispersal that hugged the shore — into Sahul, around Southeast Asia, down the Pacific coast of the Americas — will have left its earliest stations under tens of metres of water, which is one reason this hub's treatments of Sahul and of the peopling of the Americas lean so heavily on genetics and on the few surviving inland witnesses. Third, the bias is corrigible, slowly: the shelf is not destroyed, only submerged, and §XIII describes the young discipline — seismic mapping, targeted coring, diver excavation — that is learning to read it. The drowned landscapes are an archive, not a void. But they are an archive most of which remains unopened, and honesty about that proportion is the price of admission to everything this article claims.

// WHY THESE THREE //

Doggerland, Sundaland, and Beringia are not the only drowned landscapes — the Persian Gulf oasis, the Sahul Shelf, and the plains off the Americas matter too, and the Gulf and Sahul are treated where this hub treats their regions. These three are chosen because each is documented by a different strength of evidence: Doggerland by direct mapping, Sundaland by bathymetry and genetics, Beringia by genetics and palaeoecology against a near-total absence of sites — the full spectrum of how a drowned world can be known.

A final preliminary, about words. None of the three names is ancient. Doggerland was coined in 1998 (§III). Beringia was coined in 1937 (§XI). Sundaland is a biogeographer's term for the shelf region, extended by geologists to its drowned Pleistocene extent. The peoples who lived on these plains left no names for them that we can recover. The modern names are tools, not traditions, and this article uses them as such — a small, exact instance of the hub's standing rule that the record, the tradition, and the telling be kept apart.

II. The Curve — About 120 Metres of Water

The sea-level curve is the single most load-bearing document in this article, and it deserves to be introduced as evidence rather than scenery. It is built principally from two archives. The first is coral: reef corals live within a narrow depth band below the surface, so a fossil reef terrace is a fossil sea level, and uranium-thorium dating of drilled coral cores — from Barbados in the Caribbean and from Tahiti in the Pacific, far from the former ice sheets — converts terraces into a dated curve. Richard Fairbanks's Barbados record (1989) first resolved the deglacial rise into its steps; the Tahiti cores of IODP Expedition 310, published by Deschamps and colleagues in Nature (2012), tightened the fastest step of all. The second archive is the oxygen-isotope record of deep-sea sediment cores, which tracks the total volume of ice on land and so corroborates the coral curve's broad shape. Where the two agree — and on the headline figure they do — the result is as robust as anything in Quaternary science: from the glacial lowstand, the ocean rose by roughly 120 metres to reach approximately its present level in the mid-Holocene.

The rise was not smooth, and its unevenness is the human story. Long stretches of comparatively gradual rise — on the order of a metre a century, fast enough for a community to watch its fishing grounds migrate within a lifetime — were interrupted by meltwater pulses, episodes of ice-sheet collapse in which the sea rose by metres within a few centuries. Two pulses dominate the literature, and they recur throughout this article.

Meltwater Pulse 1A — c. 14,600 BP

The best-documented pulse began, on the Tahiti evidence, after 14,650 BP and ended before 14,310 BP — a window of at most about 340 years, coincident with the abrupt Bølling warming that ended the last glacial cold. Deschamps and colleagues constrain its amplitude at Tahiti to between 12 and 22 metres, with a most probable value of 14 to 18 metres: a rate of eustatic rise exceeding 40 millimetres a year, sustained for centuries. On a low-gradient shelf — and the Sunda and North Sea shelves are among the lowest-gradient coasts on Earth — a vertical rise of that speed translates into a shoreline retreating by kilometres within a human lifetime, river mouths drowning in place, and low islands planed away between one generation's memory and the next. Meltwater Pulse 1A is the event that did much of the work of drowning Sundaland (§VIII) and that cut deep into every shelf on Earth simultaneously. Its sources remain debated — the Tahiti team demonstrated a significant Southern Hemisphere (Antarctic) contribution against an older assumption of an almost wholly northern source — but its timing and scale are no longer seriously in question.

Meltwater Pulse 1B — c. 11,500–11,000 BP, and a Standing Disagreement

The second pulse is the curve's honest uncertainty, and this article reports it as one. Fairbanks's Barbados record showed a second rapid jump, of up to roughly 28 metres, in the centuries after the Younger Dryas cold interval, about 11,500–11,000 BP; he named it Meltwater Pulse 1B. Later records — including the Tahiti sequence — have not reproduced a jump of that size, and subsequent studies suggest the true figure may have been much smaller, or that the Barbados step partly reflects local effects. The pulse's existence in some form is supported by independent evidence at one critical locality: the flooding of the Bering Strait (§XII), where a well-dated core records the Pacific breaking through at about 11,000 BP in a shift its authors associate with Meltwater Pulse 1B. The balanced statement is this: a rapid phase of rise in the centuries around 11,500–11,000 BP is widely accepted; its global amplitude is disputed, with published estimates running from a few metres to Fairbanks's original figure; and any argument that needs the large number should be read as resting on the Barbados record specifically. Readers will meet this pattern again — a solid event, a contested magnitude — at the Storegga tsunami, and the hub's treatment is the same in both places.

Two refinements complete the picture. The first is the difference between eustatic and relative sea level. The coral curve describes the ocean's global volume; what any given coast experienced also depended on the land's own motion — crust still rebounding upward where ice had pressed it down (as in Scotland and Scandinavia), or subsiding under the water load and the collapsing forebulge beyond the ice margin (as in the southern North Sea, which has been sinking gently throughout the Holocene). Doggerland therefore drowned faster than the global curve alone predicts, and parts of Scotland rose even as the sea did — which is why tsunami deposits of the same age sit at different heights on different coasts (§VI). The second refinement is the tail of the curve: after about 6,000 BP the rise slowed to a creep, and coastlines approached their modern positions. Every landscape treated below was gone, in its inhabited form, before that quietus — drowned not by the sea of the farming age but by the last convulsions of the ice age itself.

III. Doggerland — A Country, Not a Bridge

The southern North Sea is shallow — mostly less than 50 metres deep — and at the glacial lowstand it was not sea at all. From the east coast of Britain across to the present Netherlands, Germany, and Denmark stretched a continuous land: low hills, broad river valleys, marshes, lakes, and, as the climate warmed, spreading woodland. The great rivers of two modern countries ran together across it. The Thames, the Rhine, the Meuse, and the Scheldt were tributaries of one system that flowed south-west down the dry bed of the English Channel to an Atlantic shoreline far west of the present Strait of Dover. Britain was not an island but a peninsula of Europe — its north-western highlands the upland margin of a plain whose centre now lies under tens of metres of grey water and shipping lanes.

The land had no agreed name until 1998, when the archaeologist Bryony Coles published "Doggerland: A Speculative Survey" in the Proceedings of the Prehistoric Society (64: 45–81). Coles took the name from the Dogger Bank, the great shallow in the middle of the southern North Sea — itself named for the doggers, Dutch fishing boats of the seventeenth century — and she was explicit that the naming was an argument: the drowned plain should be studied as a place in its own right, with its own topography, ecology, and inhabitants, not treated as a corridor between two real countries or as a blank between Britain and the continent. Her survey was, by its title's own admission, speculative: in 1998 the evidence consisted of bathymetry, of finds trawled up over the preceding century (§V), and of inference from the onshore record. What has happened since — the seismic mapping of §IV — turned her speculation into a surveyed landscape, and the name stuck precisely because the argument was right.

What kind of country was it? The reconstruction assembled by the Birmingham and Bradford teams (§IV) shows a landscape that changed character as the post-glacial warming proceeded. In the late glacial it was open tundra-steppe, grazed by reindeer, horse, and — earlier still — mammoth. Through the early Holocene it became the classic Mesolithic mosaic: oak, hazel, and alder woodland on the higher ground; reed swamp, salt marsh, and lagoons along a long, shifting coastline; rivers carrying fish runs; lakes in the hollows — among them the basin now charted as the Outer Silver Pit. Coles judged it, on its ecology, possibly the richest hunting, fowling, and fishing territory in Mesolithic Europe, and nothing in the subsequent mapping has overturned that judgement. Its inhabitants were Mesolithic hunter-gatherers whose material culture — microlithic flint industries, barbed points of antler and bone — matches the contemporary cultures of Britain, the Low Countries, Denmark, and northern Germany, for the sufficient reason that those cultures were, at the time, one world with Doggerland at its centre rather than at its edge.

That inversion — centre, not edge — is Doggerland's first lesson, and it generalises. On a modern map the North Sea coasts of Britain and the continent look like margins, and their Mesolithic sites are conventionally described as peripheral settlements of peoples whose heartlands lay inland. On the map of 10,000 BCE the geometry reverses: the heartland is the drowned plain, and the surviving coastal sites of England, the Netherlands, and Denmark are its upland fringe — the camps that happened to stand high enough to survive. The same reversal awaits any region whose centre of gravity lay on the shelf, which is to say most regions of the late glacial world. Doggerland is simply the place where the reversal has been demonstrated in detail, river by river.

THE RIVERS

Thames, Rhine, Meuse, and Scheldt joined in one system flowing down the Channel river to the Atlantic; seismic mapping has traced individual channels — among them the Shotton River, draining the Dogger Bank hills to the Outer Silver Pit lake.

THE ECOLOGY

Tundra-steppe giving way to hazel and oak woodland, marsh, lagoon, and lake: on Coles's assessment, possibly the richest Mesolithic hunting, fowling, and fishing ground in Europe.

THE PEOPLE

Mesolithic hunter-gatherers culturally continuous with Britain, the Low Countries, Denmark, and north Germany — because all four were provinces of one plain-centred world.

THE NAME

Coined by Bryony Coles in 1998, from the Dogger Bank. The naming was programmatic: a country to be mapped, not a corridor to be crossed in a sentence.

Doggerland's human story also runs far deeper than the Mesolithic. The plain was crossed and hunted through the Palaeolithic whenever climate allowed — the dredged handaxes and the Neanderthal fossil of §V are its witnesses — and its final, Mesolithic chapter is only the best-lit part of a much longer occupation. But it is the Mesolithic chapter that the drowning terminated, and its ending was a process with a chronology the literature states in round, debated figures: Britain's separation from the continent is commonly placed around c. 6500 BCE, as the Channel and the southern bight flooded through; the remaining land shrank thereafter to an archipelago, and then to the single low island of the Dogger Bank itself, whose final submergence is generally placed around c. 7000 BP (c. 5000 BCE). Between those dates falls the one violent day in the story — the Storegga tsunami of §VI — and the question of how much of the ending was the wave's work is taken up in §VII.

IV. Reading a Drowned Plain — Seismic Survey and Cores

Doggerland could not be excavated in the ordinary way: it lies under 20 to 40 metres of water, much of it buried beneath marine sediments, in one of the busiest and roughest seaways in the world. Its reconstruction is instead a worked example of the method this article returns to in §XIII — remote sensing first, ground-truth by coring second, artefacts third — and the procedure deserves to be set out as procedure, because it is the template for submerged prehistory everywhere.

Step One — Borrowed Seismic Data

The oil and gas industry had, for its own purposes, already sounded the North Sea floor in three dimensions. Seismic reflection survey — firing acoustic pulses at the seabed and timing their echoes from buried surfaces — can image not only the modern seabed but the buried land surfaces beneath it: old ground surfaces, the channels of drowned rivers, lake basins, peat beds. Between 2003 and 2007 the North Sea Palaeolandscapes Project at the University of Birmingham, led by Vincent Gaffney with Kenneth Thomson and Simon Fitch, took seismic data provided for research by the survey company Petroleum Geo-Services and reinterpreted them archaeologically, mapping some 23,000 square kilometres of the early Holocene landscape of the southern North Sea. River systems invisible from any boat — the Shotton River among them — emerged as continuous mapped features, with banks, tributaries, and confluences. The results appeared as a technical monograph, Mapping Doggerland (2007), and as the synthesis Europe's Lost World: The Rediscovery of Doggerland (Gaffney, Fitch & Smith, 2009).

Step Two — Cores as Test Pits

A seismic map shows shapes, not dates or environments. The second step is therefore to core: to drill or drive tubes into the mapped features and recover columns of sediment whose contents — pollen, plant macrofossils, molluscs, peat, and, in the newest work, sedimentary ancient DNA — reconstruct the landscape's ecology and give radiocarbon dates for its drowning. The successor programme to the Birmingham project, the European Research Council–funded Europe's Lost Frontiers project (from 2015, led from the University of Bradford), industrialised this step: working from research vessels over a study area of some 190,000 square kilometres between Britain, Denmark, and the Dover Strait, it took long core transects across the mapped river valleys — notably the valley its team calls the Southern River — and built from them a dated environmental history of the plain's last millennia, including, in its cores, sedimentary evidence bearing on the Storegga wave (§VII). A core is a keyhole, not a trench; but a hundred cores along a mapped river amount to something no other drowned landscape yet possesses: a sampled, dated countryside.

Step Three — The Finds, Put Back on the Map

The third step reunites the map with the century of chance finds described in §V. A trawled antler point with no context is a curiosity; the same point plotted against a mapped coastline of its period becomes evidence of where people hunted. Dutch practice has gone furthest in systematising this: the great land-reclamation and port works at Rotterdam (the Maasvlakte extensions) dredged enormous volumes of North Sea sediment from known offshore localities and sieved the spoil, recovering Palaeolithic and Mesolithic assemblages whose offshore origins are recorded by dredging block — context at the scale of a landscape rather than a trench, but context nonetheless. The methodological moral is worth stating plainly, because popular accounts invert it: in submerged prehistory the map comes before the site. One does not find Doggerland by diving at random; one maps a buried countryside from a ship, dates it from cores, and only then knows where a diver, a dredger, or a development-led excavation could meaningfully look.

// INDUSTRY AS INSTRUMENT //

Doggerland science runs on borrowed infrastructure: oil-industry seismic surveys, aggregate dredging, shell extraction, and offshore wind-farm site investigation. The dependence is double- edged. The same dredging that recovers a Neanderthal fossil (§V) destroys its context in the recovering; the same wind-farm survey that maps a river valley precedes construction across it. The field's standing arrangement — archaeologists embedded in industrial survey, with protocols for finds — is described in §XIII.

What the method has produced, to date, is a landscape, not a settlement pattern. No Mesolithic house floor has been excavated in the open North Sea; no Doggerland village plan exists to set beside Atlit-Yam's (§XIV). The mapped rivers are real, the dated cores are real, the trawled tools are real — but between them they establish where people could and did live, and only rarely where a named group of people did. That gap is not a failure of effort. It is the present resolution limit of the best-studied drowned landscape on Earth, and it calibrates what may responsibly be claimed for the less-studied ones: Sundaland's reconstruction (§VIII) rests on bathymetry and genetics without Doggerland's density of cores, and Beringia's (§XI–XII) on genetics and palaeoecology with, as yet, no confirmed site at all on the drowned plain itself.

V. What the Nets Brought Up — Trawled and Dredged Finds

Doggerland was known from its objects long before it was mapped. Through the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, North Sea fishing vessels working the shallow banks hauled up, in their nets and dredges, the debris of a land fauna where no land should be: mammoth molars and bones by the tonne, woolly rhinoceros, horse, reindeer, bison — and, mixed with them, unmistakably human work. The find that fixed scientific attention came in 1931, when a trawler working the Leman and Ower Banks, east of the Wash, brought up a barbed point of antler — a Mesolithic hunting weapon of a type known from dry-land sites, recovered from the seabed far off the English coast, in association with the peat beds fishermen called "moorlog" — drowned land, hauled up in lumps. The point did not prove a countryside; but it proved inhabitants, and it made the question Coles formalised in 1998 unavoidable.

The trawled and dredged assemblage has grown ever since, and its character is worth describing exactly, because it defines both the strength and the weakness of this class of evidence. Its strength is range: handaxes of the Middle Palaeolithic, Levallois flakes, Mesolithic microliths and antler work, animal bone in industrial quantities, human bone occasionally — a cross-section of the plain's whole occupation, recovered across the whole of its area, at no excavation cost. Its weakness is context: a trawl integrates everything along its tow, a dredger everything in its cut, and a find's provenance may be no finer than a named bank. Individual objects can still carry remarkable information — in 2019 a flint flake dredged off the coast of The Hague was found to retain a lump of birch-bark tar, the Palaeolithic world's standard adhesive, still adhering to its surface: hafting technology, and the birch-processing behind it, attested from the open sea. But the assemblage as a whole testifies to presence and to industry, not to settlement, season, or society.

Krijn — The Zeeland Ridges Neanderthal

One dredged object stands apart, and it is the single most important human fossil ever recovered from the sea. In 2001, in the sieving debris of a shell-extraction company working the Zeeland Ridges, some 15 kilometres off the Dutch coast, the collector Luc Anthonis recognised a fragment of human frontal bone — part of a brow ridge — among Late Pleistocene animal bones and Middle Palaeolithic artefacts, including small handaxes and Levallois flakes, dredged from the same sediments. The specimen, nicknamed "Krijn" and published by Hublin and colleagues in the Journal of Human Evolution (2009), was assigned unambiguously to Homo neanderthalensis on the morphology of the brow and on three-dimensional geometric morphometrics: a young adult male. Carbon and nitrogen isotopes in the bone show a diet of overwhelmingly terrestrial meat, matching other Neanderthals and showing no sign of aquatic foods. A lesion in the bone was diagnosed as an intradiploic epidermoid cyst — a benign tumour of a type identified here, the authors note, for the first time in Neanderthal remains. The bone cannot be dated directly; its age is inferred from its context and morphology, broadly Late Pleistocene, commonly quoted at roughly 50,000 years or more.

Krijn's significance is triple. He is the first Pleistocene hominin fossil recovered from under seawater, and the first Neanderthal recorded from the Netherlands. He demonstrates — in a single, identified individual — that the drowned plain was Neanderthal country, part of the world treated in this hub's Neanderthals and Denisovans, and that its submerged sediments preserve fossil hominin material of the first quality. And he is a parable of the method: found not by an expedition but in industrial spoil, by a collector who knew what a brow ridge looked like, in a country whose heritage law had to reckon with a seabed archive it had barely begun to manage. The richest fossil ground in north-western Europe, the southern North Sea has been called on the strength of its mammal yields alone. Krijn is what that ground can hold.

// A NOTE ON PROVENANCE //

Everything in this section arrived without excavation context, and this article grades it accordingly. Trawled and dredged finds establish presence — of species, of industries, of a dated individual — at the scale of a bank or a dredging block. They do not establish association between objects found together in a net, and no argument in this article rests on treating them as though they did. The contrast case is §XIV: Atlit-Yam, where houses, burials, and a well were excavated in situ on the seabed by divers — the standard the North Sea material, for all its richness, has not yet met in the open sea.

VI. The Storegga Wave — c. 6200 BCE

Off the coast of western Norway, where the continental shelf breaks to the deep Norwegian Sea, lies the scar of one of the largest submarine landslides known anywhere: the Storegga Slide — Storegga is Norwegian for "the great edge." Around 6200 BCE (about 8150 calibrated years BP), a slab of shelf-edge sediment some 290 kilometres long failed and ran out across the ocean floor. Published estimates of the displaced volume run from about 2,400 to 3,500 cubic kilometres — a quantity of sediment comparable, in the comparison the literature itself uses, to Iceland buried tens of metres deep. The displaced water crossed the North Atlantic as a tsunami, striking Norway, the Faroe Islands, Shetland, Scotland, and the coasts of the northern North Sea — including, on the reconstructions debated in §VII, what was left of Doggerland.

The Evidence for the Wave

A prehistoric tsunami is not observed; it is read, from the sediment it leaves behind, and the Storegga deposits are the type example of the genre. Around the coasts of the Norwegian Sea and northern North Sea, fieldworkers — Stein Bondevik, Alastair and Sue Dawson, and their colleagues prominent among them — have documented a distinctive layer at the right stratigraphic horizon: a sheet of marine sand and gravel, containing ripped-up clasts of peat, redeposited lake mud, and marine microfossils, laid down on land, over peat and lake sediment, and subsequently buried by renewed peat growth. In Scotland the layer has been traced in the Montrose Basin and the Firth of Forth, running kilometres inland and standing metres above the tide levels of its day. In western Norway the deposits indicate runups of some 10–12 metres. In Shetland, closest to the slide on the wave's path, Bondevik and colleagues traced the sand up peaty hillsides to 9.2 metres above present high-tide level; because sea level there then stood at least 10–15 metres lower than today, the runup above the sea of that morning exceeded 20 metres — the finding announced in their 2003 Eos paper as a record-breaking height for an 8,000-year-old tsunami. Radiocarbon dates on plant material immediately above and below the deposits converge, in the most recent syntheses, on about 6225–6170 BCE for the event.

Two cautions belong with the numbers, and the literature states both itself. The first is that runup is not wave height: a tsunami's inundation height at a given hillside depends on the shape of that coast, and Shetland's exceptional figures are local maxima, not a North-Atlantic-wide wall of water. The second is that sediment-based and model-based reconstructions do not perfectly agree — a 2020 discussion by Dawson and colleagues, from the Shetland evidence, sets sediment-traced inundation limits against numerical models that produce lower maxima, and leaves the discrepancy open as a problem in tsunami geoscience rather than resolving it by preference. Neither caution touches the event itself. That a very large tsunami crossed the northern North Sea in the centuries around 6200 BCE is established by deposits on four coasts, independently dated. What remains genuinely open — and it is the question that matters for this hub — is what the wave did to the people in its path.

VII. Did the Wave End Doggerland? — The Debate

The Storegga debate is this article's set-piece disagreement, and it is reported here the way this hub reports all of them: positions named, evidence distinguished from inference, and no verdict smuggled in by adjective.

The Catastrophic Reading — Weninger and Colleagues

The strong form of the claim is Weninger and colleagues' 2008 paper in Documenta Praehistorica, "The catastrophic final flooding of Doggerland by the Storegga Slide tsunami." Working from a refined radiocarbon chronology for the slide and from reconstructions of the contemporary coastline, they argued that by c. 6200 BCE the remnant of Doggerland was a low-lying archipelago whose Mesolithic population was already compressed by rising seas — and that the tsunami inundated large parts of that remnant at a stroke, with an unexpectedly high impact modelled for the western coasts of Jutland as well as for the North Sea coasts. On this reading the wave was the final flooding: not the cause of Doggerland's long decline, which the sea-level curve had been driving for millennia, but the coup de grâce that ended its habitability and severed, culturally as well as geographically, the British Mesolithic from the continent's. Popular versions of the paper have attached a figure — up to a quarter of Mesolithic Britain's population killed — which circulates widely in secondary writing; it is an extrapolation from the modelled inundation, not a count from evidence, and this article flags it as such rather than repeating it as a finding.

The Modelling Reply — Hill and Colleagues

The counter-argument came from numerical modelling. Hill, Avdis, Mouradian, Collins, and Piggott simulated the Storegga tsunami's propagation across reconstructed palaeobathymetry and reported (2017; Proceedings of the Royal Society A, 2018) that the inundation of the Doggerland remnant was less severe than the sediment-based studies implied — the wave arriving at the low southern coasts attenuated, its runup limited by the shallow, dissipative shelf it had to cross. Their conclusion redistributes the causation: the abandonment of Doggerland was, on their modelling, primarily the work of the rapid sea-level rise of the preceding centuries — the drowning itself, proceeding at up to a metre and more per century in pulses — with the tsunami a dramatic episode within a population's retreat rather than its cause. The uncertainties cut both ways, and the modellers state them: the result depends on reconstructed seabed topography and on the slide's assumed dynamics, neither of which is directly observable.

The Cores Reply — Walker, Gaffney, and Europe's Lost Frontiers

A third position has since been argued from the ground itself. Walker, Gaffney, and colleagues of the Europe's Lost Frontiers project, reporting in Antiquity (2020) on sediment cores taken across the drowned Southern River valley, presented sedimentary evidence that the Storegga wave did reach and inundate parts of the surviving Doggerland landscape — deposits in the cores that they interpret as the tsunami's signature, offshore, in the landscape itself rather than only on the surviving coasts. Their reading is deliberately intermediate: the wave was real in Doggerland and locally devastating, but it struck a landscape whose population was already reduced and retreating before inexorable sea-level rise; whether it ended settlement or merely scourged its last generation is, on the core evidence, not separable. That a research team can hold the wave's arrival as demonstrated and its demographic decisiveness as undecidable, in the same paper, is the debate's present centre of gravity.

What is not in dispute should be stated as firmly as what is. Not in dispute: the slide, its approximate date, the tsunami's existence and its severe impact on the coasts of Shetland, Norway, and Scotland, where the deposits lie on land that can still be walked. Not in dispute: that Doggerland was, by 6200 BCE, a remnant — the sea-level curve had already taken most of it, and the Dogger Bank island that outlasted the wave (§III) went under to ordinary drowning within the following millennium or so. In dispute: the wave's height and reach across the remnant archipelago, and consequently its share — decisive, contributory, or minor — in the end of Mesolithic life on the plain. This article's assessment section (§XV) returns to the shape of the disagreement, because it is exemplary: it is what honest Tier A argument looks like when the evidence is rich, the event is certain, and the human meaning is nonetheless underdetermined.

// THE DOGGER BANK, LAST ISLAND //

The Dogger Bank — the moraine-cored high ground at Doggerland's heart, named on every modern chart — was the last of the plain to drown. Reduced to a low island after the archipelago phase, it is generally placed under water by about 7000 BP (c. 5000 BCE), some twelve centuries after the Storegga wave. The wave, on any reading, did not sink the Bank; the sea did, slowly. The distinction matters to §VII's debate: "final flooding" in Weninger's title refers to the flooding of the inhabited lowlands, not to the submergence of the last dry hill.

VIII. Sundaland — A Tropical Continent, Halved

If Doggerland is a country, Sundaland is a continent. At the glacial lowstand, the shallow Sunda Shelf — today the floor of the South China Sea, the Java Sea, and the Gulf of Thailand — stood dry, joining mainland Southeast Asia to Sumatra, Java, Borneo, and Bali in a single landmass and roughly doubling the region's land area. The reconstruction used throughout the literature is Harold Voris's set of palaeogeographic maps (Journal of Biogeography, 2000), drawn from the shelf's bathymetry at successive sea levels; its most eloquent feature is its rivers. The drowned shelf was not a swampy flat but a drained landscape, crossed by great river systems whose channels are still traceable on the seabed: the North Sunda (Molengraaff) River, flowing north-east between Borneo and Sumatra to the South China Sea; the Siam River, draining across what is now the Gulf of Thailand; the Malacca Strait River, running down the present strait between Sumatra and Malaya; and the East Sunda River, crossing the Java Sea floor. Rivers of that order imply catchments, floodplains, and gallery forest — a lived-in geography, with the Wallace Line's deep-water straits to the east marking the continent's hard edge against the separate world of Wallacea and Sahul.

Sundaland's climate made it unlike the other landscapes of this article. It lay across the equator, unglaciated, its interior — on palaeovegetation reconstructions debated in detail — a mosaic of rainforest and more open, seasonal woodland and grassland, with a corridor of drier country possibly running through its centre. For the hominins who reached it, it was not a cold refuge but a terminus and a homeland of extraordinary richness: the shelf's rivers and coasts offered the same estuarine plenty as Doggerland's, at tropical scale and without a winter. Modern humans were present in the region by at least about 45,000–50,000 years ago, on their way to Sahul; archaic humans had been there for well over a million years before them (§IX). The human geography the shelf carried was therefore deep as well as wide: populations distributed along its rivers and coasts, connected by waterways that are now straits and seas, across distances that the modern map — a scatter of islands — renders unintelligible.

The drowning of Sundaland was the largest single act of landscape loss in the human story. Between the Last Glacial Maximum and the mid-Holocene, sea level in the region rose by about 120–130 metres, and — on the Nanyang Technological University team's palaeogeographic reconstruction (Kim and colleagues, §X) — roughly half of Sundaland went under, the landmass fragmenting into the Malay Peninsula and the islands of the modern map. The two meltwater pulses did disproportionate work: Meltwater Pulse 1A, at about 14,600 BP, drove the shoreline back across the shelf's flattest reaches at landscape scale within centuries, and the rapid phases around and after 11,500 BP repeated the process. What the pulses mean in human terms is worth pausing on. A vertical rise of 14–18 metres, on a shelf sloping at a few metres per kilometre, is a horizontal retreat of the coast by tens of kilometres within a few generations — whole river valleys converted to estuaries, then to open sea, inside the span of oral memory. Sundaland's peoples did not experience "sea-level rise" as a statistic. They experienced it as the continuous, unidirectional loss of the known world, at a pace no adaptation of settlement could outrun except retreat itself.

Unlike Doggerland, Sundaland has no seismic-mapping programme at comparable scale and no trawled assemblage of comparable density: its reconstruction rests on bathymetry, on the region's sparse and mostly upland or cave archaeology, and — decisively — on genetics (§X). That evidential profile should be kept in view. The landscape is not in doubt: the rivers are on the charts, the sea-level curve is the same curve as §II, and no model of the region at 20,000 BP shows anything but a continent. What rests on inference is the human detail — where populations concentrated, how they moved as the water rose, and how much of the modern genetic map of Southeast Asia is the drowning's signature. §X takes up that inference at its proper strength. First, the shelf's deep record, in the one place where it can be dated to the year-range.

IX. Ngandong — The Shelf's Deep Record

Java was Sundaland's southern shore-land: joined to the continent whenever the shelf stood dry, an island only in interglacials like the present one. Its Solo River valley holds one of the great fossil sequences of human evolution, and its last chapter is the site of Ngandong, on a terrace some 20 metres above the modern river. There, between 1931 and 1933, Dutch geological survey excavations recovered from a single bone bed twelve calvaria (skull caps) and two tibiae of Homo erectus — the youngest and most advanced form of the species known — together with a rich fauna of deer, cattle-like bovids, and other open-country animals. For most of a century the bone bed's age was fought over, with published estimates scattered across hundreds of thousands of years, because the fossils had been excavated before modern dating could be applied to their context and the terrace stratigraphy was disputed.

The resolution came from Rizal, Westaway, Ciochon, and a large international team, published in Nature (577, 2020: 381–385; online December 2019). Rather than dating the fossils in isolation, the team re-excavated Ngandong and dated the landscape: 52 radiometric age estimates — uranium-series on speleothems and on fossil bone, luminescence, and argon-argon — modelled together in a Bayesian framework to fix the diversion of the Solo River, the building of its terrace staircase, and the accumulation of the bone bed itself, which the evidence shows was laid down in flood conditions, the dead washed into a debris jam where the river narrowed. The modelled age of the Homo erectus bone bed is 117,000 to 108,000 years ago. The authors' conclusion is stated without inflation and this article adopts it at the same strength: Ngandong is, on present evidence, the last known occurrence of Homo erectus — the final dated appearance of a species that had reached Java more than 1.5 million years earlier and had persisted there, through glacial cycles that repeatedly joined and severed Java from the continent, into a world in which Homo sapiens already existed in Africa and Neanderthals in Europe.

Ngandong earns its place in this article for two reasons beyond its fame. The first is methodological kinship: its dating was achieved exactly the way Doggerland was mapped — by reconstructing a drowned and buried landscape (a river's terraces are a sea-level archive in miniature) and dating the landscape, not merely the object. The second is what it implies about the shelf. The Ngandong population lived in open, savanna-like country along a great river, in the kind of terrain that covered much of lowland Sundaland. When the team's climate reconstruction is added — rainforest spreading across Java in the warming after about 120,000 years ago, closing the open habitat — the species' end reads as an environmental story of a familiar Sundaland kind: a population of the lowland river plains, extinguished not by a rival but by the transformation of its landscape. The sea would later do the same work, at larger scale, to the plains themselves. For the wider story of the archaic populations of the region — Neanderthals' eastern cousins the Denisovans among them — see Neanderthals and Denisovans.

// LAST KNOWN, NOT LAST //

The Ngandong authors are careful, and this article follows them: a "last known occurrence" dates the evidence, not the extinction. Homo erectus may have persisted later in places whose record has not survived or not been found — Ciochon himself has made the point in interviews. The caution is the §XV argument in miniature: on a drowned and forested continent, every "last" and "first" is a statement about the surviving sample, and should be read as one.

X. The Drowning and the Peoples of Southeast Asia

What did the loss of half a continent do to its peoples? The shelf itself can offer almost no direct answer — its sites are under the South China Sea — so the argument has been built from the genomes of the living, and this section states it as what it is: inference from genetics, of varying strength, not testimony from the ground.

The first strand is mitochondrial. Hill, Soares, Oppenheimer, Richards, and colleagues, in "A Mitochondrial Stratigraphy for Island Southeast Asia" (American Journal of Human Genetics, 2007), surveyed the region's maternal lineages and found its diversity to be both high and largely indigenous: only a minority of modern lineages — at most about a fifth, on their analysis — could be tied to the much later, mid-Holocene arrival of Austronesian-speaking farmers from Taiwan that older models had cast as the region's peopling. The majority of the lineages instead dated to the late Pleistocene and early Holocene, and the authors read their pattern of expansion as marking dispersals most likely triggered by the postglacial flooding itself: populations displaced from the drowning shelf, carrying their lineages into the remnant islands and the mainland, millennia before any farming migration. On this reading, the genetic map of island Southeast Asia is substantially a map of a refugee movement — the largest and longest in prehistory, conducted at walking pace over thousands of years.

The second strand is genomic and more recent. Kim, Tanghua Li, and colleagues at Nanyang Technological University combined new palaeogeographic maps of Sundaland — reconstructed at intervals from 26,000 years ago to the present — with whole-genome data from 59 ethnic groups of Southeast and South Asia, publishing in Communications Biology (2023). Their reconstruction shows the mechanism in time-lapse: during the rapid-rise phases, the shelf's populations were fragmented as the landmass broke into islands and peninsulas, their lineages differentiating in isolation; during the warm millennia that followed, population growth in the compressed remnant lands was followed, on their model, by migration northward toward mainland Southeast Asia and South Asia, where the separated lineages met and mixed again. The team's stated conclusion — that sea-level rise demonstrably reshaped the genetic makeup of the region's peoples, in a legacy still legible in modern genomes — is the strongest form of the claim yet published, and it is a claim about pattern: the timing and structure of genetic divergences match the timing and structure of the flooding, in a model built from both datasets together.

The discipline this hub applies to such arguments is the same as at Storegga: separate the strata of certainty. That Sundaland drowned, on the §II curve and the §VIII bathymetry, is certain. That its drowning displaced populations on a continental scale is as close to certain as an unwitnessed process can be — people cannot remain on land that is under the sea. That the specific dispersals the genetic studies identify — their dates, their directions, their attribution to flooding pulses rather than to the period's other upheavals — are the correct reading of the lineage data is a scholarly inference, published and defensible, and revisable as ancient DNA from the region accumulates. What the genetics cannot yet do is name a single drowned valley's inhabitants or follow a single community's retreat. For that, Southeast Asia awaits its Doggerland programme — and the comparison is instructive, because it shows the two halves of this article's method as complements: where the shelf can be mapped and cored, as in the North Sea, the landscape carries the argument; where it cannot yet be, the genomes carry it, at one remove further from the ground.

// A WORD NOT USED HERE //

Popular treatments of Sundaland sometimes reach for the vocabulary of this hub's Tier C — a lost civilisation, a drowned Atlantis of the east. The evidence supports no such thing, and the genetic studies cited here claim no such thing: the populations displaced from the Sunda Shelf were, on all surviving evidence, hunter-gatherer and early cultivating communities of the kinds known from the region's dry-land record, not a state, a city-building culture, or a maritime empire. The drowning of Sundaland is a greater event than the Atlantis story in every dimension except one: it happened to ordinary people, and it is true.

XI. Beringia — A Homeland, Not a Corridor

The third landscape is the largest in consequence and the emptiest in finds. Beringia — the name was coined in 1937 by the Swedish botanist Eric Hultén, for the region's distinctive flora — is the country that existed, at the glacial lowstand, between north-eastern Siberia and Alaska: bounded by the Verkhoyansk range and the Lena River in the west and by the Mackenzie River in the east, and centred on the broad plain, up to a thousand kilometres from north to south, that now lies beneath the Bering and Chukchi Seas. The modern Bering Strait, 85 kilometres wide and only about 53 metres deep, is the drowned saddle of that plain. Older writing called it a "land bridge," and the phrase did real damage: a bridge is crossed, briefly, by travellers between two homes. Beringia was nobody's thoroughfare. It was a subcontinent-sized country that persisted, in its last exposure, for some twenty thousand years — long enough to be a homeland many times over.

Its ecology explains both its persistence and its habitability. Beringia lay beyond the reach of the great ice sheets not because it was warm but because it was dry: the same circulation that locked moisture into the Laurentide and Siberian ice starved the Beringian interior of snow, leaving it an unglaciated refugium at the top of the world. Its classic landscape reconstruction is the mammoth steppe: a cold, dry, productive grassland — graminoids, sagebrush, and forbs on well-drained soils — that supported herds of horse, steppe bison, and mammoth, with caribou, muskox, and their predators, across a belt that at its greatest extent ran from Europe to Canada. But the steppe was not the whole country, and the correction matters for the human story. Sediment cores from the Bering Sea floor and from Alaskan bogs — the work of palaeoecologists including Scott Elias — recover pollen, plant, and insect assemblages showing that the lowlands, especially toward the south of the land bridge, carried shrub tundra: birch, willow, and alder thickets, with patches of spruce in sheltered places. Shrubs mean firewood, construction material, and browse for game — the resources whose absence from a pure grass-steppe model long led archaeologists to doubt that people could have wintered on the bridge at all.

For the animals, Beringia's role is documented in bone on both continents: it was the filter and the mixing ground through which mammoth, bison, lion, and brown bear passed between Asia and America in both directions across the glacial cycles, each exposure of the shelf an episode of exchange, each flooding an isolation. Humans entered this system late in its history. The question that defines Beringian studies — when they came, how long they stayed, and in what sense the stay made them a distinct people — is the subject of §XII, and its full genetic treatment belongs to this hub's Peopling of the Americas. Here it is enough to fix the geographical point on which everything else depends: the population that would become the First Peoples of the Americas did not, on the prevailing model, hurry across Beringia. It lived there — through the coldest millennia of the last ice age, in the shrub lowlands of a country that no longer exists.

From the Lena and the Verkhoyansk range to the Mackenzie: a region wider than Alaska, centred on a plain up to c. 1,000 km north-to-south, now beneath the Bering and Chukchi Seas.

THE REFUGIUM

Unglaciated through the glacial maximum — too dry for ice sheets, not too warm for them. A refuge for plants, animals, and, on the standstill model, people.

THE STEPPE

Mammoth steppe grassland on the uplands; shrub tundra — birch, willow, alder, spruce patches — in the lowlands of the cores: fuel, shelter, and game habitat.

XII. The Standstill and the Closing — c. 11,000 BP

The Beringian standstill is a hypothesis built to explain a mismatch. Genetic studies of Native American populations — the detailed treatment is in Peopling of the Americas, which owns this subject — show that the ancestors of those populations separated from their Siberian relatives and accumulated a distinctive genetic signature over a long isolation, conventionally modelled at roughly 25,000 to 15,000 years ago: some ten thousand years in which a founding population lived somewhere apart, neither in Siberia nor yet spread through the Americas, while the ice sheets barred the way south. The palaeoenvironmental work of §XI supplied the somewhere. In a widely cited Science Perspective of 2014 — "Out of Beringia?" — Hoffecker, Elias, and O'Rourke argued that the genetic standstill and the ecological evidence converge: the shrub-tundra lowlands of the Beringian land bridge were habitable through the glacial maximum, offered fuel and game, and were isolated exactly as the genetics requires. A population of perhaps a few thousand people, on this model, lived out the ice age's worst in Beringia, becoming in the process a people distinct from any in Asia — and then, as the ice melted and routes opened, moved south into the Americas in the millennia after about 15,000 BP.

The hypothesis has a famous hole in it, and its proponents name the hole themselves: no archaeological site of standstill age has been found in Beringia. The dry-land margins offer candidates and controversies — the Bluefish Caves in Yukon, whose ice-age bone dates remain disputed, are the standing example — but the lowland heartland where the model places the population is under the Bering Sea. The absence is therefore exactly what §I's survival bias predicts, and it cannot be used either way: it does not confirm the standstill (an absence confirms nothing), and it does not refute it (the relevant ground has never been searchable). What can be said is that the standstill is currently the best-fitting joint explanation of the genetic isolation and the palaeoecology; that its archaeological test awaits a Doggerland-style programme in the Bering Sea that has not yet been mounted at scale; and that Beringia is thus the purest case in this article of a drowned landscape known almost entirely by its consequences.

The Closing of the Strait

Beringia's end is better dated than its occupation. The strait reopened when the rising sea overtopped the sill, now about 53 metres below the surface. Published estimates for that flooding long ranged widely — roughly 13,000 to 10,000 BP on different lines of evidence — and the question mattered beyond geography, because the timing of the Pacific's reconnection to the Arctic bears on the Younger Dryas cold event and on the routes open to the first southward movements. Jakobsson and colleagues (Climate of the Past, 2017), working from geophysical survey and a well-dated sediment core in Herald Canyon, north of the strait, identified the shift from near-shore to Pacific-influenced open-marine conditions at about 11,000 cal BP, and interpreted it as the relatively rapid breaching of the strait — associating the flooding with Meltwater Pulse 1B (§II) and placing it after the Younger Dryas. A later modelling study (Pico and colleagues, Science Advances, 2020) reconciled the apparently conflicting datasets with a two-phase flooding history between about 13,000 and 11,500 BP, driven by meltwater from the western Laurentide and Cordilleran ice sheets. The two accounts differ on whether the strait's opening was an event of c. 11,000 BP or a process spanning the preceding two millennia — a disagreement of the now-familiar shape, solid event, contested tempo — and this article reports the date in the form the evidence best supports: the Bering Strait was finally and fully open by about 11,000 BP, with the main flooding placed by Jakobsson's core at that date and an earlier partial phase argued by Pico's modelling.

Consider what that closing meant. A country that had joined two continents for twenty thousand years — that had been, on the standstill model, a people's entire world for half that span — became a strait, then a sea. Its lowlands, where the model's population had actually lived, went under first and deepest; what remained above water was the mountainous fringe: Chukotka, the Seward Peninsula, the Yukon and Siberian uplands where Beringia's margins are still studied today. The peopling of the Americas was completed, on the prevailing account, just before or just as the door closed behind it — the southward dispersals belong to the millennia around and after 15,000 BP, the strait's drowning to about 11,000 BP — so that the First Peoples entered their continents from a homeland that then ceased to exist. There is no other migration in the human story whose staging ground has so completely vanished. If Doggerland teaches the survival bias as a matter of maps, Beringia teaches it as a matter of identity: a people's formative millennia were spent in a place that cannot now be visited, excavated, or — except by the methods of the next section — even seen.

XIII. How Submerged Prehistory Is Done — The Working Frontier

Submerged prehistory is a young discipline with an old problem: its sites lie where archaeologists cannot breathe, under sediment they cannot see through, in landscapes that must be reconstructed before they can be searched. Its modern form dates from the recognition — argued for decades by Nicholas Flemming, the field's founding figure — that the drowning of a site is not the same as its destruction. Flemming's organising question, set as the title of the introduction to the 2017 synthesis Submerged Landscapes of the European Continental Shelf (Flemming, Harff, Moura, Burgess & Bailey, eds.), is why some sites and landscapes survive inundation. The answer is sedimentary: where a rising sea buried a land surface quickly under protective mud, silt, or peat — in estuaries, lagoons, and sheltered basins — the surface and its contents could be sealed before wave action planed them away; where the transgression crossed exposed, high-energy coasts, it usually destroyed what it drowned. Survival is therefore predictable in principle, and the discipline's craft lies in predicting it well: mapping the buried topography, identifying the low-energy traps, and testing them.

The Programme — SPLASHCOS and the European Synthesis

The discipline organised itself, in Europe, as SPLASHCOS — Submerged Prehistoric Archaeology and Landscapes of the Continental Shelf — a COST-funded research network (2009–2013) that brought together the marine geophysicists, archaeologists, and heritage managers of more than twenty countries. Its two synthetic volumes are the field's present foundation: Under the Sea (Bailey et al., eds., 2017), on method and interpretation, and The Archaeology of Europe's Drowned Landscapes (Bailey, Galanidou, Peeters, Jöns & Mennenga, eds., 2020), the network's final survey. The 2020 volume's census conveys the frontier's true position: across 20 countries, from the Atlantic coasts to the Black Sea and the eastern Mediterranean, it assembles nearly 3,000 underwater find spots — ranging in age from about a million years ago to the establishment of modern sea levels — of which the great majority are isolated finds or find scatters, and only a small minority are settlements excavated in situ. The archive is real, continent-wide, and almost entirely unexcavated. Flemming's reviews of the field make the same point from the global side: submerged sites are known from every inhabited continent's shelf, the methods transfer, and the limiting factor is not survival but search effort — ships, seismic time, and divers are expensive, and the shelf is vast.

The Methods, in Order of Application

In practice a submerged-prehistory project runs through the sequence §IV described for Doggerland, and each step has its instruments. Seismic and acoustic survey — reflection seismics, sub-bottom profilers, multibeam and side-scan sonar — maps the buried land surface and its river channels from the surface vessel; it is the only prospection method that works at landscape scale. Coring — vibrocorers and drilling from barges or research ships — ground-truths the map: pollen and plant remains reconstruct vegetation, peat and wood give radiocarbon dates, sedimentary DNA increasingly identifies the species present, and worked flint or charcoal in a core can flag a site horizon. Diver survey and excavation, feasible only in shallow, sheltered water, does what no remote method can: excavation in context, with the waterlogging that preserves wood, fibre, and food remains lost on dry land. And industry partnership — aggregate dredging, pipeline and wind-farm survey, port construction — supplies both the finds (§V) and the survey data, under reporting protocols of the kind developed for the Dutch and British sectors (the submerged-Mesolithic syntheses of Hans Peeters for the Netherlands and Garry Momber for the British Solent, in the SPLASHCOS volumes, are the worked examples).

The diver-excavated end of the spectrum deserves one concrete case from home waters. At Bouldnor Cliff, in the Solent off the Isle of Wight, divers of the Maritime Archaeology Trust (Momber and colleagues, 2011) have excavated, at about 11 metres depth, a Mesolithic occupation surface of about 8,000 BP sealed beneath marine mud: worked flints, burnt hazelnut shells, corded fibre, and an assemblage of worked wood — including split oak timbers — of a sophistication the team assessed as some two thousand years ahead of the dry-land British record for woodworking; sedimentary DNA from its sealed deposits later produced evidence of wheat in Britain two millennia before farming's accepted arrival, a claim its authors published in Science (2015) and which remains debated. Bouldnor is what the frontier looks like when every method converges on one small, sheltered, lucky site: a drowned riverbank excavated like a dry one, yielding a class of evidence — Mesolithic carpentry — that the dry record barely contains. It is also, by the SPLASHCOS census's own arithmetic, the exception: for every Bouldnor there are hundreds of find spots that will never be more than a line in a database and a point on a palaeogeographic map. The frontier advances by keyholes.

// THE WORKING SEQUENCE, AT ITS EXACT STRENGTH //

Map the buried landscape by seismic survey (landscape scale; no artefacts). Date and characterise it by coring (environment and chronology; artefacts only by chance). Excavate by diver only where depth, shelter, and a sealed surface coincide (full context; tiny areas). Harvest industry finds under protocol (rich assemblages; landscape-scale provenance at best). Every claim in this article can be assigned to one of these four grades of evidence — and should be.

XIV. Grading the Famous Claims — Atlit-Yam and Its Counterfeits

Submerged prehistory attracts claimants the way dry archaeology attracts treasure hunters, and a Tier A article owes its readers the grading standard applied openly. A genuinely submerged site must show: structures or deposits in situ on the seabed, in stratigraphic order; artefacts in context, not dredged from mixed spoil; dates from material in that context, by published methods; and a submergence history consistent with the local sea-level curve. One site meets the standard so completely that it serves as the type case for the entire field.

Atlit-Yam — What Survives, Excavated

Off the Carmel coast of Israel, 200 to 400 metres from the modern shore and 8 to 12 metres down, lies Atlit-Yam: a Pre-Pottery Neolithic village, discovered in 1984 by Ehud Galili during a survey for shipwrecks and excavated by divers over the following decades. Its radiocarbon dates place it at about 8100–7500 BP (Galili et al., Journal of Field Archaeology, 1993) — in calendar terms, broadly the seventh millennium BCE, at the very end of the Pre-Pottery Neolithic (the PPNC of the Levantine sequence). Across a site area of some 40,000–60,000 square metres, sealed under sand and clay, the divers recorded the rectangular foundations of stone houses; burials beneath house floors and in graves — Atlit-Yam long stood as the only Mediterranean marine site with human burials in situ; a stone-built water well, among the earliest known anywhere, sunk to reach the water table of a coast that has since retreated past it; and a semicircle of standing stones around a freshwater spring. The economy the remains document is the site's fame: domesticated cereals and pulses, domestic goat and cattle in early stages of management, wild game, and — in quantities that mark a fishing village, not a farm with a beach — fish bones, fishhooks, and net sinkers. Galili and colleagues describe it as the earliest known community combining farming, herding, and fishing on the Levantine coast. Later coring (Friesem and colleagues, Antiquity, 2022) has shown stratified occupation deposits continuing below the exposed surface — the village is not even fully read.

Atlit-Yam's lesson is double. Positively, it proves the §XIII thesis: a Neolithic village can survive submergence with its houses, its dead, its well, and its economy legible, when burial seals it faster than the sea can scour it — and what survives at Atlit-Yam is precisely the class of evidence (coastal, lowland, early-farming) that §I warned is missing from the dry record of the whole region. Negatively, it sets the bar that famous claims must clear — and the famous claims, graded by it, divide cleanly.

The Counterfeits, Graded

Yonaguni, Japan. The terraced sandstone formation off Yonaguni Island, promoted by marine geologist Masaaki Kimura as a submerged artificial monument, has been examined by geologists who dive it — Robert Schoch among them — and is regarded in the mainstream geological literature as a natural formation: jointed sandstone strata fractured and eroded into step-like terraces, of a kind the region's geology produces without human help. No artefacts in context, no occupation deposit, and no dates for human workmanship have been published. Grade: natural formation; the artificial claim is unproven on every element of the standard.

The Gulf of Khambhat (Cambay), India. In 2001, dredging in the gulf recovered wood, pottery fragments, and other material that was announced, with a radiocarbon date of about 7500 BCE on dredged wood, as a submerged city of the Harappan world's predecessor. The material came from dredge spoil, not from in-situ excavation; no structure has been demonstrated on the seabed in context; and the claims have not been sustained in the peer-reviewed literature. Grade: unproven — finds without context, of exactly the class §V teaches cannot carry a settlement claim. The contrast with Atlit-Yam, announced by excavation reports rather than press releases, is the whole lesson.

Pavlopetri, Greece, belongs on the other side of the line and is included to show the line is not a debunking reflex: a Bronze Age town off the Laconian coast, its streets, buildings, and tombs mapped by diver survey from 2009 onward under Jon Henderson and colleagues — genuine, in situ, and published, but of the second millennium BCE, millennia after this article's landscapes drowned and outside this hub's deep-past scope. Grade: attested, and not evidence about the Palaeolithic or Mesolithic world. The grading standard is chronological as well as stratigraphic: a real drowned town of 1600 BCE says nothing about drowned continents of 10,000 BCE, and the popular literature's habit of citing Pavlopetri as though it licensed Atlantis is a category error this hub declines to repeat.

XV. What the Sea Holds — An Assessment

How much of the human story does the sea hold? The honest answer has a number-shaped outline and no number. By area, the drowned shelves are continental in scale: Europe's share alone was some 2.5 million square kilometres, and the shelves of Asia, Sahul, and the Americas add multiples of that. By preference, those shelves held a disproportionate share of the late glacial and early post-glacial population, because people then as now concentrated where fresh water, game, fish, and plant foods coincide — on coasts and along rivers, at elevations the rising sea took first. By preservation, the fraction of that world that is both surviving and findable is small and unevenly distributed: a mapped plain in the North Sea, an excavated village off Israel, a census of some thousands of find spots around Europe, and, for the rest of the world's shelves, coastlines of inference. The correct summary is not that "most of prehistory is underwater" — the upland and interior record is vast and real — but that the record is missing, preferentially and permanently, exactly the environments in which much of the period's life was lived, and that no reading of the surviving record is sound that forgets it.

What that does to this hub can be stated concretely, because this article was commissioned as the epistemic coda to the hub's Tier A timeline, and its moral applies to every entry on that timeline. Every "earliest" in Tier A — earliest bread, earliest village, earliest monument, earliest seafaring — is a statement about the surviving, searched record, and the drowned shelves are the largest single reason the surviving record cannot be equated with the past. Some of those "earliests" are robust anyway: a practice invented in the uplands, or after the flooding stopped, is unaffected. Others — anything coastal, anything lowland, anything earlier than about 6000 BCE that would naturally have occurred below the modern shoreline — stand under a standing caveat that this article now makes explicit on the hub's behalf: the shelf has not been searched; absence there is not evidence; and the first instance of almost anything coastal is likelier to be under water than in a museum. The caveat does not licence speculation in the other direction. It does not make any drowned civilisation one degree more probable, and §XIV's grades stand. It marks, precisely, the boundary of what is known — which is what Tier A is for.

There is a last symmetry worth recording. The three landscapes of this article were lost to the same event that made the modern world habitable: the end of the ice age, the warming that opened the northern forests, filled the Nile's floodplain regime, and set the conditions for every farming hearth this hub treats — Nabta Playa's green Sahara, Mehrgarh's granaries, the villages of the Nile Bridge. The sea that drowned Doggerland is the other face of the meltwater that fed the early Holocene world. The peoples of the plains were not punished by a catastrophe external to their story; they lived through the founding climate event of the present age, at its sharpest edge, and their descendants — in Britain, in Southeast Asia, in the Americas — carried the story inland and uphill, into the record we can read. What the sea holds is the first draft of that story: unrecovered, largely unrecoverable, and — in the North Sea, off the Carmel coast, and in the cores of the Bering Sea — beginning, river by river and village by village, to be read.
