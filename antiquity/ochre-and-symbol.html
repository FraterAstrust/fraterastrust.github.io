I. Tier A — What This Article Is, and How to Read It

This article is Tier A — Attested. Every substantive claim in it rests on the published archaeological record: peer-reviewed excavation reports, laboratory analyses, and dated stratigraphic sequences that any reader can check against the sources listed at the end. Where scholars disagree — and on the meaning of ochre they disagree vigorously — the disagreement itself is reported, with positions attributed to the researchers who hold them. Nothing here depends on a later tradition, a revealed text, or a modern reconstruction. Those kinds of material have their own articles in this hub, under their own tier badges, and they are never blended into this one.

The subject is the deep root of everything else this archive covers. Long before there were temples, scripts, furnaces, or pharmacopoeias, there were people collecting coloured earth, grinding it to powder, mixing it with fat and gum, wearing it, marking objects with it, and — in at least one place — digging into a mountainside to get more of it. The Egyptian pigment tradition, the alchemical laboratory, and the painted tomb all stand downstream of that first decision: that a particular red rock was worth carrying home.

Three cautions govern everything that follows. First, dates are approximate and method-dependent: ages for the Middle Stone Age rest on luminescence and uranium-series techniques with error ranges of thousands of years, and this article gives those ranges where the literature supplies them. Second, presence is not purpose: a piece of ochre in a cave proves collection and transport, not what it was used for. Third, the word "symbolic" is a conclusion, not a description — it is the most contested term in this field, and Section XVII is devoted to the contest.

// A NOTE ON "BP" //

Ages in this article are given in BP ("before present," conventionally before 1950) or as "ka" (thousands of years ago), following the usage of the papers cited. For the deep record the two are effectively interchangeable at the precision available. Egyptian material in Section XVIII is dated in BCE, following Egyptological convention.

II. What Ochre Is — Iron, Earth, and Colour

Ochre is not a single mineral. As the archaeological literature uses the term, it is an informal label for a family of iron-rich rocks and earths that can be ground to a coloured powder and used as pigment. The colour comes from iron oxides and oxyhydroxides held in a matrix of clay and silica. Two minerals do most of the work:

HAEMATITE — RED

Haematite (hematite, α-Fe2O3) is the principal red pigment mineral. Earthy, fine-grained haematite is red ochre. In its coarsely crystalline, metallic form it is called specularite or specular haematite — a glittering, micaceous ore that was specifically sought at several early sites, including the Ngwenya mine discussed in Section XI.

GOETHITE — YELLOW

Goethite (α-FeOOH), a hydrated iron oxyhydroxide, gives yellow ochre its colour; the looser field term "limonite" covers mixed yellow-brown hydrated iron oxides. Yellow ochres are common and widespread, yet in assemblage after assemblage the red material is disproportionately selected — a preference examined in Sections VI and XV.

THE WIDER PALETTE

The early pigment repertoire was not red alone. Manganese oxides give black; charcoal and burnt bone give black by another route; white clays and calcite give white. At Twin Rivers in Zambia (Section IV) the collected pigments included specularite, haematite, limonite, ochreous sandstone, and manganese dioxide side by side — a palette, not a single colour.

FIRE CHANGES THE COLOUR

Heating yellow ochre drives off its water and converts goethite to haematite: yellow becomes red. The transformation begins at temperatures an ordinary hearth easily reaches. Whether Middle Stone Age people deliberately heat-treated ochre to redden it is debated site by site, but the chemistry was available to anyone who left a yellow lump at the edge of a fire — and controlled use of that chemistry is one of the behaviours the Blombos workshop (Section VII) is argued to display.

Why should coloured dirt matter so much? Because ochre is, archaeologically, unusually legible. It is inorganic, so it survives where skin, hide, wood, and fibre decay. It is visually unmistakable, so excavators notice and record it. It stains the tools it was processed with and the objects it was applied to, leaving a chain of evidence from outcrop to cave floor. And it had to be chosen: nobody trips over five kilograms of red rock inside a shelter by accident. Every ochre assemblage is therefore a record of decisions — which outcrop, which colour, how much, carried how far, ground how fine, mixed with what. The argument in this field is never really about whether those decisions happened. It is about what they meant.

A Material That Works

Ochre is also genuinely useful stuff, and any honest account has to hold that fact in view from the start. Iron oxide powder is mildly antiseptic. Mixed into plant gum it makes a better adhesive. Rubbed into hide it assists tanning and preservation. Smeared on skin it measurably blocks ultraviolet light and appears to discourage biting insects. Section VI sets out the experimental evidence for each of these functions. The point to carry forward is that "functional" and "symbolic" are not rival boxes into which each lump of ochre must be sorted: in living cultures that still use ochre daily, the same red paste is simultaneously sunscreen, cosmetic, medicine, and a statement of identity. The archaeological question is when that layered significance began — not whether the earliest users were engineers or artists.

III. How Archaeologists Know — Wear, Residue, Sourcing, Dating

Four kinds of laboratory work turn a red lump in a cave deposit into evidence. Understanding them matters, because the strength of every claim in this article depends on which of them stands behind it.

Use-Wear and Modification

A piece of ochre that has been used looks different from one that has merely been collected. Grinding produces flat, striated facets; rubbing produces smoothed, rounded surfaces; scoring and engraving produce deliberate incisions. Tammy Hodgskiss and Lyn Wadley's study of the Rose Cottage Cave assemblage — ochre spanning roughly 96,000 to 30,000 years ago — classified pieces by exactly these traces and showed that the techniques changed through time: rubbing, which transfers powder directly onto a soft surface such as skin or hide, dominates some phases, while grinding, which produces powder for mixing into paint, cosmetics, or adhesive, dominates others. The processing trace is a proxy for the use, and the use is a proxy for the intention.

The traces are read against an experimental reference collection, and that step is what makes the classification more than impressionistic. In Hodgskiss's experimental programme, geological ochre pieces were ground, scored, and rubbed under controlled conditions and the resulting wear recorded at macro- and microscopic scale: grinding against coarse or fine slabs leaves parallel striations and faceted edges; scoring leaves grooves whose frayed terminations betray repeated strokes, with microstriations inside the grooves; rubbing on soft materials leaves smoothing, edge rounding, and polish, occasionally with a metallic lustre. Only then were the archaeological pieces assessed against those signatures. At Rose Cottage, 11.2 per cent of the assemblage bore identifiable use-wear — a figure that is itself a floor, since a piece ground entirely to powder, or a crayon used up in the hand, leaves nothing behind to classify.

Residue Analysis

Ochre rarely travels alone. On stone tools from Sibudu Cave, Marlize Lombard's residue and use-trace analyses found ochre concentrated on the bases of points and segments — the parts once inserted into hafts — in close association with plant resin and gum. That distribution cannot be explained by tools resting on stained ground; it is the signature of ochre mixed into the adhesive itself. Residue work of this kind is what converted "ochre as glue ingredient" from a plausible guess into a demonstrated practice (Section VI).

The Sibudu case rests on counting, not on impression. In Lombard's 2007 extension of the study, stone segments from Sibudu and a second KwaZulu-Natal site were examined microscopically and every ochre and resin occurrence plotted: on 53 tools she documented 502 ochre occurrences and 585 resin occurrences, with about 80 per cent of the ochre and 87 per cent of the resin falling on the backed portions — the parts once seated in the haft. The comparison set matters as much as the counts: replicated tools hafted with ochre-loaded adhesives, blind-tested experimental pieces, and tools used to process plant and animal products supplied the reference patterns against which the archaeological distributions were judged, and ochre found in engraved grooves on top of a resin deposit could be recognised, by its position, as later handling contamination rather than recipe. A chemical frontier has since opened behind the microscopy: gas chromatography–mass spectrometry has identified Podocarpus resin in a hafting adhesive from Diepkloof and conifer resin on a segment from Sibudu — naming the glue's ingredients where microscopy could only map them.

Geochemical Sourcing

Ochre from different outcrops carries subtly different trace-element chemistry — a fingerprint. Neutron activation analysis, X-ray fluorescence, and related techniques can match an artefact to its geological source, sometimes over distances of a hundred kilometres. The landmark application is Brandi MacDonald and colleagues' 2024 study of Stone Age Eswatini, which fingerprinted ochres from ten archaeological sites against nine geological source localities and traced material from the Ngwenya mine across the region (Section XII). Sourcing turns "they used red pigment" into "they went there for it, generation after generation, even when nearer material would have served" — and that is a claim about tradition, not just technology.

The Eswatini workflow shows what a fingerprint costs in practice. Artefacts and source samples were first screened by portable X-ray fluorescence, chiefly to flag manganese-rich material — above about 5 per cent manganese — which distorts bulk analysis. Characterisation then ran on two complementary tracks: neutron activation analysis, applied to 388 artefacts and source samples of suitable mass and low manganese content; and laser-ablation inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry, reserved for pieces too small, too manganese-rich, or bearing use-wear that destructive sampling would erase. X-ray diffraction and Raman spectroscopy supplied the mineralogy. The grouping is statistical — principal-component analysis of the trace-element data — and it is governed by the provenance postulate: a source can only be claimed where the chemical variation between sources exceeds the variation within each source. Where sources overlap, the honest result is a shortlist, not an address; a sourcing claim is only ever as strong as the geological sampling behind it.

Ochre itself cannot be radiocarbon dated; it contains no carbon. Its age comes from its context. The methods that built the chronology in this article are:

And a date is only as good as the association between the dated material and the artefact: the long-running care taken at Blombos to confirm, by independent methods, that the engraved ochres really sat in layers of the age claimed is a model of how the field handles its most important finds.

IV. The Deep Record — Kapthurin and Twin Rivers

The ochre story does not begin with anatomically modern humans, and it does not begin at 100,000 years ago. Its earliest chapters are older, sparser, and harder to interpret — but they establish that pigment collection is a behaviour with roots in the Middle Pleistocene, practised before our species had finished becoming itself.

KAPTHURIN FORMATION, KENYA — >285,000 YEARS

At site GnJh-15 in the Kapthurin Formation, west of Lake Baringo, Sally McBrearty's excavations recovered more than seventy pieces of red ochre with a combined weight of over five kilograms, from deposits dated to more than 285,000 years ago. The same formation produced early blades and points of comparable age. Five kilograms is not an accidental inclusion: it is a collected stock of pigment, assembled by hominins of the late Middle Pleistocene — the population among which, on current evidence, Homo sapiens was emerging.

TWIN RIVERS, ZAMBIA — c. 270,000–170,000 YEARS

Lawrence Barham's re-excavation and analysis of Twin Rivers, near Lusaka, documented at least 306 pieces of pigment — specularite, haematite, limonite, ochreous sandstone, and manganese dioxide — associated with the Lupemban industry and bracketed between roughly 270,000 and 170,000 years ago. A small proportion of pieces show grinding or rubbing. Barham estimated that the 1950s excavations alone had removed on the order of 60 kilograms of colouring material. Notably, the assemblage includes glittering specularite — material whose most obvious property is that it sparkles.

What were these earliest pigment collectors doing? The honest answer is that the record cannot yet say. Neither Kapthurin nor Twin Rivers preserves engraved pieces, beads, or burials that would anchor a symbolic reading; what they preserve is selection and accumulation — coloured material, including visually striking material, gathered in quantities beyond any single act of use. Barham drew the implication carefully: if the Twin Rivers pigments and the backed tools of the Lupemban do belong to a symbolic system, then symbolism is not the exclusive property of anatomically modern humans, since the Lupemban is plausibly associated with archaic populations related to the Kabwe (Homo heidelbergensis / rhodesiensis) lineage. That conditional — if — is where the matter still rests.

Nor was pigment use confined to Africa or to our own lineage. Hematite at Maastricht-Belvédère in the Netherlands has been reported from contexts around 200,000–250,000 years old, associated with Neanderthal ancestors, and manganese oxides at Pech-de-l'Azé in France, around 50,000 years old, bear wear consistent with rubbing on a soft material — probably skin. The Neanderthal pigment record is thinner and later than the African one, but it is real, and it warns against treating ochre as a badge that only one species could earn.

// READING THE DEEP RECORD HONESTLY //

Early pigment finds are vulnerable to two opposite misreadings. The first inflates them: every red lump becomes "art," every accumulation a "ritual." The second deflates them: because a function can be imagined, no significance is allowed. The literature's own position is narrower and stronger than either. Collection, transport, colour selection, and processing are demonstrated for the Middle Pleistocene record. Meaning is inferred, with confidence growing as the associated evidence — engraving, ornament, burial, sourcing over distance — accumulates in the later record described below.

V. Pinnacle Point, c. 164,000 BP — Pigment at the Shore

Pinnacle Point is a headland of caves on South Africa's southern coast, near Mossel Bay, overlooking the Indian Ocean. In 2007 Curtis Marean and an international team reported, in Nature, the results from cave PP13B: a small occupation dated to about 164,000 years ago (with an uncertainty of roughly ±12,000 years), from a glacial interval — Marine Isotope Stage 6 — when much of Africa was cold, dry, and difficult, and the coast offered a dependable larder.

Three finds arrived together, and their conjunction is the point. First, the earliest secure evidence then known for systematic exploitation of marine foods: shellfish remains, dominated by brown mussels, together with a whale barnacle suggesting scavenging from beached whales — pushing coastal foraging back some 40,000 years beyond previous evidence. Second, small bladelet stone tools, a technology previously thought far younger. Third, pigment: fifty-seven pieces of haematite, several with flattened, grooved facets showing they had been scraped or ground to produce red powder.

Marean and colleagues interpreted the pigment as evidence of symbolic behaviour — the inhabitants marking bodies or objects in ways that communicated socially. That interpretation was, and remains, an interpretation: Alison Brooks and others noted at the time that pigment at a living site could equally have served in adhesive manufacture, and no engraved or ornamented object was found at PP13B to settle the question. What Pinnacle Point established beyond dispute was the antiquity and the company pigment kept: by 164,000 years ago, in a population sheltering through a glacial bottleneck on the Cape coast, red ochre was being worked alongside the coastal diet and bladelet technology that would characterise later Middle Stone Age life. The symbolic toolkit, whatever its first use, was assembled early — in Africa, not in Ice Age Europe.

// WHY THE DATE MATTERED //

Before Pinnacle Point, the standard model held that recognisably "modern" behaviour — symbolism included — appeared late, perhaps 50,000–40,000 years ago, and possibly first in Europe. A securely dated pigment assemblage at 164 ka did not by itself refute that model, but it moved the burden of proof. Every subsequent early find in this article — Blombos, Diepkloof, Ngwenya — has been excavated in the space Pinnacle Point opened.

VI. The Functional Repertoire — Hide, Glue, Sun, and Skin

Before turning to the finds most often called symbolic, the functional evidence deserves its full weight. Experimental archaeology — actually making and using ochre preparations with period-appropriate materials — has tested each proposed use. Several pass the test decisively. A material can be useful and meaningful; but the uses must be known before the meanings can be argued.

Adhesive — The Demonstrated Case

Lyn Wadley's replication studies, begun in 2005 and extended with Tom Hodgskiss and Michael Grant in 2009, addressed a concrete observation: Middle Stone Age tools from Rose Cottage and Sibudu Caves carry red ochre on their hafted portions. Wadley's team made adhesives as Middle Stone Age artisans could have — plant gum or resin, with and without ochre loading — and hafted replica tools with them. Unloaded gum is brittle and hard to work; ochre-loaded gum is less brittle, resists re-dissolving in damp conditions, and can be tuned: a brittle recipe lets a projectile point detach and stay in the prey, a robust recipe keeps a spearhead seated. The 2009 study pressed the point further, arguing that compound adhesive manufacture — sourcing, combining, heat-managing materials whose properties change with acidity, drying, and working — is itself evidence of complex, multi-stage cognition, whether or not any symbolism is invoked. Lombard's residue work at Sibudu (Section III) supplies the archaeological half of the argument: traces of compound adhesives, ochre included, occur on tools from around 70,000 years ago.

Hide Processing

Ethnographic and experimental work supports ochre as a hide-working agent: iron oxides assist in preserving and softening skins and may inhibit bacterial decay. Rubbing traces on ochre pieces — powder transferred directly onto a soft substrate — are consistent with application to hide as well as to skin, and the Rose Cottage sequence shows rubbing as a persistent, deliberate technique across tens of thousands of years. A hunter-gatherer group that dressed hides with ochre would leave exactly the kind of assemblage many Middle Stone Age sites contain, without any recourse to symbolism at all. That is precisely why ochre alone, in bulk, is weak evidence for symbolism — and why the engraved, perforated, and sourced finds in the following sections carry the argumentative weight.

Sun and Insect Protection — The Tested Case

Riaan Rifkin, Francesco d'Errico, and colleagues tested the sunscreen hypothesis directly. Ochre samples — including preparations made by Ovahimba women in Namibia's Kunene Region, whose red otjize paste of ochre and butterfat remains in daily use — were assessed for sun protection factor, first in vitro and then, in a 2015 PLoS ONE study, in vivo on human skin. Red ochres provided measurable ultraviolet protection, with in vitro SPF values ranging from about 2 to about 13 depending on the sample, the reddest ochres performing best; the authors argue that protection on skin, in a fat-based preparation, would exceed the powder-only laboratory figures. Rifkin's related experimental work also found ochre effective as an insect repellent — an effect Ovahimba consultants themselves reported for protection against mosquito bites. For lightly pigmented or sun-exposed foragers moving through the high-UV environments of Pleistocene Africa — and later, for populations dispersing into new latitudes and altitudes — a habitual red coating would have been a genuine adaptive technology.

Medicine and Preservation

Iron oxides are astringent and mildly antibacterial, and ochre preparations appear in documented traditional pharmacopoeias as wound dressings and skin treatments. Claims of this kind for the Middle Stone Age are necessarily indirect — no residue study can yet diagnose a poultice — but the Egyptian endpoint of this article is instructive: the Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BCE) prescribes ochre clays among its remedies (Section XVIII), showing that by the time written records begin, ochre's medical use was already established practice rather than innovation. Food preservation and insect deterrence in stored goods have also been proposed and have ethnographic parallels, though the archaeological evidence is thinner.

// THE HIMBA MIRROR //

The Ovahimba of northern Namibia prepare otjize by grinding red ochre between stones, mixing the powder with clarified butterfat (and, in some preparations, aromatic resin), and applying it to skin and hair. Documented functions of the single preparation include sun protection, insect repellence, skin cleansing and care, hair treatment — and beauty, identity, and the marking of life-stage and status. Ethnography cannot prove what a Middle Stone Age preparation meant. It can and does prove that "it protected their skin" and "it said who they were" are not alternatives. In the one living tradition where the full context can be observed, both are true of the same red paste, applied in the same act.

VII. Blombos Cave I — The 100,000-Year-Old Ochre Workshop

Blombos Cave sits in a cliff above the Indian Ocean near Still Bay, on South Africa's southern Cape coast, some 300 kilometres east of Cape Town. Excavated by Christopher Henshilwood and colleagues since the 1990s, it has produced the densest cluster of early symbolic-culture evidence anywhere in the world. Its oldest headline find is not an artwork. It is a workshop.

In 2008 excavators recovered, in a layer dated by optically stimulated luminescence to about 100,000 years ago, two complete toolkits preserved where they had been left. Each centred on an abalone shell — Haliotis midae — used as a container. Inside and around the shells lay the components of a production process: ochre, crushed bone (some of it heated, plausibly to extract marrow fat as a binder), charcoal, quartzite grindstones and hammerstones, and a bone implement suited to stirring and transferring the mixture. Residue on one shell's rim and the bright red deposit preserved inside it showed what the kit had made: a liquefied, ochre-rich compound — in plain terms, paint — produced in the shell and stored in it, one shell found with a stone still covering its opening.

"The conceptual ability to source, combine, and store substances that enhance technology or social practices represents a benchmark in the evolution of complex human cognition."
— Henshilwood, d'Errico, van Niekerk et al., "A 100,000-Year-Old Ochre-Processing Workshop at Blombos Cave, South Africa," Science 334 (2011)

The paper's argument, quoted above, is deliberately cognitive rather than artistic. To make the Blombos compound, someone had to obtain ochre (the nearest known sources lie at a remove from the cave), grind it fine, heat bone and crush it, produce charcoal, combine the ingredients in workable proportions, and store the result in a container adapted for the purpose — its respiratory holes apparently plugged so it would not leak. That is a chaîne opératoire, a planned production sequence held in the mind before it was performed with the hands, and it predates the previous oldest known pigment workshop by some 40,000 years.

What was the paint for? The authors state the position exactly: the application of the mixture is unknown, with decoration and skin protection the leading possibilities, and the absence of resin or wax in the recipe telling against an adhesive use for this particular compound. A workshop that manufactures a coloured coating, in quantity, in a container made to keep it, is not yet proof of symbolism. It is proof of something arguably prior to it: a material culture in which coloured surfaces were worth organised, repeated, knowledgeable labour. The two finds from Blombos that follow — engraved ochre and shell beads — come from the same cave, tens of thousands of years later, and they carry the argument the workshop begins.

VIII. Blombos Cave II — Engraved Ochre, c. 77,000–75,000 BP

In 2002 Henshilwood, d'Errico, and colleagues reported in Science two pieces of red ochre from the Middle Stone Age layers of Blombos that had been deliberately engraved. Each piece had first been ground to produce a flat surface; onto that prepared surface a geometric design — cross-hatching bounded by parallel lines — had been incised with a stone point. Thermoluminescence dating of burnt lithics from the containing layers gave a mean age of about 77,000 years, and an OSL age of about 70,000 years on the overlying dune confirmed that the deposits had not been disturbed from above.

The significance lies in the combination of traits. The surfaces were prepared; the marks are patterned, not random scoring; the same cross-hatched motif recurs on both pieces; and the marks serve no conceivable role in producing powder — grinding a lump does not require drawing on it first. An expanded study (Henshilwood, d'Errico, and Ian Watts, Journal of Human Evolution, 2009) described thirteen further engraved pieces from Blombos, some from levels around 100,000 years old, with designs including cross-hatching, fan-like and wavy line arrangements. Reviewing the enlarged sample, archaeologists who had been publicly sceptical of the original two pieces — Paul Pettitt among them — were reported as considering the doubt removed: the engraving was intentional and patterned, not an accident of processing.

What the designs mean is unrecoverable, and the literature does not pretend otherwise. Henshilwood's claim is the measured one: the plaques are abstract representations — external marks, made to a shared pattern, on a durable medium — and as such they are evidence that the cognitive equipment for symbol use was present and exercised in Africa at least 35,000 years before the Upper Palaeolithic of Europe. Whether a given plaque was a tally, a ownership mark, a teaching piece, a decoration, or something for which we have no category, it was a sign before it was anything else. That is the threshold this article's title names.

// BLOMBOS IN ONE SEQUENCE //

The cave's Middle Stone Age sequence, read as a whole, is the strongest single-site argument for gradual assembly rather than sudden invention: a paint-production workshop at c. 100 ka; engraved ochre pieces spanning c. 100–75 ka; a cross-hatched drawing on a silcrete flake at c. 73 ka; finely made bifacial points and formal bone tools; shellfish exploitation and fishing; and, at c. 75 ka, the shell beads of the next section. No single find carries the case. The stratigraphy does.

The Silcrete Drawing, c. 73,000 BP

The Blombos sequence has one more first. From levels dated to about 73,000 years ago comes a flake of silcrete bearing nine red lines drawn with an ochre crayon — a cross-hatched design laid on the flake's surface (Henshilwood and colleagues, Nature, 2018). It is the oldest known drawing: not pigment ground for some further use, and not an engraving cut into a plaque, but pigment applied to a surface as a design, and as nothing else.

The distinction matters to this article's argument. The workshop of §VII shows ochre made; the plaques of this section show ochre marked; the flake shows the full chain the hub's timeline names for it — pigment prepared, a point shaped, lines planned on a surface — present at Blombos tens of millennia before the painted caves of Europe.

IX. Beads — Perforated Shells and Worn Meaning

If engraved ochre is a sign on a surface, a bead is a sign on a body — and personal ornaments are the least ambiguous symbolic artefacts in the early record, because a perforated shell has no plausible function except to be strung, worn, and seen.

From the M1 phase of Blombos Cave, dated by OSL to 75.6 ± 3.4 thousand years ago (with a thermoluminescence mean of 77 ± 6 ka for the same phase), Henshilwood, d'Errico, Marian Vanhaeren, and colleagues reported forty-one perforated shells of the small estuarine gastropod Nassarius kraussianus, the tick shell. The case that they are beads rests on a chain of exclusions and observations published in Science (2004) and in full in the Journal of Human Evolution (2005):

SELECTION

The shells are all adults of a species living in estuaries some 20 kilometres from the cave. They are too small to be food refuse, cannot derive from the cave walls, and their age profile rules out accidental arrival in seaweed gathered for bedding — natural death assemblages contain all age classes; the Blombos shells do not.

PERFORATION

Eighty-eight per cent are pierced dorsally near the lip — a perforation type absent in living populations and rare in natural assemblages, but readily reproduced experimentally by piercing through the aperture with a sharp bone point.

USE-WEAR

Microscopic facets flatten the outer lip and polish the perforation edges in a pattern absent on natural shells and consistent with friction against thread, skin, and neighbouring beads — the wear of objects strung and worn.

GROUPING

The beads were recovered in clusters of up to seventeen, each cluster sharing shade, wear pattern, and perforation size — consistent with single beadwork items, lost or discarded together, rather than with shells accumulated one by one.

Blombos is not alone. Perforated Nassarius shells from Taforalt (Grotte des Pigeons) in Morocco have been dated to about 82,000 years ago (Bouzouggar, Vanhaeren, d'Errico, and colleagues, PNAS, 2007), and shell beads from Skhul and Qafzeh in the Levant have been placed between roughly 100,000 and 135,000 years ago (Vanhaeren and colleagues, Science, 2006) — ornaments made by the early Homo sapiens populations who first moved out of Africa into the Levant. Ostrich eggshell beads join the record later, by around 50,000 years ago in East Africa. The pattern across all of these is the one d'Errico and Vanhaeren emphasise: bead-making appears in widely separated places, in the same broad period, using locally available shells — the signature of a shared behavioural capacity expressed through regional traditions, not of a single invention diffusing from one cave.

A bead, it should be said plainly, communicates. It is visible at conversational distance; it is durable beyond a single wearing; its materials, colours, and arrangement can be standardised or varied. Henshilwood described the Blombos beads as evidence for the storage of information outside the human brain — meaning carried by an object, legible to others, surviving its maker. Whatever else was true of the people who wore them, that much is a description of the artefact, not a speculation about it.

X. Diepkloof — An Engraving Tradition on Ostrich Eggshell, c. 60,000 BP

Blombos shows that individuals engraved. Diepkloof Rock Shelter shows that a community did, repeatedly, to a shared standard, for thousands of years. That difference — from act to tradition — is why Pierre-Jean Texier and colleagues' 2010 report in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences is one of the load-bearing papers of this field.

Diepkloof is a large quartzitic sandstone shelter in South Africa's Western Cape, overlooking the Verlorenvlei River about 17 kilometres from the present Atlantic shore. Its Howiesons Poort layers — dated, by thermoluminescence and OSL in agreement, to between about 55,000 and 65,000 years ago — yielded 270 fragments of engraved ostrich eggshell. The eggshells were containers: ostrich eggs, opened, emptied, and used to carry and store water, are a classic hunter-gatherer technology in arid country, and they remained in daily use at the shelter. The engraving was applied to these functional objects.

"The standardized production of repetitive patterns, including a hatched band motif, suggests a system of symbolic representation in which collective identities and individual expressions are clearly communicated."
— Texier, Porraz, Parkington et al., "A Howiesons Poort Tradition of Engraving Ostrich Eggshell Containers Dated to 60,000 Years Ago at Diepkloof Rock Shelter, South Africa," Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 107 (2010)

The assemblage resolves into four repetitive linear motifs: a hatched band (two long parallel lines crossed at roughly right angles by shorter, regularly spaced strokes), parallel to subparallel lines, intersecting lines, and cross-hatching. The hatched band was made to a fixed procedure — frame lines first, hatching second — yet its dimensions vary from piece to piece: band widths of 4 to 20 millimetres, hatch spacing of 1 to 5 millimetres, intersection angles of 90 to 140 degrees. The excavators' reading of that combination is the heart of the paper: a shared rule, individually executed. The motifs also change through the sequence — the hatched band characterising earlier units, deeply engraved parallel lines later ones — which is what traditions do: they persist by being transmitted, and they drift as they are transmitted. The engraved fragments occur across eighteen stratigraphic units, so the practice endured for several thousand years.

Note what Diepkloof adds to Blombos. The medium is perishable-adjacent — eggshell survives where wood, fibre, and skin do not, and its survival here is a reminder of how much early marking must simply be lost. The marked objects are equipment, handled daily, carried between camps: graphic convention embedded in ordinary life rather than reserved for special surfaces. And the sample is large enough — 270 pieces — to speak of pattern and variation statistically, ending the era in which early symbolism rested on one or two exceptional objects that a sceptic could set aside as anomalies.

XI. Mining as Deliberate Extraction — Ngwenya and the First Quarries

Everything described so far could, in principle, have been supplied by picking up surface rock. Mining is different in kind. A mine is a place where people returned to a known deposit and removed material from the bedrock itself, with tools made for the purpose, at a scale beyond a single visit's need. It is extraction organised by knowledge — of geology, of place, and of a future in which the pigment would be wanted again. The earliest known example on Earth was dug for ochre.

Lion Cavern, Ngwenya, Eswatini

On Bomvu Ridge — bomvu means "red" in siSwati — on the slopes of Mount Ngwenya in north-western Eswatini, near the border with South Africa, lies a cavern that is not a cavern. The Lion Cavern is an artificial working: a chamber cut into a high-grade haematite deposit (iron content locally 50–60 per cent or more) by miners who extracted red haematite and glittering specularite and left the void behind. The site was recognised as a mine during the modern iron-ore workings of the 1960s, and excavated by Raymond Dart, Peter Beaumont, and colleagues, who recovered specialised mining tools — choppers, picks, and hammerstones of dolerite, a hard stone foreign to the deposit and brought in for the work.

The dating requires the care flagged in Section III. Charcoal from the workings returned radiocarbon ages of about 41,000 to 43,000 years — at the effective limit of the method — and the Eswatini submission to UNESCO's Tentative List (2008) accordingly describes deposits worked "at least 42,000 years BP" for red haematite and specularite, with working possibly continuing, in later phases, until about 23,000 BP. On that basis Ngwenya is routinely cited as the world's oldest known mine. Two qualifications belong in any Tier A account: the radiocarbon ages are minimum ages near the method's ceiling, so the workings may be older than the conventional figure; and "oldest mine" means oldest known and dated — a statement about the surviving record, not about all mining that ever occurred. Neither qualification diminishes the find. Forty-odd thousand years ago, people were quarrying underground for cosmetic and ritual pigment — the siSwati names preserve the uses into the historical period: libovu, red ochre, and ludumane, the sparkling ochre — long before anyone on Earth mined metal to smelt.

WHAT WAS MINED

Not iron for tools — smelting lay tens of thousands of years in the future. The Middle Stone Age workings targeted pigment: red haematite for powder, and specularite, whose flakes glitter, for a sparkling cosmetic. Iron smelting at Ngwenya belongs to a separate, far later phase, from about 400 CE, when Bantu-speaking agro-pastoralists worked the same mountain for metal. The mountain's biography thus runs pigment first, metal second — the argument of this whole article in one hillside.

THE MODERN MINE AND THE LOSS

Industrial open-cast mining operated at Ngwenya from 1964 to 1977 (with a later revival ending in 2014), cutting the great pits visible today and both threatening and exposing the ancient workings. The Lion Cavern survives within the Malolotja Nature Reserve; the site's visitor centre burned in 2018. The Tentative List submission remains the formal international recognition of the site's standing.

Quarrying Beyond Ngwenya

Ngwenya is the oldest underground working, but deliberate procurement at source is much older and much wider. The five kilograms at Kapthurin and the tens of kilograms at Twin Rivers (Section IV) imply organised collection at outcrops, whether or not the outcrops were formally quarried. At Blombos, ochre had to be brought to the cave from sources at a distance. Sourcing studies (Section XII) now show Middle and Later Stone Age communities in Eswatini transporting preferred ochres up to about 100 kilometres. Procurement of pigment was a scheduled activity embedded in movement across a landscape — people planned journeys that included the red mountain, the estuary with the right shells, the outcrop with the right stone. Mining, in that light, is not an anomaly. It is the most concentrated expression of a procurement system already hundreds of thousands of years old: the landscape known, named, and revisited for the materials that made bodies and objects mean something.

XII. Communities of Practice — Ochre Knowledge Held in Common

The newest work in this field asks a social question of the chemistry: not just where did this ochre come from, but whose rules did its users follow? The key study is Brandi MacDonald and colleagues' "Ochre Communities of Practice in Stone Age Eswatini" (Nature Communications, 2024), which applied neutron activation analysis and related methods to ochre assemblages from ten archaeological sites and raw materials from nine geological sources across the small kingdom — the same landscape that holds Ngwenya.

The expectation going in was simple: everyone used Ngwenya, the famous, high-quality source. The fingerprints said otherwise. Communities used diverse local ochres; neighbouring sites of the same period could hold quite different pigment assemblages; and Ngwenya material, while genuinely transported — in rare cases up to about 100 kilometres — was one thread in a regional fabric rather than its whole cloth. The authors' interpretation is the finding: long-term, region-wide stability in how ochre was provisioned and processed, combined with persistent site-specific choices, is the signature of communities of practice — groups bound by shared knowledge of where materials occur, how to judge them, how to mine and prepare them, and what they are for, transmitted by teaching and refreshed by contact, yet locally distinct. Ochre choice, in other words, tracked identity and tradition, not merely geology and convenience.

Eswatini is not the only place the pattern shows. At Porc-Epic Cave in Ethiopia, Daniela Rosso and colleagues analysed the largest East African Middle Stone Age ochre assemblage — some 40 kilograms, spanning at least 4,500 years — and found continuity in acquisition, processing, and use across the whole span, with a gradual drift in preferred grinding techniques: the same practice, held in common, slowly changing — cultural transmission and cultural drift in a single assemblage. Read together, these studies shift the field's centre of gravity. The question is no longer only "when did symbolism begin?" It is "how was pigment knowledge organised socially — taught, standardised, varied, and carried across generations?" That is a question about institutions, and ochre turns out to be one of the oldest institutions we can see.

XIII. The Record Widens I — The Swabian Figurines, c. 40,000 BP

When anatomically modern humans reached the Danube corridor around 43,000 years ago, they brought the African inheritance with them — and in the limestone caves of the Swabian Jura, in present-day south-western Germany, it surfaces in a new medium: mammoth ivory, carved with stone tools. The six caves of the Lone and Ach valleys, inscribed by UNESCO in 2017 as the "Caves and Ice Age Art in the Swabian Jura," hold the oldest known figurative sculpture in the world.

THE LION MAN — HOHLENSTEIN-STADEL

Excavated in fragments on the last day of digging in August 1939 — the pieces found by geologist Otto Völzing on 25 August — and fully restored only in 2012–13, the Lion Man stands 31.1 centimetres tall: a human body, upright, with the head and forelimbs of a cave lion, carved from mammoth ivory with a flint knife; seven parallel transverse gouges mark its left arm. Its biography is a history of patient reconstruction. More than 200 fragments were first assembled in the 1970s, corrected in the 1980s, and comprehensively restored from 1987; then re-excavation of the cave between 2008 and 2013 sifted even the back-dirt of the 1939 dig and recovered 575 further ivory fragments, some of them worked and probably belonging to the figure. The 2012–13 restoration took the figure apart, discarded the artificial additions of the earlier rebuild, and reassembled it taller — 311 millimetres against the previous 296 — resolving the figure as male. Its layer, the cave's lowest Aurignacian horizon, has been radiocarbon dated to 39,000–41,000 cal BP. Experimental replication suggests carving it with stone tools took on the order of 400 hours. Nothing in the natural world looks like this figure. It depicts a being that does not exist — which is to say, it depicts an idea, held in common by maker and audience, in the most durable medium available.

THE VENUS OF HOHLE FELS

Found in 2008 in Hohle Fels cave and reported by Nicholas Conard in Nature (2009), this six-centimetre mammoth-ivory figure of a woman, dated to between about 40,000 and 35,000 years ago, is the oldest undisputed depiction of a human being. A suspension loop replaces the head: it was made to be worn. The same caves have produced animal figurines in numbers — mammoths, horses, lions, birds — and flutes of bird bone and ivory dated to around 42,000–40,000 years ago, the oldest known musical instruments.

The Swabian material matters to this article for its place in the sequence. Figurative sculpture does not appear at 40,000 BP from nothing. It appears at the end of a hundred thousand years of documented preparation: pigment compounded and stored, surfaces engraved to shared motifs, shells standardised into ornaments, pigment mined from bedrock. The Lion Man's makers were doing something new — representing an imagined being in three dimensions — with a symbolic capacity whose African development occupies the first half of this article. The old model of a European "creative explosion" has not survived the African chronology; what happened in the Swabian caves was a florescence, on new ground, of a very old plant.

XIV. The Record Widens II — Sulawesi and Chauvet

The same correction has overtaken cave painting. For most of the twentieth century the painted caves of France and Spain were treated as the beginning of art. Uranium-series dating of the calcite crusts that grow over paintings — which gives a minimum age for the art beneath — has moved the beginning east, and back.

Sulawesi, Indonesia

In the Maros-Pangkep limestone karst of Sulawesi, Maxime Aubert, Adam Brumm, and colleagues showed in 2014 that a hand stencil was at least about 39,900 years old and a painting of a babirusa (a wild pig-deer) at least about 35,400 years old — as old as anything in Europe. The dates have since deepened: a hunting scene with part-human, part-animal figures at Leang Bulu' Sipong 4, at least about 43,900 years old (2019); a warty pig at Leang Tedongnge, at least about 45,500 years old; and in 2024, Adhi Agus Oktaviana and colleagues reported in Nature a panel at Leang Karampuang — three human-like figures interacting with a pig — dated by laser-ablation uranium-series imaging to at least 51,200 years ago, currently the oldest known representational painting and the oldest known narrative scene. Figurative art of comparable age is also known from Borneo. The painters of Sulawesi were descendants of the populations that left Africa and crossed Wallacea toward Sahul; they carried image-making with them, in red and mulberry pigments, onto cave walls at the far side of the world.

Chauvet, France

Chauvet Cave, in the Ardèche gorge, was discovered in December 1994 and sealed by an ancient rockfall that preserved it entire. Its earliest paintings — lions, rhinoceroses, mammoths, bears, rendered with shading, wall preparation, and overlapping compositions of striking sophistication — are dated by radiocarbon on their charcoal to roughly 36,000 years ago, with a second phase of activity several thousand years later. Chauvet remains a summit of Palaeolithic painting. But it is a summit at 36,000 years, on a road that runs back through the Swabian ivories at 40,000, the Sulawesi panels at 51,200, the Diepkloof eggshells at 60,000, the Blombos beads at 75,000, the Blombos workshop at 100,000, and the Pinnacle Point pigments at 164,000. The road begins in Africa, and its first milestones are made of ochre. The two caves that made cave painting famous, however, stand further along that road, in its later, Magdalenian stretch — and they are the subject of the next section.

// A DISCIPLINE OF MINIMUM AGES //

Uranium-series ages for cave art are minimum ages: the crust dated grew over the painting, so the painting is at least that old, and may be older. The Sulawesi figures in this section are therefore floors, not ceilings. As dating methods improve, the floors have consistently risen — a pattern worth remembering whenever a headline announces the "oldest" anything.

XV. The Record Widens III — Altamira and Lascaux

Altamira and Lascaux are the two names by which the wider world knows Palaeolithic cave painting, and they earn a section of their own for a reason the chronology alone would not predict. Neither is the oldest painted cave — Chauvet precedes both by some twenty thousand years, and Sulawesi by more. What they are is the fullest surviving expression of the Magdalenian image-world: painting practised at monumental scale, in organised chambers, with a mature polychrome technique, by communities who returned to the same walls across generations. They are also the two caves whose modern histories — disbelief in one case, destruction by admiration in the other — decided how much of the evidence would survive to be argued about.

Altamira — The Cave That Was Not Believed

The cave, near Santillana del Mar in Cantabria, northern Spain, was reported in 1868 by the hunter Modesto Cubillas. The landowner, Marcelino Sanz de Sautuola — a jurist and amateur archaeologist who had seen Palaeolithic objects at the Paris Universal Exhibition of 1878 — began excavating its floor in 1879. The paintings were found by his daughter. María Sanz de Sautuola, eight years old and small enough to stand upright beneath a low side-chamber ceiling her father had to stoop under, looked up and saw the bison. Sautuola, with the University of Madrid archaeologist Juan Vilanova y Piera, published the cave in 1880 as Palaeolithic in date — the first claim ever made for prehistoric cave painting.

The claim was rejected, and the rejection was brutal. At the 1880 Prehistorical Congress in Lisbon the findings were ridiculed; the French specialists, led by Gabriel de Mortillet and Émile Cartailhac, refused them; and Sautuola was accused of forgery — of having had the paintings produced by a contemporary artist on his own orders. The arguments were those of the period's expectations: people of the Stone Age were held incapable of such art, the paintings were too accomplished and too fresh, and there were no soot marks from ancient lamps on the ceilings — a point Sautuola himself later answered with marrow-fat lamps, which burn with little soot. What ended the matter was accumulation: through the following two decades, further painted and engraved caves were recognised in France, in contexts no forgery could explain, and the antiquity of cave art became undeniable. In 1902 Cartailhac published his recantation in L'Anthropologie under a title that has become famous in its own right: «Mea culpa» d'un sceptique — the mea culpa of a sceptic. Sautuola had died in 1888, fourteen years before the vindication. Altamira's discoverer never saw his cave believed.

Altamira — The Polychrome Ceiling and Twenty Thousand Years of Painting

The cave runs about 270 metres, but its fame rests on one low chamber near the entrance, its ceiling — some 18 by 9 metres — crowded with animals. The polychrome group comprises some twenty-five large figures, 125 to 170 centimetres long: a herd of steppe bison (Bison priscus) standing, curled, and fallen; two horses; a hind over two metres long; and possibly a wild boar. The procedure can still be read on the rock: outlines were engraved and drawn, the engraving deepened where detail was wanted, bodies filled with red, yellow, and brown ochre, and black — charcoal and manganese — laid in for shading and contour. The painters worked the ceiling's natural bulges into the animals, so that a boss of rock becomes a bison's shoulder or belly: the image is partly found in the stone, partly imposed on it. Occupation debris at the cave mouth belongs chiefly to the Upper Solutrean (c. 18,500 BP) and Lower Magdalenian (c. 16,590–14,000 BP), and the great ceiling is Magdalenian work; a rockfall sealed the entrance about 13,000 years ago and kept the paintings in the dark until 1868.

The ceiling, however, is not the beginning of Altamira's art but its climax. Pike and colleagues' uranium-series programme (Science, 2012) dated the calcite skin over a large claviform (club-shaped) sign in the polychrome chamber to at least 35,600 years old, and returned minimum ages across eleven Spanish caves — a red disk at El Castillo at least 40,800 years old among them — showing that cave painting in Cantabria began with the first modern human presence in the region, or earlier. Altamira itself was painted, revisited, and painted again across a span of more than 20,000 years. The bison ceiling is therefore a late panel in a gallery of unexpected depth: the same cave holds both some of Europe's earliest marks and its most famous herd, separated by a span longer than all of recorded history several times over.

Lascaux — The Hall of the Bulls

Lascaux, near Montignac in the Vézère valley of the Dordogne, was found on 12 September 1940 by four teenagers — Marcel Ravidat, Jacques Marsal, Georges Agnel, and Simon Coencas — who descended through a hole left by an uprooted tree (in the story as it settled, following Ravidat's dog Robot) into a cave no one had entered for millennia. Nine days later they returned with the Abbé Henri Breuil, the era's leading authority on cave art, who began the copying on which early study depended. The cave is a connected sequence of chambers — the Hall of the Bulls, the Axial Gallery, the Passageway, the Apse, the Nave, the Chamber of Felines, and the Shaft — carrying some 600 painted and drawn figures and nearly 1,500 engravings.

The Hall of the Bulls is the cave's rotunda and its manifesto: a main cavern some 20 metres wide whose pale walls carry horses, stags, and four enormous black aurochs, the largest more than five metres long — among the biggest figures in all Ice Age art — their bodies in profile but their horns turned toward the viewer, in the convention scholars call twisted perspective. Beside them stands the cave's strangest resident, a two-horned creature nicknamed the Unicorn, like nothing in the Pleistocene fauna. The palette is this article's palette at full stretch — red, yellow, and brown iron oxides with black — applied at a scale, and at heights, that suggest scaffolding, lamps, and organised work. The paintings are conventionally dated to about 17,000 BP, in the early Magdalenian, though published estimates for the cave's decoration run from about 17,000 to 22,000 years; unlike Chauvet, Lascaux was not a single sealed moment but a decorated cave of the Magdalenian florescence, its superimpositions recording more than one episode of work.

Lascaux — The Shaft Scene

At the cave's deepest and least accessible point, down the Shaft, is the image that has generated more interpretation than any other in Palaeolithic art — and the cave's only human figure. A bison stands disembowelled, a spear across its flank and its entrails spilling, head lowered toward a man who falls or lies stiffly before it: a stick figure with outstretched arms, four-fingered hands, an erect phallus, and a bird's head — or a bird mask. Below him a bird perches on an upright staff, echoing his head; a barbed sign lies nearby; and to the left a rhinoceros walks away, six black dots beneath its tail. It is one of the very few compositions in the painted caves that reads as a scene — agents, action, consequence — rather than an assemblage of animals. Readings have multiplied for eighty years: a hunting accident; a myth; a shaman in trance, the bird on its staff beside him — an interpretation Breuil's generation opened and later writers developed. A Tier A account can describe the panel exactly, note that the human figure's rarity is itself a datum — among some 600 painted figures, Lascaux's artists drew a human being once, at the bottom of a shaft, in a mask — and leave the story the panel tells where its makers left it: undecided.

Closure and the Replica Answer

Lascaux opened to the public on 14 July 1948. By 1955, some 1,200 visitors a day were breathing into a cave whose air had been still for millennia: carbon dioxide, heat, and humidity rose; algae, fungi, and lichen spread across the walls — the green sickness, followed by veils of calcite — and in 1963 the French state, under Minister of Cultural Affairs André Malraux, closed the cave to the public for good. Closure ended the crowds, not the vigilance: microbial outbreaks, including a Fusarium fungus introduced during maintenance work in 2001, have required continuing intervention, and the original is now entered only by conservation staff under strict control. Altamira travelled the same road more slowly: closed completely in 1977, reopened to strictly limited numbers in 1982, and closed to general public access in 2002 after green mould appeared on the paintings.

The conservation answer, at both caves, has been the replica — the copy that takes the footfall the original cannot. Altamira's Neocueva, opened with its museum in 2001, reproduces the entrance and the polychrome ceiling at full scale. Lascaux II (1983), built some 200 metres from the original, reproduces the Hall of the Bulls and the Axial Gallery; Lascaux III (from 2012) tours panels of the Nave and the Shaft worldwide; and Lascaux IV (opened December 2016), the International Centre for Cave Art at Montignac, reproduces the entire cave at full scale from laser scans, its paintings recreated with the originals' own materials — iron oxide, charcoal, and ochre. The facsimile has become a discipline in its own right: the paintings are now studied, and seen, largely in copies, while the originals rest in monitored darkness. It is a strange and fitting fate for the Magdalenian image-world — its survival strategy is reproduction.

Set beside Chauvet, the pair define what changed in twenty thousand years of painting — and what did not. Chauvet, at c. 36,000 BP, already commands shading, overlap, and wall preparation; its bestiary is the Aurignacian one of lions, rhinoceroses, and mammoths, drawn largely in charcoal and red, and it contains no scene and no polychrome programme. Altamira and Lascaux add the Magdalenian register: true polychromy, colour laid in graded washes over engraved drawing; animals at architectural scale, organised chamber by chamber into something closer to a programme than a gallery; walls returned to and reworked across episodes — at Altamira, across twenty millennia; a developed repertoire of abstract signs alongside the animals; and, in the Shaft, the tradition's one great narrative panel, with a human being inside the story. The capacity Chauvet proves was old. What the Magdalenian caves show is that capacity become an institution — taught, revisited, and sustained — which is the same transition this article traced in ochre itself, from collected lumps to mined pigment and communities of practice.

XVI. Early Textiles and Fibre — The Invisible Technology

Every technology in this article so far has survived because it is mineral. Ochre does not rot; stone does not rot; even eggshell outlasts its makers by sixty thousand years. Fibre is the counter-case, and it may have mattered more. Cordage binds, carries, hafts, nets, and traps; cloth warms, covers, and displays; a sewn seam turns skins into fitted clothing. A community with string can do things a community without it cannot — yet string, net, and cloth decay in almost every soil on Earth. The fibre record is therefore read differently from the ochre record: in microscopic fragments, in impressions pressed into clay, in the wear on tools, and in rare pockets of preservation. This section assembles what that indirect record shows, because without it the deep past is systematically misdescribed — its hardest, least typical materials standing for the whole.

Dzudzuana Cave — Fibres Under the Microscope

Dzudzuana Cave sits in the foothills of the Caucasus in Georgia. Excavated from 1996 under Ofer Bar-Yosef, Tengiz Meshveliani, and Anna Belfer-Cohen, it preserves Upper Palaeolithic occupations in three main units: Unit D, radiocarbon dated to 32,000–26,000 14C years BP (about 36,000–31,000 calendar years ago); Unit C, about 28,000–24,000 calendar years ago; and Unit B, about 15,500–13,000 calendar years ago. The fibres were not found by looking for them. Eliso Kvavadze was analysing clay samples for pollen — 86 samples of 50 grams each, taken from five locations in the excavated area in 2007–08 — when the microscope showed non-pollen objects among the palynomorphs: plant fibres, in quantity.

The counts deserve stating, because they are the evidence. All 27 clay samples from Unit D produced fibres — 488 in that unit alone; Unit C yielded 787 further fragments, and fibres occur throughout the sequence. Some are spun; some are twisted, one thread explicitly so; some are knotted; and a proportion are coloured — 58 dyed fibres in Unit D, mostly black-to-grey and turquoise, with a wider palette across the assemblage running through yellow, red, blue, violet, black, brown, green, and khaki. Kvavadze and colleagues (Science, 2009) identified the fibres as wild flax, from their microscopic structure — their thickness, their segmented build, and the dislocations, or nodes, along their length — and proposed the uses the excavators still cite: cords for hafting stone tools, woven baskets, and thread for sewing garments. The fibres' company supports a working area rather than stray contamination: wild goat (tur) hair, and the remains of skin beetles and moths — insects that feed on skin, fur, and cloth — suggesting that hides and textiles were being processed in the cave. On the strength of the oldest unit, the find was announced as the oldest fibres known to have been used by humans, more than 34,000 years old.

The identification carries a published dispute, and a Tier A account must carry it too. In a formal Comment in Science (2010), Christian Bergfjord and colleagues argued that the features cited — a central lumen and dislocations — establish only that the fibres are bast fibres, the fibre class drawn from plant stems and bark, which includes flax, hemp, nettle, and lime bast, because bast fibres of different species are notoriously hard to separate under the microscope; on their reading, the Dzudzuana material is proven bast, not proven flax. Kvavadze and colleagues replied in the same issue, defending the identification on the fibres' internal morphology. The dispute stands. What it does not touch is the core of the find: the fibres are ancient, they are bast, and some of them were spun, twisted, and dyed by the cave's Upper Palaeolithic occupants. Later literature has settled into citing Dzudzuana accordingly — as 30,000-year-old bast fibres, worked — and this article follows that usage.

Making Fibre — The Procedure

Because the finished objects decay, the procedure has to be reconstructed from fibre states, from the survivals described below, and from the fully documented bast-fibre craft of later periods, whose steps the Neolithic and Egyptian evidence shows already in place. For flax and its fellow bast plants the chain runs as follows. The plant is pulled, not cut — pulling preserves the fibre at full stem length, root to tip. Seed bolls are stripped by drawing the stems through a comb (rippling). Then comes the step on which everything depends: retting, the controlled rotting of the stem so that microbes digest the pectin gluing the fibre bundles to the woody core. In water retting the bundled stems are submerged, weighted down, in still or slow water for days; in dew retting they are spread in rows on grass and turned over weeks of rain and dew. Retting is judged by hand and eye — under-retted stems will not release their fibre; over-retted fibre rots with the stem — and it is the first of several points where the craft is knowledge, not labour alone.

The retted stems are dried, then broken: the brittle woody core (the shive, or boon) is crushed and snapped by beating or by a hinged brake, while the flexible fibre survives. Scutching follows — the broken stems are scraped with a blunt wooden knife against a board to knock out the shive fragments. Hackling combs the fibre through beds of upright teeth, coarse to fine, aligning the long fibres (the line) and combing out the short ones (the tow) for coarser yarn. Only then can thread be made, and here the record preserves a fork in the technology. In draft spinning, prepared fibre is drawn out continuously and twisted, classically with a weighted spindle. In splicing, thread is built from discrete fibre strips: bundles some 60 centimetres long are laid with their ends overlapping by several centimetres, and the overlap is twisted — rolled between the palms or on the thigh, wetted so that moisture and the flax's own pectin help the join hold — producing a continuous yarn whose joins can then be staggered and plied for strength. Splicing is the technique that later defines Egyptian linen, whose Predynastic beginnings the Egyptology division treats in The Predynastic Period. Twisting two or more yarns together against their spin (plying), knotting, looping, and weaving on a loom complete the repertoire the Neolithic record displays; dyeing, as the Dzudzuana colours show, was already part of it in the Palaeolithic.

The Wider Early Record

Dzudzuana is the oldest point, not the only one. At Dolní Věstonice and Pavlov in Moravia, fired and unfired clay preserves the impressions of what pressed against it around 27,000 BP: Olga Soffer, James Adovasio, and colleagues have identified multiple cordage types, textile and basketry structures, and knotted netting among the imprints — woven technology known only because clay happened to record its negative. At Ohalo II, on the shore of the Sea of Galilee, actual twisted fibres survive from about 19,300 BP — S-spun cordage given a final Z twist, plausibly a fragment of netting or fishing gear (Nadel and colleagues, 1994). A fragment of rope was even recovered at Lascaux in the 1950s, reported by the Abbé Glory — the painted cave preserving, by chance, a scrap of the perishable kit its painters also used. And the technology is not ours alone: at Abri du Maras in France, a three-ply cord fragment still adhering to a stone flake, dated to roughly 46,000–52,000 years ago, has been attributed to Neanderthals (Hardy and colleagues, 2020) — twisted fibre, like pigment, appears in more than one human lineage.

The Neolithic Horizon

With farming, fibre becomes visible at settlement scale. Flax was among the earliest cultivated plants in the Near East — cultivated linseed is attested by the eighth millennium BCE — and the dry caves of the Judean Desert preserve what villages used: at Nahal Hemar, linen yarn and fabrics made without a loom, including looping and knotted netting, carry calibrated dates around 8000 BCE, while plain-weave impressions survive in clay at Jarmo, in northern Iraq, around 7000 BCE. At Çatalhöyük in Anatolia, carbonised woven cloth 8,500–8,700 years old — among the earliest woven textiles known — was identified for decades as linen; scanning electron microscopy published in 2021 (Rast-Eicher, Karg, and Bender Jørgensen, Antiquity) showed it to be the bast of local oak trees, harvested, in all likelihood, when oaks were felled for timber. The correction is the Dzudzuana lesson repeated at the other end of the story: fibre identifications made without microscopy are provisional, and tree bast — the unglamorous fibre of bark — was a mainstay long after flax was available. By the later Neolithic the full kit is ordinary equipment across the Near East and Egypt: cordage and netting for fishing and carrying, woven cloth for clothing and for wrapping the dead, and spindle whorls and loom weights entering the durable record as the tools of a craft whose products still mostly vanish.

// THE PERISHABILITY BIAS //

Fibre survives only where decay is cheated: in the dry clay of a Caucasus cave, in waterlogging, in carbonisation, as an impression in clay, or mineralised against metal. Every "earliest" in this section is therefore a floor set jointly by preservation and by whether anyone thought to look — the Dzudzuana fibres were found because a pollen analyst examined her samples closely, in a cave excavated for other questions. The same bias governs the whole of this article's subject: the marks on wood, bark, skin, and fibre that must have carried most early graphic and practical culture are gone, and the ochre record is the durable residue of a largely perishable world. "Earliest textile" means earliest textile that survived and was recognised. It has never meant first.

That is why fibre belongs in an article about ochre. The two records are the same record read at different durabilities: colour ground from rock at one end, colour dyed into thread at the other — some of the Dzudzuana fibres were dyed black, grey, and turquoise by people who also lived in the world of Sections IV to XIV. The invisible technology carried the visible one: pigment travelled in nets and packs, tools were hafted with cord, bodies were clothed against the cold of the cave paintings' world. What archaeology can see is the stone, the shell, and the stain. What people mostly made was string.

XVII. When Does Pigment Use Become Symbolism? — The Interpretive Debate

The evidence assembled above is not, in its main lines, disputed. The interpretation is. This section sets out the principal positions as their holders state them, because the debate — conducted over four decades in the field's leading journals — is itself part of the attested record.

The "Human Revolution" and Its Critics

The older model, associated above all with Richard Klein, held that fully modern behaviour appeared abruptly around 50,000–40,000 years ago, possibly underwritten by a neurological change, and announced itself in the European Upper Palaeolithic: art, ornament, and complex technology arriving as a package. Against it, Sally McBrearty and Alison Brooks's landmark review "The Revolution That Wasn't" (Journal of Human Evolution, 2000) assembled the African evidence trait by trait and showed that nearly every element of the supposed package — blades, points, bone tools, pigment use, long-distance transport, specialised hunting — appears in the African Middle Stone Age, at different times and places, across more than 200,000 years. On their reading, modern behaviour was not switched on; it accumulated, in Africa, among populations becoming anatomically modern as they did so. The finds published since 2000 — Pinnacle Point, the Blombos workshop, Diepkloof, the Eswatini sourcing — have largely been received as extending McBrearty and Brooks's argument rather than Klein's, though the tempo and causes of the accumulation remain contested.

The Gradualist Case from Blombos

Henshilwood and Curtis Marean, in "The Origin of Modern Human Behaviour" (2003), argued from the southern African record that symbolic behaviour emerges gradually through the Middle Stone Age, becoming archaeologically visible in pulses — rich at Blombos and its neighbours around 75,000–100,000 years ago, sparser in some later intervals — plausibly tracking population size and connectedness rather than cognitive capacity. On this view, the capacity is old; its visibility is demographic. Symbols need audiences, and traditions need enough people, in contact, to keep them alive — a reading the Diepkloof tradition and the Eswatini communities of practice fit well.

The demographic mechanism behind the pulses has since been modelled formally. Adam Powell, Stephen Shennan, and Mark Thomas (Science, 2009) showed that the maintenance of cultural complexity depends on demography — on the density of subpopulations within a region and on the migration and contact between them — and that genetic estimates put subpopulation densities in early Upper Palaeolithic Europe at levels similar to those in sub-Saharan Africa when modern behaviour first appeared there. On their model, the African pulses and the later European crystallisation need no change in cognitive capacity at all: skills accumulate where enough people, in contact, keep them from being lost, and thin out where populations fragment. The model is influential but not unchallenged: Mark Collard and colleagues (2016), reviewing the ethnographic and archaeological evidence, argued that hunter-gatherer cultural complexity tracks environmental factors more strongly than population size, and urged that the demographic hypothesis be tested formally against its rivals rather than assumed. The gradualist case, in other words, now has both a mechanism and a standing objection — which is the condition of every live position in this section.

The Functionalist Caution

Lyn Wadley's work supplies the debate's most productive caution. The ochre in Sibudu's adhesives was not colouring anything for display; it was engineering. Wadley's conclusion is not that Middle Stone Age people lacked symbolism — the same sites produce other evidence — but that ochre, the field's favourite proxy, cannot testify to symbolism on its own, because its best-documented uses are practical. Her constructive proposal relocates the cognitive claim: compound technology itself, with its multi-stage planning and transformed materials, demonstrates complex cognition without needing the contested word. Any Tier A account must carry this caution at full strength — it is why this article treats engraving, perforation, standardisation, and long-distance sourcing, rather than mere ochre presence, as the load-bearing evidence.

The replication work specifies what "engineering" means here. Wadley, Hodgskiss, and Grant (2009) found that the Sibudu artisans had not merely coloured their glue red: the ochre loading deliberately transformed it — shifting its chemistry from acidic toward less acidic, managing its dehydration beside wood fires, and altering its workability — through steps the authors argue cannot be performed without multitasking and abstract thought, holding in mind properties of gum and iron oxide that cannot be directly measured. Their conclusion is deliberately double-edged. It removes this ochre from the symbolism debate only to place something stronger in its stead: a demonstration of complex cognition that does not require the contested word at all. Lombard's own framing of the Sibudu residue evidence makes the same peace from the other side — the hafting data, she states, are not offered as an alternative or replacement hypothesis for ochre's possible symbolic role. The caution, properly read, is not that the symbolic case fails. It is that it must be built from the evidence that can bear it — engraving, perforation, standardisation, sourcing — and not from the mere presence of a useful red powder.

The Red Hypothesis

A bolder reading, proposed by Chris Knight, Camilla Power, and Ian Watts in 1995 and developed in Watts's subsequent analyses of ochre assemblages, holds that red pigment was symbolic from early in its habitual use: that collective ritual, centred on the colour red — the colour of blood — was the engine of the "human symbolic revolution," and that the statistical preference for the reddest, most saturated ochres across Middle Stone Age assemblages is its material trace. The preference for red is empirically documented — it recurs at Rose Cottage, Porc-Epic, and many other sites, against the wider availability of yellow material. Whether the preference records symbolism, the superior functional properties of red ochre demonstrated in the sunscreen and adhesive experiments, or both at once, is precisely the kind of question the record may never fully close. The hypothesis is presented here as its authors state it: influential, contested, and not established.

Criteria — What Would Count?

Francesco d'Errico's work, alone and with April Nowell and others, has pressed the field to state its criteria in advance. A mark is evidence of symbol use, on the stricter accounts, when it is: deliberate (not a by-product of processing), patterned (following a convention others could share), non-functional in the narrow sense (not required by the object's use), and ideally repeated across a tradition. Measured against those criteria, the strongest early cases are the Blombos engraved ochres (deliberate, patterned, non-functional), the Diepkloof eggshells (all four, at tradition scale), and the perforated shell beads (deliberate, standardised, worn). Hodgskiss's summary of the field's present mood is widely quoted because it refuses the old fork in the road: ochre shows people using their environment with developing minds, and it bridges the divide between art and science. The current consensus, such as it is, holds that functional and symbolic uses coexisted from early on, that their proportions varied by place and period, and that by about 75,000–60,000 years ago — beads, engravings, traditions — symbolically mediated behaviour in Africa is not a hypothesis but a description.

XVIII. Continuity — From the Middle Stone Age to Predynastic Egypt

This hub exists to trace what came before Kemet — and the ochre line runs unbroken into the Nile Valley. It does not run as a single documented chain of custody; no one can follow a lump of pigment from Blombos to Naqada. It runs as continuity of practice: the same minerals, prepared the same way, for the same layered reasons, in the same broad African world, until — in Egypt — the practice acquires writing, and we can finally read what its practitioners said about it.

In Predynastic Egypt (c. 5500–3100 BCE), the grinding kit is a standard grave good: a stone palette and a pebble grinder, with the pigments still staining them. A. Lucas's classic analysis, "Cosmetics, Perfumes and Incense in Ancient Egypt" (Journal of Egyptian Archaeology, 1930), established the repertoire. The earliest eye paint was green malachite, known from Badarian and earliest Predynastic burials; black galena appears later in the Predynastic and became the principal eye paint (kohl) of Pharaonic Egypt. And on the palettes, and as stains on the grinding stones, Lucas identified the red face paint: a naturally occurring red oxide of iron — generally called haematite, he noted, but more correctly described as red ochre. It was, he observed, the only red pigment known in ancient Egypt until very late, and it served everywhere: cheeks coloured with it in life, walls and objects painted with it in the tomb workshops, the conventional red-brown skin of men in Egyptian painting rendered in it, and — by the New Kingdom medical compilations — ochre clays prescribed in remedies, including in the Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BCE) for intestinal and eye complaints. Cosmetic, artistic, medical, and symbolic at once: the Egyptian evidence, readable in texts, displays exactly the functional-symbolic layering that the Middle Stone Age evidence can only suggest.

Egypt also preserves the endpoint of the mining story. The Predynastic palettes were ground from minerals obtained beyond the valley — malachite and galena from the Eastern Desert and Sinai, worked through the same culture of expedition and extraction that this article met at Ngwenya, now organised by an emerging state and recorded, eventually, in inscriptions. And red itself acquired a theology the archive treats elsewhere: desher, red — the colour of the desert, of blood, of fire, of dangerous and necessary power — set against the black of Kemet, the Black Land, whose very name anchors the alchemical tradition examined in the Egyptology division. The full Predynastic picture — the cultures, cemeteries, and craft technologies of the formative Nile Valley — is given in The Predynastic Period.

// THE LONG THREAD //

Set the two ends side by side. At Blombos, c. 100,000 BP: ochre ground, mixed with heated bone and charcoal, stored in a shell. At Naqada, c. 3500 BCE: ochre ground on a stone palette with a pebble, mixed for application, buried with its owner. Between them lie ninety-six thousand years in which the recipe was never lost — because it was never only a recipe. It was sunscreen and glue, medicine and rouge, a mark of the group and a colour for the dead, taught hand to hand in communities of practice from the Cape to the Nile. The archive this article opens is, in the end, the record of what people did with that inheritance once they could also write, smelt, build, and philosophise. The red earth came first.

XX. What Remains Unknown

A Tier A article ends with its gaps, because the gaps are attested too. The application of the Blombos workshop's paint is unknown. The meanings of the Blombos and Diepkloof motifs are unknown — no key survives, and none will be invented here. The proportion of Middle Stone Age ochre devoted to symbolic versus practical ends is unknown, and may be unknowable in principle, since the two were probably not separate categories for their users. The makers of the Twin Rivers and Kapthurin assemblages — which hominin populations, exactly — remain uncertain. The true antiquity of the Ngwenya workings is unsettled, bounded below by radiocarbon at the edge of its range. And the perishable majority of early symbolic culture — marks on wood, bark, skin, fibre, and sand — is lost beyond recovery, which means the record in this article is a floor beneath the reality, not a measure of it.

What is not unknown is the shape of the story. Coloured earth was collected before our species was finished evolving; it was compounded in workshops a hundred thousand years ago; it was engraved, perforated, standardised, traded across regions, and mined from bedrock; and the practices around it were taught and held in common for spans of time beside which all of recorded history is a moment. Everything else in this archive — the mines of the pharaohs, the pigments of the tombs, the laboratories of the alchemists — is commentary on that beginning.
