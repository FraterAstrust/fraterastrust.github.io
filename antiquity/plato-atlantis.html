I. Tier B — What This Article Is, and Is Not

This is a Tier B article: ancient tradition. That label has a precise meaning in this hub. Tier B does not mean that the events described are attested fact. It means that an ancient text makes a claim, that the claim can be read, checked, and quoted in named translations, and that ancient and later readers argued about it — and all of that can be documented.

Nothing in this article attests a real Atlantis. No Egyptian inscription naming Atlantis has been found. No source older than Plato mentions Atlantis at all. No archaeological site has been shown to be Plato's island on Plato's own terms — its place, its date, and its scale at once. The article's job is narrower and, for that reason, stronger: to set out what Plato actually wrote, how he framed the story's transmission, what work the story does inside his philosophy, and how the ancient world itself received it.

That discipline matters because almost all later Atlantis lore — the lost-continent literature of the nineteenth century, the occult Atlantean root-races, the modern hunt for a drowned city in every ocean — claims descent from this one text. The modern telling is treated in its own Tier C article, Mu and Atlantis Reborn. This article is the text that telling must answer to. Where the modern version adds aircraft, explosives, energy crystals, or a global mother-civilisation, the reader can return here and check: Plato says none of that.

// TIER DISCIPLINE — A NOTE ON VERBS //

Throughout this article, Atlantis claims are attributed, never narrated as fact: the priest in Plato's dialogue says…, the Critias describes…, Proclus reports that Crantor held… When a sentence states something without attribution — that Plato wrote two dialogues, that the Critias breaks off unfinished, that Strabo reports a view of Posidonius — that is a claim about a text or a documented scholarly position, and it is sourced as such.

Quotations from Plato in this article are taken only from named, published translations actually consulted for it, principally Benjamin Jowett's translation of the Timaeus and the Critias, with the Loeb translations of Strabo and Plutarch and Thomas Taylor's translation of Proclus used for the reception material. Each quotation is cited to its translator. Everything else that renders an ancient author's wording is paraphrase, and is presented as paraphrase.

II. Two Dialogues, One Source

Atlantis appears in exactly two works by the Athenian philosopher Plato (c. 429–347 BCE): the Timaeus and its unfinished sequel, the Critias, both generally placed among his late dialogues and dated to around 360 BCE. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's article on the Timaeus (Donald Zeyl) notes that the orthodox view places the dialogue in Plato's late period, while recording that this dating has been debated — G. E. L. Owen famously argued for a middle-period date. The exact year of composition is not settled, and this article does not pretend otherwise.

The two dialogues divide the story between them. The Timaeus gives the frame, the transmission claim, and a compressed summary of the whole tale: the island, the empire, the war, and the destruction, all within a few pages (Timaeus 24e–25d). The Critias was to give the tale in full — the two cities described at length, then the war itself — but it breaks off before the war is narrated, in the middle of a speech by Zeus.

A third dialogue, the Hermocrates, was projected as the conclusion of the sequence and was never written. The sequence as planned ran from the creation of the cosmos (the main business of the Timaeus, in a long speech by Timaeus of Locri on the making of the world and of humankind), through the earliest human history (the Critias), to the present age (the unwritten Hermocrates). Atlantis, in other words, sits inside a much larger design: a history of everything, from the origin of the universe to Plato's own day.

THE TIMAEUS

A dialogue whose main subject is cosmology — the creation and structure of the universe. Atlantis enters in the introductory conversation (20d–27b) as a story Critias offers in answer to Socrates' request, and is summarised by the Egyptian priest's speech at 24e–25d. The Atlantis material is a preface to the cosmology, not its subject.

THE CRITIAS

The sequel in which Critias retells the story at full length: primeval Athens first (109b–112e), then Atlantis (113c–121c) — its god, its kings, its city, its plain, its laws, and its moral decline. The text stops at 121c, mid-sentence, as Zeus rises to pronounce judgement.

The speakers matter. In both dialogues the Atlantis story is told not by Socrates, and not by Plato in his own voice — Plato never speaks in his own voice anywhere in the dialogues — but by Critias: an Athenian aristocrat, a relative of Plato's, and, in history, a figure Plato's readers knew as one of the Thirty Tyrants who ruled Athens brutally in 404–403 BCE. Plato puts his "true story" in the mouth of a man his audience associated with a violent, failed regime. Scholars have long noted the oddity; at minimum, it is a reason not to read the dialogue's truth-claims as Plato's own affidavit.

Jowett, in his introduction to the Critias, states the conclusion he draws from the whole construction in plain terms: in his judgement the entire narrative is due to the imagination of Plato, who used the name of Solon and introduced the Egyptian priests to give verisimilitude to his story. That is a translator's scholarly judgement, offered here as such — Section XIII below sets out how far modern scholarship agrees with it, and where it dissents.

III. The Chain of Transmission, As Plato Frames It

Plato does not present the Atlantis story as his own research, and he does not present it as a myth in the ordinary sense either. He presents it as a tradition with a pedigree — a chain of named tellers, each link of which the reader is invited to inspect. Understanding that chain is essential, because the chain itself, not any external evidence, is the story's entire claim to authority.

The Occasion: Socrates Asks for the Ideal City in Action

At the opening of the Timaeus, Socrates recalls a conversation of the previous day about the best city — a discussion closely resembling the argument of Plato's Republic — and says, in paraphrase, that a description of a city at rest does not satisfy him: he wants to see that city in motion, engaged in a war worthy of it, negotiating, fighting, and showing in action the character its constitution gave it in theory (Timaeus 19b–c).

Critias answers that he knows just such a story. It is, he insists, a true one — and its truth is the point, because only a real deed, he argues, can show the ideal city really at work. The Atlantis tale is thus introduced, from its first lines, as the answer to a philosophical commission: show us the city of the Republic, alive and at war. Section XII returns to what that commission implies.

The Chain, Link by Link

As Critias tells it in the Timaeus (20d–21b), the chain runs:

LINK 1

An Egyptian Priest at Sais

An unnamed, very aged priest of the goddess Neith at Sais in the Nile Delta tells the visiting Athenian Solon what, he says, the temple's written records preserve: the deeds of Athens 9,000 years earlier, forgotten by the Greeks themselves.

LINK 2

Solon of Athens

Solon (c. 640–c. 560 BCE), the Athenian lawgiver and poet, counted among the Seven Sages, hears the account in Egypt. In the dialogue's telling he intended to work it into a poem, but was prevented by the political troubles he found on his return home.

LINK 3

Dropides

Solon, a relative and friend of Critias' great-grandfather Dropides, tells the story to him. In the Critias (113a–b), Critias adds that Solon's own written version — his notes for the projected poem — passed to Dropides and remained in the family's possession, and that he himself studied it as a boy.

LINK 4

Critias the Elder

Dropides' son, Critias' grandfather — nearly ninety years old at the time of telling, in the dialogue's frame — repeats the story to his grandson, then about ten years old, during the festival of the Apaturia, when children recited poetry and Solon's poems were sung.

LINK 5

Critias the Narrator — and Plato

The younger Critias, now a grown man, retells the story to Socrates, Timaeus, and Hermocrates — and Plato writes the retelling down, some two centuries after Solon's lifetime. Every word of Atlantis that survives reaches us through this final link alone.

Two features of the chain deserve emphasis. First, it is entirely internal to Plato's text. No writing of Solon's about Atlantis survives — indeed, no fragment of Solon's surviving poetry mentions it — and no source independent of Plato records the conversation at Sais. Later writers who name the priest or repeat the story (Section XVI) are working from Plato, not from a second archive.

Second, Plato builds verisimilitude into the chain with unusual care. The Critias even pauses (113a–b) to explain, in paraphrase, why the foreign names in the tale sound Greek: Solon, preparing his poem, asked what the Egyptian renderings of the names meant, and translated the meanings back into Greek — so the names the reader hears are, within the fiction of the transmission, translations of translations. A storyteller inventing a pedigree could hardly do more to make it feel documented; a historian reporting a genuine archive would not need to. Readers from Jowett onward have drawn opposite morals from the same detail, and both responses are recorded in Section XIII.

// THE FESTIVAL FRAME //

Critias dates his hearing of the tale to the Apaturia, the Athenian festival of phratries (kinship groups), on the day called Koureotis, when boys were enrolled and recited poems. The detail roots the transmission in family and festival — memory carried by a household, not by a state archive. That is exactly the kind of memory Plato's own priest will say Greece cannot rely on.

Critias also stresses, at the start of the Critias (108e–110a in paraphrase), that he has reconstructed the story by a night's effort of memory, turning over in his mind what he heard as a child and comparing it with Socrates' account of the ideal city. The dialogue itself, read closely, flags the tale as something recalled, reconstructed, and fitted to a philosophical brief — a point Christopher Gill's study of the Atlantis story presses with care, as Section XII notes.

IV. Why Egypt? Sais, Neith, and Athena

The story could have been set anywhere old. Plato sets it in Egypt, and the choice is load-bearing. Egypt does three kinds of work in the framing: it supplies antiquity (records older than any Greek memory), it supplies kinship (a goddess shared between the two cities), and it supplies authority (a priesthood that kept written archives while Greece, in the priest's telling, repeatedly lost its own past).

Sais and Its Goddess

At Timaeus 21e, in the translation by R. G. Bury for the Loeb Classical Library as reproduced on Perseus, Critias locates the scene: in the Delta of Egypt, where the Nile's stream parts, lies the Saitic district, whose chief city is Sais — the home of King Amasis — whose founding goddess, the text says, bears the Egyptian name Neith and is asserted to be the same as the Greek Athena. Jowett's rendering makes the same identification in the same place.

Neith was a real and ancient Egyptian goddess, attested from the earliest periods of Egyptian history, whose chief cult centre was indeed Sais in the western Nile Delta; her emblems were the bow, crossed arrows, and shield, and she was a goddess of war, of hunting, and in later theology a creator. Her cult rose to particular prominence in the Twenty-sixth Dynasty (664–525 BCE), when Sais was the capital of Egypt — the very period in which Solon is placed in Egypt, and the reign of Amasis (570–526 BCE), the philhellene king the text names, overlaps Solon's later years. The framing, that is, is historically well-dressed: the city, the goddess, and the king all belong to Solon's world as a Greek of Plato's day could have known it, not least from Herodotus, whose Histories (Book II) describe Sais, Neith's festival of lamps, and Amasis at length.

The citizens of Sais are, in the dialogue's words, great lovers of the Athenians, and claim a kinship with them — because the same goddess founded both cities. PARAPHRASE OF TIMAEUS 21E, AFTER JOWETT AND BURY

What the Identification Does

The Neith–Athena identification is the hinge of the whole story. Because Athena founded Athens — in the priest's account, a thousand years before she founded Sais itself — the Egyptian archive is not a foreign curiosity but, within the tale, a family record: the younger sister-city's temple has preserved the elder city's forgotten deeds. The priest can therefore tell Solon, an Athenian, the history of Athens itself, and the Athenian has no standing to dismiss it as alien fable. Plato's construction makes Egypt the memory of Greece.

There is also a real Greek habit behind the device. Greek writers of the fifth and fourth centuries BCE routinely identified foreign gods with their own — Herodotus does it throughout his Egyptian book — and routinely treated Egypt as the source of great antiquity: of geometry, of astronomy, of religious rites, of laws. Solon's Egyptian journey was itself a Greek tradition older than Plato: Herodotus (1.29–30) already sends Solon travelling, and to Egypt and Amasis among other destinations, though Herodotus says nothing of Atlantis. Plato's frame, in short, uses materials his audience already believed — Egypt is old, Egypt keeps records, Solon went there — to carry a story no one had heard before.

For this hub's purposes, one caution is essential. The dialogue's Egypt is a Greek literary Egypt: a Sais of the Greek imagination, staffed by a priest who speaks like a Platonic character and whose "records" contain a war between a Greek ideal city and a western sea-empire. Nothing in the Egyptian record — no temple inscription, king list, or papyrus from any period — contains Atlantis, primeval Athens, or this war. The Egyptian colouring is Plato's framing device, and Tier discipline requires it to be described as exactly that. The companion Tier B article on the antediluvian king lists shows, by contrast, what genuine ancient Near Eastern records of the deep past look like when they survive independently.

V. The Priest's Rebuke — Memory, Floods, and Forgetting

Before the priest tells Solon anything about Atlantis, he tells him something about the Greeks — and it is the most famous sentence in the whole Atlantis corpus. In Jowett's translation (Timaeus 22b), the aged priest says:

“O Solon, Solon, you Hellenes are never anything but children, and there is not an old man among you.” PLATO, TIMAEUS 22B — TRANS. BENJAMIN JOWETT

The priest explains the rebuke, in paraphrase of 22b–23c: the Greeks are children in soul because they keep no old opinion handed down from antiquity and no knowledge hoary with age. The reason is catastrophe. Many destructions of humankind have occurred and will occur — the greatest by fire and water, lesser ones by countless other agents. The priest retells, as an example, the Greek story of Phaethon, who drove the sun's chariot and burned the earth: the story, he says, has the form of a myth, but its truth is a periodic deviation of the heavenly bodies that brings long cycles of destruction by fire upon the earth.

Egypt, by contrast, is protected: the Nile preserves it from both fire and the deluges that drown other lands, and so in Egypt all notable deeds — Greek deeds included — are written down in the temples and preserved. Whenever a flood wipes out a Greek civilisation, the survivors are, in the priest's account, unlettered mountain herdsmen who keep only the names of their ancestors and none of their deeds; civilisation, literacy, and memory must begin again from nothing. That, the priest says, is why Solon can recite Greek genealogies yet knows nothing of the Athens of 9,000 years before: his city has forgotten itself many times over.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR READING THE TEXT

The priest's speech is Plato's theory of why the story has no corroboration. The absence of any Greek record of Atlantis is not, within the dialogue, an embarrassment to be explained away later — it is predicted by the frame itself: of course the Greeks forgot; forgetting is what floods do. A modern reader should see the device for what it is, a self-sealing structure: the story explains in advance why no evidence for it can be produced. That does not make the device dishonest — Plato is writing philosophy, not filing a report — but it does mean the frame cannot be used as evidence.

The speech also does philosophical work beyond the frame. Its doctrine of recurrent cosmic catastrophe — destructions by fire and water at long intervals, civilisations rising and perishing in cycles — belongs to the cosmology of the Timaeus as a whole, and Plato returns to it in the Laws (Book III), where the Athenian Stranger reconstructs the rebirth of society after a flood. Atlantis is one episode in a universe where such episodes are the rule. Primeval Athens and Atlantis are both, in the priest's telling, civilisations of a previous cycle — which is precisely why their story can instruct the present one.

VI. The 9,000 Years — The Text's Own Figure

The single most consequential number in the Atlantis story is given twice, once in each dialogue. In the Timaeus (23e), the priest dates the founding of Athens to 9,000 years before Solon's visit — and the founding of Sais to 8,000 years before, a thousand years later. In the Critias (108e), the narrator repeats the figure as the elapsed time since the war: nine thousand years, in Jowett's rendering of the passage, was the sum of years since the war between those who dwelt outside the Pillars of Heracles and all who dwelt within them.

Three precisions are required, because popular retellings routinely blur all three.

ANCHORED TO SOLON

The figure is 9,000 years before Solon — before the priest's conversation with him in Egypt, conventionally placed in the 590s–570s BCE — not 9,000 years before Plato, and not 9,000 years before the present. Plato's text never converts the figure into any other era.

“c. 9600 BCE” IS ARITHMETIC, NOT TEXT

The familiar date of about 9600 BCE is a modern sum: Solon's floruit plus 9,000. It appears nowhere in Plato. It is a fair gloss of the text's claim, provided it is labelled as a computation from the text, not a quotation of it.

A CLAIM, NOT A DATE

The figure is something a character in a dialogue attributes to temple records that no one else ever saw. It belongs to Tier B: it is what the tradition says. At c. 9600 BCE, the Aegean held Mesolithic hunter-gatherers; there was no Athens, no writing, no state, and no war of empires for a record to record.

The scale of the figure should also be felt. Nine thousand years before Solon is roughly as far before Solon as Solon is before the present day — and then half as far again. Plato, whose historical horizon reached back a few centuries to the Trojan War as a dimly remembered event of perhaps 700 years before his time, is deliberately placing his tale in a depth of time no Greek tradition could fill with content. The number functions in the dialogue the way the Egyptian frame does: it puts the story beyond checking, in a past accessible only through the one archive the story itself supplies.

Later attempts to rescue the story's historicity have almost all had to begin by altering this number — most famously the proposal, discussed in Section XVII, that the figures were inflated tenfold somewhere in transmission, so that 9,000 years "should" be 900. The reader should note what that manoeuvre concedes: on the text's own figure, no known civilisation fits, so the text must be corrected before it can be matched to history at all. L. Sprague de Camp's much-quoted maxim in the Atlantis literature applies with force here, in paraphrase: one cannot change all the details of Plato's story and still claim to have Plato's story.

VII. Primeval Athens — The Other City in the Story

Half of the Atlantis story is not about Atlantis. The priest's tale, and still more the Critias, describe two cities, and the first of them — given precedence, as Critias says, in the telling — is an Athens of 9,000 years before, utterly unlike the Athens of Plato's day.

In the Critias account (109b–112e, paraphrased from Jowett's translation), Attica fell by lot to Athena and Hephaestus together — deities of wisdom and craft — who settled in it a race of good men, native to the soil, and taught them the order of a state. The land itself was richer then: deeper soil, abundant springs, forests on the mountains, pasture where Plato's contemporaries saw bare rock. The dialogue lingers, remarkably, over the erosion of Attica — the soil washed to the sea over the millennia, leaving what Jowett's summary calls the mere skeleton of the land — a passage often noted as one of the earliest surviving observations of landscape degradation in Greek literature.

The city on the Acropolis — a larger Acropolis than the classical one, in the account, extending to the rivers Eridanus and Ilissus and taking in the Pnyx and Lycabettus — was ordered by classes: craftsmen and farmers below, and apart from them a warrior class, the guardians, who lived in common dwellings around the temples of Athena and Hephaestus, held their possessions in common, took their meals together, kept their numbers steady at about twenty thousand men and women fit for war, and neither hoarded gold and silver nor lacked what was needful. Men and women of the guardian class trained and fought in common — Critias points, in the account, to the armed statue of Athena as surviving evidence of that ancient custom.

Primeval Athens is the Republic's city with an address and a date: guardians in common, property held in common, women sharing the work of war — set down in Attica, 9,000 years before Solon. EDITORIAL SUMMARY OF CRITIAS 110C–112E, AFTER JOWETT

No reader of Plato can miss the resemblance, and none is meant to. The primeval Athenians are the guardian class of the Republic made historical: the same communism of property and family life, the same separation of the warrior order, the same indifference to wealth. Socrates asked at the Timaeus' opening to see the city of the previous day's conversation in action; Critias' Athens is that city, wearing the dress of antiquity. Atlantis, when it comes, will be everything this city is not.

The priest's summary in the Timaeus gives primeval Athens its deed: when the Atlantic power attacked, Athens led the Greeks; when her allies deserted her, she stood alone, defeated the invader at the extremity of danger, freed those already enslaved within the Pillars, and preserved the rest from enslavement (24e–25c, after Jowett). The pattern — Athens alone against a vast invading empire from across the sea, Athens as liberator of Greece — is the pattern of the Persian Wars of 490 and 480–479 BCE as Athenian memory cherished it, transposed nine thousand years into the past. Section XII takes up that transposition directly.

VIII. Atlantis in the Timaeus — Geography, Empire, War, and End

Everything the Timaeus says about Atlantis itself is contained in the priest's speech at 24e–25d. It is short — a few hundred words in Greek — and it is the only Atlantis the earliest readers ever had. In Jowett's translation, the priest relates that the records tell of a mighty power that advanced from the Atlantic Ocean against the whole of Europe and Asia, and that Athens put an end to it. The passage, in Jowett's wording, runs in part:

“In those days the Atlantic was navigable; and there was an island situated in front of the straits which are by you called the Pillars of Heracles; the island was larger than Libya and Asia put together, and was the way to other islands, and from these you might pass to the whole of the opposite continent which surrounded the true ocean.” PLATO, TIMAEUS 24E–25A — TRANS. BENJAMIN JOWETT (PARAPHRASED LINE BREAKS)

The geography deserves slow reading, because it is the geography of a Greek mental map, not of a surveyed place. The Pillars of Heracles are the Strait of Gibraltar. Beyond them lies the outer ocean — the "true ocean," of which the Mediterranean, in the priest's image, is merely a harbour with a narrow entrance. In front of the straits lies the island, larger than Libya (North Africa as the Greeks knew it) and Asia (Asia Minor and the Persian world) combined. Beyond the island, other islands; beyond them, a continent enclosing the ocean on the far side. Plato's cosmos here is a ring: the known world around its harbour-sea, the ocean around that, the opposite continent around the ocean. Atlantis is the stepping-stone power of the outer ring.

The Empire and the War

On this island, the priest says (25a–b, after Jowett), arose a great and wonderful empire: its kings ruled the whole island, many other islands, and parts of the opposite continent; and on the near side of the straits they had conquered Libya as far as Egypt and Europe as far as Tyrrhenia (Etruria, in modern Italy). This assembled power then attempted, in a single campaign, to enslave all the lands within the straits — Egypt and Greece included. Athens resisted, first as leader of the Greeks, then alone, and won.

The Destruction

The end comes, in the priest's account, not as a consequence of the war but after it — a catastrophe that falls on victor and vanquished alike. In Jowett's translation (25c–d):

“But afterwards there occurred violent earthquakes and floods; and in a single day and night of misfortune all your warlike men in a body sank into the earth, and the island of Atlantis in like manner disappeared in the depths of the sea.” PLATO, TIMAEUS 25C–D — TRANS. BENJAMIN JOWETT

The priest adds the aetiological tail of the story: the sea in those parts is, in his day, impassable because of the shoal of mud left by the subsided island. Note the completeness of the erasure. The Athenian army perishes with its enemy; no veterans return to tell the tale; no wreck, ruin, or colony survives to be visited; even the sea-route to the site is blocked by mud. As with the forgetting of Section V, the story furnishes, inside itself, a reason for every absence of evidence outside itself. That is either the mark of a tradition that has thought hard about its own survival — or the mark of a construction built to be uncheckable. The scholarly argument of Section XIII is, at bottom, an argument about which of those two readings the construction deserves.

IX. Atlantis in the Critias — Poseidon, Cleito, and the Ten Kings

Where the Timaeus summarises, the Critias builds. Critias' full account (113c onward) begins in the age when the gods divided the earth among themselves by lot — peaceably, the dialogue insists, without the strife of the old myths — and each god settled and tended his own portion as a shepherd tends his flock. Atlantis fell to Poseidon, god of the sea and of earthquakes: the two powers whose combination will, in the end, destroy it.

In the centre of the island, the account continues (113c–d, after Jowett), lay a plain said to be the fairest of all plains, and near its centre a low hill, where dwelt one of the earth-born men of that country, Evenor, with his wife Leucippe and their only daughter, Cleito. Her parents died; Poseidon desired her; and to enclose and secure the hill he broke the ground around it into concentric zones — the origin of the ringed city that every later image of Atlantis reproduces.

“…breaking the ground, he inclosed the hill in which she dwelt all round, making alternate zones of sea and land, larger and smaller, encircling one another; there were two of land and three of water…” PLATO, CRITIAS 113D–E — TRANS. BENJAMIN JOWETT

Poseidon also brought up two springs within the enclosure, one of hot water and one of cold — a detail later readers have connected with volcanic islands, though the text itself connects it with nothing but the god's provision for the place. By Cleito, Poseidon fathered five pairs of twin sons. The eldest of the first pair was Atlas, whom Poseidon made king over the whole island; from Atlas, the account says, the island took its name — Atlantis, the island of Atlas — and the surrounding sea its name, Atlantic. Atlas's twin, called Gadeirus in the local tongue (Eumelus in Greek, the dialogue explains), received the extremity of the island toward the Pillars of Heracles, the district of Gades — modern Cádiz. The other eight brothers were made rulers over the island's other regions, and the kingship in each line passed to the eldest son, generation after generation (114a–c, after Jowett).

A CONFEDERACY OF TEN KINGS

Atlantis, in the Critias, is not a single monarchy but a confederacy: ten royal houses, descended from ten brothers, each absolute in his own territory, bound to one another by the laws of Poseidon inscribed on a pillar of orichalcum, and forbidden to make war on each other. The eldest line, Atlas's, holds a primacy the others acknowledge.

NAMES THAT POINT AT THE MAP

Gadeirus–Gades anchors one end of the island at the straits themselves. Atlas carries a second charge: in Greek myth Atlas is the Titan who holds up the sky at the world's western edge, and the Atlas Mountains of north-west Africa bore his name. Plato's naming is cartography by mythology — the far west, where the sun sets and the known world ends.

The descent from a god through a mortal woman gives the Atlantean kings a literally divine portion in their nature — and the whole moral machinery of the Critias depends on it. As long as the divine portion predominates, the kings are just; as generation dilutes it with mortal admixture, they corrupt. Atlantis's fall, when Plato finally narrates its cause in Section XI, is written into its founding genealogy from the start.

X. The Ringed City, the Plain, and Orichalcum

The central books of the Critias (115c–118e) contain the most circumstantial description of an imaginary place in all of Plato's work: dimensions, materials, hydraulics, agriculture, and military establishment, given in numbers. The precision is part of the text's effect — and part of its problem, since precision invites measurement, and measurement, as Section XVII shows, is where every proposed identification eventually fails.

The Rings and the Canal

Later kings, the account says (115d–116a, after Jowett), bridged Poseidon's rings, built the royal palace on the central island, and cut a canal from the sea through the rings to the centre: three plethra (about 300 feet) wide, a hundred feet deep, and fifty stades long. The rings themselves are measured: the central island five stades in diameter; the innermost water ring one stade wide; the next land and water rings two stades each; the outermost water and land rings three stades each. Ships could pass through the rings by passages cut in the land zones, roofed over in places where the rock allowed the docks to be hollowed beneath the ring's edge. The whole ringed citadel, rings included, measures some twenty-seven stades across — roughly five kilometres.

Walls, Metals, and the Temple of Poseidon

The walls around the rings were built, in the account (116a–b), of stone quarried from the island itself in three colours — white, black, and red — and were coated in metals: the outermost wall with brass, the next with tin, and the wall of the citadel itself with orichalcum, which, in Jowett's rendering, flashed with a red light. Orichalcum — "mountain copper" — was, the text says (114e), mined in many parts of the island, and was then the most precious metal known after gold, though by Plato's day it had become, in his phrase, only a name. Modern writers have variously identified it with brass, with a gold–copper alloy, and with other materials; Plato's text identifies it with nothing but its value, its colour, and its loss.

Within the citadel stood the temple of Poseidon and Cleito, enclosed by a golden wall and never entered, and within it Poseidon's own temple, a stade in length, its exterior covered with silver and its pinnacles with gold, its interior roof of ivory worked with gold, silver, and orichalcum, and at its centre a statue of the god standing in a chariot drawn by six winged horses, his head touching the roof, ringed by a hundred Nereids on dolphins (116c–e, after Jowett). Around it stood golden statues of the ten kings and their wives, the altars, the palaces, the hot and cold baths, the grove of Poseidon, and — in the larger land ring — a racecourse for horses a stade wide running the ring's whole circuit.

The Plain and the Army

Beyond the city lay the island's great oblong plain (118a–e, after Jowett): three thousand stades long and two thousand wide — on the usual conversion of the stade (about 185 metres), a rectangle of roughly 555 by 370 kilometres, a territory larger than many modern states, on an island already said to exceed Libya and Asia together. The plain faced south, sheltered from the north winds by mountains famed for their size and beauty; it was ringed by a ditch of staggering stated dimensions — a stade wide, a hundred feet deep, and ten thousand stades in total length — and cross-cut by canals a hundred feet wide at hundred-stade intervals, fed by the mountain streams and carrying timber and produce to the city.

The plain was divided into sixty thousand lots of ten stades square, each lot furnishing its share of the island's war establishment: in aggregate, the account reckons (118e–119a) ten thousand chariots, and the crews of twelve hundred ships, with hoplites, archers, slingers, javelin-men, and chariot teams in proportion. The island itself produced, in the catalogue at 114e–115b, timber, metals, fragrant gums and roots, fruits hard and soft — and elephants, "the largest of animals," in herds. The elephants matter to the argument of Section XVII: they are an African and Asian animal in a text about an Atlantic island, and every proposed location must somehow house them.

// NUMBERS ARE PART OF THE TEXT //

These figures are not decoration added by later copyists; they are in the received Greek text of the Critias, and they are the terms on which any claim to have "found Plato's Atlantis" must be judged. A candidate site a few kilometres across, dated eight thousand years too late, on the wrong side of the straits, may be a fascinating place — Thera is — but it matches the story only by discarding the story's own measurements. Section XVII applies that test case by case.

XI. Laws, Bulls, Corruption — and a Text That Stops Mid-Sentence

The Critias spends its second half inside Atlantis: its constitution, its central rite, and its fall from within. The account of the kings' laws and of the bull sacrifice at the pillar of orichalcum is the dialogue's most circumstantial passage — and the dialogue does not finish it. Zeus rises to pronounce judgement on the corrupted kings, and the text stops.

The Pillar of Orichalcum and the Bull Sacrifice

The ten kings met, the account says (119c–120c, after Jowett), at the temple of Poseidon in alternate periods of five and six years, to deliberate on common affairs and to judge breaches of the law inscribed by the first kings on a pillar of orichalcum standing in the temple. The ritual of judgement is described in unusual detail: bulls ranged free in the temple precinct; the ten kings, left alone, prayed to the god, then hunted the bulls with staves and nooses, using no iron; the bull caught was led to the pillar and slain above it, its blood flowing over the inscription; each king drank from a bowl of the blood mixed with wine, swore to judge by the written laws and to punish the transgressor, and, when night fell, put on azure robes and sat in the ashes of the sacrifice to give judgement until dawn, writing their sentences on a golden tablet. The chief of the laws: never to take up arms against one another, to aid any of their number who was attacked, to deliberate in common about war, and for no king to put a kinsman to death without the consent of more than half of the ten.

The Corruption of Atlantis

Then comes the moral turn on which the whole dialogue pivots (120e–121c, after Jowett). For many generations, the account says, while the divine nature in them still lasted, the Atlanteans obeyed the laws, were gentle and wise, and despised everything but virtue, bearing lightly the burden of their gold and possessions and never becoming drunken with luxury. But as the divine portion was weakened by repeated mortal admixture, and the human nature prevailed, they lost their seemliness: to anyone with eyes, they had become base — filled with unrighteous avarice and power — though to those without knowledge of the true life, they seemed, at the very moment of their corruption, most glorious and blessed.

Zeus, god of gods, who rules by law, perceiving the dishonourable state of a once honourable race, resolved to punish them, that they might be chastened and improved. He gathered all the gods into their most holy habitation, at the centre of the world, whence they behold all created things — and, having assembled them, he spoke.

And there the Critias ends — in the middle of Zeus's sentence, and of the dialogue. What Zeus said, and how Atlantis was punished, Plato never wrote, or never finished. CRITIAS 121C — THE TEXT BREAKS OFF

The abruptness has been debated since antiquity. Plutarch, in his Life of Solon (32), offers the famous ancient explanation, in paraphrase: Solon began the tale but abandoned it under the pressure of his public life, and Plato, who took it up as a kinsman's inheritance and built for it, in Plutarch's image, great porches and enclosures such as no story ever had, left it unfinished because death overtook the work — so that readers regret the unwritten part as one regrets an unfinished palace. Modern scholars divide: some treat the break-off as a genuine incompleteness (the majority view, and the natural reading of the manuscripts), while Pierre Vidal-Naquet argued that Plato intended the ending we have — that the dialogue stops where it must, on the threshold of a war its author never meant to narrate, since the war's outcome has already been given in the Timaeus. The Bryn Mawr Classical Review's notice of Vidal-Naquet's book records the argument and a reviewer's dissent from it; the question remains open, and this article leaves it open.

What is not open is the consequence for the tradition: the Critias never narrates the war. Everything the world "knows" about the war itself — Athens alone, victorious, liberating the enslaved — comes from the priest's paragraph of summary in the Timaeus. The great set-piece battle of Atlantis, so often imagined in modern retellings, does not exist in Plato.

XII. The Philosophical Function — Atlantis as Counter-Image

To ask what Atlantis is for, in Plato, is to ask the question Plato's own frame answers. Socrates commissioned a story that would show the ideal city in motion, at war. Critias supplies two cities, not one — and the war between them is the demonstration. Primeval Athens embodies the city of the Republic: ordered classes, common property among the guardians, contempt for wealth, excellence in war without appetite for empire. Atlantis embodies its inversion: fabulous wealth, maritime reach, engineering splendour, dynastic pride — a sea-empire that begins in divine order and rots, from within, into avarice and aggression. Atlantis is the counter-image: the ideal city's negative, built at the same scale so the contrast can be exact.

Vidal-Naquet: Atlantis and Athens

The most influential modern statement of this reading is that of the French historian Pierre Vidal-Naquet, first in his article "Athènes et l'Atlantide" (Revue des Études Grecques 77, 1964) and finally in his book L'Atlantide: petite histoire d'un mythe platonicien (2005; English translation, The Atlantis Story: A Short History of Plato's Myth, 2007). Vidal-Naquet's thesis, as summarised in the Bryn Mawr Classical Review's notice of the book, is that the Atlantis story is a Platonic invention pure and simple — and, more specifically, that it is constructed as a mirror for Athens itself. Primeval Athens is Athens idealised, Athens as Plato wished it to understand itself; Atlantis is Athens as it had threatened to become — the imperial, naval, wealthy Athens of the fifth century, the Athens of the Delian League turned empire, of the Sicilian expedition, of hubris punished at Aegospotami. On this reading the war of 9,000 years before is a thought-experiment about Plato's own city: the good Athens defeats the bad Athens, and the bad Athens is drowned.

The mirror has a second face that Vidal-Naquet and others have pressed: Atlantis also resembles Athens' great enemy. A vast land-and-sea power advancing from outside to enslave Greece, defeated by a small free city standing alone, is Persia in the Persian Wars as Athens remembered them — and Jowett, in his introduction to the Critias, already read the conflict as prophetic or symbolic of the struggle of Athens and Persia, perhaps also of the Greek wars against Carthage in the west. The two resemblances do not exclude each other. Plato's invader can carry both charges at once: the empire Athens fought, and the empire Athens became.

Gill: A Tale of Two Cities, and a Problem About Truth

Christopher Gill, in Plato: The Atlantis Story (1980), his edition and commentary on Timaeus 17–27 and the Critias, reads the tale, in his phrase as reported in the secondary literature, as a tale of two cities set in an idealised past, in which Atlantis functions as a foil or contrast to the ideal represented by primeval Athens. Gill also draws attention to the dialogue's own unease about its truth-claim: Critias insists his story is true and accurate, yet it looks — in Gill's formulation as quoted in modern summaries — suspiciously unlike a true story, and more like a philosophical fable. That tension, Gill argues, is deliberate: Plato, who elsewhere (notably in the Republic) discusses the uses of fiction and the "noble lie," is exploring what a story's claim to truth does, even — especially — when the claim cannot be checked.

ATLANTIS AS NAVAL EMPIRE

Plato wrote in a city that had lived the arc he describes: Athens built a sea-empire on tribute and triremes, grew rich, grew overbearing, attacked Syracuse — a city, note, whose general Hermocrates, the very man who defeated the Athenian expedition of 415–413 BCE, sits listening to the Atlantis story in the dialogue, waiting to speak third. The casting is not accidental.

ATLANTIS AS MORAL PHYSICS

Within the Timaeus' cosmos, Atlantis's corruption is a local instance of a universal process: the divine order in a thing diluted by matter and time, excellence decaying into appetite. The priest's cycles of destruction (Section V) are the same process at cosmic scale. Atlantis falls the way worlds age.

None of this requires Atlantis to be "mere allegory" in a thin sense. Plato's story works as political thought precisely because it is built like history — with archives, genealogies, measurements, and a named chain of witnesses. The scholarly question is not whether the story is meaningful (everyone agrees it is) but whether its history-dress conceals a historical kernel. That is the argument of the next section.

XIII. Invented History? The Scholarly Consensus and Its Dissenters

The mainstream position in classical scholarship — held, with variations of emphasis, by the great majority of Plato scholars — is that Atlantis is Plato's invention: a story composed by him, from contemporary and traditional materials, for the philosophical purposes set out in Section XII, with no historical Atlantis behind it. Gerard Naddaf's survey of the question ("The Atlantis Myth: An Introduction to Plato's Later Philosophy of History," Phoenix 48, 1994) puts the consensus plainly, in paraphrase: the vast majority of classical scholars take the story to be what Plato explicitly denies it to be — invented myth — while a number of those same scholars add that the invention is a pastiche of history, built in part from real ingredients. Both halves of that sentence matter, and this article keeps them together.

The Grounds of the Consensus

The case rests on several converging grounds, each independently documented in the literature:

Silence before Plato. No writer before Plato mentions Atlantis, primeval Athens, or the war — not Herodotus, who collected Egyptian lore and Solon traditions eagerly; not any surviving fragment of Solon; not any Egyptian text of any period. Jowett already noted that there is no such legend in any writer previous to Plato. For a war said to have decided the fate of the entire Mediterranean world, the silence is total.

Fit to the philosophical brief. The story answers, point for point, the commission Socrates lays down at Timaeus 19b–c, and its virtuous city reproduces the Republic's constitution in detail (Section VII). A genuine tradition would be an astonishing coincidence of fit; an invention explains the fit directly.

Plato's known practice. Plato elsewhere composes myths and pseudo-histories for philosophical ends — the myth of Er, the Phaedo's geography of the underworld, the Statesman's myth of cosmic reversal, the Laws' post-diluvian prehistory — and discusses, in the Republic, the deliberate use of a founding fiction. Atlantis is continuous with that practice.

The self-sealing frame. As Sections V and VIII note, the story internally explains the absence of all external evidence — Greek forgetting, Egyptian exclusivity, the perished army, the mud-choked sea. A construction that anticipates every objection is more economically read as a construction.

The Dissent, Stated Fairly

The consensus has never been unanimous, and Tier discipline requires the dissent to be stated at its real strength. Three forms of it recur in serious literature.

A historical kernel, distorted. Some scholars and many archaeologically minded writers hold that Plato built his fiction around a genuine memory — most often the Thera eruption and the Minoan world (Section XVII) — transmitted, perhaps, through Egyptian channels in garbled form. On this view the story is invented in its particulars but not ex nihilo. This is the strongest form of dissent, and it is compatible with most of the consensus case: it concedes that no Atlantis as described existed, and argues only about Plato's raw materials.

Plato reported a tradition he believed. A smaller number of scholars have argued that Plato received the tale — from his own family tradition, from Egyptianising Greek sources, or from the general stock of Solon legend — and retold it in good faith. The difficulty this view faces is the silence before Plato: a tradition old enough to reach Solon left no trace in any hand but Plato's.

Crantor's testimony. The oldest dissent is ancient: Crantor, Plato's first commentator, treated the story as plain history, and Proclus reports that Egyptian priests of Crantor's acquaintance claimed the tale was written on pillars still preserved (Section XV). Alan Cameron's examination of that report (Classical Quarterly 33, 1983) shows how much less it says than later Atlantis literature claims for it — but the report exists, and it is the closest thing to independent ancient corroboration the story has ever had. It is a claim about a claim about pillars no one produced.

// ON “CONSENSUS” //

“Consensus” here means the working position of the relevant scholarly field — classical philology and ancient philosophy — as represented in its standard editions, commentaries, and surveys (Gill, Vidal-Naquet, Naddaf, the Stanford Encyclopedia). It does not mean that every classicist agrees on every point: the date of the Timaeus, the intended ending of the Critias, and the weight of the Thera parallel are all live questions inside it.

XIV. Ancient Reception I — Posidonius, Strabo, and the Aristotle Problem

The ancient reception of Atlantis begins, so far as surviving evidence shows, with readers who were already arguing about whether to believe it. Their argument is preserved for us mainly at second and third hand — a fact the reader should hold in mind throughout this section and the next: almost everything "ancient" said about Atlantis after Plato is a report of a report.

Posidonius, Reported by Strabo

The Stoic philosopher and geographer Posidonius (c. 135–c. 51 BCE), in his work On the Ocean, discussed the rising and sinking of land by earthquake and sea — and, the geographer Strabo reports (Geography 2.3.6), cited Plato's Atlantis in that connection. In the Loeb translation by H. L. Jones, as quoted in Alan Cameron's study of the passage, Strabo says that Posidonius did well to cite Plato's statement that it is possible the story of the island of Atlantis is not a fiction — Plato having related that Solon, after inquiry of the Egyptian priests, reported that Atlantis once existed but had disappeared, an island no smaller than a continent.

Cameron's analysis of the context is important for calibrating what Posidonius actually committed himself to. The passage sits inside a discussion of earthquakes and sea-waves — of historical cities destroyed by the combination of quake and inundation. Posidonius, on Cameron's reading, was not professing belief in Atlantis; he was keeping the possibility open, on the scientific ground that Plato's mechanism of destruction — earthquake and flood together — was a real mechanism with real parallels. The Bryn Mawr review of Vidal-Naquet makes the same correction: it would be more accurate to say Posidonius proposed to keep an open mind. An open mind, in a discussion of seismology, is the whole of the evidence.

“Its Inventor Caused It to Disappear” — and the Aristotle Problem

Strabo's sentence continues, in Jones's translation as quoted by Cameron and by Thorwald C. Franke's study of the question: Posidonius thinks it better to put the matter that way

“…than to say of Atlantis: ‘Its inventor caused it to disappear, just as did the Poet the wall of the Achaeans.’” STRABO, GEOGRAPHY 2.3.6, REPORTING POSIDONIUS — TRANS. H. L. JONES (LOEB)

This is the passage behind the most famous one-liner in the whole Atlantis tradition: the quip, endlessly repeated in modern books, that Aristotle said of Atlantis that the man who dreamed it up also destroyed it — Plato invented the island and sank it himself. The quip is a fine line. Its attribution to Aristotle, however, does not survive inspection of the text, and this article follows the instruction of the evidence:

In Strabo 2.3.6, Aristotle is not named. The "inventor" sentence is presented as the alternative way of putting the matter that Posidonius rejected; no author is given for it, and the Loeb editors' note has even read the "inventor" as Solon rather than Plato. The Aristotle connection comes from a different passage, Strabo 13.1.36, where Strabo does name Aristotle — but about a different subject: Homer's wall of the Achaeans in the Iliad, whose erection and destruction, Strabo there says, Aristotle held to be the invention of the poet. Franke's tracing of the citations (2013; English version 2017) shows that the claim "Aristotle said it of Atlantis" enters the literature with the astronomer Jean-Baptiste Joseph Delambre in 1816, rests on a reading of Casaubon's 1587 commentary on Strabo that the commentary does not support, and has been copied from footnote to footnote ever since; the alleged saying has never been admitted to the standard collections of Aristotle's fragments.

// HOW THIS ARTICLE CITES THE QUIP //

The sentence is real, and it is quoted above from Strabo 2.3.6 in Jones's translation. What is not verified — and what the evidence, as Franke and Cameron present it, tells against — is that Aristotle spoke it about Atlantis. Readers who meet the line in modern books attributed flatly to Aristotle are meeting a two-hundred-year-old scholarly error, not an ancient testimony. Aristotle's surviving works, it should be added, nowhere discuss Atlantis at all.

XV. Ancient Reception II — Crantor, Proclus, and the Allegorists

If Posidonius kept an open mind, Plato's first commentator closed his — in the story's favour. Crantor of Soli (c. 340–c. 275 BCE), a pupil of Xenocrates, Plato's successor's successor in the Academy, wrote the first commentary on the Timaeus. The commentary is lost. What we know of its treatment of Atlantis comes from the Neoplatonist Proclus (410–485 CE), whose own vast commentary on the Timaeus, written some seven centuries after Crantor, reports the earlier views in order to supersede them.

What Proclus Actually Reports

In Thomas Taylor's translation of Proclus (1820), the report runs, in its opening, as follows — quoted here in the form in which it is reproduced in modern discussions of the passage:

“With respect to the whole of this narration about the Atlantics, some say that it is a mere history, which was the opinion of Crantor, the first interpreter of Plato…” PROCLUS, COMMENTARY ON THE TIMAEUS, BOOK 1 — TRANS. THOMAS TAYLOR (1820)

Proclus's report continues, in Taylor's translation as there reproduced: Crantor said that Plato was derided by contemporaries for not being the inventor of his Republic but for transcribing what the Egyptians had written; that Plato took the derision seriously enough to assign this history of the Athenians and Atlanteans to the Egyptians, and to hold that the Athenians once lived under that polity; and Crantor adds that this is testified by the prophets — the priests — of the Egyptians, who assert that these particulars are written on pillars which are still preserved.

Every clause of that report has been fought over. Cameron's 1983 examination establishes the key precisions. Proclus does not say that Crantor visited Egypt, saw the pillars, or read hieroglyphs confirming Plato — the vivid scene in which Crantor stands in the temple of Neith at Sais checking the inscription, beloved of Atlantis literature from Otto Muck to J. V. Luce's more sober The End of Atlantis, is a modern expansion of the text. What Proclus reports is a claim at third hand: priests (unnamed, of Crantor's day or reported to him) assert that the particulars are written on pillars still preserved. No pillar was produced, quoted, or described; no Egyptian text corresponding to the claim has ever been identified. And Crantor's motive, as both Proclus's context and Harold Tarrant's study of Plato's first interpreters bring out, was defensive: treating the story as unadorned history let Crantor answer the critics who charged Plato with copying his Republic from Egypt — if the Egyptian tale was history, Plato's use of it was scholarship, not plagiarism, and its plainness was proof it concealed no allegory Crantor's rivals could exploit. The first believer, on the evidence, believed for reasons of Academic politics.

The Allegorical Readers

Proclus's survey, in the same passage, preserves the spectrum of ancient reading that Crantor was reacting against — and it is worth setting out, because it shows that antiquity itself never settled what kind of text this is. In Taylor's translation as reproduced in modern discussions: others held the narration to be a fable, a fictitious account of things that never existed, indicating permanent natures in the cosmos; Amelius, the pupil of Plotinus, read the war cosmically — the Athenians as the fixed stars, the Atlanteans as the planets, their war the opposition of their revolutions, noting that the Critias divides the island into circles; Origen (the Platonist of that name) referred it to the opposition of daemons; Numenius, to the discord of souls — the better souls pupils of Athena, the worse given over to generation and its god, Poseidon; Porphyry mingled the daemon and soul readings. Proclus himself, following Iamblichus and his own teacher Syrianus, held a mediating position characteristic of later Neoplatonism: the story could be received as history and read as an image of eternal oppositions — the historical and the allegorical were not, for him, exclusive.

The lesson of Proclus's survey for a modern reader is sobering in both directions. Against the modern believer: no ancient reader, not even Crantor, produced evidence for Atlantis beyond Plato's text and a reported priestly assertion. Against the glib modern dismissal: ancient readers did not agree that the story was "obviously" fiction either — they argued about it for eight centuries, in exactly the categories (history, fable, cosmic image) that readers still use. The argument is as old as the commentary tradition, and Plato's text was built — Section XII argues — to sustain it.

OTHER ANCIENT WITNESSES, BRIEFLY

Philo of Alexandria (De aeternitate mundi) repeats the sinking of the island, greater than Libya and Asia, as Plato told it — a repetition, not a corroboration. Diodorus Siculus describes Atlantioi of north-west Africa, a people of the Atlas region with their own myths; the name is geographical, and his account owes nothing demonstrable to Plato's island empire. The Latin West received the Timaeus chiefly through Calcidius's partial translation and commentary (4th century CE), which carried the Atlantis summary into the medieval world. In every case the line of descent runs back to Plato's two dialogues and to no other source.

XVI. Ancient Reception III — Plutarch on Solon in Egypt

The biographer Plutarch (c. 46–c. 120 CE), writing more than four centuries after Plato, gives the Atlantis tradition its most influential ancient afterlife — not by arguing for the island, but by writing Solon's Egyptian journey into biography, with names Plato never gave.

In the Life of Solon (26.1), in Bernadotte Perrin's Loeb translation as reproduced on Perseus, Plutarch relates that Solon went first to Egypt, living, as Solon himself says in a quoted verse, where the Nile pours forth its floods near the Canobic shore; and that he spent time in study with Psenophis of Heliopolis and Sonchis of Sais, very learned priests — from whom, as Plato says, he heard the story of the lost Atlantis, and tried to introduce it in poetical form to the Greeks.

Plato's aged priest is anonymous. The name “Sonchis” is Plutarch's contribution, four and a half centuries later — tradition accreting detail around the text, as tradition does. EDITORIAL NOTE ON PLUTARCH, SOLON 26.1 (TRANS. PERRIN)

Plutarch repeats the pairing in On Isis and Osiris (ch. 10), where Solon is listed among the Greek sages who travelled to Egypt and conversed with priests — Thales, Eudoxus, Pythagoras, and Plato himself among them — with Solon assigned to Sonchis of Sais and Pythagoras to Oenuphis of Heliopolis. Proclus, later still, names yet other priests for Solon's visit — a Pateneit at Sais among them — in a list that does not agree with Plutarch's. The disagreement is itself the evidence: by late antiquity the story had a cast of named priests, but the names were supplied by the tradition, not recovered from a record, and the tradition could not keep them consistent.

Plutarch's second contribution is his account of the story's unfinished state (Solon 32, paraphrased in Section XI): Solon, he says, began the Atlantis poem late in life and abandoned it — not, Plutarch insists, for want of leisure, as Plato somewhere suggests, but daunted by the scale of the work in his old age; and Plato, taking up the theme as a family inheritance, embellished it magnificently and left it, at his death, incomplete. Plutarch thus treats the whole transmission — Solon's poem, Plato's elaboration — as literary history: a theme handed down, worked on, and twice abandoned. It is the most sensible ancient account of the matter, and it quietly assumes what the consensus of Section XIII concludes: that what was transmitted was a story in the making, not an archive.

A note on method, for the reader who will meet Sonchis elsewhere: in Atlantis literature the name is often used to thicken the story's pedigree — "Solon learned it from Sonchis, a historical priest." Plutarch is a real and valuable source for many things, but for Atlantis he is a source about Plato's story, writing in the second century CE, explicitly flagging his dependence with the words "as Plato says." A dependent witness, however eminent, is not a second witness.

XVII. Possible Real-World Inspirations — At Their Evidence Strength

Between "Plato invented everything" and "Atlantis was real" lies the question scholars actually debate: did Plato build his fiction from identifiable real materials? Almost certainly yes — invention in Plato is rarely creation from nothing — and the candidates below are the ones with a serious literature behind them. Each is stated with its evidence strength, and each is tested against the text's own terms (Section X): place beyond the Pillars, date 9,000 years before Solon, island larger than Libya and Asia, destruction by earthquake and flood in a day and a night. A candidate can be a genuine inspiration while failing completely as an identification — and conflating those two claims is the single most common error in Atlantis writing.

1. Thera (Santorini) and Minoan Crete — The Strongest Candidate, and Its Problems

The volcanic island of Thera, modern Santorini, erupted in the second millennium BCE in one of the largest eruptions of the Holocene — conventionally dated to the late seventeenth or sixteenth century BCE, with the precise year still disputed between radiocarbon and archaeological chronologies. The eruption buried the flourishing town now excavated at Akrotiri, sent sea-waves across the Aegean, and ash over Crete, some 100 kilometres south, where the palace civilisation of Minoan Crete — wealthy, maritime, artistic, and literate — stood at its height.

The connection to Atlantis was first proposed by K. T. Frost in 1909, who identified Plato's island with Minoan Crete, and was given its classic form by the Greek archaeologist Spyridon Marinatos, whose 1939 article in Antiquity, "The Volcanic Destruction of Minoan Crete," argued from pumice deposits at Amnisos that Theran sea-waves had destroyed the Minoan palaces. Marinatos later excavated Akrotiri itself (from 1967 until his death at the site in 1974). The seismologist Angelos Galanopoulos, and after him popularisers such as J. V. Luce, developed the identification fully: Atlantis was Crete and Thera together, its ringed harbour the drowned caldera, its end the eruption.

The attractions are real: a rich sea-power, a sudden catastrophe by sea and earth, a flooded ring-shaped island, hot springs, bull imagery in Minoan art that recalls the Critias' bull ritual, and a plausible channel — Egyptian records and reports — by which a memory could have travelled the very route Plato describes. The problems are equally real, and they are fatal to the identification (though not to a looser claim of inspiration):

PLACE

Thera and Crete lie inside the Mediterranean, in the Aegean — the wrong side of the Pillars of Heracles by some 2,400 kilometres, in the best-charted waters of the Greek world. Plato's island lies beyond Gibraltar, in the outer ocean.

DATE

The eruption falls around 1600 BCE — some 1,000 years before Solon, not 9,000. The identification requires dividing all of Plato's figures by ten, a correction proposed by Galanopoulos on the hypothesis of a translation error between Egyptian and Greek number-words. No textual evidence supports the correction; it is required only to make the identification fit.

SCALE

Crete is about 260 kilometres long. Plato's plain alone is 3,000 by 2,000 stades — roughly 555 by 370 kilometres — on an island larger than Libya and Asia together. Dividing by ten must be applied here too, and to the armies, the ditch, and the canal, until little of the text is left standing.

THE DESTRUCTION ITSELF

Modern Aegean archaeology has revised Marinatos's mechanism: the Minoan palace destructions (the Late Minoan IB horizon) fall generations after the eruption — on current chronologies, roughly a century and a half later — and their causes are debated in their own right. The eruption devastated Thera and damaged coastal Crete; it did not, in a day and a night, end Minoan civilisation, which continued, rebuilt, and flourished again. Cameron adds the textual point: Plato is precise that earthquake and flood destroyed Atlantis, not volcanic eruption.

Evidence strength: as an identification of Atlantis, rejected by the mainstream of Aegean archaeology and classical scholarship. As a possible ingredient in Plato's imagination — a memory, however garbled, of a real Bronze Age catastrophe reaching him through Egyptian or Greek channels — it remains the most respectable of all such proposals, and the only one with a substantial scholarly literature. Even its proponents' version requires Plato's numbers, place, and scale all to be wrong.

2. Helike — The Catastrophe of Plato's Own Lifetime

In the winter of 373/2 BCE, within Plato's adult lifetime and little more than a decade before the Timaeus is usually dated, the Achaean city of Helike, on the southern shore of the Gulf of Corinth, was destroyed in a single night by an earthquake and a great wave, and submerged beneath the gulf. Helike was a real city, a member of the Achaean League, and — the detail that has always drawn Atlantis scholars — a noted sanctuary of Poseidon Helikonios, god of the sea and of earthquakes. Ancient sources (Strabo, Diodorus, Pausanias, among others) describe the disaster and the submerged ruins, visible, they said, beneath the water for centuries. Modern excavation by the Helike Project (directed by Dora Katsonopoulou) has located the Classical city — buried, as it proved, under lagoon sediments on the coastal plain rather than in deep water.

Adalberto Giovannini argued (1985) that the submergence of Helike may have inspired Plato to end his Atlantis story with a submersion. The proposal has a virtue the Thera hypothesis lacks: it requires no correction of Plato at all. Plato needed no Bronze Age memory to imagine a city drowned by earthquake and wave; the defining natural catastrophe of his own generation had just shown him one, complete with the wrath of Poseidon as its traditional explanation. Evidence strength: a plausible, economical inspiration for the mode of Atlantis's destruction; it explains nothing else in the story, and its proponents generally claim nothing more.

3. Tartessos — Wealth Beyond the Pillars

West of the Pillars, in the lower Guadalquivir valley of south-western Iberia, Greek tradition placed Tartessos: a land and city of fabulous mineral wealth, known to Herodotus (1.163; 4.152) as the far-western trading partner of Phocaean Greeks and the realm of the long-lived king Arganthonios. Tartessos was real — an Iberian culture of the early first millennium BCE, rich in silver and tin — and it lay exactly where Plato puts Atlantis's sphere: beyond the straits, at the edge of the ocean, next to Gades, the very district the Critias assigns to Atlas's twin Gadeirus.

Adolf Schulten's Tartessos (1922) proposed the identification outright. It fails as an identification on chronology and on kind: Tartessos flourished in the centuries just before Plato, vanished (by absorption and collapse, not by sinking) within a century or two of his birth, was no island, and sank beneath no sea. As an ingredient, however, it is credible in the way de Camp suggested: the wealth of Tartessos — the far west's proverbial treasure-house — is one of the real materials from which a philosopher inventing a wealthy western power would naturally build. Evidence strength: weak as an identification; reasonable as one strand in the pastiche.

4. Other Proposed Ingredients, Briefly

The Persian Wars. Not a place but an event-pattern: a vast empire advancing to enslave Greece, defeated by Athens standing at last alone (Section XII). As a structural ingredient this commands wide scholarly assent — it is part of the consensus reading, not an alternative to it.

The Sea Peoples and the end of the Bronze Age. Egyptian inscriptions of Merneptah and Ramesses III record invasions from the sea around 1200 BCE, repelled by Egypt. Eberhard Zangger's The Flood from Heaven (1992) proposed that Atlantis is a garbled memory of Troy and that world; the proposal was examined and rejected by Bronze Age specialists (Renfrew; Bloedow and Spina, as surveyed in the Antiquity literature on Atlantis locations). As a general background — Egypt really did preserve records of repelled sea-invasions — the parallel is suggestive; as an identification it has not been sustained.

The Sicilian expedition and Carthage. Athens' own catastrophic western adventure of 415–413 BCE, and the wealthy maritime power of Carthage beyond the straits' approaches, both belong to the mental furniture of Plato's generation and are regularly cited (Jowett among them) as colours in the portrait. Neither is an "identification"; both are the kind of contemporary material the pastiche model predicts.

// THE TEST, APPLIED UNIFORMLY //

Place, date, scale, manner of end: Plato states all four, and a candidate that must discard three of the four to fit the fourth has not been identified — it has been used as a mirror. Thera matches the manner of end (partly) and nothing else. Helike matches the manner of end exactly, in Plato's own lifetime, and claims nothing else. Tartessos matches the place and the wealth, and nothing else. The honest conclusion is the pastiche: Plato had many real catastrophes, empires, and treasure-lands to draw on, and needed no lost continent to assemble his island.

XVIII. What Plato's Text Does Not Say

Because the modern Atlantis is so much larger than Plato's, a negative catalogue is a genuine scholarly service. The following are not in Plato — not in the Timaeus, not in the Critias, not anywhere in his work. Each entered the tradition later, most of them in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and each is treated in its proper place in the Tier C article, Mu and Atlantis Reborn.

NOT IN PLATO: TECHNOLOGY

No aircraft, no explosives, no searchlights, no submarines, no energy crystals, no death rays. The most advanced machine in the Critias is the trireme; its marvels are hydraulic and architectural — canals, docks, baths, and metal-clad walls. A widely cited observation in the older literature (L. Sprague de Camp's Lost Continents) makes the point exactly: apart from orichalcum, Plato describes no technique unknown to his own time.

NOT IN PLATO: A MOTHER CIVILISATION

Plato's Atlantis does not seed Egypt, Mesopotamia, or the Americas with colonists bearing civilisation. Egypt, in his story, is older in its records and a thousand years younger than Athens in its founding — and quite independent of Atlantis, which tried to enslave it and failed. The diffusionist Atlantis that fathers all ancient cultures is Ignatius Donnelly's (1882), not Plato's.

NOT IN PLATO: SURVIVORS AND REFUGEES

No Atlantean survivors escape to found new nations; no fleet carries the wisdom west or east. The island disappears entire, and its enemies' army with it. Every "Atlantean diaspora" in later literature is an addition with no textual warrant.

NOT IN PLATO: A DATE IN YEARS BCE

Plato gives a round figure of years before Solon (Section VI). He gives no year, no era, no synchronism with any datable event. All precise Atlantean dates in later literature — including the famous c. 9600 BCE — are computations or inventions laid over that round figure.

Conversely — and in fairness — some things the modern reader may assume are modern embroidery are in Plato, and should be credited to him: the concentric rings of land and water; the red-glowing metal orichalcum; the elephants; the hot and cold springs; the bull sacrifice over the inscribed pillar; the opposite continent beyond the ocean; and the moral mechanism of the fall — divine nature diluted by mortal mixture, wealth corrupting a once-lawful race. Plato's invention was vivid enough that two millennia of readers have mistaken its vividness for reportage. That is a tribute to the writing, not evidence for the island.

XIX. Reading a Tier B Text Well

Atlantis is the type specimen of a Tier B text: an ancient claim, magnificently framed, independently unattested, and argued about since antiquity itself. The disciplines this article has tried to practise are the ones the hub applies to every Tier B subject, from the antediluvian king lists onward, and they are worth stating plainly at the end.

Read the text before the tradition. Two short dialogues contain everything Plato said. Most of what "everyone knows" about Atlantis is not in them (Section XVIII), and most of what is in them — primeval Athens, the priest's theory of forgetting, the unfinished war — is missing from the popular version.

Keep the chain of custody visible. Every later witness — Crantor, Posidonius, Plutarch, Proclus — reaches Atlantis through Plato, and says so or shows it. A tradition with one source has one source, however many voices repeat it.

Let a claim be what it is. The 9,000 years is a claim a character makes about records no one saw. The pillars of Crantor are a claim priests reportedly made. The Aristotle quip is a claim modern scholarship made about Strabo, and got wrong. Tier B writing earns its keep by labelling each of these exactly, neither inflating a claim into a fact nor sneering at it for being a claim. Plato's story has survived twenty-four centuries of intelligent readers; it does not need our credulity, and it has never been much harmed by our scepticism.

What remains, when the labelling is done, is not a small thing: the earliest surviving work of political fiction in the Western tradition, a story a philosopher built to show his ideal city at war and his real city its own worst enemy — and built so well that the ancient world began, immediately, to argue about whether it was true. That argument, faithfully continued, is this hub's whole subject. The record, the tradition, and the telling are kept apart here — so that each can be appreciated for what it actually is.
