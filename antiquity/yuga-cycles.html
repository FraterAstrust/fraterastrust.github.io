I. What This Article Is — A Tier B Treatment

This is a Tier B article: Ancient Tradition. That label governs everything that follows, in the same way it governs this hub's treatments of the Sumerian King List and of Egypt's divine dynasties. Tier B does not mean "attested fact," and it does not mean "modern invention." It means that the material here survives in ancient texts, written in ancient languages by identifiable communities of authors, compilers, and astronomers, and that this article treats those texts as texts — what they say, when they were composed, how their numbers are built, and what later readers have made of them.

The tradition treated here is the Indian doctrine of the yugas: the four ages of the world — Kṛta, Tretā, Dvāpara, and Kali — through which righteousness declines by quarters until the cycle dissolves and begins again. Around that core the tradition built the largest time-scheme of the ancient world: ages nested inside ages, up to a day of Brahmā lasting 4,320,000,000 human years and a lifetime for Brahmā of 311,040,000,000,000 human years — every figure set out in Sections IV to VI, with the arithmetic shown and checked.

Tier discipline, in practice, means this. When the Manusmṛti assigns 4,800 divine years to the Kṛta Yuga, this article reports that the text assigns them. It does not narrate a golden age that began millions of years ago. When the tradition dates the present Kali's beginning to 3102 BCE, this article presents that date as the tradition's own chronological anchor — a claim about 3102 BCE, made in texts of the first millennium CE — not as an event of 3102 BCE. The texts are real, their real dates are stated throughout, and the deep antiquity the scheme asserts is presented as the tradition's claim.

// A NOTE ON LANGUAGE THROUGHOUT //

Verbs matter in this article. "The Manusmṛti states," "the Purāṇas reckon," "Āryabhaṭa counts," and "the tradition dates" are the normal forms here. Where a scholar's interpretation is given, the scholar is named. Where a number comes from a specific text, the text is named. No cosmic duration, and no date for the beginning of an age, should be read as the Archive's statement of historical fact.

That restraint is not scepticism for its own sake. It is what makes the scheme legible as an achievement: a doctrine that begins as dice-names, is systematised by a law-book of the centuries around the turn of the Common Era, acquires its superstructure in the Purāṇas, and is then taken up — numbers and all — as the computational framework of classical Indian astronomy, is a documentable intellectual history of the first importance. Its texts, its arithmetic, and its two millennia of continuous use are the subject here.

One boundary, at the outset: the full treatment of Indian thought belongs to a future India hub of this archive, planned but not yet built. What follows is the tradition's Tier B presentation within Before Kemet — complete on the yuga scheme itself, and deliberately not a survey of Indian religion as a whole.

II. The Doctrine in Outline — Four Ages, One Turning Wheel

The doctrine, in classical form, can be stated in a paragraph. Time runs in a repeating cycle of four ages, called yugas. The four, in order, are the Kṛta Yuga — also called the Satya Yuga, the age of truth — the Tretā Yuga, the Dvāpara Yuga, and the Kali Yuga. The sequence is a descent: in the Kṛta, dharma — righteousness, order, the way things ought to stand — is complete; in each succeeding age a quarter of it is lost, until in the Kali one quarter remains. When the Kali has run its course, the cycle does not end in a final judgement. It dissolves, and a new Kṛta begins.

Everything else is elaboration, running in two directions. Downward, the decline is described in human terms: lifespan shortens, stature diminishes, strength, memory, and happiness decay, and social order inverts. Upward, the cycle is nested inside larger cycles of the same shape — four ages form one mahāyuga, seventy-one mahāyugas a manvantara, fourteen manvantaras a kalpa, and kalpas the days, years, and lifetime of Brahmā. Section III takes the downward elaboration; Sections IV to VI take the upward one.

1 — KṚTA (SATYA) YUGA

The perfected age. Dharma stands complete, on four legs. The texts describe an age without disease, decay, or labour as later ages know it, in which human life is at its longest and righteousness requires no effort.

2 — TRETĀ YUGA

Dharma diminished by one quarter; the bull stands on three legs. Sacrifice and ordered ritual arise, in several accounts, precisely because spontaneous righteousness no longer suffices.

3 — DVĀPARA YUGA

Dharma at one half; the bull on two legs. The age of division and doubt, in which scripture, lifespan, and virtue are all halved against the Kṛta standard.

4 — KALI YUGA

Dharma at one quarter; the bull on a single leg. The present age, in the tradition's own placement: the age of strife, short life, and inverted order, at whose end the cycle dissolves and renews. Not to be confused with the goddess Kālī, a distinction Section III notes.

Two features of the outline should be fixed now, because modern retellings routinely lose both. First, the sequence is cyclical without remainder: the Kali is not a terminus but the last quarter of a wheel that has turned before and will turn again, at every scale. Second, the present age is, on the tradition's own reckoning, near the beginning of its Kali, not near its end — the Kali lasts 432,000 years, of which the tradition counts only a little over five thousand elapsed. The popular image of an imminent yuga-ending draws on the compressed modern re-mappings of Section XVI, not on the ancient numbers.

A word on the word: Sanskrit yuga derives from the root yuj, to yoke or join — the same root that gives yoga. In the earliest Vedic texts it can mean a generation or a span quite generally; its technical sense, one of the four world ages, is the product of the development traced in Sections VII to X. The doctrine's moral physics should also be noted now: dharma is treated as a quantity in the world that is spent across the cycle, and the durations of the ages — standing in the exact ratio 4:3:2:1 — are the arithmetic form of that spending. Section V returns to the ratio, and to the one major classical author who rejected it.

III. Dharma in Decline — The Bull Loses Its Legs

The tradition's favourite image for the decline is an animal. Dharma is figured as a bull — the earth a cow beside him — standing on four legs in the Kṛta, three in the Tretā, two in the Dvāpara, and one in the Kali. The image receives its most developed telling in the Bhāgavata Purāṇa (1.17), where the bull, encountered on a single leg, names his four legs in paraphrase as austerity, purity, mercy, and truth, and recounts how the advancing Kali has broken three of them, leaving truth alone — and that one under attack. The passage is a dialogue, not a chronicle: theology in narrative dress, and cited here as such.

What declines, in the texts' own lists, is comprehensive. The Manusmṛti (1.81–86, in paraphrase) gives the human measure directly: lifespan is four hundred years in the Kṛta, three hundred in the Tretā, two hundred in the Dvāpara, and one hundred in the Kali — the lifespan and the yuga shrinking together, each in its own proportion. Purāṇic descriptions extend the catalogue: stature diminishes age by age, as do strength, memory, beauty, and happiness, and the bonds of the social order loosen and invert.

The Kali Catalogue

The fullest catalogue of the Kali's symptoms is the Bhāgavata Purāṇa's twelfth book (12.2, in paraphrase): wealth alone will count as the sign of good birth; might will be taken for right; a sacred thread will suffice to make a brahmin; kings will be little better than thieves, and thieves will take to thrones; life at the age's depth will shrink to twenty or thirty years. The Viṣṇu Purāṇa (4.24 and 6.1) carries a parallel catalogue. These passages are the tradition's moralised portrait of its own present age, composed in the early centuries CE and after — decline-literature of a kind Hesiod's five races exemplify in Greek, though no historical connection between the schemes is claimed here.

A point of terminology, because the confusion is constant: the Kali of Kali Yuga is not the goddess Kālī. Kali, with a short a, is the losing dice throw, then the age, and the male personification of strife who strikes the bull in the Bhāgavata passage; Kālī, with a long ā, is the fierce goddess of the Devī tradition. The yuga doctrine's Kali belongs to the dice board first — as Section VII will show.

What the decline is for, within the tradition, should not be missed: the Kali catalogues are diagnostic, not despairing. To know which age one lives in is to know which duties are possible, and several texts conclude that in the Kali the simplest practices carry the weight that elaborate sacrifice carried in the Kṛta. Dharma is diminished by quarters, but never, within the cycle, extinguished. One leg still stands.

IV. The Arithmetic, I — Divine Years, Dawns, and Twilights

The yuga scheme is the ancient world's age-doctrine that most insists on its own arithmetic — exact, consistent, and checkable. This section and the two that follow set it out in full, principally from the Manusmṛti (1.64–73) and the Viṣṇu Purāṇa (1.3), whose statements agree. Every multiplication has been re-performed for this article.

The Unit — The Divine Year

The durations are stated, in the first instance, not in human years but in divine years. The conversion is fixed by the texts: one divine year equals 360 human years. The Manusmṛti (1.66–67, in paraphrase) reaches the ratio through the gods' calendar — a human year is a day and night of the gods, their day being the sun's northern course and their night its southern — so a divine year of 360 divine days amounts to 360 human years. The figure 360 is the schematic year of ancient calendrics generally: a mark, here as at Babylon, of constructed arithmetic rather than accumulated record.

Dawn, Day, and Twilight — Sandhyā and Sandhyāṁśa

Each yuga has three parts: a dawn (sandhyā), the yuga proper, and a twilight (sandhyāṁśa). Dawn and twilight are equal, each one tenth of the main period: the Kṛta's main period is 4,000 divine years, its dawn 400 and its twilight 400, and the age as a whole — the figure used in all larger sums — is 4,800 divine years. The same construction runs through all four ages, as the table gives it. The ages are not bare numbers but periods with thresholds: the doctrine builds transition into the unit.

Yuga Dawn (sandhyā) Main Period Twilight (sandhyāṁśa) Total — Divine Years Total — Human Years (× 360)
Kṛta (Satya) 400 4,000 400 4,800 1,728,000
Tretā 300 3,000 300 3,600 1,296,000
Dvāpara 200 2,000 200 2,400 864,000
Kali 100 1,000 100 1,200 432,000
One mahāyuga (all four ages) 12,000 4,320,000

The sums check in both units: 4,800 + 3,600 + 2,400 + 1,200 = 12,000 divine years, and 1,728,000 + 1,296,000 + 864,000 + 432,000 = 4,320,000 human years; the main periods total 10,000 divine years and the eight transitional portions 2,000. No figure in the table is an editorial reconstruction; each is stated, in this form, by the Manusmṛti and repeated across the Purāṇic literature.

Below the yuga, the Manusmṛti's time-chapter (1.64) builds the scale from the bottom up — from the blink of an eye, through kāṣṭhā, kalā, and muhūrta, to the human day — and climbs without a break from the blink to the day of Brahmā. The yuga arithmetic is the upper storey of a single continuous ladder of time. One figure in the table will recur in Section XIV: the Kali's total, 1,200 × 360 = 432,000, is the same figure Berossus assigns to his ten antediluvian kings — a pattern to record, not a borrowing to assert.

V. The Arithmetic, II — The Mahāyuga and the 4:3:2:1 Ratio

The four ages together form one great age: the mahāyuga, also called the caturyuga, the "four-yuga" set — 12,000 divine years, or 4,320,000 human years. Within it the four ages stand in the exact ratio 4:3:2:1, holding at every level of the anatomy: main periods (4,000 : 3,000 : 2,000 : 1,000), dawns and twilights (400 : 300 : 200 : 100), totals (4,800 : 3,600 : 2,400 : 1,200), and human-year equivalents (1,728,000 : 1,296,000 : 864,000 : 432,000). It is the same ratio in which dharma itself declines — four legs, three, two, one — and that identity is the scheme's central idea expressed numerically: duration and righteousness are the same quantity, counted two ways.

The ratio is also a set of shares: the Kṛta takes 40% of the mahāyuga, the Tretā 30%, the Dvāpara 20%, and the Kali 10% — and it matches, rung for rung, the dharma-bull's legs (4, 3, 2, 1) and Manu's lifespans (400, 300, 200, 100 years). The mahāyuga is the scheme's working unit for everything above it: the period after which the cycle of ages simply repeats, within the larger structures of Section VI, and the period the astronomers adopted wholesale as their computational frame (Section XII). That double life, theological and astronomical, is unique among the ancient world's age-schemes: no Sumerian sar-total was ever asked to do duty as a working constant of mathematical astronomy. The yuga was.

One classical divergence belongs here, at the level of the ratio itself, though its author is treated in full in Section XII. The astronomer Āryabhaṭa (born 476 CE), in the Āryabhaṭīya of 499 CE, keeps the mahāyuga at 4,320,000 years but divides it into four equal quarters — four pādas of 1,080,000 years each — rejecting the 4:3:2:1 proportion of the law-book and the Purāṇas. His reasons were astronomical: an equal division suited the mean-motion calculations his system was built to perform. The divergence is real, it is his, and later Indian astronomers and commentators noticed and debated it. This article flags it wherever the ratio is stated, because a reader who meets Āryabhaṭa's quartered mahāyuga elsewhere deserves to know that the two divisions are different systems sharing a total — not a scribal error in either.

// TWO DIVISIONS, ONE TOTAL //

Purāṇic and legal tradition: 4,320,000 years divided 4:3:2:1. Āryabhaṭa: 4,320,000 years divided 1:1:1:1 (1,080,000 each). Both are classical; they are not interchangeable, and any presentation that silently mixes them has left both sources behind.

Schematic ratios of this sort are how ancient systematic thought announces itself — as with the King List's whole sar and the Egyptian Old Chronicle's whole Sothic cycles. The pattern is the evidence: of construction by learned systematists working to a design. That the numbers are designed is not a debunking; it is a description of the texts' genre, and the precondition of reading them well.

VI. The Arithmetic, III — Manvantara, Kalpa, and the Life of Brahmā

Above the mahāyuga, the scheme keeps climbing — the point at which it separates itself from every other ancient system of ages. The Sumerian lists total hundreds of thousands of years; the Egyptian sums, tens of thousands. The Indian superstructure, stated in the Manusmṛti (1.72–79) and developed across the Purāṇas, totals billions at its working level and hundreds of trillions at its limit, without breaking its own arithmetic at any rung.

The Manvantara — The Reign of a Manu

Seventy-one mahāyugas form one manvantara — the period "between Manus," the reign of one Manu, progenitor and lawgiver of mankind for that period: 71 × 4,320,000 = 306,720,000 human years. Each manvantara, in the Purāṇic elaboration, has its own Manu, Indra, gods, and seven ṛṣis — a whole administration of the world, replaced entire at each period's end. The current manvantara is the seventh of fourteen, that of Vaivasvata Manu: the tradition's own coordinate for the present moment.

The Kalpa — One Day of Brahmā

Fourteen manvantaras, with their twilights, form one kalpa — one day of Brahmā. The arithmetic repays care. Fourteen manvantaras at 71 mahāyugas each give 14 × 71 = 994 mahāyugas. The kalpa also contains fifteen transitional twilights (sandhis) — before the first manvantara, after the last, and between each pair — each of Kṛta length: 4,800 divine years, or 0.4 of a mahāyuga. Fifteen sandhis contribute 15 × 0.4 = 6 mahāyugas. The total is exact: 994 + 6 = 1,000 mahāyugas = 4,320,000,000 human years.

Period Composition In Mahāyugas In Human Years
Mahāyuga (caturyuga) 4 yugas, 4:3:2:1 1 4,320,000
Manvantara 71 mahāyugas 71 306,720,000
15 sandhis (twilights) 15 × Kṛta length (1,728,000 yrs) 6 25,920,000
Kalpa = 1 day of Brahmā 994 + 6 mahāyugas 1,000 4,320,000,000
1 full day-and-night of Brahmā Day + night 2,000 8,640,000,000
Life of Brahmā 100 years of Brahmā 72,000,000 311,040,000,000,000

The remaining multiplications, for the record: 15 × 1,728,000 = 25,920,000, and 4,294,080,000 + 25,920,000 = 4,320,000,000 exactly. Brahmā's night equals his day, so his day-and-night is 8,640,000,000 years; his year counts 360 of them (3,110,400,000,000 years); his life counts one hundred of his years: 311,040,000,000,000 human years — 311.04 trillion, the largest time-total in any ancient literature known to this archive. The kalpa's round thousand mahāyugas is achieved, not assumed — 994 + 6.

The tradition also gives the present moment a full set of coordinates inside this immensity. In the received Purāṇic placement, Brahmā is in the first day — the Śvetavārāha kalpa — of his fifty-first year; within that day, the seventh manvantara is current; within it, the twenty-eighth mahāyuga; and within that, the Kali, begun — by Section XI's reckoning — in 3102 BCE. Every element of that address is a claim of the tradition, not a finding of this archive. But as systematic world-building it has no ancient rival: the scheme can tell you, to the yuga, where you stand in a 311-trillion-year life.

One comparison of scale, offered as context and not as adjudication: the kalpa, at 4.32 billion years, is of the same order as the age modern geology assigns to the Earth (about 4.54 billion years). The resemblance is a coincidence of scale, not a correspondence of content — the Purāṇic figure is generated by pure internal arithmetic (1,000 × 12,000 × 360), not by observation. What needs no overstatement is the appetite for the scale itself: an ancient tradition thinking habitually in billions, when its neighbours' largest totals ran to hundreds of thousands.

VII. Dice Throws — The Names Before the System

The yuga system was not revealed whole. Its names are older than its arithmetic, and they come from the dice board. In Vedic India, dice — cast with vibhītaka nuts — were thrown in fours, and the throws had names. The throw of four was the winning cast, called kṛta, "made, accomplished"; of three, tretā; of two, dvāpara; and of one kali, the losing cast, the throw of strife and ruin. The four age-names of the later system are these four throws, in descending order of value — a passage Gonzalez-Reimann's The Mahābhārata and the Yugas (2002), the standard study of the doctrine's formation, traces in detail.

The earliest surviving text to set the four names in a row as ages is the Aitareya Brāhmaṇa, a prose commentary on the Ṛgveda of the middle of the first millennium BCE (conventionally c. 800–600 BCE, with the usual cautions for orally transmitted Vedic prose). In a celebrated passage (Book 7), the ages are personified by their postures, in paraphrase: Kali lies asleep; Dvāpara is stirring; Tretā is standing; Kṛta walks abroad. The passage's burden is practical — fortune favours the one who keeps moving — but its importance here is taxonomic: the four dice-names already stand in canonical descending order as figures for states of the world, centuries before any surviving text assigns them durations. The names begin as throws; they become postures; they will become periods.

KṚTA — THE THROW OF FOUR

The winning cast: the throw that is "made," complete, accomplished. As an age-name it carries its dice sense with it — the age in which everything comes up whole. Its other name, Satya, "truth," is a theological overlay on the gambler's term.

Tretā, from tri, three, and Dvāpara, from dva, two, complete the series between them; Kali, the throw of one, is the losing cast and a word for strife and discord — as the last age's name it needs no allegory: the world has thrown its lowest number.

Dice are not a decorative origin; they explain the system's shape. A four-cast series is finite, ordered, and exhaustive — there is no fifth throw — and its values descend in the proportion the later arithmetic will formalise: four, three, two, one. The epic's great dice match, in which the Pāṇḍavas lose a kingdom on loaded throws, is the doctrine's narrative twin, and the Ṛgveda's gambler's hymn (10.34) shows how central the cast was to early Indian reflection on fortune and loss. The yuga doctrine is that reflection, systematised into cosmology.

What the Brāhmaṇa passage does not yet contain: no durations, no divine years, no mahāyuga, no cosmic cycle of dissolution and renewal. Between the Aitareya's postures and the numbered ages of the Manusmṛti lie centuries of development the record lets us trace only in part — through the epic, next, where the ages acquire their moral narrative.

VIII. The Epic — Yugas in the Mahābhārata

The Mahābhārata — the vast Sanskrit epic compiled over centuries and conventionally dated, in its growth toward the received form, to roughly 400 BCE–400 CE — is the first surviving literature in which the yuga doctrine does sustained narrative work. The epic is set, by its own internal placement, at the junction of the Dvāpara and Kali ages: the great war, and the death of Kṛṣṇa that follows it, mark the hinge on which the present, worst age turns. That placement is the epic's claim about itself.

Two passages carry the greatest weight. In the Vana Parvan (Book 3), the sage Mārkaṇḍeya — presented as a seer who has survived dissolutions of the world — discourses to the exiled Yudhiṣṭhira on the ages and the end of the Kali, in material Gonzalez-Reimann treats as central to the doctrine's epic stratum. Elsewhere in the same book, Hanumān explains the ages to his brother Bhīma — in paraphrase, that in the Kṛta dharma was whole, that each succeeding age loses a quarter, and that he himself belongs to an earlier age and has diminished with the world's turning. The epic also knows the first form of the expectation of Section XIII: at the Kali's depth, a restorer — named Kalkī, later Kalki — will arise to renew the Kṛta.

What the Epic Adds — and What It Still Lacks

The epic stratum gives the doctrine three things the Brāhmaṇa lacked: a moral narrative, a placement for the present (the epic's own events stand at an age-boundary), and a forward expectation (the restorer at the end). What it does not yet present in any single passage is the full numbered scheme — the divine years, dawns and twilights, and superstructure set out as one ladder. The earliest fully systematic statement, the whole arithmetic in one place, belongs to the law-book taken up next. The distinction matters: the doctrine has a datable literary history, and "the texts say" is always shorthand for specific texts, of specific centuries.

The epic's yuga material also fixes the doctrine's enduring emotional register. In the Brāhmaṇas the ages are a priestly taxonomy; in the Purāṇas they will become a theologian's and an astronomer's system. In the epic they are the explanation offered to exiles for why the world they live in is worse than the world their grandfathers described — a theodicy of decline, holding both that dharma is real and that it is losing. Every later systematisation carries that charge: the numbers are long because the loss they measure is slow, and the cycle renews because the loss is not final.

IX. Manu — The First Systematic Statement

The earliest fully systematic statement of the yuga arithmetic to survive is in a law-book: the Manusmṛti, the Laws of Manu, at 1.64–86, conventionally dated to roughly the 2nd century BCE to the 2nd century CE in its compilation. Its date as a text is a date of the centuries BCE/CE, and the cosmic antiquity its first chapter describes is its doctrine, not its age. The Tier B discipline consists, once again, in holding those two facts in one sentence.

The passage's structure is a ladder, climbed in order: 1.64–65 build human time from the blink of an eye to the day and night; 1.66–67 establish the divine year and the 360:1 conversion; 1.68–71 give the four yugas with their dawns and twilights — the table of Section IV is, in substance, these verses — and their sum of 12,000 divine years; 1.72–73 state the kalpa, a thousand mahāyugas as Brahmā's day with a night of equal length; 1.74–79 take up the manvantaras; and 1.81–86 return to the human scale, giving the declining lifespans — 400, 300, 200, 100. In twenty-three verses the whole scheme of Sections IV to VI is present.

Why is the cosmos in a law-book at all? The text's own frame answers: the Manusmṛti presents its law as mediated through Manu — not a man but an office, recurring in every manvantara. The time-scheme of Chapter 1 is the law's letterhead: it establishes the scale on which the lawgiver speaks, the authority behind the speaking, and the condition of the audience — a Kali-age mankind whose hundred-year span the same chapter has just measured. Mitchiner's work on the Manus and the seven ṛṣis (Sources) is the standard guide to this personnel of the superstructure.

This article's paraphrases of Manu follow the two standard modern translations — Patrick Olivelle's The Law Code of Manu (Oxford, 2004) and Wendy Doniger and Brian K. Smith's The Laws of Manu (Penguin, 1991) — whose renderings of the time-chapter agree on every figure given here. No quotation from Manu appears in this article, in line with the hub's rule; the doctrine is numerical, and its numbers quote themselves.

X. The Purāṇas — The Scheme in Its Canonical Form

The scheme reaches its canonical form in the Purāṇas — the great Sanskrit compendia whose received forms belong to the early centuries CE and onward into the first millennium CE, compiled over long periods from older material. Rocher's The Purāṇas (1986), the standard survey, supplies the necessary caution: the Purāṇas are not books with publication dates but traditions with strata, and any date given for one is a date for a received form. Within that caution, the yuga material is among their most stable content — because it is arithmetic, and arithmetic survives copying intact.

The Viṣṇu Purāṇa states the time-scheme at 1.3, in a chapter paralleling Manu's ladder, and returns to the Kali at 4.24, where a prophetic history of Kali-age kings closes with the age's end and its restorer, and at 6.1 with a Kali catalogue. H. H. Wilson's English translation of 1840 — the first of any Purāṇa into a European language — is the channel through which the scheme first reached Western readers, and is cited in the Sources with its age stated, to be used as Wilson's successors use it: gratefully, and with checking. The Bhāgavata Purāṇa contributes the bull of dharma (1.17), the Kali catalogue and Kalki prophecy (12.2), and the classification of dissolutions (12.4).

What the Purāṇas Add

THE MANVANTARA PERSONNEL

The fourteen Manus are named, in order, with their Indras, gods, and seven ṛṣis — the superstructure of Section VI furnished with a full civil service, period by period.

GENEALOGY AND PROPHECY INSIDE THE FRAME

The Purāṇas' royal genealogies are hung inside the manvantara frame, and Viṣṇu Purāṇa 4.24 presents the Kali age's kings as prophecy — dynasties whose earlier reaches correspond in part to historical kings of the centuries BCE/CE, written as foretold, in the manner scholarship calls vaticinium ex eventu. The kinds of dissolution, pralaya, are likewise classified and assigned to their rungs (Section XIII).

The Purāṇic stratum also completes the scheme's geography of the present: the full coordinate address of Section VI — kalpa name, manvantara number, mahāyuga number — becomes standard equipment, repeated in the saṅkalpa, the formal statement of time and place with which Hindu ritual still begins. A cosmology recited before ordinary worship, as a date-line, has passed from doctrine into calendar — the bridge to the next two sections, where the scheme acquires its single fixed date and its second career in astronomy.

XI. The Kali Epoch — 3102 BCE as the Tradition's Anchor

A cycle of 4,320,000 years is a frame, not a chronology. To become a chronology it needs one fixed point, and the Indian tradition has one: the present Kali Yuga, the tradition holds, began in 3102 BCE. This section states exactly what kind of date that is, because no claim in this article is more often misstated in both directions — credited as a record of an event in 3102 BCE, or dismissed as a modern invention. It is neither. It is an ancient astronomers' epoch: a computed starting point, attested in texts and inscriptions of the first millennium CE, from which elapsed Kali years are counted.

The attestation is precise. The astronomer Āryabhaṭa, in the Āryabhaṭīya of 499 CE, dates his own work by the epoch: in paraphrase of the Kālakriyā section, when sixty times sixty years — 3,600 years — of the Kali had elapsed, he was twenty-three years old. The arithmetic checks: 3,600 years counted from 3102 BCE, without a year zero, lands in 499 CE. Āryabhaṭa is not reporting a tradition at a distance; he is using the epoch as his calendar's zero point. A century later the reckoning is monumental: the Aihole inscription of Pulakeśin II, dated 634 CE (Śaka 556), gives its date in paraphrase as 3,735 years after the Bhārata war — and 3,735 counted back from 634 CE reaches the same 3102 BCE.

Witness Date of the Witness What It States Implied Epoch
Āryabhaṭa, Āryabhaṭīya 499 CE 3,600 years of Kali elapsed at composition; author aged 23 3102 BCE
Aihole inscription of Pulakeśin II 634 CE 3,735 years since the Bhārata war; Śaka 556 3102 BCE

What is claimed to have happened at the epoch? In the tradition's terms, the Kali's beginning coincides with the close of the epic age — the Bhārata war and the departure of Kṛṣṇa stand at its threshold. That is the tradition's claim about 3102 BCE, labelled accordingly: no text of 3102 BCE records it, no archaeology attests an age-boundary there, and the epoch's documentary trail begins in 499 CE, some three and a half millennia after the date it names. Mitchiner's work on yuga chronology (Sources) examines how the figure was reached — most plausibly as a back-calculation to the astronomers' posited planetary conjunction at the Kali's start, a computational origin-point rather than an observed event. The methodological point is the King List's point: a computed, schematic origin, sincerely used for two millennia, is a fact about the tradition that used it — not, without independent evidence, a fact about the year it names.

// HOW TO STATE THE DATE //

Correct, in this hub's usage: "the tradition dates the present Kali's beginning to 3102 BCE; the epoch is attested in use from 499 CE." Incorrect: "the Kali Yuga began in 3102 BCE" (a claim narrated as an event), and equally incorrect: "3102 BCE was invented by modern writers" (its users include Āryabhaṭa and a seventh-century royal inscription).

The epoch is also still in use: Hindu almanacs (pañcāṅgas) print the elapsed Kali year — the count now stands a little past 5,100 — and the saṅkalpa recitation of Section X locates present rituals by it. The yuga scheme is not a dead doctrine for historians to reconstruct. It is a living calendar with an ancient epoch.

XII. Astronomy's Yuga — The Siddhāntas and Āryabhaṭa's Variant

The yuga scheme's second career is the one no other ancient age-doctrine had: it became the working frame of a mathematical astronomy. The siddhāntas — the systematic astronomical treatises of classical India, composed across the first millennium CE — adopted the mahāyuga and the kalpa as their computational periods, for a technical reason: if a period exists in which every planet completes a whole number of revolutions, all mean positions can be computed from that period's revolution-counts by simple proportion, for any date. The mahāyuga of 4,320,000 years serves as exactly such a period, and mean-motion astronomy becomes arithmetic on its integers.

Āryabhaṭa's Āryabhaṭīya (499 CE) states its counts in the opening Gītikā section, in paraphrase: in one mahāyuga the Sun makes 4,320,000 revolutions — one per year, by construction — and the Moon 57,753,336, from which the mean lunation follows by division; the counts for the planets, nodes, and apsides work identically. The yuga, in this literature, is not a belief about the past. It is a least common multiple with a theology attached. Clark's translation (1930) and Shukla and Sarma's edition (1976) are the standard English points of entry (Sources).

Āryabhaṭa's Equal Quarters — The Divergence Stated Plainly

The Purāṇic mahāyuga divides 4:3:2:1. Āryabhaṭa divides his mahāyuga into four equal pādas (quarters) of 1,080,000 years each: 4 × 1,080,000 = 4,320,000 — the same total, differently shared. On his division the Kali quarter is no shorter than the Kṛta quarter; the ages of his astronomical system are equal watches, not a descending scale, and his commentators discuss the consequence at length. The divergence is the single most consequential variant in the scheme's classical history, and it is stated wherever this article states the ratio.

Feature Purāṇic / Legal Scheme Āryabhaṭa's Scheme
Mahāyuga total 4,320,000 years 4,320,000 years
Division 4:3:2:1 Four equal pādas
Kṛta length 1,728,000 years 1,080,000 years
Kali length 432,000 years 1,080,000 years (as a quarter)

Later astronomers did not simply follow him: the siddhānta tradition largely worked with kalpa-based counts and the Purāṇic frame, and commentators noted the friction. "The Indian tradition" is not a monolith — it is a millennium of named authors agreeing on a total, disagreeing on a division, and saying so in surviving texts. A related point: because the siddhāntas posit the planets' mean positions in conjunction at the Kali epoch, 3102 BCE functions in their mathematics as a defined zero-point of mean longitude, whose utility does not depend on any planet having observably stood there. Reading the epoch as the record of an observed alignment mistakes a computational convention for an observation report.

XIII. End and Renewal — Pralaya and the Kalki Expectation

Every rung of the ladder of Section VI has its dissolution, named pralaya — the resolution of the manifest world into its unmanifest ground. The Bhāgavata Purāṇa (12.4, in paraphrase) classifies four kinds: the constant dissolution (nitya), the ceaseless perishing of beings; the occasional (naimittika), when Brahmā's day ends and the lower worlds are submerged for his night; the elemental (prākṛtika), at the end of Brahmā's hundred years; and the final (ātyantika), the liberation of the individual self, which is not an event in cosmic time at all. Endings are as tiered as durations — and every one, except the last, is followed by re-emergence.

Within the mahāyuga, the transition from Kali back to Kṛta is gentler than the kalpa-endings: the Purāṇic accounts describe a destruction of the age's corrupted order and the survival of a remnant, in the tradition's geography gathered at the village of Śambhala, from whom the renewed Kṛta is peopled. The wheel's smallest turn is a reset, not an annihilation — and the texts' emphasis falls on the figure who performs it.

Kalki — The Restorer at the Tradition's Later Edge

That figure is Kalki — Kalkī in the epic's spelling — the coming avatāra of Viṣṇu, presented here at the tradition's later edge, where he stratigraphically belongs. The epic knows the prophecy in an early form (Section VIII); the Viṣṇu Purāṇa (4.24, in paraphrase) gives him a birthplace — Śambhala — a father, Viṣṇuyaśas, and a work: the destruction of the Kali's corrupt rulers and the re-establishment of dharma, after which the Kṛta begins; the Bhāgavata Purāṇa (12.2) repeats the expectation with the swift horse and the sword; and a later Kalki Purāṇa expands his story into a full biography. The development — epic seed, Purāṇic form, late expansion — grows more detailed the later the text: the signature of a living tradition elaborating its hope, not of a report being preserved.

// NOT AN IMMINENT DATE //

On the classical numbers, Kalki belongs to the Kali's end — some 427,000 years in the tradition's future. Presentations that place the age's end near the present are using the compressed modern cycles of Section XVI, in which the whole Kali lasts 1,200 years. The ancient scheme is the long one.

The Kalki expectation completes the doctrine's answer to its own bleakest catalogue. The system never leaves the Kali masterless at its end: the renewal Kalki brings is not an interruption of the cycle but its mechanism — the means by which the wheel, having descended to its lowest throw, comes up Kṛta again. Decline, dissolution, and restoration are one doctrine in three tenses.

XIV. Comparative Deep Time — Beside Sumer, Egypt, and Iran

Four schemes stand side by side in this hub. The Sumerian King List assigns eight kings before the flood 241,200 years, built from whole sar of 3,600. The Egyptian tradition, in the Turin Canon and Manetho, places gods and Followers of Horus before Menes with sums in the tens of thousands of years. The Iranian tradition, in the Zoroastrian Bundahishn, bounds the world's whole career within 12,000 years, in four periods of 3,000. And the Indian scheme runs to 4,320,000 years for a single mahāyuga and 311,040,000,000,000 for the life of Brahmā. The first two stand in full at Antediluvian King Lists and Divine Dynasties; the Bundahishn — a Pahlavi compilation of c. the 9th century CE preserving older Sasanian and Avestan material — is summarised here in paraphrase, its scheme tiered as that tradition's claim.

Scheme Text / Tradition Largest Stated Span Shape of Time
Sumerian King List (WB 444 tradition) 241,200 yrs before the flood Linear succession, flood as hinge
Egyptian Turin Canon; Manetho 23,200 + x yrs before Menes (Canon's cumulative sum) Linear succession from the First Time
Iranian Bundahishn (Zoroastrian) 12,000 yrs for the world's whole career Bounded and linear: creation, mixture, renovation
Indian Manusmṛti; Purāṇas; siddhāntas 311,040,000,000,000 yrs (life of Brahmā) Cyclical at every scale; still running

The Iranian scheme is the yuga system's closest structural neighbour and sharpest contrast. In the Bundahishn's account, in paraphrase, limited time runs 12,000 years in four periods of 3,000: creation in spiritual form; creation material and unassailed; then the span of mixture, in which evil's assault is worked out through Zoroaster's age toward the final renovation (frašō.kərəti). The resemblances are real — numbered periods, an embattled present, a restorer at the end, the Saošyant answering in function to Kalki. The difference is total: the Bundahishn's 12,000 years happen once, and its renovation is final. The yuga wheel, at the same structural position, simply turns again.

One numerical thread needs this hub's usual care. Berossus (c. 290 BCE) assigns his ten antediluvian kings 432,000 years — 120 saroi — as the King List article records; the Kali's length is likewise 432,000, and the mahāyuga's 4,320,000 is that figure tenfold. Hamlet's Mill (1969) read the number-family as diffused precessional arithmetic, a reading the Tier C article discusses with its critical reception. What Tier B may say is narrower: the figure, built in Mesopotamia from the sar and in India from the divine year, stands at the centre of both systems, and the coincidence — transmission, shared schematic habits, or chance — is a documented pattern. A borrowing, in either direction, is asserted by neither side's texts, nor here.

XV. What the Yuga Scheme Does That the Others Do Not

Set side by side, the four schemes divide on three axes, and on each of them the yuga system stands alone — which is why this doctrine, alone among the four, remained a living instrument into the present.

IT IS CYCLICAL, NOT SUCCESSIVE

The King List and the Canon are successions: kingship descends, passes from holder to holder, and arrives at the compiler's present, behind which the deep past lies finished. The yuga past is not finished. The mahāyuga that contains the present Kali has turned twenty-seven times before in this manvantara alone, on the tradition's count, and the wheel's turning is the permanent condition of the world, not its prehistory.

IT IS STILL RUNNING

The Bundahishn's 12,000 years run once, toward a final renovation. The yuga present is an early morning of a very long day: five thousand years into a 432,000-year age, inside a kalpa that is one day in a life of 311 trillion years. Deep time, here, is not behind the believer. It is around him, mostly still to come.

ITS DEEP PAST IS ARITHMETIC, NOT KING-LIST

Sumer and Egypt people their deep past: named kings, named gods, reign by reign. The yuga scheme's deep past is unpeopled at its own scale — the manvantaras have Manus and sages, but the mahāyugas themselves repeat without individual histories, and the vastness is carried by the numbers alone. Where the King List argues legitimacy through succession, the yuga scheme argues order through proportion: the past is deep because the ratio, compounded, makes it so.

IT DOUBLES AS AN INSTRUMENT

No sar-total ever computed an eclipse. The mahāyuga did — as the siddhāntas' common period for planetary revolutions (Section XII), and the kalpa with it. A scheme of ages that a working astronomy adopts as its frame acquires a second kind of survival: it is preserved not only by those who believe its doctrine but by everyone who needs its arithmetic.

A fourth difference concerns the direction of consolation. The King List and the divine dynasties dignify the present by descent: kingship now is legitimate because it is the same office the first holders held. The yuga scheme dignifies nothing about the present — the Kali is, by its own account, the worst of times. What it offers instead is scale as a form of patience: the worst of times is a quarter of a cycle, the cycle a thousandth of a day, the day one morning in a life of a hundred years. Decline is real, in the doctrine, and also — by arithmetic — temporary, local, and small. No other ancient scheme built a ladder tall enough to make that move.

A South Asian after-note, stated carefully: the Tamil lost-land tradition treated in this hub's Lemuria article — Kumari Kandam — presupposes, in its modern form, exactly the deep South Asian past the yuga frame supplies. The two traditions are distinct in origin, and are linked here only at the level of that shared frame.

XVI. The Modern Compression — Theosophy and Yukteswar

This article has one section, and one only, for the modern fate of the scheme — because that fate belongs to Tier C and has its own article in this hub. The short form: the ancient numbers are unusable as a practical chronology, since no community can locate itself meaningfully inside a present age of 432,000 years, of which five thousand have passed. Modern esotericism solved the problem by compression — keeping the four ages, their names, and their 4:3:2:1 proportions, while exchanging the cosmic scale for a human or precessional one.

The compression's classic form is Sri Yukteswar Giri's, in The Holy Science (1894): the divine-year multiplier of 360 is declared a later error, the yuga lengths revert to their divine-year numbers read as human years — 4,800, 3,600, 2,400, 1,200 — and a full cycle of descent and re-ascent comes to 24,000 years, approximating the precessional cycle, with the present in an ascending Dvāpara. Theosophy likewise absorbed the yuga vocabulary into its root-race chronology. Both re-mappings are documented in full in this hub's Tier C article, Traditional Chronologies, at its Section V.

Why restore the ancient numbers in a separate Tier B article? Because the compression, left alone, inverts the record. In the modern versions the scheme is a modest 24,000-year cycle with an imminent turning — a doctrine of the near future. In its own texts it is a scheme of 4,320,000-year cycles inside a 311-trillion-year life, whose present age has barely begun — a doctrine of almost inconceivable patience. The two are different teachings sharing a vocabulary, and the tier system exists to keep exactly such pairs apart. Here the tradition stands at full size; there, its re-mappings stand documented as the work of named modern teachers, from 1894 forward.

XVII. Reading the Yuga Cycles Today

A reader who has followed this article holds the scheme whole, and can now be offered the closing service this hub's other Tier B articles offer: a short toolkit for meeting the yuga doctrine in the wild. Three checks will sort nearly everything.

CHECK THE KALI'S LENGTH

432,000 years: the ancient scheme (Manu, the Purāṇas, the almanacs). 1,200 years: Yukteswar's compression of 1894 and its descendants. The figure a source gives for the Kali is its fingerprint — it tells you at once which tradition, ancient or modern, you are reading.

CHECK THE DIVISION

4:3:2:1: the Purāṇic and legal scheme. Four equal quarters: Āryabhaṭa's astronomy. A source that gives equal ages while quoting Purāṇic durations — or the reverse — has blended two classical systems that their own authors kept distinct.

CHECK THE VERB

"The Kali Yuga began in 3102 BCE" narrates a claim as an event. "The tradition dates the Kali's beginning to 3102 BCE; the epoch is attested in use from 499 CE" states the record. The distance between those sentences is the whole method of this hub.

What remains, after the checks, is not a diminished doctrine but a clarified one — and an extraordinary survival. A set of dice-names became, across some fifteen centuries of datable texts, the armature of the ancient world's largest time-scheme; the scheme was adopted as the computational frame of a great astronomical tradition; it fixed an epoch, in 3102 BCE, that inscriptions used within a millennium and almanacs use today; and it still supplies the date-line recited at the opening of living ritual. The yuga scheme alone runs unbroken from a Brāhmaṇa's personified dice throws to a printed calendar on a present-day wall — a Tier B tradition in the fullest sense: ancient, textual, internally exact, and not yet finished.

The full treatment of Indian thought — the Vedic corpus, the philosophical schools, the astronomical tradition in its own right, and the yuga doctrine's place among them — belongs, as Section I noted, to this archive's future India hub, for which this article will serve as the yuga chapter's first draft. Within Before Kemet, its work is narrower and, the archive judges, sufficient: to seat the Indian ages beside the Sumerian kings and the Egyptian gods, at their texts' real dates, with their arithmetic intact and their claims labelled as claims — the record, the tradition, and the telling kept apart.
