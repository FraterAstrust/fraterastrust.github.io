This is a Tier A article: everything in it rests on excavated, dated, and published archaeological evidence. It is also, by the nature of its subject, an article about inference — and the two facts belong together, because this hub's tier discipline is nowhere more necessary than here. No boat built before the Holocene survives anywhere in the world. Not one hull, paddle, or fragment of a Pleistocene vessel has ever been recovered. The entire case for early seafaring is therefore built from things that are not boats: people present on landmasses that could only be reached over water; stone tools on islands that were never joined to any mainland; and cargoes — above all, obsidian — whose geological source can be identified in a laboratory and whose presence on the far side of a strait is a receipt for a voyage. Where the evidence is a dated artefact from a sealed layer, this article says so. Where it is a necessary inference from geography, it says that instead. And where a claim is contested in the published literature — as the Palaeolithic tools of Crete and the pygmy-hippo bone bed of Cyprus both are — it is printed at contested strength, with the critics named beside the claimants.

The shape of the argument is a funnel, narrowing from the certain to the documented. It begins with the strongest fact in the whole field: by about 65,000 years ago, modern humans had crossed the deep-water straits of Wallacea and settled Sahul — the Pleistocene continent of Australia and New Guinea — and no lowering of sea level ever made that journey walkable (treated in full in the companion article Sahul and Australia). Behind that fact lies an older and stranger one: hominins who were not modern humans at all were present on the Wallacean islands of Flores, Sulawesi, and Luzon hundreds of thousands — in the Flores case, about a million — years earlier, and they too arrived across water. It ends, in the Aegean of about 13,000 years ago, with the first sea-borne cargo that can be proved piece by piece: a few blades of Melian obsidian in Franchthi Cave, on the Greek mainland, chemically matched to quarries on the island of Melos. Between those poles, this article sets out the method that makes the second kind of proof possible — obsidian characterisation, step by step — and follows the Neolithic networks that obsidian sourcing has mapped from Cappadocia to Sardinia to the Carpathians, before weighing what island colonisation, taken as a whole, does and does not entitle us to claim.

One boundary is stated now and kept throughout. This article reconstructs that early people crossed water, what they carried, and — within limits — how often and how far. It does not reconstruct their boats. Every image of a Pleistocene vessel in popular books — the lashed raft, the bark canoe, the reed bundle — is a reasoned guess built from ethnography, experiment, and the physics of flotation, and §VI presents those guesses labelled as guesses. The archive's rule for this subject is the rule Cyprian Broodbank's work exemplifies: the sea crossings are data; the watercraft are modelling. Confusing the two is how a Tier A subject acquires a Tier C literature, and this article declines to add to it.

I. The Missing Boats — The Inference Problem

Begin with the absence, because everything else in this article is a way of reasoning around it. A watercraft is, almost by definition, among the least preservable objects a society can make. It is built of the most perishable materials in the prehistoric repertoire — wood, bark, reed, fibre, hide — assembled without metal fastenings. It is used in the most destructive environment those materials can meet: salt water, sun, and boring marine organisms. And when it is lost or abandoned, it is lost precisely where preservation is worst and recovery hardest, at a shoreline. For a Pleistocene boat to survive to the present, it would have to be abandoned in one of the rare settings that arrest decay — a peat bog, an anaerobic lake bed, permanently waterlogged sediment — and then remain undisturbed for tens of thousands of years while the sea rose some 120 metres and redrew every coastline it had ever floated from. The wonder is not that no Pleistocene boat survives. The wonder is that any early boat survives at all (§III).

The rising sea compounds the loss with a second, quieter one. The people who made the earliest crossings lived, as all coastal peoples do, at the water's edge — and the water's edge of 20,000, 50,000, or 65,000 years ago now lies far out under the modern sea, on the drowned continental shelves examined in this hub's Drowned Landscapes article. The camps from which the Sahul voyages were launched, the beaches where Aegean foragers beached their craft, the workshops where hulls were shaped: the great majority of the direct evidence for early seafaring, if it was ever deposited, sits on the seabed, dispersed and unrecoverable by any method archaeology currently possesses. Early seafaring is thus a subject whose central artefact class is missing twice over — once to decay, and once to submergence. What remains is the wake, not the vessel.

Reasoning from a wake requires discipline about what each kind of trace can bear, and the literature of early seafaring has not always shown it. A human bone on an island proves arrival; it does not prove a return voyage, a deliberate voyage, or a vessel of any particular kind. A stone tool on an island proves arrival by someone, at some date — and if the date rests on the tool's shape rather than on a dated layer, it proves considerably less than its discoverers may wish (§XV, on Crete). A piece of obsidian on a mainland shore proves more than either, in one respect: obsidian is a cargo, not a passenger. It does not swim, drift in a viable state, or arrive clutched by a castaway in any quantity that matters. A sourced blade is evidence of transport — of a crossing made with the intention, or at least the effect, of carrying goods — and repeated sourced blades are evidence of a route. That is why this article gives obsidian its centre: of all the traces early seafarers left, it is the only one that documents the traffic and not merely the landfall.

There is a third discipline, of language. The literature distinguishes, and this article follows, between sea crossing (any traversal of a water gap, including by accidental drift), seafaring (deliberate, repeatable voyaging in built craft), and colonisation (the establishment of a self-sustaining population on the far shore). The three are nested but not interchangeable, and much confusion in popular accounts comes from sliding between them. A single gravid castaway on a storm-blown raft is a sea crossing; she is not evidence of seafaring. A breeding population established across a 90-kilometre strait, by contrast, cannot be explained by one accident — population modelling of the Sahul crossing (§IV) requires either many voyages or voyages by many people, and at that point the word seafaring is earned. Each claim in this article is attached to the weakest of the three words that its evidence supports, and no stronger.

II. Grading the Evidence — A Scale of Strength

Because the whole subject is inferential, this article grades every claimed crossing it reports on a single explicit scale, stated here once and applied throughout. The scale has four grades. It is this article's own presentational device — the underlying judgements are those of the cited literature — but making the grades explicit is the only honest way to put a sourced Neolithic cargo and a typologically dated surface find in the same article without letting them borrow each other's strength.

Two features of the scale deserve emphasis. The first is that Grade 2 — necessary inference — is not a weak grade. It is, in strict logic, the strongest position in this article: the settlement of Sahul cannot be explained without watercraft, because the geography admits no alternative, and a conclusion that admits no alternative does not become doubtful merely because its vehicle has rotted away. What Grade 2 cannot supply is detail — the shape of the craft, the number of voyages, the navigational method — and the failures of the popular literature are almost all failures to notice that the detail, not the crossing, is what is missing. The second feature is the asymmetry between grades 1 and 4. A Grade 1 attribution is repeatable: another laboratory, given the same blade, will reach the same source. A Grade 4 claim is, at present, not repeatable in that way — it rests on judgements (of typology, of geological association) that competent specialists dispute — and it must therefore never be used as a premise for further conclusions. The Crete material of §XV may one day be promoted, by excavation and direct dating, to Grade 2. Until then it is reported as what it is: the most interesting open question in Mediterranean seafaring, and nothing more.

// A WORD ON ACCIDENTAL VOYAGES //

Drift — castaways carried by wind and current on natural rafts of vegetation — is a real dispersal mechanism, documented for many animal species and invoked for some hominin island records. It is tested, not assumed, in each case below. The tests are demographic and geographic: drift can deliver individuals, but a viable founding population, repeated cargo, or arrival against prevailing currents each demands more than drift can plausibly supply. Where drift remains a live possibility (the first Flores crossing, §V), this article says so; where it has been modelled and found insufficient (Sahul, §IV), it says that too.

III. The Preserved Horizon — The Pesse Canoe and the First Dugouts

The oldest boat in the world is a small, plain object: a dugout canoe hollowed from a single Scots pine log, 298 centimetres long and 44 centimetres wide, found in 1955 during roadworks through a peat bog near the village of Pesse in the Netherlands, and now in the Drents Museum at Assen. Radiocarbon dating places its construction in the early Mesolithic, between about 8040 and 7510 BCE. It is, by the standards of the subject of this article, a recent artefact — younger than the Franchthi obsidian run by some three thousand years, younger than the Sahul crossing by nearly sixty thousand. And it is the earliest physical watercraft that exists. Everything before the Pesse canoe is wake without vessel; the canoe marks the preserved horizon, the shoreline in time beyond which hulls themselves can be handled, measured, and replicated.

The canoe's survival is a lesson in the taphonomy of §I. It endured because it came to rest in peat — waterlogged, acidic, anaerobic sediment in which the organisms that consume wood cannot work — and because the bog was never subsequently drained, ploughed out, or eroded by a rising sea. Those conditions are a lottery, and the early Holocene winners of it form a small, instructive fleet. From the peat and lake sediments of northern Europe come the dugouts of the Ertebølle and earlier Mesolithic worlds; from Nigeria, the Dufuna canoe, a dugout of about 8000 BP recovered from buried floodplain sediments near the Yobe River and for decades the oldest known boat in Africa. Later still, the record thickens — sewn-plank and logboat traditions of the Neolithic and Bronze Age, preserved in rivers and fens. Every one of these craft is Holocene, freshwater or sheltered-water in context, and preserved by burial in sediment. Not one is Pleistocene; not one is a sea-going vessel recovered from the sea.

What the preserved horizon can and cannot teach follows directly. It teaches that the dugout — a log hollowed by adze and controlled burning — is an ancient and widely convergent solution, present at the very start of the surviving record and therefore certainly older than its first survivor. A technology already mature at 8000 BCE was not invented in 8000 BCE. But the horizon cannot teach the antiquity of any other hull form. Rafts leave nothing to hollow; bark canoes are skins of material that decays in seasons, not millennia; reed bundles and hide boats are, archaeologically, near-invisible even in favourable conditions. The preserved record is thus systematically biased toward the most durable hull type — massive timber — and toward inland waters. To read it as a history of boat invention would be to mistake the filter for the fleet. The honest reading is the one this article adopts: dugouts are proven by c. 8000 BCE; all earlier and lighter craft are inferred, at the grades of §II, from what they demonstrably carried and whom they demonstrably landed.

// THE REPLICA TEST //

The Pesse canoe has been replicated and paddled: a copy built with stone-age methods floats and carries a paddler, settling an early scepticism that so small a craft — barely three metres long — could be a boat at all rather than, as was once suggested, a trough or a coffin. The episode is a small model of this article's method. Function, like provenance, is testable: build it, float it, and the argument from incredulity dissolves.

One more consequence of the horizon deserves a sentence, because it disciplines everything that follows. When §VI asks what kinds of craft crossed to Sahul or Melos, the answer cannot be drawn from the preserved fleet: none of those vessels is a candidate ocean carrier, and none was meant to be. The candidates must be built from other evidence — ethnographic craft traditions of the relevant regions, the materials available in them, experimental voyages, and the minimum performance the crossings themselves require. That is a weaker kind of knowledge than a hull in a museum, and the article keeps it at that strength. But it is not no knowledge. A crossing of 90 kilometres of open water, made by a founding population, is itself a specification: whatever carried those people floated, carried weight, survived days at sea, and could be built again. Specifications can be reasoned from even when artefacts cannot be produced.

IV. Sahul — The Crossing That Cannot Be Argued Away

Sahul is the name given to the Pleistocene continent that joined Australia, New Guinea, and Tasmania into a single landmass whenever sea level fell. Its northwestern shore faced, across a chain of deep-water straits, the islands of Wallacea — and beyond them, across further straits, the southeastern edge of Sunda, the Pleistocene continent of mainland Southeast Asia. The geography carries the whole argument, so it must be stated exactly. The straits of Wallacea are cut through deep water. At the lowest sea levels of the last glaciation — roughly 120 metres below the present — the land bridges that joined Britain to Europe or Siberia to Alaska never formed here. Sunda and Sahul remained separate continents, divided by a sequence of water gaps that had to be crossed, in some order, by any creature that passed from one to the other. The biogeography of the region is the proof written in animals: Wallace's Line, the famous faunal boundary running through the archipelago, marks precisely the straits that terrestrial species could not cross unaided. Placental mammals stop at the line. Marsupials begin beyond it. Only creatures that fly, swim strongly, raft by rare accident — or build boats — appear on both sides.

Humans appear on the Sahul side, with tools, art, and occupation debris, at a date the current evidence places at about 65,000 years ago. The key site is Madjedbebe, a rock shelter in Arnhem Land, northern Australia, where Clarkson and colleagues (2017, Nature) applied single-grain optically stimulated luminescence dating to the artefact-bearing sediments and placed the first occupation at c. 65,000 BP, with stone artefacts, ochre, and other cultural material in the dated layers. The date is debated at its margins — Allen and O'Connell have argued for a more conservative arrival around 50,000 BP, and the exchange between the positions continues — and this article reports the crossing, accordingly, at the strength the whole range supports: whether the first landfall was 65,000 or 50,000 years ago, it was a landfall across open sea, tens of millennia before any other proven ocean crossing by our species. The full treatment of the settlement — the routes modelled through Wallacea, the founding population estimates, the spread across the continent — belongs to the companion article Sahul and Australia; what matters here is the crossing's logic.

The Voyages the Geography Requires

Modelled reconstructions of the two plausible routes — a northern route through Sulawesi and the Moluccas toward New Guinea, and a southern route island-hopping toward Timor and thence to the Kimberley coast — agree on the structure of the problem. Each route is a staircase of crossings, most of them short hops between intervisible islands, but including at least one long leg: on the southern route, a final crossing on the order of 90 kilometres of open water from the Timor region to the Sahul shelf, on which the destination would have been out of sight for much or all of the passage at the sea levels of the time. Crossings of that length are not swims, and they are not single-person events if they are to found a population. Demographic modelling — most influentially by Bird and colleagues (2018), building on the route modelling of Norman and colleagues — indicates that a viable founding population for a continent requires numbers in the hundreds at minimum, and more plausibly a founding group, or a sequence of arriving groups, in the high hundreds to low thousands. Accident does not move a thousand people across a 90-kilometre strait. What moves them is repeated, deliberate voyaging in craft that can carry families, water, and food for a passage of days — seafaring, in the full sense defined in §I, practised by hunter-gatherers of the late Pleistocene with tools of stone, bone, and plant fibre.

Note what this argument does not need. It does not need a boat to survive, a depiction of a boat, a word for a boat, or a single maritime artefact of any kind. It needs a map, a sea level curve, a dated occupation site, and the demographic arithmetic of founding populations. That is why the Sahul crossing stands at Grade 2 of this article's scale as its load-bearing case: it is a necessary inference, and necessary inferences of this structure — where every alternative has been examined and fails on geography or demography — are as secure as anything in deep prehistory gets. The settlement of Sahul is, at the time of writing, the earliest proven seafaring by any human population, and it precedes the oldest surviving boat by some fifty-five thousand years. Hold those two numbers together and the subject of this article comes into focus: seafaring is not a Neolithic invention that diffused from some maritime hearth. It is older than farming by an order of magnitude, older than pottery, older than the oldest cave painting yet dated — a founding capability of the modern human career, present at the first expansion out of the Afro-Asian homeland into the island world of the Pacific margin.

THE BARRIER

The Wallacean straits are deep-water channels. Even at glacial lowstands of c. −120 m, Sunda and Sahul were never joined; every route crosses open sea, the longest leg c. 90 km.

THE DATE

Madjedbebe rock shelter, Arnhem Land: single-grain OSL places occupation at c. 65,000 BP (Clarkson et al. 2017). A conservative minority reading puts arrival c. 50,000 BP. Either figure requires watercraft.

THE POPULATION

Demographic modelling (Bird et al. 2018) finds a viable continental founding needs hundreds to low thousands of arrivals — repeated voyages or crewed fleets, not a single castaway event.

THE GRADE

Grade 2 — necessary inference. No craft survives and none is described; the crossing itself admits no alternative explanation consistent with the geography.

V. The Deeper Precedent — Earlier Hominins in Wallacea

Sahul was settled by modern humans. But the water gaps of Wallacea had been crossed long before our species existed to cross them — by earlier hominins, whose presence on islands east of Wallace's Line is among the most quietly astonishing facts in palaeoanthropology. Three island records carry the case, and each is reported here at its own strength, because they are not equally strong and the differences instruct.

Flores — Tools at a Million Years

Flores, in the Lesser Sunda chain, was never connected to the Asian mainland: the straits on either side of it are deep, and at no Pleistocene lowstand could it be reached on foot. Yet the So'a Basin of central Flores holds an archaeological sequence of remarkable depth. At Wolo Sege, stone artefacts lie in situ in deposits sealed beneath an ignimbrite dated by argon-argon methods to 1.02 ± 0.02 million years ago — the artefacts are older than the volcanic layer that covers them, so a million years is a minimum age for hominin presence on the island (Brumm, van den Bergh, Morwood and colleagues, Nature 2010). At nearby Mata Menge, excavations recovered hominin fossils themselves — a jaw fragment and teeth of a strikingly small hominin — dated to about 700,000 years ago, published by van den Bergh and colleagues in 2016 alongside a full revision of the basin's chronology. And at Liang Bua, the cave in western Flores made famous in 2004, the skeletal remains of Homo floresiensis — the metre-tall hominin whose announcement redrew the human family tree — are now dated, after a thorough stratigraphic revision (Sutikna and colleagues, Nature 2016), to between about 100,000 and 60,000 years ago, with associated stone artefacts spanning roughly 190,000 to 50,000 years ago. The popular version of the hobbit story, in which the species survived until 12,000 years ago and nearly met farmers, rested on a misread cave stratigraphy and is no longer the published position; this article uses the revised chronology throughout.

The Flores sequence therefore documents hominins — tool-making, fire-era hominins of the Homo erectus grade, on the prevailing reading, and their dwarfed island descendants — on an always-island landmass for a span approaching a million years. Someone crossed the straits west of Flores at least once, at or before a million years ago. How they crossed is a genuinely open question, and honesty requires the alternatives to be stated in their strongest forms. The crossings required at Pleistocene lowstands in this part of Wallacea may have been as short as a few kilometres to perhaps twenty — far shorter than the Sahul legs — and the region is tectonically and volcanically violent: a tsunami can tear loose rafts of vegetation with animals, and conceivably hominins, aboard, and natural rafting has serious defenders for the founding Flores event specifically. Against pure accident stands the persistence of the record: tools recur in the basin across hundreds of thousands of years, and the Mata Menge population was established, breeding, and evolving in isolation — though a single founding group, however it arrived, could in principle account for all of it. This article therefore grades Flores as Grade 2 for the crossing — presence on an unreachable island is necessary inference of arrival over water — and expressly ungraded for the mechanism: deliberate watercraft and catastrophic natural rafting both remain in the published field, and no artefact yet found decides between them.

Luzon — A Butchered Rhinoceros at 709,000 Years

Luzon, the largest island of the Philippines, was likewise never joined to mainland Asia. At the site of Kalinga, in the Cagayan Valley of northern Luzon, a French–Filipino team excavated an almost complete, disarticulated skeleton of an extinct rhinoceros (Rhinoceros philippinensis, now Nesorhinus philippinensis) together with 57 stone tools. Thirteen of the rhinoceros bones carry cut marks; both humeri bear percussion marks consistent with marrow extraction. Electron spin resonance dating of tooth enamel and quartz placed the bone bed between 777,000 and 631,000 years ago, with a central estimate of about 709,000 years (Ingicco and colleagues, Nature 2018). No hominin fossil was found — the butchers of Kalinga are known only by their work — but a butchered rhinoceros with its toolkit, on an island that could only be reached over water, is hominin presence of the most concrete kind. The Kalinga evidence is in one respect stronger than Flores: the association of tools, cut-marked bone, and a single dated deposit is tight and excavated. In another respect it is thinner: it is, so far, one site and one event-horizon, where Flores offers a sequence. Both are Grade 2 crossings. A third Wallacean record, from Sulawesi — stone artefacts at Talepu dated to between about 118,000 and 194,000 years ago (van den Bergh and colleagues, Nature 2016) — adds a further island, likewise never mainland-joined, to the list of pre-modern crossings, with still older Sulawesi claims under active investigation at the time of writing and not relied on here.

Taken together, the Wallacean records change the framing of the whole subject. Sea crossing is not a behaviour that modern humans invented once, at some identifiable moment of cognitive breakthrough, and then carried around the world. It is a behaviour that hominins of more than one species, at more than one grade of brain size and technology, performed — deliberately or by surviving accident — whenever geography put water between them and the next shore, for at least a million years. The modern human achievement at Sahul was not the invention of the crossing but its escalation: longer legs, larger groups, deliberate colonisation of a continent. That escalation is real and it matters. But it was an escalation of something ancient, and the deep precedent should temper every narrative — still common in popular writing — in which the boat arrives late in the human story, as a Neolithic refinement. The boat, in some form, is among the oldest technologies we can infer at all; it simply happens to be made of the materials that time eats first.

// WHAT "EARLIER HUMANS" DOES NOT MEAN //

The Flores and Luzon crossings are sometimes recruited, in popular writing, into claims of million-year-old "navigation" or even of lost maritime civilisations. The evidence supports none of that. It supports arrival across water gaps — possibly short, possibly assisted by currents or catastrophe — by small populations who then lived on their islands with stone-tool technologies that changed very slowly. A crossing is not a shipping network. The first documented traffic in this article is the Franchthi obsidian of §VII, nearly a million years after Wolo Sege, and the gap between those two facts is itself one of the honest wonders of the record.

VI. What Kind of Craft? — Rafts, Bark, Reed, and Hide

If no early boat survives, on what basis can anything be said about early boats at all? On three bases, each weaker than a hull and stronger than a guess: the materials and technologies demonstrably available in each region; the craft traditions of the historically documented peoples of those regions, used as analogy and not as survival; and experimental archaeology, in which candidate craft are built with period tools and sailed — or fail to sail — on the actual crossings. This section walks the candidate types, with their evidence and their limits, in ascending order of constructional commitment.

The Raft — Logs, Bamboo, and Lashing

The raft is the minimal watercraft: buoyant material lashed together, propelled by paddle or pole, requiring no hollowing, no bending, no watertight joinery — only cordage and the knowledge that some timbers float better than others. In Wallacea the premier raft material grows wild: bamboo, whose hollow, sealed internodes make it among the most buoyant structural materials in the pre-industrial world, light enough to carry, strong enough to lash into multi-layer platforms. Joseph Birdsell, modelling the Sahul crossing in 1977, proposed bamboo rafts for the Wallacean legs on exactly these grounds — material abundance, minimal tool requirements, and adequate performance — and the proposal has remained the default reconstruction for the region ever since: not proven, but never displaced by a better-fitting candidate. Rafts have real limitations — they are wet, slow, and cannot beat against wind and current — but for crossings measured in days, downwind or across known currents, with landfall on a continental shore hundreds of kilometres long, the limitations are tolerable. Experimental voyages in replica bamboo and log rafts have repeatedly demonstrated crossings of the relevant scale in Indonesian waters, including voyages undertaken specifically to test the Timor–Australia leg. The raft is this article's graded verdict for Wallacea: most plausible candidate, not attested.

Bark and Hide — The Skin-on-Frame Family

At the other constructional pole stand craft built as a waterproof skin over a light frame. The ethnographic record is rich: bark canoes of Aboriginal Australia and Tasmania — sheets of bark sewn or tied at the ends, stiffened with stretchers, light enough for one person to carry, used historically for crossings to offshore islands including, in Tasmania, voyages of several kilometres in rough water; the hide boats of northern Eurasia and the Americas; the reed and skin craft of Mesopotamia's marshes. These traditions prove something important and often missed: sophisticated, seaworthy small craft can be built with no durable components whatsoever — no plank, no nail, no hollowed log — and can therefore be archaeologically invisible in principle, not merely by bad luck. A Pleistocene world full of bark canoes would look, in the surviving record, exactly like the Pleistocene world we have. That cuts both ways, and this article keeps both edges visible: invisibility makes bark and hide craft available as explanations everywhere, which is precisely why they can be asserted nowhere. They are the standing possibility that forbids any confident statement that early craft were "only" rafts — and the standing warning that plausibility is not evidence.

Reed Bundles and Dugouts

Between raft and skin boat sit two further families. Reed-bundle craft — buoyant stems lashed into a hull-shaped bundle — are documented from Lake Tana to Lake Titicaca to the marshes of southern Iraq, and in Egypt, where papyrus skiffs are depicted from the earliest dynastic art; they are cheap, quickly built, and waterlog over weeks, suiting them to rivers, lakes, and short sheltered crossings rather than ocean legs. Dugouts, by contrast, are the one early type with a surviving pedigree (§III): the Pesse canoe proves the type by c. 8000 BCE, and experimental work has since shown what a dugout can do at sea — in 2019, a team led by Yousuke Kaifu paddled a dugout canoe hollowed with replica Palaeolithic-style tools from Taiwan to Yonaguni Island, a crossing of some 225 kilometres of open ocean, completed in just over 45 hours, as a direct test of one proposed Palaeolithic migration route into the Ryukyu Islands. The experiment does not prove that any Palaeolithic voyage used a dugout. It proves the negative space of the argument: the performance required by the documented crossings is within the capability of the simplest attested hull type, given skilled paddlers and chosen weather. No appeal to lost advanced technology is needed anywhere in this article — and, by the same token, none is permitted.

Propulsion and navigation close the section, briefly and at the same graded strength. Propulsion for all candidate craft is the paddle — steering oar and sail belong to later, documented worlds, and no evidence places a sail before the late fourth millennium BCE, in Egypt and the Gulf. Navigation on the documented early crossings requires less than romance suggests: intervisible island chains for most legs; for the long Sahul leg and the Aegean runs of §VII, the standard toolkit of pre-instrument seafarers — swell patterns, bird flight at dawn and dusk, cloud formation over land, and the smell and colour of water — all of it ethnographically documented among Pacific and Indonesian voyagers, none of it directly attested for the Pleistocene. The honest summary of this whole section is a single sentence: the crossings are proven; the craft that made them are modelled, and the models are constrained by materials, experiment, and the crossings' own minimum specifications — nothing stronger is claimed anywhere below.

VII. Melian Obsidian at Franchthi — The First Documented Cargo

Franchthi Cave stands above the shore of the Argolic Gulf in the southern Argolid, Greece, facing the Aegean. Excavated from 1967 under Thomas Jacobsen, and published in a long monograph series — the lithic industries by Catherine Perlès above all — it holds one of the longest continuous sequences in Greek prehistory, from the Upper Palaeolithic through the Mesolithic into the Neolithic. In the cave's Upper Palaeolithic levels, dated to the closing millennia of the Pleistocene, the excavators found something that has no business being there: pieces of obsidian, a volcanic glass that does not occur anywhere on the Greek mainland. Characterisation analysis — among the earliest applications of the method of §IX, by Aspinall, Feather, and Renfrew (1972) — assigned the Franchthi pieces to the island of Melos, in the Cyclades. Melos was an island then as now: it was never joined to the mainland at any relevant sea level. The obsidian crossed the Aegean by boat, in the hands of late Palaeolithic hunter-gatherers, at a date the current evidence places from about 13,000 years ago.

The date deserves its own paragraph, because it has been garbled in popular retelling — frequently rendered as "13,000 BCE," which would make the material some two thousand years older than the evidence supports. The stratigraphic position of the earliest Franchthi obsidian is the end of the Upper Palaeolithic; direct dating of the pieces themselves, by obsidian hydration measurement using the SIMS-SS method (Laskaris and colleagues, Journal of Archaeological Science 2011), returned ages for the exploitation of the Melian sources spanning roughly the 13th millennium to the end of the 10th millennium BP — that is, centred on c. 13,000 BP, or about 11,000 BCE. This article uses c. 13,000 BP throughout, and flags the BCE rendering as an error wherever the reader may meet it elsewhere. Precision here is not pedantry: the Franchthi material matters because it is the earliest well-documented case of its kind, and a claim to priority that rests on a misread unit is a claim already lost.

What Was Carried, and How Much

The quantities are small, and their smallness is informative. In the Upper Palaeolithic levels, obsidian is a trace presence — a few pieces, at most about one per cent of the lithic assemblage, in a toolkit otherwise made of local flints and cherts. Through the Mesolithic the share climbs — by the Upper Mesolithic it reaches roughly three per cent of the lithics, a thirtyfold increase on the earliest levels, though still a minor material in absolute terms — and the form of the material changes in a way that tells us about the traffic itself. In the Mesolithic levels the obsidian arrives, on Perlès's analysis, substantially as unworked blocks and nodules that were knapped on site at Franchthi; in the Neolithic, the pattern shifts again toward prepared cores and finished blades produced by specialist knappers, the pressure-flaked blade industry for which the Aegean became famous. Perlès has argued — against a simpler picture of each community fetching its own stone — that even the early acquisition was unlikely to be casual: the Melos run required a boat, knowledge of the source's location on an uninhabited island, navigational skill, and days of travel, and she proposed that the bearers were people for whom seafaring was a specialism, the obsidian perhaps collected in the course of the deep-water fishing trips whose traces — tuna bones among them — also appear in the Mesolithic levels at Franchthi. The details of organisation are interpretation; the transport is not. Obsidian moved from Melos to the mainland, repeatedly, across at least two millennia of the cave's occupation, before farming reached Greece.

Set the Franchthi evidence beside Sahul and the shape of the whole record appears. Sahul proves that people could cross serious water sixty-five thousand years ago; Franchthi proves that, thirteen thousand years ago, people did cross Aegean water as a matter of recurring practice, carrying a named cargo from a named quarry to a named shore — the first sea transport of a raw material anywhere that can be documented piece by piece, source to site. It is Grade 1 evidence on this article's scale, the grade the rest of the obsidian record will repeatedly reach. And it reframes the Neolithic that follows: when farming communities spread across the Aegean and the Mediterranean in the seventh and sixth millennia BCE, they did not take to an unknown sea. They inherited routes, sources, and seafaring knowledge already thousands of years old — a point to which the assessment (§XVII) returns, because the hub's own timeline, like most, has usually begun the story of exchange with the farmers and left the sailors out.

// WHY MELOS MATTERS //

Melos held the Aegean's premier obsidian — abundant, fine-grained, and occurring in two main source areas (§VIII) — and it held something else of equal archaeological value: no early settlement. The island shows no established occupation until the later Neolithic. The Franchthi obsidian therefore cannot be explained as the product of a settled Melian community trading outward; it documents voyaging to an uninhabited source island and back — procurement expeditions, with a return leg, which is seafaring in the full sense of §I.

VIII. Melos Itself — Sta Nychia and Demenegaki

Obsidian sourcing begins at the quarry, and the Melian quarries are the type case for the whole Mediterranean. Melos is a volcanic island, and its obsidian occurs principally in two source areas, known since the pioneering survey work of the 1960s and characterised in the founding studies of §IX: Sta Nychia (often rendered Adamas, after the nearby port), on the island's north coast, and Demenegaki, on the east. The two are chemically distinguishable — a fact of the first importance, because it means the method can resolve not merely "Melos" but which Melian source a given blade came from, and can therefore watch procurement shift between quarries across time. At both sources the obsidian occurs as nodules and blocks weathered out of the volcanic deposits, collectable from the surface and from shallow extraction pits; the prehistoric working floors at Sta Nychia, dense with knapping debris, show the first stage of the industry — the testing and rough shaping of material — happening at the source itself, so that what went into the boats was already partly sorted: sound stone, worth its carriage weight.

The Melian case also teaches the first caution of sourcing: a "source" is an analytical unit, not a dot on a map. A volcanic island may hold several flows of different ages and chemistries; a single flow may outcrop at several places; nodules erode downhill and downstream into secondary deposits far from the flow that made them. The laboratory groups of §IX — Sta Nychia versus Demenegaki, the Monte Arci subgroups of §XI, Carpathian 1 versus Carpathian 2 of §XII — are the discipline's answer: chemically defined populations of geological samples, against which artefacts are matched. Where two outcrops share one chemistry, sourcing can honestly report only the group; where a group's samples are few, the attribution carries wider uncertainty. The method's power, which the next section sets out stepwise, is real — but it is the power of a well-built reference collection, and it extends exactly as far as that collection's coverage and no further.

From Melos, the Neolithic distribution fans out across the Aegean and beyond: Melian obsidian is the standard fine cutting stone of the Cyclades, Crete, and the coastal mainland, reaching sites hundreds of kilometres from the island and dominating assemblages at distances where the Anatolian sources (§X) do not compete. A second Aegean source, on the small island of Giali in the Dodecanese, supplied a distinctive spherulite-bearing obsidian used more locally and for particular products — a reminder that even within one sea, procurement was a patchwork of sources with different reaches, qualities, and social geographies. The map that sourcing draws of the Neolithic Aegean is not a single trade system but overlapping supply worlds, each with its own fall-off curve (§XIII) — and every boundary on that map is a set of laboratory attributions of individual artefacts, each one a small, repeatable proof of contact.

IX. How Obsidian Sourcing Works — The Laboratory Method

Obsidian is a volcanic glass: magma rich in silica, erupted and cooled so fast that no crystals could grow. That violent, instantaneous origin is what makes it sourceable. A magma body is chemically homogeneous in a way a sedimentary rock never is — a single flow is one batch of melt, mixed before eruption — while different volcanoes, and often different eruptions of the same volcano, draw on melts with measurably different recipes. The differences live mainly in the trace elements: the parts-per-million seasoning of rubidium, strontium, yttrium, zirconium, niobium, barium, and their fellows, present in proportions fixed by the magma's history. Major-element composition (silica, alumina, iron, the alkalis) separates obsidians only coarsely; the trace-element pattern is the fingerprint. The founding demonstration was made by Colin Renfrew, J. R. Cann, and J. E. Dixon in the 1960s — their 1968 paper, "Further Analysis of Near Eastern Obsidians" (Proceedings of the Prehistoric Society 34), together with its 1966 predecessor, established that the obsidians of the Near East and Mediterranean could be assigned to their sources by trace-element analysis, and thereby converted a generation of vague talk about "contact" into maps of specific supply. The principle underneath the whole method was later given a name, the provenance postulate (Weigand, Harbottle & Sayre, 1977): sourcing is possible exactly when the chemical variation within one source is smaller than the variation between sources. Obsidian meets the postulate better than almost any material in archaeology, which is why it — and not flint, pottery, or shell — became the tracer dye of early exchange.

The Procedure, Step by Step

What follows is the method as actually practised, in the order a laboratory performs it. It is given at full procedural length because the conclusions of §§VII, X, XI, and XII are only as good as this pipeline, and the reader is entitled to inspect the pipeline.

// SOURCING A BLADE — THE REPEATABLE PROCEDURE //

Step 1 — Build the reference collection first. Before any artefact is touched, geologists and archaeologists survey the source: every known flow and outcrop is located, and multiple geological samples — typically dozens per source — are collected across its full extent, including secondary (eroded, redeposited) occurrences. A source characterised from three nodules is a rumour; from thirty, a population.

Step 2 — Characterise the sources. The geological samples are analysed by the same instrument and protocol that will be used for artefacts. Their results define, for each source, a chemical group: a cluster in multi-element space, with means and spreads for each measured element. Sources whose clusters overlap cannot be separated by that technique, and the laboratory says so at this stage — not later, over an artefact.

Step 3 — Select and document the artefact sample. Artefacts are chosen to answer a stated question (one context, one phase, one household), recorded by context and type, and cleaned. For non-destructive methods the object is analysed whole; for destructive ones, a small sample — a chip, a drilled powder — is taken under the holding institution's permission.

Step 4 — Measure. The artefact is run under the identical protocol, interleaved with certified standard reference materials (the obsidian standard RGM-1 is the field's usual anchor) so that instrument drift is detected and concentrations are calibrated against known values, allowing results to be compared between laboratories and across decades.

Step 5 — Assign, statistically. The artefact's composition is compared with the source groups: first on bivariate plots of the most discriminating element pairs (zirconium against strontium, or rubidium, is the classic XRF display), then, for formal attribution, by multivariate statistics — Mahalanobis-distance probabilities of group membership are the field's standard. The output is not a verdict but a probability of belonging to each characterised group.

Step 6 — Report the failures. An artefact that matches no characterised group is reported as unassigned — the honest and scientifically valuable outcome that signals an uncharacterised source, an unsampled flow, or a mixed signal. A laboratory that assigns one hundred per cent of its artefacts is not succeeding; it is forcing. Published sourcing studies report their unassigned fraction, and this article trusts, other things equal, the ones that do.

Step 7 — Read the pattern, not the piece. A single attribution proves one object's origin. Archaeology begins when attributions accumulate: proportions of sources per site, per phase, per context — the dataset from which distribution maps (§§X–XII) and fall-off curves (§XIII) are built.

The Instruments — XRF, NAA, and Their Successors

Three generations of instrumentation carry the field, and the differences between them matter to what can be claimed. The founding work used optical emission spectroscopy, destructive and semi-quantitative, sufficient to separate the great source groups of the Near East on elements such as barium and zirconium. The workhorse of the mature field is X-ray fluorescence (XRF): the sample is irradiated with X-rays, the atoms of each element fluoresce at characteristic energies, and the spectrum yields concentrations. In its laboratory form (wavelength- or energy-dispersive XRF) it is precise, quantitative, and — decisively for museum material — non-destructive: the blade is measured whole and returned intact. M. Steven Shackley's laboratory protocols, set out in his Obsidian: Geology and Archaeology in the North American Southwest (2005) and applied worldwide, are the standard statement of the method: whole-artefact analysis, calibration against international rock standards, and reporting of the mid-Z elements — rubidium, strontium, yttrium, zirconium, niobium, with iron, titanium, and manganese — that discriminate obsidian sources best. Handheld portable XRF brings the measurement to the field and the museum drawer, at some cost in precision for the lightest elements. The second great technique is neutron activation analysis (NAA): a small sample is irradiated in a nuclear reactor, and the gamma rays emitted as the activated nuclei decay give an exceptionally sensitive multi-element fingerprint — including rare earths and elements such as tantalum, hafnium, and thorium that XRF measures poorly. NAA is destructive of its small sample and hostage to reactor access, but for separating closely related sources — the Carpathian groups of §XII were discriminated by instrumental and epithermal NAA — it remains a reference method. A third generation, LA-ICP-MS (laser ablation inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry), vaporises a microscopic spot from the artefact's surface and measures dozens of elements at parts-per-billion sensitivity: effectively invisible damage, the widest element menu, and the current state of the art for difficult cases. The techniques agree where they overlap, and a sourcing claim that matters — a new source attribution, an unexpected long-distance piece — is expected, in current practice, to be confirmable by more than one of them.

What, exactly, does a successful attribution prove? That this blade's glass came from that volcano — full stop. It does not prove who carried it, by what route, in how many hands, or in exchange for what. Those further claims are built, in §§X–XIII, from the pattern of many attributions: which sources appear at which sites, in what proportions, in what technological form (raw nodule, prepared core, finished blade — each implying a different organisation of production and movement), and how the proportions decay with distance. The single attribution is the atom of the method; the distribution is its molecule. Keeping the two apart is the whole of the discipline — a sourced blade from an unexpected place is a fact, while the trading network drawn through it is a model, and this article labels each as it goes.

X. The Neolithic Networks I — Cappadocia and Çatalhöyük

The largest and best-documented obsidian system of the early Neolithic is not maritime at all — and it belongs in this article for two reasons: it is the calibration case, the network whose scale and organisation the sea-borne systems must be compared against; and its western arm fed the Aegean world, where the same material took to the water. The sources are the volcanoes of Cappadocia, in central Anatolia, above all the Göllü Dağ complex — whose eastern flows supplied the industry's mainstay glass — and Nenezi Dağ, with the Acıgöl sources contributing a smaller share. Cappadocian obsidian had been moving since the Epipalaeolithic, and with the first farming villages it became the cutting edge of the entire Near Eastern Neolithic: sourced Cappadocian pieces occur from Cyprus to the Zagros foothills, at distances exceeding a thousand kilometres from the flows.

The type consumer is Çatalhöyük, the great Neolithic town of the Konya Plain (treated in this hub's article Çatalhöyük), occupied c. 7400–5700 BCE. The town sits about 190 kilometres from the Cappadocian sources, across the plain and into the volcanic uplands — a journey of days, made on foot — and it consumed obsidian on an industrial scale: obsidian is the primary material of its chipped-stone industry throughout its occupation. The sourcing programme at the site, led by Tristan Carter with M. Steven Shackley and colleagues across several laboratories and techniques (EDXRF, PIXE, ICP-MS among them), gives the pattern its precision. In a representative study (Carter & Shackley, Archaeometry 2007), 42 artefacts from a single Neolithic building were sourced by energy-dispersive XRF: their signatures resolved into exactly two groups, matching East Göllü Dağ and Nenezi Dağ. Larger programmes across the site's levels confirm the pair as the town's staple supply, while detecting, in the earliest levels, a minority component of eastern Anatolian glass — from the Lake Van region sources, hundreds of kilometres further east — and tracking shifts in the proportions of the two Cappadocian sources against changes in blade technology over the town's seventeen centuries. The technological reading is as rich as the chemical one: material arrived substantially as prepared cores, and the production of the long, regular pressure-flaked blades for which Çatalhöyük is famous was specialist work, its products distributed within and beyond the town.

Read the Çatalhöyük pattern slowly, because it is the template for reading every network in this article. First: two sources, not one — procurement was never a single pipeline, and the coexistence of Göllü Dağ and Nenezi Dağ glass in one building's assemblage shows supply lines braiding at the level of the household. Second: a minority exotic — the eastern Anatolian pieces — riding the same system at low frequency, the signature of down-the-line movement (§XIII) bringing small quantities very far. Third: change through time in source proportions, correlated with technology — the network has a history, not just a geography. Fourth, and most important for this hub: none of it needed a state, a market, or a merchant class. The largest raw material distribution system of the early farming world ran on foot, through villages, by mechanisms — direct procurement expeditions, reciprocal exchange between communities, itinerant specialists — that the fall-off analysis of §XIII exists to discriminate. When the same material, in the same centuries, appears across the Aegean and the Tyrrhenian, the boats of §§VII–VIII are simply this system extending itself over water: the sea legs are one transport medium among others in a world already knitted together by moving stone.

Cappadocia, central Anatolia: East Göllü Dağ and Nenezi Dağ are Çatalhöyük's staples; Acıgöl contributes; Lake Van sources (Nemrut Dağ, Bingöl, Süphan Dağ) supply the wider Near Eastern system's eastern arm.

THE FORM

Prepared cores in, specialist pressure-flaked blades out: the town was a production centre, not merely a consumer — obsidian as industry, not just import.

XI. The Neolithic Networks II — Lipari, Monte Arci, and the West

West of Sicily, the Mediterranean's obsidian geography inverts the Aegean's: every source is an island. The western Mediterranean has exactly four obsidian source islands — Lipari in the Aeolian archipelago north of Sicily, Palmarola in the Pontine Islands west of Naples, Pantelleria in the Sicilian Channel, and Sardinia, whose Monte Arci complex held the west's richest glass. There is no mainland source at all. Every piece of obsidian in Neolithic Italy, southern France, Corsica, Sicily, Malta, and the North African coast — and sourcing programmes, above all Robert Tykot's, have attributed thousands — therefore crossed the sea. The western networks are the purest maritime case in the obsidian world: not a system with sea legs, but a system made of them.

Monte Arci — One Volcano, Several Fingerprints

Monte Arci, in west-central Sardinia, is the analytical showpiece of the western system. Its obsidians divide into chemically distinct subgroups — conventionally SA (southern slopes), SB (western slopes, itself divisible), and SC (eastern slopes) — differing in trace-element pattern and in physical character, from translucent glassy SA to the more opaque SC. Because the subgroups can be told apart, the laboratory can follow not just "Sardinian obsidian" but the products of particular slopes of one volcano as they move: SC glass, for instance, travelled widely into Corsica and the Italian mainland, while the distribution of the other subgroups maps different catchments and periods. Tykot's analyses of hundreds of artefacts from Sardinian and Corsican sites showed the subgroups' use changing through the Neolithic — a fine-grained procurement history, of access, nodule size, and preference, recoverable only because the characterisation is fine-grained enough to sustain it. Monte Arci obsidian reached mainland Italy and southern France in quantity, crossing the Tyrrhenian and the Ligurian Sea; Corsica, visible from Sardinia across the Strait of Bonifacio, was supplied from Monte Arci almost as a home shore.

Lipari, Palmarola, Pantelleria — The Specialist Sources

The smaller island sources each carry a signature distribution. Lipari's obsidian — from flows including Gabellotto and Canneto Dentro, themselves distinguishable — was the prestige glass of the central Mediterranean: it dominates assemblages in Sicily and southern Italy, reached Malta and the Adriatic, and travelled as far as the Dalmatian coast and southern France, with the island itself becoming, by the later Neolithic, a production centre whose workshops exported prepared cores and blades. Palmarola, a tiny island with a single modest source, supplied a regional catchment on the facing Italian mainland — proof that even a small source entered the system where geography favoured it. Pantelleria's greenish, peralkaline obsidian — visually unmistakable, the one Mediterranean glass identifiable by eye alone — crossed the Sicilian Channel to appear in Sicily, Malta, and Tunisia: a sea crossing of roughly a hundred kilometres at its longest, made routinely enough to leave a dated distribution on three shores. The western pattern as a whole is the Franchthi pattern at Neolithic scale — islands as quarries, sea as road — generalised across an entire sea, and it begins with the Neolithic settlement of the islands themselves (§XV): the first farmers of Sardinia, Corsica, Sicily, and Malta arrived by boat, and the obsidian web is the continuing traffic of the world their arrival created.

A methodological footnote from this literature deserves promotion into the text, because it shows the method correcting itself. A small number of western Mediterranean artefacts were attributed, by fission-track analysis in the 1980s, to distant Melos — a sensational east–west link. Re-analysis by compositional methods reassigned the pieces to Palmarola. The episode is printed here deliberately: sourcing methods have error modes, early results get revised, and the revisions are part of the published record rather than embarrassments to be hidden. It is also why §IX insists on multiple techniques for claims that matter. The east–west Melian link, at present, does not stand, and this article does not use it.

XII. The Neolithic Networks III — The Carpathian Sources

Europe's only mainland obsidian system lies in the Carpathian Basin, where Miocene volcanism left glass in the Tokaj–Zemplín hill country of present-day northeastern Hungary and southeastern Slovakia, with further occurrences across the Ukrainian border in Transcarpathia. The characterisation literature divides the exploited material into two great groups: Carpathian 1, the high-quality, translucent glass of the Zemplín Hills — its primary source at Viničky, its most-used material in fact gathered from the rich secondary accumulations of nodules around Brehov and Cejkov — and Carpathian 2, the less glassy obsidian of the Tokaj Mountains around Mád and Tolcsva; a third, Ukrainian group (Carpathian 3) is recognised in the literature and was little used in prehistory. The discrimination of these groups, first drawn by Williams-Thorpe and colleagues (1984) and refined by neutron activation studies (Kilikoglou and colleagues, 1996–1997; Oddone and colleagues, 1999), is a worked example of §IX's postulate under strain: the Carpathian sources are chemically closer to one another than the Mediterranean island groups are, and separating them took the field's most sensitive techniques.

The distribution repays the effort. Carpathian obsidian was used from the Middle Palaeolithic onward, and in the Neolithic it became the signature raw material of the eastern Linear Pottery and Bükk culture worlds: in eastern Slovakia, near the sources, assemblages can be made almost wholly of obsidian, and the material travelled — down the Tisza and Danube systems and overland — to sites in Hungary, Romania, Poland, Austria, and Ukraine, at distances of several hundred kilometres. River valleys here do the work that straits do in the Aegean: they are the low-friction corridors along which the fall-off of §XIII can be measured, and the Carpathian case is a standing reminder that "exchange network" names a pattern of movement, not a kind of terrain. For this article's argument, the Carpathian system contributes the continental control case. Everything the obsidian method shows at sea — source catchments, distance-decay, braided supply, long-distance minority pieces — it also shows in a landlocked basin where no boat was ever required. The maritime networks are therefore not a separate phenomenon needing a separate explanation. They are the general Neolithic pattern of moving valued stone, extended across water by people for whom water was already, and had long been, a road rather than a wall.

// OBSIDIAN AS TRACER DYE //

The metaphor is exact. A tracer dye is chosen because it is detectable at extreme dilution, unique to its injection point, and chemically faithful — it does not change identity en route. Obsidian is all three: detectable in a single flake, unique to its flow, and unchanged by knapping, use, or burial. The perishable traffic of the Neolithic — grain, animals, people, ideas — left faint and ambiguous traces. The obsidian moving along the same routes left a permanent, laboratory-grade record of the routes themselves. That is the whole of its evidentiary privilege, and this article has claimed nothing beyond it.

XIII. Distance-Decay — Renfrew's Fall-Off Analysis

Sourcing tells us where obsidian came from; distribution analysis asks the harder question — how did it move? The founding quantitative method is Colin Renfrew's fall-off analysis, developed across his obsidian studies of the 1960s and stated as a general principle in 1977: the Law of Monotonic Decrement. In Renfrew's formulation, in the absence of highly organised, preferential exchange, the abundance of an exchanged commodity falls off monotonically — steadily, without rising again — with effective distance from its source. The law is a baseline, not a description: its power lies in the shape of the fall-off and in the departures from it, each shape being the expected fingerprint of a different mechanism of movement.

The Procedure

The method, as practised, runs in five steps. First, for every site in the study region, measure abundance in a stated, comparable unit: classically, the percentage of obsidian in the site's total chipped-stone assemblage by count or by weight; alternatively, weight of obsidian per volume of excavated deposit. Second, measure each site's effective distance from the characterised source — not necessarily straight-line distance: cost-distance, allowing for terrain, and for island sources the sea route actually sailed. Third, plot abundance (usually on a logarithmic scale) against distance. Fourth, fit the curve and read its form. Fifth, compare the form against the mechanisms: a curve that is high and flat near the source, then falls, divides the map into a supply zone — within which communities had direct access to the source, in the Near Eastern case extending some 300 kilometres — and a contact zone beyond it, where material arrived only through exchange. A smooth exponential fall across the whole range is the signature of down-the-line exchange: each community passes a fraction of what it receives to the next, keeping the rest, so that abundance decays geometrically with the number of hands — Renfrew's 1975 model, built on the ethnography of reciprocity, and still the default reading of the Near Eastern and much of the Mediterranean data. A curve with a plateau or a secondary peak far from the source signals something else: a redistribution centre, a port, a place where the commodity was re-concentrated — directionality that the law's baseline throws into relief.

The method's limits are part of its literature and are stated here with them. Abundance measured as a percentage is hostage to the denominator: a site rich in good local flint will show low obsidian percentages even if its absolute obsidian consumption is high. Excavated samples differ in size and recovery; distances are models, not measurements; and several different social mechanisms can generate similar curves — the equifinality problem that later work, including simulation and network modelling of the Near Eastern data (Ibáñez and colleagues, 2016), has pressed hard, finding the real Neolithic systems more structured than a pure down-the-line chain. None of this retires the method. It defines its correct use: fall-off analysis does not read a mechanism off a curve by decree; it constrains the mechanisms a distribution can represent, and it does so quantitatively, in public, with stated units — which is why, fifty years on, every obsidian distribution study in §§X–XII still begins by drawing the curve. For this article's subject the method contributes one decisive capability: it can detect sea-borne re-concentration. Where an island or coastal site holds far more obsidian than its distance predicts — where the curve jumps at the shoreline — the distribution is announcing a node in a maritime network: a place the boats called at. The fall-off curve, drawn honestly, is the closest thing prehistory has to a shipping register.

XIV. Island Colonisation I — Cyprus and the Pygmy Hippos

Obsidian documents traffic; colonisation documents commitment — the moment a voyage becomes a settlement. The Mediterranean's island colonisations, read cumulatively, are the proof that the traffic of §§VII–XII was no anomaly: again and again, across ten thousand years, people crossed to islands and stayed. The pattern has been mapped by John Cherry since 1981 and analysed most recently by Cherry and Thomas Leppard (2017/2018): pre-Neolithic presence clusters on the larger, less remote islands; permanent settlement becomes general only with the Neolithic, when colonists arrived carrying what Cherry and Leppard call a transported landscape — cereals, pulses, sheep and goats — that made small, dry islands farmable. The sea, in other words, was crossable long before the islands were worth settling permanently, and the colonisation record is as much a history of motivation as of capability. Cyprus, the Mediterranean's third-largest island and never joined to any mainland, is the test case where capability, motivation, and consequence collide in a single small rock shelter.

Akrotiri Aetokremnos — The Site and the Claim

Aetokremnos — "Vulture's Cliff" — is a collapsed rock shelter on the southern coast of Cyprus's Akrotiri Peninsula, excavated in the late 1980s under Alan Simmons. Its lower bone-bearing stratum (Stratum 4) contained the remains of more than 370 pygmy hippopotamuses (Phanourios minor, the dwarf hippo endemic to Cyprus), together with a few dwarf elephants; its upper cultural stratum (Stratum 2) held hearths, some thousand flint artefacts, and food refuse of shellfish, fish, and birds. An unusually high proportion of the hippo bone — around thirty per cent — is burned. Radiocarbon determinations place the occupation at roughly 12,000 years ago (the excavators' preferred reading centres a short occupation near 11,775 BP within a broader c. 12,500–11,000 BP envelope) — the earliest well-documented human presence on Cyprus, and among the earliest on any Mediterranean island. Cyprus was separated from the Levantine and Anatolian coasts by crossings of some 30 to 70 kilometres at contemporary sea levels: the people of Aetokremnos arrived by boat. That much — presence, by sea, at c. 12,000 BP — is Grade 2, and is not seriously disputed. Everything beyond it is.

The Debate — Did the First Cypriots Kill the Hippos?

Simmons's claim, argued from the 1988 preliminary reports through his monograph Faunal Extinction in an Island Society (1999) and his synthesis Stone Age Sailors (2014), is that Aetokremnos is an extinction site: that the hippo bone bed and the human occupation are one event, and that the island's dwarf megafauna was driven to extinction by the first human arrivals — the Mediterranean's earliest case of the island-extinction pattern later repeated worldwide. The critics' case is taphonomic and stratigraphic, and it has strengthened with time. No hippo bone from the site bears a demonstrated butchery mark. The association between the cultural material and the bone bed has been questioned since the excavation itself — Vassos Karageorghis, in his foreword to the final report, recorded his criticism of the stratigraphic interpretation — and alternative readings treat the shelter as a natural hippo accumulation, later visited by people. In 2015, Zazzo and colleagues published direct radiocarbon dates and physico-chemical analyses of the burned bones themselves: their Bayesian modelling placed the accumulation of the bone bed in the first half of the 13th millennium cal BP, with the burning occurring several hundred years later — the bones, on their analysis, were old, mineralised bone gathered and burned as fuel by Epipalaeolithic visitors, at a time when the dwarf hippos were probably already extinct or nearly so. Climate — the bone bed accumulates at the onset of the Younger Dryas cold snap — stands in their account as a candidate driver of the extinction that requires no human agency.

This article's adjudication, applying §II: the colonisation is attested; the extinction agency is Grade 3 and currently unresolved, with the burden of proof shifted by the 2015 analyses toward those who claim a human kill. What survives every reading is the fact that matters most to this article: hunter-gatherers reached Cyprus, an island never connected to the mainland and not intervisible along its whole coast, by c. 12,000 BP — before farming existed anywhere — and the Aetokremnos assemblage, however the hippos died, includes the material of people who lived from the sea: shellfish, fish, and sea birds. After Aetokremnos the Cypriot record shows a gap of roughly a millennium before the aceramic Neolithic settlement of the island by farmers — a gap Cherry and Leppard's framework predicts: visits and even short occupations precede colonisation proper, and the island's permanent settlement waited for the transported landscape of the Neolithic package, which itself arrived, of necessity, by boat, animals and all. Compare the parallel threshold in this hub's Göbekli Tepe article: the same centuries that saw monument-building hunter-gatherers in Anatolia saw their contemporaries putting to sea. The late Pleistocene world was experimenting on every axis at once.

XV. Island Colonisation II — Crete, Sardinia, and Corsica

Crete — The Contested Handaxes of Plakias

Crete is the Mediterranean's fifth-largest island, separated from the mainland by deep straits throughout the Pleistocene, and its early record is the sharpest test of this article's grading discipline. Between 2008 and 2009, the Plakias Survey — a Greek–American project directed by Thomas Strasser and Eleni Panagopoulou — collected over 2,000 stone artefacts from 29 localities around Plakias on Crete's southwest coast. Most are Mesolithic. But from nine localities came larger tools of Lower Palaeolithic character — handaxes, cleavers, and heavy scrapers, mostly of quartz — found in geological contexts, raised marine terraces and mature fossil soils, whose dating the team used to assign the oldest artefacts a terminus ante quem of at least 130,000 years (Strasser and colleagues, Hesperia 2010). If the attribution and the dating both hold, archaic hominins — Neanderthals or their predecessors — crossed to Crete in the Middle Pleistocene, and Mediterranean seafaring is older by an order of magnitude than the Franchthi horizon.

The claim is printed here at Grade 4, and the reasons are methodological, not temperamental. The Plakias Palaeolithic material is surface-collected, not excavated from a sealed, independently dated occupation layer; its age is inferred from the geological units in which artefacts were embedded or with which they were associated, by terrace correlation and soil maturity — dating of the context by analogy, yielding minimum ages for the deposits, whose transfer to the artefacts depends on the association being primary, which erosion and redeposition on terraced slopes make hard to guarantee. The typological attribution — that the quartz pieces are genuinely Lower Palaeolithic handaxes rather than heavy tools of a later period — has been accepted by some specialists and questioned by others. Sympathetic reviewers (Howitt-Marshall & Runnels, 2016) conclude there is no a priori reason to reject archaic hominin presence on the Greek islands, while insisting that targeted excavation is what would test it; sceptics note that a decade and a half of attention has not yet produced the excavated, dated site the claim requires. This article's adjudication: the Plakias material establishes a serious, testable possibility of Middle Pleistocene crossings to Crete; it does not establish the crossings, and no conclusion in this article is built on it. The claim's proper fate is the one its own literature prescribes — excavation — and the archive will promote it the day a dated layer, not a terrace correlation, carries it.

Sardinia and Corsica — The Western Pair

Sardinia and Corsica formed a single landmass at glacial lowstands, reachable from the Tuscan archipelago by short crossings, and their record runs at the middle grades of the scale. The earliest Sardinian claim is a human finger bone from Corbeddu Cave (Oliena), attributed by Sondaar and colleagues to roughly 20,000 years ago — which would make it the oldest human fossil on any Mediterranean island — together with limestone tools from the cave's lower layers argued to span c. 14,600–12,500 BP. Both the bone's date and the tools' attribution have been contested, and reviewers of the island record (including the Cherry–Leppard survey) treat the Corbeddu Palaeolithic as possible but unproven: Grade 3 shading to 4. What is secure is the Mesolithic: by about 9,000–10,000 years ago, hunter-gatherers were established on Sardinia (Su Coloru and companion sites) and had crossed the Strait of Bonifacio to Corsica (Strette, c. 9,140 BP; Curacciaghiu, c. 8,560 BP) — crossings made when both islands were true islands, requiring boats for a strait some eleven kilometres wide at its narrowest and considerably more from the likely points of departure. The Neolithic then made both islands nodes of the Monte Arci system of §XI — Sardinia as the source, Corsica as its first customer — and the sequence from visit, to Mesolithic presence, to obsidian-supplied farming settlement is the western Mediterranean's complete colonisation arc, preserved in miniature.

Stand back from the three cases and the cumulative structure of the evidence is the finding. Cyprus: presence by c. 12,000 BP, attested. Sardinia–Corsica: presence secure by c. 10,000 BP, possible millennia earlier. Crete: Neolithic settlement certain, Mesolithic presence documented by the Plakias survey's later material, Palaeolithic presence claimed and contested. Add Franchthi's cargo from c. 13,000 BP and the Cyclades' progressive Neolithic settlement, and the Mediterranean of the terminal Pleistocene and early Holocene is not a sea occasionally dared but a sea routinely worked — by foragers first, at low intensity, on the larger and nearer islands, and then, with farming, everywhere. Cherry's original 1981 question — why so late, given the capability? — has inverted in the answering: the capability is now known to be far older than the Mediterranean record alone would ever have shown (§§IV–V), and the islands' late settlement reads as a history of incentives, not of inability. People settle islands when islands become worth settling. The boats were never the bottleneck.

XVI. The Forward Horizon — Remote Oceania

[Forward horizon — beyond this hub's remit, stated in one paragraph and not developed.] The voyaging tradition that begins, in this article, with the Sahul crossing has a second and greater act that belongs to a later hub and a later treatment: the settlement of Remote Oceania. After some forty thousand years in which voyaging in the Pacific remained within the intervisible island world of Near Oceania, the Lapita cultural complex — Austronesian-speaking farmers and sailors, carrying pottery, domestic animals, and a transported landscape of cultigens — broke eastward from the Bismarck Archipelago from about 3,300 years ago, reaching Fiji, Tonga, and Samoa across crossings of hundreds of kilometres, and founding the Polynesian world. From West Polynesia, after a long pause, voyagers in double-hulled canoes with crab-claw sails settled the far corners of the Polynesian Triangle — Hawai'i, Rapa Nui, and Aotearoa New Zealand — in a final expansion completed, on current evidence, in the centuries around 1000–1300 CE, navigating by stars, swell, and bird flight across thousands of kilometres of open ocean: the greatest seafaring achievement of the pre-industrial world, and the direct descendant, in capability if not in unbroken tradition, of the crossings with which this article began. It is marked here as a horizon only — the hub's subject is the deep past before Kemet, and Remote Oceania's settlement belongs to the age of the pharaohs' successors and beyond — but the line on the map runs unbroken from Madjedbebe to Aotearoa, and the reader should see it drawn.

XVII. What the Sea Proves — An Assessment

What, in sum, does the evidence assembled here show? First, at the level of demonstration: watercraft are a Pleistocene technology of the first antiquity. Hominins crossed sea gaps in Wallacea by about a million years ago, by means unproven; modern humans crossed them deliberately, in colonising numbers, by c. 65,000 BP; hunter-gatherers were carrying quarried stone across the Aegean by c. 13,000 BP and reaching Cyprus by c. 12,000 BP; and the Neolithic world then ran obsidian supply systems — characterised blade by blade in the laboratory — across every sea in this article, from the Aegean to the Tyrrhenian to the Sicilian Channel, in the same centuries and by the same mechanisms as its overland systems in Cappadocia and the Carpathians. Not one of those sentences rests on a surviving boat. All of them rest on evidence of the kinds graded in §II, and the grading, not the rhetoric, is what entitles them to stand in a Tier A article.

Second, at the level of interpretation: seafaring was a presupposition of the world this hub describes, not one of its achievements. The timeline of the Antiquity hub records farming villages receiving obsidian across hundreds of kilometres, islands settled, coasts colonised, materials moving between continents — and until this article, the capability that half of those entries silently require stood nowhere in the record, because it leaves no monuments and its tools rotted. Cyprian Broodbank's The Making of the Middle Sea (2013) made the Mediterranean version of the argument at full length: the sea was not a barrier that Bronze Age sailors finally overcame but a medium that had structured human movement since the Palaeolithic, its "thalassographies" of routes, seasons, and island stepping-stones older than any state. The Wallacean evidence extends the same point beyond any single sea and beyond our species. Wherever the record is good enough to test the question — arrival, cargo, colonisation — the answer comes back the same: the water was crossed, early, repeatedly, and as a matter of course.

What the evidence does not show must be entered with equal plainness, in the manner of this hub's assessments. It does not show the boats: every hull form in §VI is a model, and the confident popular image of the Palaeolithic raft or the Mesolithic skin boat is an illustration, not a find. It does not show million-year-old navigation: the Flores and Luzon crossings may owe as much to catastrophe and current as to craft, and this article has graded them for the arrival and not the seamanship. It does not show a human hand in the Cypriot hippos' extinction, which the most recent direct dating has made harder, not easier, to claim. It does not show Palaeolithic Crete, which awaits an excavated date. And it does not show sails, keels, or steering oars anywhere in the periods covered: the entire immense achievement recorded here was accomplished, so far as the evidence indicates, under paddle power, at walking pace, by people reading water and weather with their bodies. The limitation is the glory of it. Seafaring did not wait for civilisation, for metal, for writing, or for the sail. It is one of the founding capabilities of the human career — older than farming, older than art as currently dated, older than our species' possession of the world — and the obsidian blade in a mainland cave, matched atom by atom to an island quarry, is its oldest surviving signature: not the boat, which the sea and the centuries took, but the cargo, which they could not take back.
