I. Tier B — The Deluge as Text, Not as Event

This is a Tier B article: Ancient Tradition. The tier is stated here, in words, because it governs every sentence that follows. Tier B material survives in ancient texts, in ancient languages, copied and recopied by ancient scribes and poets. This article treats those texts as texts: it gives each one's composition date as scholarship establishes it, names the manuscripts or the transmission chain by which it reaches us, names the edition or translation actually consulted, and attributes every claim to the text that makes it.

That discipline matters more for the flood than for almost any other subject in this division of the Archive, because the flood is the point at which four great literatures are most often melted down into one. Popular retelling speaks of "the Flood" as if there were a single story — a single vessel, a single hero, a single mountain — of which Ziusudra, Noah, and Deucalion were local names. The texts do not say that. The Sumerian story gives its hero seven days and seven nights of storm and no birds at all in its surviving lines. The Akkadian poem of Atrahasis sends the flood because human beings have become too loud. Genesis sends it as a moral judgement and ends it with a covenant no Mesopotamian text contains. The Greek story rescues exactly two people, in a chest, and repopulates the earth from stones. Egypt, as Sections XIII to XVI argue at length, has no such story at all.

To harmonise those tellings into one "real flood" behind them all is to destroy the evidence in the act of presenting it. What comparison can legitimately show — literary transmission, shared motifs, deliberate theological reworking — is taken up in Sections IX and XVIII, with the scholarly positions stated fairly, including the positions this article does not adopt. What comparison cannot show, on the evidence of the texts themselves, is a single historical deluge of which all four traditions are garbled memories. No text in this article claims that status for itself, and this article claims it for none of them.

// THE RULE OF THIS ARTICLE //

Every flood in what follows belongs to a named text, in a named language, of a named date. "The poem says," "Genesis narrates," "Apollodorus reports," and "the Egyptian text does not say" are the normal forms here. Where a scholar's interpretation is given, the scholar is named. Quotations are confined to editions actually opened in the preparation of this article, and are named as such. Everything else is paraphrase, and is presented as paraphrase.

A companion article in this division, The Antediluvian King Lists, treats the flood as a chronological hinge — the single line in the Sumerian King List at which 241,200 years of claimed reigns give way to history. This article is its counterpart: where the King List gives the flood one line, the narratives gathered here give it whole poems. Read together, the two articles show the same event functioning as a date in one genre and as a theology in another — which is precisely what it means to treat the deluge as text.

II. A Map of the Traditions — Four Literatures, Four Floods

Four literary worlds stand behind this article, and they should be kept distinct from the outset, because their flood traditions differ in age, in genre, and in purpose as much as in detail.

MESOPOTAMIA — THE LONG LINE

A Sumerian flood story centred on Ziusudra; an Old Babylonian Akkadian poem centred on Atrahasis; the same story retold as personal testimony in Tablet XI of the Epic of Gilgamesh, centred on Utnapishtim; and a Hellenistic Greek retelling by the Babylonian priest Berossus, centred on Xisouthros. Four compositions, one evolving tradition, spanning roughly two thousand years of copying. Sections III to VII.

GENESIS — THE WOVEN TEXT

Genesis 6–9, the account of Noah, his ark, and the covenant, read by mainstream scholarship as two older strands — conventionally called J and P — interwoven by editors into a single narrative whose seams are still visible in its numbers, its animals, and its two endings. Sections VIII and IX.

GREECE — THE LATE, BRIEF TELLING

The flood of Deucalion and Pyrrha: alluded to by Pindar in the fifth century BCE, summarised by the mythographer Apollodorus, and given its fullest literary form by the Roman poet Ovid. Later than the Mesopotamian line in its surviving witnesses, briefer than any of them, and distinct in its vessel, its survivors, and its stone-born repopulation. Sections X to XII.

EGYPT — THE ABSENCE

No native Egyptian text tells of a universal deluge destroying mankind by water. Egypt's great destruction myth destroys by the fiery Eye of Ra, not by flood; its primeval waters, Nun, are the beginning of the world, not its catastrophe; and its annual inundation is the year's blessing, not its terror. The absence is itself the finding. Sections XIII to XVI.

The order of the article is the order of the evidence. The Mesopotamian line comes first because its witnesses are oldest and fullest, and because every serious comparison in this field is a comparison with it. Genesis comes second because its relationship to the Mesopotamian material — close in motif, distant in theology — is the most studied and the most debated. Greece comes third because its telling is later, shorter, and shaped by Greek concerns that owe less to Mesopotamia than popular accounts assume. Egypt comes last, and at length, because the honest answer to "what is Egypt's flood myth?" is that there isn't one — and the reasons why are among the most instructive in all comparative mythology.

III. The Sumerian Flood Story — Ziusudra and the Eridu Genesis

The earliest surviving Mesopotamian flood narrative is Sumerian. The composition is cited in the Oxford Electronic Text Corpus of Sumerian Literature as ETCSL t.1.7.4, under the title The Flood Story; it is also widely known by the title Thorkild Jacobsen gave it, the Eridu Genesis. This article quotes the ETCSL translation, which it opened directly, and follows the ETCSL's own presentation of the text — gaps, queries, and missing lines included.

The Manuscript and Its Date

The story survives principally on a single tablet from Nippur, excavated by the University of Pennsylvania expedition and recognised by Arno Poebel in the early twentieth century, with further fragments from Ur and a later bilingual fragment associated with the library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh. The main Nippur tablet is dated to around 1600 BCE — that is, it is an Old Babylonian-period copy of a Sumerian composition whose own date of composition cannot be fixed more precisely than the early second millennium BCE tradition it belongs to. The distinction matters, and this article keeps it throughout: c. 1600 BCE is the date of the surviving clay, not a claim about when the story was first told.

The tablet is badly broken. Estimates of the loss vary, but between a third and two-thirds of the composition is missing, and the ETCSL translation marks the losses openly: Segment A opens with approximately 36 lines missing, further gaps of some 32, 34, and 38 lines interrupt the narrative, and whole passages are marked too fragmentary for translation. Any summary of "the Sumerian flood story" that does not carry those gaps with it is silently filling them from later versions — a practice Bendt Alster's work on the Sumerian flood tradition warns against, and one this article avoids. In particular, the boat-building instructions, so detailed in the Akkadian versions, fall in a lacuna in the Sumerian text. We do not know what Ziusudra's vessel looked like. We know only that the text calls it, when the storm comes, a huge boat.

What Survives

The surviving shape, in the ETCSL translation, is this. After An, Enlil, Enki, and Ninhursaja fashion humankind and make the animals multiply, kingship descends from heaven and the first five cities are founded and assigned to their gods: Eridug (Eridu) to Nudimmud, that is Enki; Bad-tibira to the Mistress, Inana; Larag (Larak) to Pabilsag; Zimbir (Sippar) to the hero Utu; and Shuruppak to Sud. These are the five antediluvian cities of the King List, in the same order — the two compositions share a tradition's map of the first age, a point developed in the companion article, The Antediluvian King Lists.

After a break, the gods resolve on destruction. The text, as the ETCSL presents it, shows the divine assembly swearing an oath, the goddess Nintud lamenting, and Enki taking counsel with himself. The hero is Zi-ud-sura — Ziusudra, a name understood as "life of long days" — presented as a king and a gudug priest, humble, committed, and reverent, standing day by day at his devotions. Enki, bound by the oath, does not address the man directly. He addresses a wall, with Ziusudra standing at its side listening. The verified wording of the warning, in the ETCSL translation opened for this article, is given in the pull-quote below.

"A flood will sweep over the ...... A decision that the seed of mankind is to be destroyed has been made." — The Flood Story, Segment C, ETCSL translation t.1.7.4 (Oxford)

The ellipses are the ETCSL's own: they mark broken clay, and they are part of the quotation, not an editorial embarrassment. A text that reaches us in this condition is evidence of a particular and valuable kind — it shows exactly how much of the earliest flood narrative we actually have, and how much of the familiar "Sumerian version" in popular books is Atrahasis and Gilgamesh wearing Sumerian dress.

When the text resumes, the storm has come: all the windstorms and gales arise together, and the flood sweeps over the land. The huge boat is rocked by waves and windstorms for seven days and seven nights. Then Utu the sun god comes out, illuminating heaven and earth; Ziusudra drills an opening in the boat, prostrates himself before Utu, and sacrifices oxen and innumerable sheep. In the closing preserved movement, An and Enlil treat Ziusudra kindly: in the ETCSL's wording, they grant him life like a god and bring down to him eternal life, and they settle him in an overseas country, in the land of Dilmun, where the sun rises, because he has preserved the animals and the seed of mankind.

// WHAT THE SUMERIAN TEXT DOES NOT CONTAIN //

In its surviving lines the Sumerian story has no birds released to test the waters, no mountain on which the boat grounds, no dimensions for the vessel, and no stated motive for the gods' decision that survives the breaks. Those elements belong to the later Akkadian tellings. Reading them back into Ziusudra's story is the single most common error in popular flood comparison, and the easiest to avoid: read the gaps as gaps.

IV. Atrahasis — Noise, Plague, and the Flood as Last Resort

The fullest early telling of the flood is not Sumerian but Akkadian: the poem modern scholarship calls Atrahasis, after its hero, whose name means "exceedingly wise." The standard edition is W. G. Lambert and A. R. Millard, Atra-ḫasīs: The Babylonian Story of the Flood (Oxford, 1969), and the poem is most widely read in Stephanie Dalley's translation in Myths from Mesopotamia (Oxford University Press, 1989). This article's summary follows the Lambert and Millard edition as reported in the scholarly literature consulted for it; renderings of the poem's wording are given as paraphrase.

Date, Copy, and Transmission

The poem was composed in the Old Babylonian period. Its best-preserved early copy is a three-tablet edition whose colophons name its scribe — read in earlier scholarship as Ku-Aya and now generally as Ipiq-Aya — and date the copying to the reign of Ammi-saduqa of Babylon, in the 17th century BCE (Ammi-saduqa's reign is conventionally placed c. 1646–1626 BCE). As with the Sumerian tablet, the date is the copy's date; the composition itself is older by an interval scholarship puts at no more than a century or two on Lambert and Millard's judgement. The poem runs to roughly 1,200–1,250 lines across its three tablets in the reconstructed edition. It did not stop being copied in the Old Babylonian period: later Assyrian recensions and fragments, including material from Ashurbanipal's library and a fragment from Ugarit, show the poem still being transmitted more than a thousand years after its best early copy was written.

The Poem's Argument

Atrahasis is not only a flood story, and reading it as one flattens it. It is a complete account of why human beings exist and why their lives have the shape they have. In paraphrase of the poem's opening movement: the lesser gods, the Igigi, labour at digging the rivers and canals, rebel at the workload, and burn their tools. Humankind is created — from clay mixed with the flesh and blood of a slain god, in the poem's striking account — to carry the labour in the gods' place. Humanity multiplies. And then the poem gives its famous and deliberately unglamorous motive for everything that follows: noise. The land, in the image the translators render with a bellowing bull, has grown loud with people; the god Enlil cannot sleep for the uproar — rigmu in Akkadian — and moves, step by step, to reduce it.

The steps matter, because the flood is the last of them, not the first. Enlil sends plague; Enki, through Atrahasis, counsels the people to concentrate their worship on the plague god until the plague lifts. Enlil sends drought, and then famine; each measure is frustrated the same way, by Enki's quiet countermeasures delivered through his servant. Only then does Enlil bind the gods by oath and decree the flood. Enki circumvents the oath exactly as in the Sumerian telling — speaking not to the man but to the wall of his house — and Atrahasis builds the vessel, loads his family and the animals, and rides out a storm the poem sets at seven days and seven nights. Afterward he sacrifices; the gods, who have gone hungry without human offerings, gather to the sacrifice; Enlil rages at the survival and is answered by Enki and by the mother goddess with the poem's settlement: humanity will continue, but under permanent new limits — mortality as now experienced, barrenness for some women, and celibate classes of priestesses — presented as the checks that make another flood unnecessary.

// NOISE, NOT WICKEDNESS //

The distinction between Atrahasis and Genesis begins here. In Atrahasis, on the standard reading following Lambert and Millard, the flood's cause is demographic and acoustic: too many people, making too much noise, for one god's rest. There is no charge of human wickedness, violence, or corruption. A reader who imports Genesis's moral cause into the Akkadian poem — or the poem's noise into Genesis — has stopped reading either text.

A later Old Babylonian witness deserves mention here because it shows the tradition still at work on practical detail. The tablet Irving Finkel published as The Ark Before Noah (2014) — an Old Babylonian Atrahasis-tradition tablet of roughly 1900–1700 BCE — gives boatbuilding instructions of unusual specificity: a great round vessel on a circular plan, a giant coracle of coiled palm-fibre rope, wooden ribs, and bitumen. The Mesopotamian ark, in its most detailed surviving specification, is round because Mesopotamian river boats were round. The long ship-shaped ark of later Western illustration belongs to a different tradition's imagination.

V. Gilgamesh Tablet XI — Utnapishtim Tells His Own Story

The most famous Mesopotamian flood account is a story inside a story. In Tablet XI of the Standard Babylonian Epic of Gilgamesh — the version traditionally associated with the scholar Sin-leqi-unninni — the hero Gilgamesh, grieving for Enkidu and terrified of his own death, crosses the waters of death to find the one man who was granted unending life, and asks him how he got it. The flood story is the answer. The standard modern edition is Andrew R. George, The Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic (Oxford University Press, 2003), with George's Penguin translation, The Epic of Gilgamesh (1999), as the usual point of entry; Dalley's Myths from Mesopotamia also translates the tablet. This article paraphrases the tablet following George's edition as reported in the scholarship consulted.

Manuscripts and the 1872 Sensation

The Standard Babylonian epic is known principally from seventh-century BCE copies from Ashurbanipal's library at Nineveh, among other witnesses, including a fragment from Ugarit of around 1200 BCE that George published in 2007. The Nineveh flood tablet — British Museum K.3375 and its joins — is the object George Smith read when he announced a Babylonian flood account to the Society of Biblical Archaeology on 3 December 1872. The sensation that announcement caused — a flood narrative older than Genesis, in cuneiform — permanently changed the study of both corpora, and it is the historical reason the comparisons in Section IX carry the charge they do. The tablet Smith read is a copy roughly a thousand years younger than the Atrahasis copy of Ammi-saduqa's reign, retelling a story already ancient when its scribe wrote.

Utnapishtim's Narration

The hero is Utnapishtim — rendered Utanapishtim in some editions — "the Faraway," son of Ubar-Tutu, of Shuruppak on the Euphrates: the King List's last antediluvian city, and its last king's household. In paraphrase following George: the great gods resolve on the flood; Ea — Enki under his Akkadian name — betrays the secret to the wall of Utnapishtim's reed hut; the hero is told to tear down his house and build a boat whose length and breadth are to be equal, roofed over, and to load it with the seed of all living things, his family and kinsfolk, craftsmen, cattle and wild beasts, and his silver and gold. The vessel of Tablet XI is, in its stated proportions, a great cube of 120 cubits — a specification unlike both the Ark Tablet's coracle and Genesis's oblong ark, and a reminder that even within one tradition the boat would not stay the same shape.

The storm, in the tablet's telling, lasts six days and seven nights — one day shorter than the Sumerian and Atrahasis storms, a small variant that honest comparison should note rather than smooth away. All humankind turns to clay. The boat grounds on Mount Nimush — Nisir in older translations — and holds fast. On the seventh day, in the sequence every reader of Genesis should meet first in its Babylonian form, Utnapishtim releases birds: a dove, which finds no perch and returns; a swallow, which likewise returns; and a raven, which sees the waters receding, eats, scratches the ground, and does not return. He releases the animals, sacrifices on the mountain, and the gods gather to the offering's savour. Enlil, furious that anyone has escaped, is rebuked — and in settlement blesses Utnapishtim and his wife, making them like the gods, to dwell far away at the mouth of the rivers.

Scholarship is plain about the tablet's literary source: Lambert and Millard concluded, and George's edition confirms, that the flood story in Tablet XI is adapted from Atrahasis — at points nearly line for line — and reshaped as personal testimony. The reshaping has a purpose inside the epic, and the epic states it by what happens next. Utnapishtim's immortality, his narration insists, was a unique grant made in circumstances that will never recur. Challenged simply to stay awake for six days and seven nights, Gilgamesh falls asleep at once, and Utnapishtim's wife bakes a loaf for each day of his sleep so that the failure cannot be denied. The man who cannot conquer sleep will not conquer death. The flood, in Gilgamesh, is not told for its own sake: it is told to close a door.

VI. Berossus — Xisouthros and the Books Buried at Sippar

The Mesopotamian line's last great ancient retelling is not in cuneiform at all. Berossus (Berossos), a Babylonian priest of Bel writing in Greek under Antiochus I in the early third century BCE, composed the Babyloniaca, a history of Babylonia for a Hellenistic audience, published around 278 BCE on the usual dating. The work is lost as a whole. It survives in fragments and summaries transmitted through Alexander Polyhistor, Josephus, Eusebius, and the Byzantine chronographer George Syncellus, among others — the flood fragment principally through Syncellus, in the translation of G. P. Verbrugghe and J. M. Wickersham consulted for this article in paraphrase. Every statement about Berossus should carry that chain with it: we read a ninth-century CE chronographer's excerpts of a first-century BCE epitomator's excerpts of a third-century BCE priest's Greek history of traditions already two thousand years old.

In Berossus's second book, as the fragments report it, ten kings reign before the flood for a total of 120 sar — 432,000 years — and the tenth and last is the flood hero himself: Xisouthros (Xisuthros), the Greek form of Ziusudra's name, son of the ninth king — Otiartes or Ardates in the fragments' variant spellings, corresponding to Ubara-Tutu. The promotion is the point: where the Sumerian story makes Ziusudra a king in Shuruppak and the King List makes him no king at all in its own list — Ubara-Tutu is its last antediluvian king — Berossus completes the identification and puts the hero on the throne. The tradition, fifteen centuries after the Weld-Blundell Prism, is still editing itself.

The flood account the fragments preserve, in paraphrase, runs as follows. The god Cronus — Ea/Enki, under the Greek identification of gods that scholars call interpretatio graeca — appears to Xisouthros in a dream and reveals that on the fifteenth day of the month Daisios mankind will be destroyed by a great flood. Xisouthros is ordered first to bury all the writings — the first, the middle, and the last tablets, in the fragment's phrase — at Sippar, the city of the sun, and then to build a boat and board it with his family, his best friends, provisions, and animals of every kind. The flood comes and abates. Xisouthros releases birds, which return finding no food and no place to rest; releases them again after some days, and they return with mud on their feet; a third release does not return. The vessel grounds in Armenia, in the fragments' geography; Xisouthros disembarks with his wife, his daughter, and the pilot, sacrifices to the gods — and disappears, translated to live with the gods for his piety, with his wife, daughter, and pilot sharing the honour. A voice from the air instructs the remaining companions to return to Babylonia, dig up the buried tablets at Sippar, and hand them over to mankind. They do so, refound cities and temples, and restore Babylon. The fragments add that a piece of the vessel still remained in the Gordyenian mountains of Armenia in Berossus's telling, and that people scraped its bitumen for use as a protective charm.

// THE PRIEST'S EMPHASIS //

The buried books are Berossus's own emphasis, and they are a Babylonian priest's emphasis. What survives his flood is not only life but the written record — beginnings, middles, and ends, dug up and republished. For a priest explaining his tradition's antiquity to a Greek king, no detail could be more pointed: civilisation, in Berossus, is a text that a flood cannot drown.

VII. The Mesopotamian Line — A Thousand Years of Retelling

Before turning to Genesis, the Mesopotamian evidence should be allowed to state its own lesson, because it is the control case for every comparison that follows. Here is a flood tradition whose transmission we can actually watch, in dated copies, across roughly two thousand years — and what we watch is not preservation but continuous revision.

Three conclusions follow, and all three are conclusions about texts, not about weather. First, the stable core of the tradition is small: a divine decision, one warned man, a vessel, a storm, a sacrifice, an exceptional fate for the hero. Everything else — the cause, the boat's shape, the storm's length, the birds, the mountain, the hero's kingship — moves. Second, the movement is not random decay: each retelling reshapes the story toward its own work — Atrahasis toward an argument about population and labour, Gilgamesh XI toward an argument about the unrepeatability of immortality, Berossus toward an argument about the antiquity of Babylonian learning. Third, and most important for what follows: if this much change is what a tradition undergoes when we can watch it, in dated manuscripts from one civilisation, then the amount of change to expect across languages, centuries, and theologies — the Genesis case — should surprise no one. Similarity of motif is evidence of contact or shared inheritance. It is not evidence of transcription, and it is not, by itself, evidence of an event.

VIII. Genesis 6–9 — Two Strands Woven Into One Narrative

Genesis 6–9 tells of Noah: of human wickedness and violence, of God's decision to blot out life from the earth, of the ark built to exact dimensions, of the animals gathered, of rain and of the fountains of the great deep, of the ark resting on the mountains of Ararat, of a raven and a dove sent out over the waters, of Noah's sacrifice and God's resolve never again to curse the ground, and of the covenant sealed with the bow in the clouds. This section presents the account as mainstream biblical scholarship presents it — stated as the scholarly account, fairly, and attributed to that scholarship throughout. It is not the only way the text has been read: Jewish and Christian tradition read it for millennia as a single unified narrative, and many readers, including many scholars of faith, still do. The documentary account is given here because this article's subject is the text's composition and transmission, and on those questions it is the account a reader will meet in the standard literature — in Julius Wellhausen's Prolegomena zur Geschichte Israels (1878), in Richard Elliott Friedman's Who Wrote the Bible? (1987), and in the introductions to modern critical translations. Quotations from Genesis in this article follow the New Revised Standard Version (NRSV), in the excerpts consulted for this article.

The Documentary Account

On the documentary hypothesis in its classical form, the Pentateuch was woven by editors from older written sources, conventionally labelled J (the Yahwist), E (the Elohist), D (the Deuteronomist), and P (the Priestly source), reaching its final form in the fifth century BCE or thereabouts. Modern scholarship debates the model's details vigorously — the sources' dates, their extent, and whether "documents" or successive layers of supplementation better describe the process — but the compositeness of the flood narrative itself is among the least contested results of the whole inquiry, because the seams are visible in the received text without any theory at all. Two tellings have been interwoven, verse by verse, and each can still be followed through the weave.

The seams, as the standard analyses — Friedman prominent among them — lay them out, include these. The narrative opens twice: once with the LORD seeing human wickedness and grieving (Genesis 6:5–8, the J strand, in the conventional attribution), and again with the earth corrupt and filled with violence in God's sight (6:11–13, the P strand). The animals are commanded twice, differently: P's Noah is to bring two of every kind (6:19–20); J's Noah is to bring seven pairs of every clean animal and one pair of the unclean, with seven pairs of the birds (7:2–3) — the clean animals in surplus because J's Noah, unlike P's, will sacrifice some of them when the waters fall. The flood's mechanism and measure differ by strand: J's flood is rain, falling forty days and forty nights; P's flood is cosmic, the fountains of the great deep bursting forth and the windows of the heavens opening, with the waters swelling for 150 days within a dated chronology that stretches the whole event to about a year, from the six hundredth year of Noah's life, second month, seventeenth day, to dry ground in the six hundred and first year, second month, twenty-seventh day. And the narrative ends twice: first with Noah's altar, the pleasing odour, and God's resolve in his heart never again to curse the ground (8:20–22, J); then with the formal covenant, its sign the bow in the clouds, addressed to Noah, his descendants, and every living creature (9:8–17, P).

"never again shall all flesh be cut off by the waters of a flood, and never again shall there be a flood to destroy the earth" — Genesis 9:11, NRSV, as quoted in the J/P flood analysis consulted for this article

Read as the documentary account proposes — two complete tellings, cut and interleaved by an editor who preserved both rather than choosing between them — the doublings stop being problems to be harmonised and become evidence to be read: evidence that Israel, like Mesopotamia, told its flood story more than once, in more than one theological dialect, and that its editors valued the inherited tellings too highly to discard either. The parallel with Section VII is exact, and it is a parallel about scribes, not about water.

Dating Genesis as a Text

On the conventional datings within the documentary model, the J strand is the earlier — placed by Friedman and others in the early monarchy, around the ninth or tenth century BCE in its classical form, though later datings have their advocates — and the P strand is exilic or post-exilic, of the sixth to fifth centuries BCE. The Babylonian exile (586–538 BCE) is the setting scholarship most often proposes for the intensive contact between Israelite scribes and Babylonian learning that the flood parallels, taken up in the next section, seem to require — though contact through earlier centuries of a shared ancient Near Eastern world cannot be excluded and is also argued. What can be said without controversy is the order of attestation: the Mesopotamian flood texts are attested in writing many centuries before any date proposed for any strand of Genesis. Priority of attestation is a fact about manuscripts. What it means is the subject of Section IX.

IX. Genesis and Mesopotamia — Vessel, Birds, Mountain, Sacrifice

The relationship between Genesis 6–9 and the Mesopotamian flood texts has been debated since George Smith's announcement in 1872, and the debate is best conducted, as this section conducts it, at the level of specific motifs — comparing what each text actually says, item by item, without harmonising the items into a single story neither text tells.

THE WARNING AND THE VESSEL

In every Mesopotamian version the hero is warned indirectly — a god speaking to a wall, a dream in Berossus — because the warning god is bound by an oath or acting against the divine council's resolve. In Genesis, God warns Noah directly, in speech, and gives the vessel's design in the same speech: an ark of cypress wood (gopher wood in older translations), 300 cubits long, 50 wide, and 30 high in P's specification, with rooms, a roof, a door, and pitch inside and out. The oblong, measured ark is Genesis's own; no Mesopotamian version gives those proportions — Gilgamesh's vessel is a cube, the Ark Tablet's a coracle.

THE CAUSE

Atrahasis: noise. Gilgamesh XI: the gods' unexplained resolve. Genesis: human wickedness, corruption, and violence, stated at length in both strands — the LORD's grief at the inclination of the human heart in J, the earth filled with violence in P. The moralisation of the flood's cause is Genesis's most consequential reworking of the inherited material, and it is a reworking: nothing in the Mesopotamian line charges humankind with sin as the flood's reason.

THE BIRDS

Gilgamesh XI: dove, swallow, raven — the first two return, the raven does not. Genesis 8: a raven first, which flies to and fro until the waters dry; then a dove, sent three times at seven-day intervals — returning, returning with an olive leaf, and finally not returning. The bird sequence is the closest single parallel between the two corpora, and even here the order, the species, and the olive leaf are Genesis's own. Berossus's three releases of unnamed birds, the last betrayed by muddy feet, stand between the two in structure — a reminder that motifs travel in more than one direction and more than one form.

THE MOUNTAIN AND THE SACRIFICE

Gilgamesh's boat grounds on Mount Nimush; Genesis's ark rests on the mountains of Ararat — a region, in the Hebrew text's geography, not a single named peak; Berossus grounds his vessel in Armenia, the same broad northern geography Ararat names. In all three, the survivor sacrifices on landing, and the divine response to the sacrifice is the story's hinge: the Mesopotamian gods gather hungrily to the savour and quarrel; Genesis's God smells the pleasing odour and resolves — in J, in his heart; in P, by covenant and sign — never to do it again. The motif is shared. The theology built on it is not.

The Scholarly Positions, Stated Fairly

Three broad positions organise the literature. The first, and the mainstream position in Assyriology and biblical studies alike, holds that the Genesis flood narrative depends — directly or through intermediaries now lost — on the Mesopotamian tradition: that Israel inherited the story from the older literary world around it, whether in the exile or earlier, and reworked it thoroughly toward its own theology: one God instead of a divided council, a moral cause instead of noise, a mortal hero who fathers the renewed world instead of an immortal removed from it, and a covenant where Mesopotamia has a quarrel and a settlement. The chain of specific parallels — warning, vessel, birds, mountain, sacrifice, in that narrative order — is, on this view, too detailed and too sequential to be independent invention.

The second position qualifies the first rather than denying it: that both corpora draw on a common ancient Near Eastern flood tradition, older than any surviving text, of which the Mesopotamian versions are the earliest written witnesses but not necessarily the source of every other telling. On this view "dependence" is too simple a word for a world in which stories moved orally and in writing, in several languages, for a thousand years. The third position — full independence, parallel invention from parallel riverine experience — is held by few specialists today for the Genesis–Mesopotamia pair specifically, precisely because of the bird sequence and the sacrifice; it remains a serious position for more distant comparisons, including the Greek case in Sections X to XII. Confessional readings that affirm Genesis's theological truth are compatible with several of these literary positions and are not adjudicated here: literary dependence, if it occurred, is a fact about scribes and manuscripts, not a verdict on what a community believes its text to be. This article's own practice follows the mainstream framing — a shared inheritance, reworked in each tradition — while keeping the hero's names, the vessels, and the theologies strictly apart, as the texts themselves do.

X. Deucalion I — Pindar and the Earliest Greek Witnesses

The Greek flood is the flood of Deucalion, son of Prometheus, and his wife Pyrrha, daughter of Epimetheus and Pandora. Its surviving witnesses are later than the Mesopotamian line's by many centuries, briefer at every stage, and — this is the point most often lost — they do not agree among themselves about the story's most basic furniture: whether there was a chest or a small boat, whether animals were saved, where the vessel landed, and why Zeus sent the water at all. The Greek tradition is a tradition, with variants, exactly as the Mesopotamian one is. It deserves to be read with the same care.

Before Pindar — Genealogy Without a Narrative

Deucalion's family is old in Greek tradition even where his flood is not yet narrated. The Hesiodic Catalogue of Women — a genealogical poem of the archaic period, surviving in fragments — already places Deucalion in the line of Prometheus and names his descendants, including Hellen, the eponymous ancestor of the Hellenes. What the early fragments give is a genealogy, not a deluge account: Deucalion matters first as a node in the family tree from which Greek peoples traced themselves. That priority — the flood hero as ancestor first, flood survivor second — shapes everything the Greek tradition later does with him, and distinguishes it at the root from both Mesopotamia, where the hero is removed from the world, and Genesis, where the hero refounds it by ordinary descent. Deucalion refounds it, in the Greek telling, by stones.

Pindar, Olympian 9 (466 BCE)

The earliest surviving Greek text to tell the flood itself as an episode is Pindar's Olympian Ode 9, composed for Epharmostus of Opus and dated to 466 BCE, consulted for this article in Diane Arnson Svarlien's translation at Perseus. Pindar's telling occupies a handful of lines inside an ode about a wrestling victory, and its economy is the evidence: by 466 BCE the story could be alluded to, in a few strokes, before an audience expected to know it. In Svarlien's translation, in the passage this article opened, Pyrrha and Deucalion come down from Parnassus by Zeus's ordinance, make their first home, and found a race of stone offspring without the marriage-bed — the stones, Pindar notes, giving the people their name, in the Greek pun on laos, people, and laas, stone. Pindar adds, in paraphrase, that they tell how the strength of the waters overwhelmed the dark earth, and how by Zeus's skill the ebbing tide suddenly drained the flood away.

"Pyrrha and Deucalion came down from Parnassus and made their first home, and without the marriage-bed they founded a unified race of stone offspring" — Pindar, Olympian 9.41–46, trans. Diane Arnson Svarlien (Perseus)

Note what Pindar does not give: no chest, no warning, no duration, no birds, no mountain landing narrated as an event — Parnassus is where the pair come down from, already survived — and no cause beyond the story's frame. The stones, by contrast, are already central. Whatever entered Greek tradition from the east, the stone-born repopulation is Greek work: it answers a Greek question — where do peoples come from, and what are the Locrians of Opus, Pindar's patrons, descended from? — that no Mesopotamian text asks.

XI. Deucalion II — Apollodorus, Ovid, and Lycaon

Apollodorus, Library 1.7.2

The fullest prose summary of the Greek flood is the Library (Bibliotheca) attributed to Apollodorus — a mythographic compendium generally dated to the first or second century CE and cited as Pseudo-Apollodorus in modern scholarship — consulted for this article in J. G. Frazer's Loeb translation at Perseus, Library 1.7.2. In Frazer's translation, in paraphrase with the key phrases verified: when Zeus would destroy the men of the Bronze Age, Deucalion, on Prometheus's advice, constructed a chest — a larnax — stored it with provisions, and embarked in it with Pyrrha. Zeus poured heavy rain from heaven and flooded the greater part of Greece; all men were destroyed except a few who fled to the high mountains nearby. Deucalion floated in the chest over the sea for nine days and as many nights, drifted to Parnassus, landed when the rain ceased, and sacrificed to Zeus Phyxios, Zeus the god of Escape. Zeus sent Hermes and let Deucalion choose what he would; he chose to get men; and at Zeus's bidding the pair threw stones over their heads — Deucalion's stones becoming men, Pyrrha's becoming women — whence, the text says, people were called laos metaphorically, from laas, a stone.

Three features of Apollodorus's account deserve emphasis. First, its stated cause is not Lycaon but the Bronze Age men — Hesiod's violent bronze race of the Works and Days — whose destruction Zeus wills as the end of an age. Second, no animals are rescued in this version: the chest holds two people and their provisions, nothing more. The menagerie belongs to Genesis and to Berossus, not to the Greek chest. Third, the flood is, in Apollodorus's own geography, principally a Greek flood: it overwhelms the world outside the Isthmus and Peloponnese, Thessaly's mountains part, and a few unnamed others survive by fleeing uphill — qualifications no universalising retelling should silently remove. Apollodorus himself, at Library 3.8, preserves the alternative Arcadian tradition in which the flood's occasion is the impiety of Lycaon's sons — the two explanations coexist in the same handbook, unharmonised, exactly as J and P coexist in Genesis.

Ovid, Metamorphoses 1 — and Lycaon

The version most readers know is Roman. Ovid's Metamorphoses, completed around 8 CE, tells the flood at length in Book 1 (lines 125–415 in the usual numbering), consulted for this article in Brookes More's translation at Perseus. Ovid's framing story is Lycaon's. Jupiter, having heard of the age's wickedness, comes to earth in human form and is received by Lycaon, king of Arcadia, who tests his guest's divinity by serving him the flesh of a hostage. Jupiter destroys the house, and Lycaon flees and becomes a wolf — the name itself carrying the Greek word for wolf, lykos. Before the council of the gods, Jupiter declares the species corrupt and chooses the flood — setting the thunderbolt aside, Ovid has him reason, lest fire catch the sky itself — and looses the rains and the rivers together: the south wind with its downpour, Iris drawing up water for the clouds, Neptune striking the earth with his trident. Ovid's flood is the most vividly painted in ancient literature — dolphins among treetops, wolves swimming among sheep, birds dropping exhausted into a sea without shores — and the least nautically equipped: in Ovid there is no chest built in advance and no warning at all. Deucalion and Pyrrha survive in a small boat, spared, Ovid says in paraphrase, because no man was ever more devoted to justice and no woman more reverent to the gods. The boat grounds on Parnassus, whose twin peaks alone stand above the water.

Ovid's closing movement is the oracle of Themis. Alone in a silent world, the pair consult the goddess and are told — in Brookes More's translation, verified for this article — to veil their brows, ungird their robes, and cast behind them the bones of their great mother. Pyrrha at first refuses, fearing to violate her mother's bones; Deucalion solves the riddle: the great mother is Earth, and her bones are stones. They throw the stones, and the stones soften into human form — Deucalion's into men, Pyrrha's into women — whence, Ovid concludes, humankind is a hard race, used to toil: the stones explain the species' character as neatly as Genesis's covenant explains its future and Atrahasis's settlement explains its mortality. Each tradition ends its flood by explaining the present. That shared function, more than any shared detail, is what a flood story is for, in every literature this article surveys — Egypt, as we shall now see, performing the same function by entirely different means.

XII. The Greek Flood in Its Own World — Local, Brief, and Distinct

Greek tradition knew more floods than Deucalion's, and placing his among them corrects the picture that comparison with Genesis alone creates. Greek local histories remembered a flood of Ogyges, placed in Boeotia and Attica and dated by later chronographers earlier than Deucalion's; a flood in the time of Dardanus, attached to Samothrace and the Troad; and the ordinary, recurrent flooding of Thessaly's enclosed plain, whose draining through the Vale of Tempe Greek writers themselves discussed in geological as well as mythical terms. Deucalion's was the flood that became Panhellenic — because his son was Hellen — not the only flood Greece remembered. A tradition with several local floods, of which one is promoted to general significance by genealogy, is behaving exactly as the Mesopotamian tradition behaved with its city traditions, and exactly as a reader of texts, rather than of harmonised "mythology," should expect.

The witnesses also disagree about the landing place. Parnassus is the majority tradition — Pindar, Apollodorus, Ovid — but Hellanicus, quoted by the scholia on Pindar, placed the chest's landing on Mount Othrys in Thessaly, and other local traditions claimed Mount Athos and even Etna. The variants are preserved in the scholarship consulted for this article and are stated here for what they show: Greek cities and regions competed for the flood's geography as they competed for every prestigious myth's geography. The landing place was a claim, not a datum.

// DID THE GREEK FLOOD COME FROM THE EAST? //

The question is legitimate and unsettled. The Greek telling shares the warning, the vessel, the mountain, and the post-flood sacrifice with the eastern line, and Greek contact with the Near East in the archaic period is not in doubt. Against a simple borrowing stand the telling's brevity in its early witnesses, the absence of animals, birds, dimensions, and durations of the eastern kind, the centrality of the stones — a Greek folk-etymology with no eastern parallel — and the existence of independent Greek local floods. The honest summary is the one the evidence supports: some eastern influence on the Greek flood tradition is widely considered plausible; the surviving Greek texts are, in every line we can actually read, Greek compositions doing Greek work. Both halves of that sentence must be kept.

XIII. The Egyptian Question — Stated Plainly

Egypt has no native universal-flood myth. The statement should be made exactly that plainly, because it is the finding, and because it is so often evaded — by popular works that list "the Egyptian flood" alongside the Mesopotamian, biblical, and Greek ones as if the symmetry of the list were itself evidence. It is not. No Egyptian text, in three thousand years of surviving literature — Pyramid Texts, Coffin Texts, Book of the Dead, temple inscriptions, tales, hymns, and royal funerary books — tells of a deluge of water sent to destroy mankind, survived by a warned hero in a vessel with animals, ending on a mountain with a sacrifice. There is no Egyptian Noah, no Egyptian ark, no Egyptian raven or dove. The slots in the comparative table that other traditions fill are, for Egypt, empty — and Sections XIV to XVI argue that the emptiness is systematic: it follows from what water meant in Egypt, and the texts that occupy the neighbouring slots — the inundation theology, the Destruction of Mankind, and the primeval waters Nun — show the system at work.

Two apparent counterexamples should be dealt with now, because both dissolve on inspection and both are instructive. The first is the goddess Mehet-Weret, whose name means "Great Flood" or "Great Swimmer," a celestial cow goddess associated with the waters of heaven and with the inundation's arrival. Her name contains a flood — but the flood it names is the annual inundation itself, the beneficent rising of the Nile, personified as a mother who brings fertility. A goddess of the yearly flood is evidence for Section XIV's argument, not against this section's: in Egypt the flood is a goddess who feeds the land, not a weapon that empties it. The second is the red beer of the Heavenly Cow myth, poured over the fields in Section XV — a flood, of a kind, of seven thousand jars of dyed beer. It is a flood that saves mankind, by intoxicating its destroyer, and it works precisely by resembling blood on inundated fields. Egypt's one "flood" in a destruction myth is the antidote to the destruction, not its instrument.

// ON ABSENCE AS EVIDENCE //

An absence is evidence when the corpus is large, well-preserved, and topically complete — and Egypt's is. Egyptian literature preserves creation accounts, destruction accounts, judgment accounts, and water theology in abundance, across three millennia. That none of them is a universal deluge narrative is not a gap in preservation. It is a fact about the tradition, of the same order as Mesopotamia's possession of four tellings. The Archive's Egyptology division treats the relevant texts in depth: see Hathor on the Heavenly Cow, and Ennead and Ogdoad on Nun and the primeval waters.

XIV. The Inundation — Water as Order and Renewal

The explanation scholars standardly give for the Egyptian absence begins with geography, and it is a good explanation as far as it goes. Mesopotamia's rivers, the Tigris and Euphrates, flood irregularly, in spring, at harvest's edge, with destructive force that no calendar can fix; catastrophic river flooding was a recurring trauma of southern Iraqi life, and its flood traditions grow from that soil. Egypt's river behaves oppositely. The Nile's inundation — the akhet season — arrived with famous regularity in summer, rose predictably, spread over the basin-irrigated fields, deposited its silt, and withdrew in time for sowing. It was measured, officially and anxiously, on Nilometers, because too low a flood meant famine and too high a flood meant destroyed villages and dikes — but within its normal range, which was most years, the flood was not the enemy of Egyptian life. It was its engine. A civilisation does not make its annual blessing into its primeval catastrophe.

The theology matches the hydrology. The inundation was personified as Hapy — not a god of the Nile as a river, a distinction Egyptologists are careful about, but the god of the inundation itself, pictured as a fecund, heavy-breasted figure bringing water and food plants. The rising water was understood as the primeval waters of Nun welling up into the ordered world — creation's water, returning yearly, on schedule, to renew the land's fertility (see Section XVI). In the language of Egyptian theology, the inundation is maat in action: order, regularity, and rightness made visible as a river behaving as it should. Its failure modes were failures of absence or excess at the margins — famine years remembered bitterly in texts such as the Famine Stela (a Ptolemaic composition set in Djoser's reign) — not a myth of total destruction by water in the primeval past. Where Mesopotamia's deepest water-memory is of a flood that ended an age, Egypt's is of a flood that begins every year.

This is also the place to note, with the fairness this article owes every tradition, that the geographical explanation is an explanation of plausibility, not a proof. Scholars who advance it — and it is the standard account in Egyptological introductions — are explaining why a deluge myth would have had no native soil in Egypt, not demonstrating a law that river behaviour dictates mythology. The Nile could flood destructively, and Egyptians knew it; the theologians' achievement was to have built, over centuries, a system in which that knowledge was housed at the margins — in famine texts, in warnings about high Niles — while the centre of the system held water as order. Systems, not rivers, are what myths are made of; the river is the occasion, the theology is the cause. For the Archive's treatment of order and its opposite, see Ma'at and Isfet in the Egyptology division — a file verified as present in the local archive for this cross-link.

XV. The Book of the Heavenly Cow — Destruction, But Not by Water

Egypt does have a great myth of mankind's near-destruction, and its difference from the flood traditions is the whole point. The Book of the Heavenly Cow — also titled in its first movement The Destruction of Mankind — is a New Kingdom royal funerary composition, written in Middle Egyptian and possibly of Middle Kingdom origin on the usual scholarly view. Its earliest known copy is the incomplete version on the outermost gilded shrine of Tutankhamun; complete versions survive on the walls of the tombs of Seti I, Ramesses II, and Ramesses III, with a further Ramesside papyrus copy in Turin. The standard modern edition is Erik Hornung's Das Buch von der Himmelskuh (1982); English readers meet the text in Miriam Lichtheim's Ancient Egyptian Literature and in the translation by Hornung in William Kelly Simpson's edition of The Literature of Ancient Egypt (2003). This article paraphrases the myth; it quotes none of it, not having opened a full translation for this article, and it follows the account as presented in the Egyptology scholarship consulted, including this Archive's own Hathor article.

The story, in paraphrase: Ra, who came into being by himself, has grown old as king of gods and mankind together — his bones silver, his flesh gold, his hair true lapis lazuli, in the text's famous image — and mankind plots against him. Ra summons his council in secret: his Eye, Shu, Tefnut, Geb, Nut, and the primeval parents who were with him in Nun, with Nun himself bringing his courtiers. Ra declares that he will not slay mankind before hearing counsel. Nun's counsel is to send out the Eye. The council's word, in paraphrase, is that no eye is more able to smite the conspirators, and that it should descend as Hathor. The goddess goes out and slaughters mankind in the desert. She returns exulting, and Ra — who wanted the rebels punished, not the species erased — asserts his kingship and calls the slaughter off: in the Archive's Hathor article's paraphrase of the turn, he will keep power over them as king, and she is to hold off diminishing them. At that turn the text marks a transformation in a single clause: and so Sekhmet — the Powerful One — came into being. The raging Eye, now beyond recall, will wade in blood the next morning as far as Herakleopolis.

Ra's countermeasure is the myth's second famous movement, and the only flood in it. Messengers run south to Elephantine for red ochre; the ochre is ground into beer-mash; seven thousand jars of beer, red as blood, are brewed and, in the night, poured out over the fields. The goddess comes at dawn to slaughter, finds the fields flooded with what she takes for blood, drinks, grows drunk and merry, and takes no more cognisance of mankind. Humanity is saved — by a simulated inundation of beer. The myth's second half then reorganises the cosmos: Ra, weary of ruling earth directly, withdraws to the sky on the back of Nut become the heavenly cow, supported by Shu and the Heh gods; Thoth is appointed his deputy for the night; and the present, imperfect order of the world — gods withdrawn, death and suffering within it — is established as the consequence of the rebellion and its punishment. Hornung's influential reading, as the scholarship consulted reports it, treats the work as a theodicy: an account of why the world is fractured and fallen, functionally parallel to the flood myths' accounts of why human life is limited — and achieved without a drop of destructive water.

// THE COMPARISON, EXACTLY //

Set the Heavenly Cow beside Atrahasis and the pattern is precise. Both are primeval histories in which a supreme god, provoked by mankind — by noise in Babylon, by rebellion in Egypt — moves to destroy it, is restrained by his own second thought, and settles for a permanently diminished human condition. The slot is the same slot. The instrument is opposite: in Babylon the destroyer is water and the restraint is a boat; in Egypt the destroyer is the fiery Eye and the restraint is a flood — of beer. Traditions answer shared questions with the materials their own theologies give them. That, and not a shared deluge, is what the parallel shows.

XVI. Nun — Primeval Waters That Begin the World

The deepest reason Egyptian water cannot be a deluge is that Egyptian water is already spoken for: it is the beginning. Nun — nwn — is the primeval water that exists before creation: undifferentiated, inert, boundless, the state in which nothing has yet been distinguished from anything else. From Nun, in the Heliopolitan account, the creator — Atum, who came into being by himself — emerges on the primeval mound, the first dry place, and creation proceeds by differentiation: Shu and Tefnut, air and moisture; Geb and Nut, earth and sky; and the ordered world. In the Hermopolitan account, the Ogdoad — eight primeval powers in four pairs, of whom Nun and his counterpart Naunet, the primeval water and its depth, are the first — precede the mound's rising and the sun's first appearance upon it. The Archive's Egyptology division treats both systems in depth: Atum for the creator in and from Nun, and Ennead and Ogdoad for the eightfold primeval company.

Three properties of Nun decide the flood question. First, Nun is pre-moral: it is not sent, it does not punish, it has no quarrel with mankind, who do not yet exist when it reigns. A deluge requires an agent and an offence; Nun supplies neither. Second, Nun never disappears. Creation does not drain it; the ordered world remains an island within it — Nun beneath the earth, from which the inundation and the wells rise; Nun above the sky, from which rain can fall; Nun beyond every horizon. The yearly flood, on this theology, is Nun visiting the island on schedule and by invitation, not besieging it. Third — and here Egyptian eschatology completes the picture — the world will one day return to Nun. In the famous passage of Book of the Dead Spell 175, as the Egyptology scholarship presents it, Atum declares that after millions of years he will destroy what he has made, that the land will return to Nun, to the floodwaters, as at its first state, and that he himself will remain, transformed, with Osiris. The end of the Egyptian world is a return to water — but it is a dissolution at the end of immeasurable time, by the creator's own word, with no hero, no vessel, no survivors to repopulate, and no offence punished. It is the opposite of a deluge myth in every feature except the water.

Egypt, in short, did not lack the imaginative materials for a flood story. It had primeval waters, a destruction of mankind, a world-ending return to the deep, and a river that rose every year. It assembled them — over three thousand years, in text after text — into a system in which water creates, renews, and finally receives the world, and in which destruction, when it comes for mankind, comes by fire from the sun's Eye. The absence of the deluge is not a missing piece. It is the shape of the whole. Readers who meet "the Egyptian flood myth" in popular works are meeting, without exception, one of three things: the Heavenly Cow misdescribed, Mehet-Weret's name mistranslated into a narrative, or the biblical and Mesopotamian stories projected onto a civilisation that conspicuously declined to tell them.

XVIII. What Comparison Can and Cannot Show

What Comparison Can Show

Comparison, practised on texts with dates and manuscripts, can show literary transmission, and in this field it has shown it beyond reasonable dispute at least twice. Tablet XI's dependence on Atrahasis is a demonstrated case: shared lines, shared structure, and a known direction of borrowing, from the older poem into the later epic. The Genesis case, argued in Section IX, is a strong case of the same kind at one remove: a shared sequence of specific motifs — warning, vessel, birds in a testing sequence, mountain, sacrifice, divine resolve — between a younger literature and an older, neighbouring, demonstrably circulating one, with the exile providing a historically concrete channel on the mainstream account. Comparison can also show reworking: the systematic substitution, in Genesis, of a moral cause for a demographic one, of a covenant for a quarrel, of descent for translation — substitutions too patterned to be accidental, which reveal the receiving tradition's theology more clearly than its own statements of it. And comparison can show independence where it exists: the Greek stones, the Egyptian Eye, and Nun's priority are not borrowings, and establishing that is as much a result as establishing a loan.

What Comparison Cannot Show

Comparison cannot, by itself, show an event. A motif shared by four literatures is evidence that literatures share motifs — by contact, by common inheritance, or by the convergent pressures of similar environments — and each of those explanations must be argued case by case, as Sections IX and XII argue them. The step from "these stories are related" to "these stories remember one historical deluge" requires evidence of a different kind: geological, dated, and proportioned to the claim. That evidence has been sought, seriously and repeatedly, and its present state should be reported exactly.

In Mesopotamia itself, excavators at Ur, Kish, Shuruppak, and Uruk found water-laid silt layers, and catastrophic river flooding was a recurring reality of life between the rivers. Leonard Woolley's famous flood layer at Ur, announced in 1929 as evidence of the biblical deluge, proved on examination to be a local deposit of one period; the layers at the several sites date to different periods and do not combine into a single event. They establish — what no one doubted — that southern Mesopotamians knew destructive floods at first hand, and they make a local, devastating flood or floods a plausible occasion for the tradition's imagery. They do not identify an event of which the texts are memories, and no text dates itself to any of them.

The most prominent modern attempt to supply the missing event is the Black Sea deluge hypothesis, proposed by William Ryan and Walter Pitman in 1997 and popularised in their Noah's Flood (1998): that around 5600 BCE, on their original dating — revised in later work by Ryan and colleagues to an earlier date — Mediterranean seawater breached the Bosporus sill catastrophically, drowning some 100,000 square kilometres around a freshwater Black Sea lake, displacing farming communities whose dispersed memories became the flood traditions of the Near East. The hypothesis is stated here as its authors state it, and its present scholarly standing must be stated with it: it is contested. Subsequent geological work — notably studies arguing for a gradual reconnection and a far smaller rise, including Liviu Giosan and colleagues' Danube delta research published in 2009, which put the rise at some 5–10 metres rather than 50–60 and the drowned area at a small fraction of the original claim — has persuaded much of the geological community that any Early Holocene Black Sea flooding was modest and progressive rather than catastrophic, and archaeological surveys have not produced the displaced-settlement horizon the hypothesis predicts. Even were the catastrophic version to be sustained, a further step would remain: connecting a seventh- or sixth-millennium BCE event, around the Black Sea, to texts first written down in southern Iraq more than four thousand years later requires a chain of transmission for which there is at present no direct evidence. The hypothesis is a legitimate scientific proposal that has not carried its field; it is reported here as exactly that, neither as the flood's explanation nor as a folly.

// THE ARTICLE'S OWN POSITION //

The texts are the evidence this Archive is competent to present, and on the texts the conclusions are firm: a Mesopotamian tradition, transmitted and revised for two thousand years; a Genesis narrative woven from two strands and standing in a real, debated, motif-level relationship to that tradition; a Greek telling, later and distinct, doing genealogical work; and an Egyptian system with no deluge at all, for reasons its own texts make intelligible. Whether any historical flood stands behind the Mesopotamian line's first telling is a question the texts cannot answer and this article does not pretend they can. The deluge as text is not a lesser subject than the deluge as event. It is the subject for which evidence actually exists.

For the flood as the hinge of claimed chronology — the eight kings, the 241,200 years, and the single line of water in the King List — see the companion article, The Antediluvian King Lists. For Egypt's gods ruling before its kings, the tradition with which Egyptian deep time actually begins, see Divine Dynasties; and for a later Greek construction built on a drowned land, told by a philosopher who names his own sources and his own doubts, see Plato's Atlantis — the instructive contrast of a Tier B tradition that announces its own artifice.
