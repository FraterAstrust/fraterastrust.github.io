I. The Genre That Carried the Myths

Ask where a fifth-century Athenian actually encountered the stories this hub retells — Oedipus and his house, in The Theban Cycle; the sack of Troy and its long aftermath, in The Trojan Cycle; Medea at Corinth, in The Argonauts — and the honest answer is not “in Homer.” Epic was recited, certainly, and Homer stood at the centre of schooling. But the living, current, argumentative form in which myth was remade year by year, before the largest audience any Greek literary genre ever commanded, was tragedy: the competitive drama staged at Athens' festivals of Dionysos. For roughly a century and a half, from the late sixth century BCE to the end of the fifth, Athenian poets produced new tragedies by the hundred, almost all of them built from the inherited mythic stock, and almost all of them lost. What the city watched, argued about, quoted in its lawcourts and assemblies, and carried in its memory was this genre. When a later Greek — or a Roman, or a modern reader — speaks of what “the myth says” about Medea or Helen or Oedipus, the version being cited is, far more often than is realised, the version a tragedian fixed on the Athenian stage.

That claim has three parts, which the sections keep distinct. First, tragedy was an institution before it was a literature: a state-run religious festival, financed by a tax on the rich, judged by lot-drawn citizens, attended as an act of citizenship (Sections II–IV). Second, it was a craft with a documented technical history — one actor, then two, then three; the chorus's share shrinking; scene-painting — known chiefly from one fourth-century witness, Aristotle, whose testimony must be weighed as retrospection, not as a contemporary record (Sections V–VI). Third, it was the ancient world's most powerful engine of mythic revision: a machine that took variant-rich stories, forced each poet to embody one version before the whole city, and thereby canonised some versions and buried others — including, in the hardest case examined here, a Medea that may be one poet's invention and is now simply “the myth” (Sections VII–XI). The plays' survival, by school-selection and manuscript accident, closes the article (XIII–XIV).

This article quotes no ancient text verbatim. Tragedy is a genre whose famous lines circulate in dozens of competing translations, and the hub's rule — quotation only from a verified published translation, named in place — is met here by the stricter course: every rendering of an ancient author's sense is written as paraphrase and labelled as paraphrase at the point of use. The editions from which the paraphrases are made are named in Section XIV and in the Sources.

A word on what tragedy was not. It was not, in the first instance, cult. The plays were performed in a sanctuary, in the god's presence — his image was carried into the theatre, his priest sat in the front row — and that frame matters (Section II). But a tragedy is a literary artefact, composed by a named poet for a prize, and its gods are characters in an argument, not objects of worship. The hub's distinction holds throughout: sacrifice, procession, and dithyramb are cult practice; the tetralogy beside them is literature, and the two must not be collapsed into a claim that tragedy “was ritual.” Equally, a tragedy is not a transcript of what Athenians believed. It is what three competing poets could make their fellow citizens sit through, weep at, and vote for — and the difference between belief and prize-winning representation is precisely the space in which tragedy did its work on myth.

THE SCALE OF THE LOSS

The three tragedians whose work survives complete — Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides — wrote, on the ancient catalogues' figures, some three hundred plays between them, and they were three among dozens of competing poets. Thirty-two tragedies survive whole. Section XIII accounts for the arithmetic and for the selection that produced it.

Where Wing I retells a myth, the version retold is usually a tragedian's. The Prometheus Bound discussed in Makers and Fire, the descents catalogued in Descents, the house of Atreus, the house of Laius — all passed through this genre's hands, and bear its fingerprints.

One more preliminary, of method. The evidence is uneven: magnificent for the plays themselves, inscriptional for the festival (victor-lists, the production records called didascaliae, choregic monuments), and thin for the early history, where we depend on one fourth-century theorist and late biographical traditions of mixed reliability. Where a cherished fact rests on a single late witness, the article says so at the point of use.

II. The City Dionysia: A Festival Before It Was a Theatre

The City Dionysia — the Great Dionysia, held in the month Elaphebolion, roughly March into April — was Athens' grandest festival of Dionysos, and the home of tragic competition. Its timing was itself a civic statement. By Elaphebolion the winter seas had reopened; the city was full of foreign envoys, allied delegations, traders, and visitors, and Athens staged the festival, in part, before that international audience. The god honoured was Dionysos Eleuthereus, named for Eleutherae, the border town from which his cult image was said to have come to Athens; his sanctuary lay on the south slope of the Acropolis, and the theatre grew up inside it. The god of the festival — his myths, his ecstasies, and his initiatory cults — is treated in this hub in The Mysteries; what belongs here is the festival as the city ran it.

The festival ran for several days, and its exact day-by-day order is reconstructed — the word should be noted — from inscriptions, orators, and scholia that do not always agree; the sequence given here is the standard modern reconstruction, stated at that strength. It opened with the pompē, the great procession: the god's image, escorted out of the city and brought back by torchlight in a re-enactment of his first arrival, was carried through streets lined with offerings to his sanctuary, and bulls were led to sacrifice and a public feast. Citizens marched in their orders; resident foreigners marched too, in distinguishing dress; colonies and allies sent offerings. The procession was the festival's cultic heart, and it preceded the plays: whatever tragedy later became, it grew up inside a rite in which the city first presented itself, ranked and ordered, to its god.

The Civic Ceremonies

Around the performances the city wrapped ceremonies that had nothing to do with drama and everything to do with the occasion. In the theatre, before the assembled allies, the year's tribute from the subject-allies of Athens' empire was displayed, talent by talent — a deliberate exhibition of power. Generals poured libations; public honours were proclaimed. And the war orphans — sons of citizens killed in the city's service, reared at public expense — were paraded in full armour provided by the state and given seats of honour: the democracy exhibiting its side of a bargain, that the city raises the sons of those who die for it. That this ceremony shared a programme with Persians and Antigone is the frame that tells us what a tragedy was: not entertainment after the serious business, but one instrument among several by which the assembled city defined and argued about itself.

The Dithyramb

Before the dramas came the dithyramb, the choral hymn to Dionysos, organised by the ten tribes of the citizen body. Each tribe fielded two choruses — one of men, one of boys — fifty voices each: a thousand citizen performers in all, in tribal competition. The dithyramb matters here for two reasons. It is the likeliest matrix from which tragedy grew, on Aristotle's account (Section VI); and it shows the festival's deep grammar: competition was not an ornament on worship but the form worship and citizenship jointly took — tribe against tribe, poet against poet, choregos against choregos, in the god's honour, before the whole city. The dithyrambic prize was a bronze tripod, set on monuments along the Street of the Tripods east of the theatre; one still stands in Athens, that of the choregos Lysicrates, dated 335/4 BCE — a fourth-century stone witness to a competition structure already two centuries old.

THE ARCHON'S CHOICE

The festival was administered by the archon eponymous, Athens' chief civil magistrate. Months ahead, he selected the competing poets — in the tragic contest, three — and assigned each a choregos. A poet refused a chorus was, for that year, silenced: the archon's grant was the gate through which every tragedy had to pass, and ancient tradition preserves the refusals and favouritisms as political acts, which they were.

THE JUDGES

Prizes were awarded by judges drawn by lot from lists submitted by the tribes, sworn, and sequestered against bribery and pressure — and even so, the count was hedged: on the standard account of the procedure, only a portion of the judges' ballots, drawn by lot from those cast, determined the result. The detail matters for Section X: a poet's recorded placing — first, second, third — is the output of a partly randomised procedure, not a clean plebiscite of the audience.

THE AUDIENCE, AT HONEST STRENGTH

That the audience was overwhelmingly citizen, male, and seated by tribe is secure. Whether women attended is a genuine scholarly dispute: no surviving source states the matter plainly either way, arguments from silence point in both directions, and responsible accounts now state the question as open. This article states it as open.

Comedy belonged to the festival too — five comic poets in competition, admitted to the City Dionysia in 486 BCE by the standard dating; its evidence for tragedy is so valuable that it gets its own section below (Section XII). Tragic and comic contests were parts of one programme, before one audience, under one god. Tragedy was not a shelf in a library. It was three days in a crowded sanctuary, between a procession and a drinking-party, in a city talking to itself.

III. The Tetralogy, the Choregia, and the Theatre of Dionysos

The unit of tragic competition was not the play but the tetralogy. Each of the three selected poets presented, on his day, three tragedies followed by a satyr play — four plays in sequence, from morning into the afternoon, in daylight, in the open air. The three tragedies were often, especially in the earlier fifth century, connected: a trilogy on one mythic sequence, as the Oresteia is (Section VII). But connection was a choice, not a rule — Sophocles, the ancient tradition reports, abandoned the writing of connected trilogies, and Euripides' tetralogies were normally four treatments of unrelated stories. The satyr play that closed the set was a different register entirely: a burlesque on a mythic theme, performed by a chorus of satyrs — the half-animal companions of Dionysos — with the myth's heroes travestied in their company. It returned the audience, after three plays of suffering, to the god of the festival in his earthiest form, and it reminded them whose occasion this was. Of the hundreds of satyr plays produced, exactly one survives complete: Euripides' Cyclops, a drunken travesty of Odysseus' encounter with Polyphemus — its survival, and what it does and does not let us generalise from, is taken up in Section XIII.

The Choregia: Paying for the Chorus

A tetralogy was expensive, and the city did not pay for it directly. It taxed it. Under the liturgy system — the mechanism by which Athens funded public services from private wealth — the archon appointed a wealthy citizen as chorēgos, sponsor, for each poet. The choregos recruited the chorus and paid for its training, costumes and masks, the piper, and the rehearsal space, across months of preparation; the state supplied the theatre and, by arrangements that shifted over the period, the principal actors. The poet himself directed: he composed the music, choreographed the dances, trained the chorus, and in the early period acted in his own plays. Choregia was a burden — a major production could cost in the range of a trireme's fitting-out, the other great liturgy — and simultaneously one of the chief honours a rich Athenian could win, because the prize was shared: victor-lists name poet and choregos together, and choregic monuments are the sponsor's self-advertisement in stone. The splendour of the chorus that embodied Theban elders before the city was thus a rich man's competitive display — a fact about tragedy's material basis that its texts, read in silence, conceal.

The Theatre

The Theatre of Dionysos stood — and its remains stand — on the south slope of the Acropolis, within the god's sanctuary, beside his temple. Its elements are the elements of every Greek theatre: the theatron, the “seeing-place”, a great semicircular bowl of seating rising up the slope; the orchestra, the circular dancing-floor at its foot, where the chorus performed; and the skēnē, the stage building behind the orchestra, whose front served as the plays' backdrop — a palace door, a temple, a tent — and whose roof and doorways the action used. Two cautions govern everything said about this building. First, the theatre a visitor sees today is largely the stone rebuilding associated with the statesman Lycurgus, around 330 BCE, with later Roman alterations: the fifth-century theatre of Aeschylus and Sophocles was a more provisional structure of wooden seating on the same slope, and its capacity — estimates for the stone theatre run into the range of fourteen to seventeen thousand — should not be projected back onto the wooden original, which held, on most reckonings, several thousand fewer. Second, the stagecraft the texts imply was achieved with simple means: a wheeled platform, the ekkyklēma, rolled out to display interior scenes — characteristically, corpses; and a crane, the mēchanē, that swung a god or a flying hero over the stage building. The second of those machines gives this article one of its key terms in Section IX. Neither machine is described by any fifth-century source; both are inferred from the plays and mocked, helpfully, by comedy. The inference is standard, and is stated here as inference.

Performance conditions followed from the architecture. Plays were given once, in daylight, without intervals, the chorus's odes marking the joints of the action. Masks covered the whole head; costume distinguished king from messenger, Greek from foreigner, god from mortal, at distances where facial expression was useless anyway. Three actors, by rapid changes of mask and dress, populated plays with a dozen speaking parts — which is why tragic scenes are overwhelmingly confrontations of two, the third actor's entrances husbanded for maximum pressure. The form's famous economy is the direct imprint, on the texts we still read, of a hillside, a daylight sky, three masked men, and fifteen dancing citizens.

IV. The Lesser Dionysia: Lenaia and the Rural Festivals

The City Dionysia was the summit, but it was not the whole mountain. Athens and Attica kept a calendar of Dionysiac festivals, and two of them carried dramatic competitions of their own. They matter to the record because some canonical facts about tragedy — including the first performance of the comedy that judges tragedy in Section XII — belong to them, and because they show the genre operating at other scales: mid-winter, inward-looking, and local.

The Lenaia

The Lenaia fell in Gamelion, roughly January — mid-winter, when the seas were closed and the audience was Athens talking to itself, without the allied delegations and foreign visitors of the spring festival. Its sanctuary, the Lenaion, lay somewhere in the city — its exact site is disputed and unknown — and its competitions were administered by the archon basileus, the “king archon”, rather than the eponymous archon. Comedy was the Lenaia's senior genre: comic competition there is older than the tragic contest, which was added around the 430s BCE on the usual dating. Tragedy at the Lenaia was a smaller affair — two poets, on the standard reconstruction, with two tragedies each and no satyr plays. Yet the festival's inwardness gave it a distinctive evidentiary value. Aristophanes' Frogs, the richest ancient document on tragedy's standing in its own city, was produced at the Lenaia of 405 BCE (Section XII) — a play about the tragic stage, performed in winter, to the home crowd, weeks after the deaths of both Euripides and Sophocles had emptied that stage. Aristophanes jokes, in a parabasis paraphrased here, that at this festival there are no tribute-bearing allies to flatter: the Lenaia was where Athens spoke most freely, because nobody else was listening.

The Rural Dionysia

The Rural Dionysia belonged not to the city but to the demes — the villages and townships of Attica — and fell in Poseideon, roughly December. Each deme kept its own festival and procession, and the larger demes maintained theatres and staged plays: Piraeus had a notable theatre and festival; Eleusis, Ikarion, Thorikos and others are attested with performances of varying ambition. Ikarion carries a particular charge, as the named home deme of Thespis (Section V). The rural programmes mixed new work with revivals and formed the base of a pyramid whose apex was the City Dionysia; the deme theatres — Thorikos preserves one of Attica's earliest stone theatres — are the archaeology of the genre's diffusion. From 386 BCE a decree added revivals of old tragedies to the City Dionysia's programme: the first formal admission that the genre now had classics (Section XIII).

V. Origins: Thespis and the Limits of the Record

Every account of tragedy's origin must begin by confessing the shape of its evidence. The genre emerges, fully competitive and state-run, in records of the late sixth century BCE; the process by which it emerged occupies the preceding decades, for which we have no contemporary documents at all. What we have is a tradition — assembled by later Greeks from records, memories, and inference — and one great theorist, Aristotle, writing in the fourth century with access to production records now lost. The tradition's central figure is Thespis, and the honest treatment of Thespis is this article's test case for handling the whole origin question: he must be presented at exactly the strength of his attestation — no stronger, and no weaker.

The attestation, in summary, is this. The Parian Marble, a chronological inscription carved on Paros in the third century BCE, records — in the entry conventionally dated to the years around 534 BCE — that Thespis was the first to produce a tragedy at Athens and to win the prize, in the tradition's phrasing a goat. Aristotle's Poetics, earlier and weightier, describes the mechanism of the beginning without naming Thespis at all: tragedy arose, he says, from the leaders of the dithyramb — the solo voice that led off the choral hymn stepping out, answering the chorus, and becoming, in that exchange, the first actor (Section VI). Later sources fill in the man: Thespis of Ikarion, the Attic deme with its own Dionysiac traditions (Section IV); Thespis as the inventor of the acted prologue and speech, in Themistius' much later summary of the Aristotelian tradition; Thespis in Horace's Art of Poetry, a Roman poet's version, touring the villages with a wagon, his players' faces smeared with wine-lees. A list of his play-titles survives in the Byzantine Suda. And the word for actor in Greek, hypokritēs — literally “answerer” — fits Aristotle's mechanism exactly: the one who answers the chorus.

// THE THESPIS TRADITION, AT HONEST STRENGTH //

What is solid: by the later sixth century BCE, tragic competition existed at the City Dionysia, and Greek tradition from the third century BCE onward attached its invention to a named man, Thespis, at a date around 534 BCE. What is not solid: every biographical particular. The wagon, the wine-lees, the village tours are late literary colouring; the surviving play-titles and the four lines quoted under Thespis' name were already suspected in antiquity — a tradition reported by Diogenes Laertius holds that the Thespian corpus was forged by Heraclides Ponticus in the fourth century BCE. Modern scholarship divides between treating Thespis as a historical innovator whose achievement the tradition simplified, and treating him as the kind of culture-hero figure to whom Greek tradition habitually assigned inventions. This article presents him as the tradition's named founder, at that strength, and builds nothing further on the details.

The deeper question — what tragedy grew from — has attracted answers of very different evidential kinds, and they should be sorted, not blended. Aristotle's account is formal: tragedy developed from the dithyramb's improvisations, passing through a phase he calls satyric — close to the satyr play in metre and dance — before acquiring, late, its dignity and its iambic metre. Whatever its gaps, this is a fourth-century scholar's reconstruction from records, and it is the account with the best claim on our assent. The etymology of tragōidia, “goat-song”, is a fact of language in search of a referent: the goat may be the prize (as the Parian Marble's tradition suggests), the sacrifice at which the song was sung, or an animal whose guise the singers wore — all three explanations are ancient or modern guesses, and none is provable. The ritual-origin theories of the early twentieth century — the Cambridge ritualists' derivation of tragedy from a ritual of the dying and reviving year-spirit — are presented in modern handbooks as speculation that outran its evidence, and this article does not build on them; the hero-cult hypothesis (tragedy from lament at heroes' tombs) rests on the true observation that the plots are overwhelmingly hero-myths, but plots are not origins. What can be said without speculation is narrower and sufficient: tragedy appears in the record as a choral performance for Dionysos that acquired, at a date tradition fixes around 534 BCE, an actor who spoke against the chorus — and that single acquisition, dialogue, is the seed of everything in this article.

Between Thespis and Aeschylus the record offers names almost without texts: Choerilus, competing by the 520s; Pratinas of Phlius, credited with shaping the satyr play as a separate form; and Phrynichus, the first tragedian of whom real historical notices survive. His Capture of Miletus, staged within a year or two of Miletus' fall to Persia in 494 BCE, dramatised a catastrophe so raw that — Herodotus reports, in paraphrase — the theatre burst into tears, the city fined the poet a thousand drachmas for reminding it of its own sorrows, and the play was banned. The story is the earliest evidence that tragedy could treat near-contemporary history — the precedent for Persians (Section VII) — and that the city policed the stage's emotional power when it turned on the audience itself. Phrynichus' Phoenician Women treated the Persian defeat from the enemy court's side, and Aeschylus, who is said to have acknowledged the debt, took its opening as his own Persian play's model.

VI. Aristotle's Poetics: The Earliest Theory, Read as Retrospection

Our earliest theory of tragedy is also our earliest history of it, and it is the same book: Aristotle's Poetics, composed in the fourth century BCE — most plausibly in the 330s–320s, when its author was teaching in Athens — roughly two centuries after Thespis and a full lifetime after the deaths of Sophocles and Euripides. The Poetics survives incomplete: the treatise on tragedy is substantially whole; the companion treatment of comedy is lost. What it says about tragedy's development, in its fourth chapter, is the foundation on which every modern account is built, and it must be read with its date held firmly in mind. Aristotle is not a witness. He is a researcher — the first, and for this subject the only one whose work survives — reconstructing a two-hundred-year process from production records, from the plays, and from a philosophical commitment to seeing arts as organisms that grow toward their natural form. His history is therefore evidence of two things at once: of what the records available in the fourth century showed, and of what a fourth-century teleologist made of them. The sections below use him constantly and trust him accordingly: completely on the technical sequence, cautiously on the motives, and not at all where his theory needs a fact the record cannot have given him.

The Technical Sequence

Aristotle's developmental claims, paraphrased from Poetics chapter 4, are these. Tragedy began in improvisation, from the leaders of the dithyramb; in its early phase it was satyric in character, with short plots and a dancing metre, and it acquired seriousness late, exchanging that metre for the iambic — the metre, Aristotle notes, closest to speech. Aeschylus raised the number of actors from one to two, reduced the chorus's part, and made dialogue take the leading role. Sophocles added the third actor and scene-painting. Thereafter tragedy stopped developing, in Aristotle's phrase, when it had attained its natural form. The sequence is corroborated in its broad effect by the plays themselves: the earliest extant Aeschylus is markedly more choral than the latest Euripides, and no surviving scene requires a fourth speaking voice. But the neatness should provoke caution. Attributing each step to a single named innovator is the pattern Greek tradition imposed on every art's history — compare Thespis in Section V — and scene-painting is credited elsewhere, in the tradition preserved by Vitruvius, to the painter Agatharchus working for Aeschylus, a generation before Aristotle's Sophoclean date. The conflict is recorded here, unresolved: Aristotle is the earlier and better source, and the article builds on the sequence, not on the attributions.

THE RANKING OF THE PARTS

Plot (mythos) first, character second, then thought, diction, song, and spectacle last — Aristotle's hierarchy, paraphrased from Poetics chapter 6. Its consequence for this hub is direct: for Aristotle, the story-structure is the tragedy, and the inherited myth is raw material for plot-making. The poet, he says in chapter 9, should be a maker of plots rather than of verses — and is not bound to the received stories, though the famous names anchor belief.

THE CRITICAL INSTRUMENTS

Reversal (peripeteia), recognition (anagnōrisis), and suffering are Aristotle's names for plot's moving parts, and his exhibit for their perfect conjunction is Sophocles' Oedipus the King, which the Poetics treats throughout as the genre's paradigm. That a single play should stand as the model of the form — for a theorist writing a century later — is itself a canonising act, and Section XIII shows the school tradition completing it.

Aristotle's famous definition — tragedy as the imitation of a serious, complete action, effecting through pity and fear the katharsis of such emotions — is paraphrased here, not quoted, and every term in it, katharsis above all, has been disputed for centuries. Note also what the Poetics omits: the festival entire. His tragedy floats free of the Dionysia — no choregia, no judges, no procession — and that silence is the standing warning against reading the genre through theory alone.

Two of Aristotle's judgements deserve separate statement, because later sections lean on them. In chapter 13, paraphrased, he names the finest tragic figure: a man neither wholly good nor bad, brought down not by vice but by some hamartia — a word whose rendering, as “error” rather than moral “flaw”, is one of the field's consequential translation disputes, flagged here rather than settled. And in chapter 15, paraphrased, he lays down that a plot's resolution should arise from the plot itself and not from a machine — naming Euripides' Medea as the offending exhibit, since Medea's escape is effected by the Sun's chariot rather than by anything the action has prepared. That sentence is the birthplace of deus ex machina as a critical reproach; Section IX returns to it. Aristotle's Euripides is in any case a study in a divided mind: the most criticised of the three in the Poetics, and yet named there — paraphrased here — the most tragic of the poets, because his plays end in the misery the genre exists to show.

VII. Aeschylus: History on Stage and the One Surviving Trilogy

Aeschylus (c. 525/4–456/5 BCE), of Eleusis, is the first tragedian whose work survives. The ancient catalogues credit him with some seventy to ninety plays; his first production came around 499 BCE and his first victory in 484; he fought at Marathon — his epitaph, in the tradition the biographies preserve, commemorated the battle and not the poetry — and he died at Gela in Sicily, having produced plays for the Sicilian tyrant Hieron as well as for Athens. Seven plays survive under his name. One of the seven, the Prometheus Bound, is now widely held not to be his: the case — metrical, lexical, dramaturgical — is set out in this hub's Makers and Fire, Sections IX–X, whose labelling this article adopts: transmitted under Aeschylus' name, authorship disputed, with a substantial modern case for a later production, possibly by his son Euphorion, staged under the father's name. The reader should carry that label through every generalisation about “Aeschylus” in what follows.

Persians: The Enemy's Court, Eight Years After

The Persians of 472 BCE — choregos, the young Pericles, on the production record — is the only surviving tragedy on a historical subject, and it is very near-contemporary history: Salamis was fought in 480, eight years before, and men who had rowed in it sat in the audience. The play is set at the Persian court at Susa, among the defeated: Queen Atossa, the chorus of elders, the ghost of Darius raised to judge his son's folly, Xerxes returning in rags. The messenger speech narrates Salamis entirely from the losing side, so the audience heard its own victory as the enemy's catastrophe, in the enemy's mouth. Two disciplines govern the reading. First, Phrynichus' precedent (Section V) shows the mode was dangerous: history on stage could be fined when it hurt wrong. Aeschylus' solution — the victory framed as the gods' punishment of royal overreach — let the city celebrate itself at one remove, through pity. Second, the play is not documentary: its Persia is a Greek construction, its Darius a mouthpiece for Greek political wisdom. Persians is evidence for how Athens wished to remember Salamis in 472 — a different, and in some ways richer, thing than evidence for Salamis.

The Oresteia: The One Surviving Trilogy

In 458 BCE Aeschylus presented the Oresteia — Agamemnon, Libation Bearers, Eumenides, with the satyr play Proteus, now lost — and won first prize. It is the only complete trilogy to survive from the entire genre, and its survival lets us see, once, what the connected form could do at full stretch. The story is the house of Atreus, whose full cycle is told in this hub in The Trojan Cycle: Agamemnon, home from Troy, murdered by Clytemnestra; Orestes, commanded by Apollo, returning to kill his mother; the Furies — the Erinyes, ancient powers of blood-vengeance — pursuing the matricide to Athens, where the third play moves the action out of myth's Argos into the audience's own city and founds, on stage, a court. The trilogy's architecture is its argument: an inherited curse working itself out through three generations of reciprocal killing is not answered by a fourth killing but by an institution — a homicide court, a jury of citizens, a tied vote, and the persuasion of the Furies themselves, who are not destroyed but housed, given a shrine in Athens and a new name, Eumenides, the Kindly Ones. Whether that ending is the poet's endorsement of a specific reform is the subject of Section XI; here the formal point stands on its own. Only the trilogy form could stage a problem at the scale of a curse and solve it at the scale of a city. When later poets abandoned connected trilogies, they abandoned that scale with it — and the Oresteia's uniqueness in survival means the genre's largest instrument is known to us from exactly one example.

Aristotle's notice — Aeschylus raised the actors from one to two and made dialogue the protagonist — has a concrete meaning. With two actors, a play can be built from encounters: not a hero narrating himself against a chorus, but two wills in collision, each masked figure able to exit and return as another character. The Oresteia is the surviving monument of that technology: Clytemnestra over the purple tapestries, persuading Agamemnon to walk on cloth fit for gods into the house that will kill him; Cassandra alone with the chorus, tearing the prophetic veil aside. What the third actor would add — simultaneity, the triangle, two parties contending for a third who must choose — belongs to the next section.

VIII. Sophocles: The Third Actor, and the Trilogy That Never Was

Sophocles (c. 497/6–406/5 BCE), of the deme Colonus, had the longest career of the three: first victory in 468 BCE — defeating Aeschylus, on the tradition Plutarch preserves, in a contest remembered for its upset — and still writing at his death in his nineties, his last play produced posthumously by his grandson. The ancient catalogues credit him with some hundred and twenty plays and with eighteen to twenty-four victories, never placing lower than second on the biographical tradition's account; he was also a public man in a way neither of the others was — a general twice, a state financial officer, a member of the board that governed Athens after the Sicilian disaster. Seven of his plays survive. Aristotle credits him with two technical innovations, and this article has already flagged the second of them: the third actor, and scene-painting (Poetics chapter 4, paraphrased in Section VI, with the competing Agatharchus tradition for scene-painting recorded there). The ancient biographical tradition adds a third change, an increase of the tragic chorus from twelve to fifteen; it is reported here as that tradition reports it.

The third actor's consequences are legible in the texts. Three speaking figures on stage at once makes possible the scene the whole later drama inherits: not dialogue but triangulation — two parties arguing over, deceiving, or contending for a third. Sophocles' surviving masterpiece of the form is the recognition-structure of Oedipus the King: the Corinthian messenger, come to free Oedipus from his fear of the oracle, instead supplies the fact that destroys him, while the Theban herdsman — the one man who knows the whole truth — is dragged on stage to face both king and messenger together. The scene needs three voices in one place at one time, and it is unbuildable in Aeschylus' two-actor theatre. Equally characteristic is what Sophocles did to the trilogy. The ancient tradition — the Suda's notice is the standard citation — reports that he was the first to compete with single plays against single plays rather than with connected tetralogies: that is, he abandoned the connected trilogy as his normal form, submitting three tragedies on unrelated stories. The report is late and is stated as tradition; but it matches the evidence of the surviving plays, none of which connects with any other, and it sets up the caution this section exists to deliver.

// THE “THEBAN PLAYS” ARE NOT A TRILOGY //

Three of Sophocles' seven surviving plays treat the house of Laius — Antigone, Oedipus the King, Oedipus at Colonus — and they are marketed, taught, and bound in modern editions as “the three Theban plays”, a phrase that invites the reader to treat them as a trilogy. They are not one, and the dates prove it. Antigone was produced around 442/1 BCE. Oedipus the King followed some fifteen years later — its date is not recorded and is inferred, commonly to the later 420s, partly from the plague that opens the play and Athens' own plague of 430; the inference is standard but should be held as inference. Oedipus at Colonus, written at the very end of the poet's life, was produced posthumously in 401 BCE. Three plays, three separate competitions, some forty years apart, written in a different order from the order of the story they tell, with inconsistencies between them that no trilogy would tolerate — Antigone's Creon and Colonus's Creon are not one continuous conception, and the burial that is Antigone's whole subject is settled differently in the later play. Each was composed to stand alone, on one day, against two other poets' unrelated work. Read them as three separate visits, across a lifetime, to the same accursed house — which is the more interesting fact anyway.

The full Theban story — Laius, the sphinx, the crossroads, the house's extinction — is told as a cycle in The Theban Cycle; the point here is generic. Sophocles' relation to the myth is the clearest exhibit of tragedy's normal working method. He did not inherit a fixed Oedipus story and versify it. He inherited a field of variants — the epic Oedipodea had told the story with details Sophocles discards wholesale, including a second marriage and children by another mother — and on each visit he selected, suppressed, and invented for the argument of a single day's play. The Oedipus the King's relentless investigation-structure, in which the detective is the criminal, has no known precedent in the earlier tradition and is treated by scholarship as, in its Sophoclean form, the poet's construction. Aristotle then took that construction as the paradigm of tragedy itself (Section VI) — and the paradigm's authority flowed back over the myth, until “Oedipus” simply means Sophocles' version, and the epic variants survive only as scholia and summaries. Section XI generalises the mechanism; Sophocles is its most consequential single case.

The remaining survivors fill out the set: Ajax, the ruined hero whose burial his enemies debate; Women of Trachis, Heracles' agonising end — told in Heracles' Labours — by way of his wife's fatal remedy; and Electra (c. 420–410 BCE, debated), the matricide of 458 restaged with the moral weather reversed. Note what the seven are: the school tradition's selection (Section XIII), not a cross-section of the hundred and twenty — and three of the seven treat one mythic house. That disproportion is the selection's fingerprint, not the poet's output.

IX. Euripides I: The Agon and the God from the Machine

Euripides (c. 480–406 BCE) first produced in 455 BCE, a year after Aeschylus' death, and competed for half a century; he won first prize only four or five times in his life — the sources' figures differ slightly — with a fifth, posthumous victory for the tetralogy that included the Bacchae and Iphigenia at Aulis, produced after his death in Macedon by his son. He died at the court of King Archelaus, and Athens, on the biographical tradition's story, mourned him publicly. By the ancient catalogues he wrote some ninety plays. Eighteen tragedies survive under his name — including the disputed Rhesus — plus the satyr play Cyclops: more than Aeschylus and Sophocles together. That disproportion is not a verdict of the fifth century, which ranked him third of the three in victories. It is a product of his later popularity and of a manuscript accident, and Section XIII gives the mechanism. The fifth-century evidence points the other way: Euripides was the controversialist of the three, the poet the comic stage could not leave alone, and the one whose methods the earliest theory felt obliged to criticise by name.

The Agon: Myth as Courtroom

The formal signature of Euripidean tragedy is the agōn — the set debate, borrowed from the lawcourts and the assembly, in which two characters deliver matched opposing speeches on the play's central question and a third figure, or the chorus, sits in judgement. Euripides did not invent the debate scene — Aeschylus' Eumenides is built around a trial, and Sophocles' confrontations are arguments — but he made it the load-bearing structure of whole plays, and he sharpened it with the new rhetoric of the sophists, whose teaching was remaking Athenian public speech in his lifetime. In the Trojan Women (415 BCE), Helen and Hecuba plead for and against Helen's life before Menelaus, in speeches that cite evidence, attack witnesses, and argue probability like advocates — the myth of Troy retried as litigation, in the year after Athens had destroyed Melos and in the year it launched the Sicilian expedition; the play's relation to those events is debated and is labelled here as the standing scholarly argument it is, not as a key that unlocks the play. In Hippolytus (428 BCE, first prize), the agon between Phaedra's nurse and Hippolytus detonates the plot. In the lost plays the pattern was the same: the fragments preserve a poet whose heroes argue like defendants and whose gods are cross-examined. The consequence for myth is the one this article tracks throughout. A story submitted to an agon is no longer a story. It is a case, with two sides, and the audience is empanelled to judge it. Euripides' theatre is where Greek myth learned to plead.

The Deus ex Machina

At the end of many Euripidean plays, the crane described in Section III swings a god out over the stage building: the deus ex machina, in the Latin phrase the critical tradition settled on — in Greek terms, the god from the mēchanē. Apollo, Athena, Artemis, the Dioscuri, Thetis appear in play after play to stop the action, announce the future, found a cult, order a burial, allot marriages and deaths, and close the myth. Aristotle's reproach, paraphrased in Section VI, was that resolution should grow from the plot and not be lowered onto it, and he named the Medea's serpent-chariot escape as the offence. Two correctives keep the record honest. First, the device is older than Euripides — Aeschylus and Sophocles both end plays with divine settlement, Athena's whole role in the Eumenides being the grand example — and Euripides' distinction is frequency and placement, not invention. Second, the Euripidean god is rarely a tidy resolution. These gods arrive too late, speak with an indifference the human survivors must then live under, and frequently expose — as Artemis does at the end of Hippolytus, abandoning her dying favourite because gods may not weep where another god has willed a death — the moral cost of the divine order they enforce. Whether the machine-gods are Euripides' piety, his irony, or his structural solution to the problem of ending a myth whose traditional sequel he has dismantled is a standing dispute in the scholarship, with named positions on all sides; the article records the dispute and notes only what the texts compel: that in Euripides the god on the crane is as often an accusation as an answer.

// THE PROLOGUE, AND THE WOMEN //

Two further signatures, briefly. The expository prologue — a god or lone figure telling the audience plainly where the story stands — sacrifices suspense so attention can go to how, not what. And his women — Medea, Phaedra, Hecuba, Andromache, Electra, Helen — carry an intellectual centrality the comic poets found scandalous; the biographical slanders comedy built from them are fiction, and are treated by scholarship as such.

The Bacchae, produced posthumously in 405 BCE, closes the career with the god of the festival himself: Dionysos on stage as a character — disguised, smiling, and utterly destructive — punishing Thebes for denying his divinity, in the god's own myth of resistance and recognition, told from the cult side in this hub's Mysteries and Descents. A king is torn apart by his mother on the mountain; the god surveys the ruin and is not merciful. Whether the dying poet recanted his rationalism, wrote his coldest exposure of religious violence, or neither, is unanswerable from the evidence and is stated here as open.

X. Euripides II: The Versions That Displaced Older Ones

Euripides' deepest effect on myth was not formal but editorial: again and again, the version of a story he staged drove the older versions out of circulation, until his invention — where it was invention — became indistinguishable from tradition itself. Two cases, the two hardest in the whole hub, exhibit the mechanism and its evidential traps. Both are presented here as the open questions they are.

Medea's Children: The Neophron Problem

The Medea of 431 BCE — placed third, last, in its competition; the tetralogy's victor was Euphorion, Aeschylus' son, with Sophocles second — ends with the act for which Medea is now known above all else: having destroyed Jason's new bride and her father Creon with poisoned gifts, Medea deliberately kills her own two sons, to annihilate Jason's house and his future, and escapes to Athens in the chariot of her grandfather the Sun. The full Corinthian story, Medea's place in the Argonautic cycle, and Jason's end are told in The Argonauts. The question for this article is narrower and prior: did Euripides invent the deliberate infanticide?

The older tradition, so far as it can be recovered, says the children died otherwise. In the Corinthian local tradition — preserved in scholia and in the travel-writer Pausanias, who saw the children's monument at Corinth and collected the versions — the Corinthians themselves killed Medea's children: in one form, fourteen children, seven boys and seven girls, stoned at the altar of Hera Akraia where they had taken refuge; in another, the children died by the vengeance of Creon's kin, and Corinth expiated the pollution with an annual rite — seven boys and seven girls of noble families serving a year in Hera's sanctuary — that Pausanias reports as lasting into Roman times. A scholion preserves the uglier anecdote that the Corinthians bribed Euripides with five talents to transfer the killing to Medea and so clear the city: the story is unverifiable, is treated by scholarship as slander or legend, and is valuable chiefly as evidence that antiquity itself believed the transfer had happened and needed explaining. On this body of evidence, the standard modern position — stated classically by Denys Page in his 1938 edition of the play, and widely followed since — is that the deliberate infanticide is Euripides' invention: no earlier source makes Medea her children's killer, and the invention explains, at a stroke, both the play's last-place finish before an audience meeting the horror for the first time and the total displacement of the Corinthian version thereafter. After 431 BCE, Medea the child-killer is simply Medea, in Seneca, in Ovid, in every later retelling — including, the reader should note, this hub's own.

// THE NEOPHRON PROBLEM: AN OPEN QUESTION //

Against the invention thesis stands one ancient notice. An anonymous hypothesis — a scholarly preface transmitted with the play — reports, on the authority it attributes to Dicaearchus and to Aristotle's Hypomnēmata, that Euripides took the play, with rearrangement, from the Medea of Neophron of Sikyon, an earlier tragedian whose work survives in three short fragments. If Neophron's Medea preceded Euripides' and already contained the infanticide, then Euripides inherited the horror and the invention thesis collapses. The positions, named: Page and the majority tradition hold that the fragments show Neophron imitating Euripides — that Neophron is the later writer, his date (the tradition makes him implausibly long-lived and prolific) unreliable, and the hypothesis's borrowing-notice a piece of ancient plagiarism gossip of a kind the hypotheses trade in. A minority of scholars, examining the same fragments, have held that nothing in them compels a date after 431 and that the notice may preserve a real priority. The evidence is three fragments and a preface; it will not bear a verdict. This article therefore presents the matter as it stands in the scholarship: the deliberate infanticide is probably Euripides' invention; the Neophron notice prevents certainty; and the displacement of the Corinthian version, by whichever poet first staged the killing, is complete and is a fact.

Helen in Egypt: The Eidolon and the Palinode

The second case runs in the opposite direction: an older poet's revision, which Euripides inherited and made canonical. The received epic story — told in this hub in The Trojan Cycle — takes Helen to Troy with Paris, and ten years of war are fought for her. But a counter-tradition, as old as the sixth century, held that Helen never went to Troy at all. Its author was the lyric poet Stesichorus of Himera. The story of his palinode — his “song sung back”, a recantation — is told by Plato in the Phaedrus, paraphrased here: Stesichorus, struck blind for his slander of Helen in an earlier poem, understood the cause as Homer, in the legend, had not, and composed a recantation declaring the story false — Helen did not sail, did not reach Troy — and recovered his sight. The palinode's key lines survive because Plato quotes their sense and a papyrus commentary preserves wording; in keeping with this article's rule they are rendered here as paraphrase: that tale is not true; you did not go in the well-benched ships; you did not come to the citadel of Troy. What went to Troy, in the developed version, was an eidōlon — a phantom, an image of Helen fashioned from air — while Helen herself waited out the war, chaste and furious, in Egypt, under the protection of King Proteus. Herodotus, in Book 2 of the Histories, retails the Egyptian priests' version at length — Paris blown to Egypt, Helen detained there, the Greeks refusing to believe the Trojans' truthful protests — and endorses its logic, in paraphrase: had Helen been in Troy, Priam would have given her back.

Euripides staged the counter-tradition twice. In the Trojan Women (415 BCE) the phantom is absent and Helen pleads her case in person, in the agon of Section IX. In the Helen of 412 BCE he built a whole play on the palinode's version: Helen in Egypt, the war fought for a cloud, Menelaus shipwrecked with the phantom — which dissolves as the real wife is recognised — and the couple escaping together by a trick on the Egyptian king. The result's genre has been argued ever since: tragedy by its competition slot, it moves like romance and ends in reunion; the label is the scholarship's problem, the ancient slot a fact. What matters here is the transmission: the eidolon-Helen was available from Stesichorus and Herodotus, but it was Euripides' play that made the phantom a permanent second Helen in the tradition. Innovation, once staged by a canonical poet, does not announce itself. It puts on the mask of the older truth it claims to restore — the palinode's whole point — and the mask, in time, is taken for the face.

XI. What Tragedy Did to Myth: Innovation Wearing the Mask of Tradition

The cases of Sections VIII and X — Sophocles' Oedipus, the Medea question, the phantom Helen — are instances of a general mechanism, and the mechanism is this article's central finding about the genre. Myth before tragedy was multiform: a stock of stories existing in local, familial, epic, and lyric variants, no one of which was the version, all of them available to any singer for the occasion in hand — the condition this hub documented for epic in The Homeric Epics. Tragedy changed the ecology. A tragedy is a single, fixed, embodied version: one poet's selection from the variants, cast, costumed, and performed before the whole city on a day when the city was assembled precisely to receive it. Performance on that scale is a canonising act. The version staged acquires the authority of the occasion; the versions not staged lose their venue. Repeat the process for a century, with the poets reading each other, correcting each other, competing for the same myths — the three Electras, the successive Orestes and Medeas — and the multiform stock narrows, generation by generation, into something that looks, to later ages, like a single body of “Greek myth”. It is, in large part, the repertory of the Athenian stage, mistaken for the tradition entire.

The poets' own practice made the narrowing invisible. Tragedy almost never announces its innovations as innovations. The prologue presents the story as given; the chorus treats the myth as the audience's shared possession; a poet who has reversed a story's moral, invented its central act, or imported a variant from a local cult or a lyric predecessor delivers the result in the same hieratic register as the oldest material in the play. Innovation wears the mask of tradition because the genre's authority depends on the mask: these are the old stories, the city's inheritance, and the audience must feel itself remembering, not learning. Aristotle saw the mechanism plainly and approved it — the poet of Poetics chapter 9, paraphrased in Section VI, keeps the famous names and the broad outline, because the audience's belief attaches to them, and invents freely within the frame. The discipline this imposes on a modern reader of myth is the one this hub applies throughout: for any tragic version, ask whose it is, when it was staged, and what it displaced. A myth with a tragedian's name and a production date is a datable artefact. A myth without them may be older than any text — or may be a fifth-century invention that has worn the mask successfully for two and a half millennia.

Myth as Civic Argument: The Oresteia and the Areopagus

The genre's second great effect follows from its venue. A myth staged before the assembled democracy, in a festival the democracy ran, became available — irresistibly — as an instrument of civic argument: a way of debating the city's present arrangements in the displaced, deniable, and therefore speakable form of the heroic past. The standing example is the Oresteia of 458 BCE and the court it founds. In the Eumenides, Athena establishes a tribunal on the Areopagus — the ancient Athenian council, sitting on the hill of Ares — to try Orestes for homicide, empanels citizen jurors, casts the deciding vote for acquittal herself, and charters the court, in a speech paraphrased here, to hold homicide jurisdiction in Athens forever, a bulwark of the city, neither anarchic nor despotic, keeping a salutary fear awake in the citizens' hearts. Four years before the production, in 462/1 BCE, the reformer Ephialtes had stripped the historical Areopagus of its broad political powers, leaving it — precisely — its homicide jurisdiction, in a democratic reform so divisive that Ephialtes was murdered for it. The coincidence of scope is exact, and no serious reading of the play ignores it.

// THE AREOPAGUS QUESTION: POSITIONS //

What Aeschylus' aetiology means politically is a named dispute. One line of reading — associated with the play's great modern editors and with much mid-twentieth-century scholarship — takes the charter as endorsement: the poet gives the reformed court a divine foundation-myth, reconciling the city to Ephialtes' settlement by lodging the new, narrowed jurisdiction in the oldest sanctity. An opposing line reads the emphasis on fear, reverence, and the court's ancient dignity as a conservative counterweight — a warning to the democracy not to rejoice too loudly over the old council's fall. A third position cautions that a trilogy conceived as a whole, whose third play must end a curse older than Athens, needed a court for reasons internal to the myth, and that the reform supplies resonance rather than programme. The article records the dispute, notes that the chronological and jurisdictional facts are not in dispute, and adopts the minimal position the evidence compels: the audience of 458 could not have watched Athena charter the Areopagus for homicide trials without thinking of 462/1 — and a poet of Aeschylus' experience knew it.

Antigone and the Unwritten Laws

The second standing example is a single speech. In Sophocles' Antigone (c. 442/1 BCE), the heroine, arrested for burying her brother Polynices against Creon's edict, answers the king — in the passage paraphrased here from Antigone lines 450–460 — that the edict was not Zeus's proclamation nor Justice's; that no mortal's proclamation can override the gods' unwritten and unfailing laws; that those laws live not from today or yesterday but forever, and no one knows when they first appeared; and that she would not, for fear of any man's resolve, break them and pay the gods' penalty. The speech became, in the fourth century, a common property of Athenian argument: orators quote the play in court, Aristotle cites the passage in the Rhetoric as the type of an appeal to universal law, and from that classical afterlife the lines descend to their modern career as the charter-text of conscience against positive law, of natural law against the state's command. Two disciplines apply, and this hub's treatment of the play elsewhere observes both. First, the speech is a character's, in a play that gives Creon arguments too and destroys both houses impartially; extracting it as Sophocles' political testament flattens a tragedy into a pamphlet. Second — and this is the section's point — the flattening began in antiquity, and began because tragedy had made the speech available to the city as an instrument. The orator quoting Antigone before a jury is using the stage exactly as the stage taught the city to use it: myth as a shared stock of precedents, citable like laws. Between the Eumenides' charter-myth for a court and Antigone's unwritten laws quoted in one, the genre's civic function stands fully documented: tragedy did not reflect the democracy's arguments. It supplied their language, their precedents, and their authority — the authority of the old stories, which the genre itself had written.

One quieter effect completes the account: tragedy fixed myth's geography in Athenian terms. Orestes is tried at Athens, Oedipus dies under Athens' protection at Colonus, Heracles' children are sheltered by Athens, Medea is promised refuge by Athens' king. In their tragic forms the myths are Athenocentric to a degree their epic and local forms were not — the final fingerprint of the genre, and the reason a reader must ask of a tragic myth not only whose version it is, but where it was staged, and for whom.

XII. Comedy as Witness: Aristophanes' Frogs

The best ancient evidence for what tragedy meant to its own city comes, by a happy paradox, from comedy. The comic poets competed in the same festivals, before the same audiences, and they treated the tragedians as public property: quoting them, parodying their lines within days of performance, mocking their persons, their metres, their prologues, their staging tricks. A parody is a measurement. It works only if the audience knows the original intimately and recently, and the density of tragic parody in surviving comedy is therefore primary evidence — better than any treatise — for how deeply the tragic repertory saturated the citizen audience's memory. Aristophanes (c. 446–c. 386 BCE) is the witness who matters, because one of his plays is, entire, a judgement on tragedy: the Frogs, produced at the Lenaia of 405 BCE — the winter festival of Section IV — and awarded first prize.

The play's premise is the genre's crisis, stated as farce. Euripides has died in Macedon; Sophocles has died within the year; the stage, in the play's judgement, is left to mediocrities. Dionysos — the god of the theatre himself, costumed as Heracles and terrified of everything — descends to the underworld to fetch Euripides back, in a katabasis that belongs, as plot, with the descents catalogued in Descents. In Hades the play's second half stages a contest for the throne of tragedy among the dead: Aeschylus against Euripides, before Dionysos as judge. The two attack each other's art line by line — prologues parsed and parodied, lyrics weighed, in the most famous scene, literally: verses are placed in a great scale's pans, and Aeschylus' lines, freighted with chariots, corpses, and rivers, sink Euripides' airy phrases every time. They attack each other's effects on the city: Aeschylus' heroes, in his defence as Aristophanes writes it — paraphrased here — taught the citizens martial spirit and nobility; Euripides' clever, talkative characters taught them to chatter and shirk. When Dionysos finally demands the political advice Athens needs in 405 — the war collapsing, the city months from surrender — he chooses, on that ground, Aeschylus, and takes him back up.

WHAT THE FROGS PROVES

That by 405 BCE the tragic canon was already a civic institution with a history and a crisis: two generations of repertory, held in common memory in quotable detail, ranked in a recognised order, and argued about as a matter of public safety. Comedy could not have built a prize-winning play on the comparison otherwise.

WHAT IT DISTORTS

Everything it touches, on purpose. Aristophanes' Euripides is a caricature assembled from the plays and the gossip; his Aeschylus is a monument of the poet's own construction, all weight and thunder. The Frogs is evidence for reputations and for the terms of the argument, not for the qualities it assigns — the distinction this article applies to comedy throughout.

THE UNUSUAL HONOUR

The Frogs was granted a distinction almost unique in the record: a second production, on the ancient scholarly notice's report, because of its parabasis — the chorus's direct advice to the city to restore the disfranchised and reconcile its factions. The honour makes the play a civic act as well as a literary judgement.

The just-dead Sophocles, by contrast, is treated with a gentleness granted neither contestant: in the plot's report he kissed Aeschylus on arriving in Hades and declined to contest the throne. Even comedy's malice keeps the ranking — the three of the Frogs are the three of the canon, already, in 405.

The Frogs also fixes, by accident, the date at which this article's story turns. Within months of its production Athens lost the war; within a generation the city was licensing official state texts of the three tragedians — the Lycurgan recension of the 330s BCE, on the tradition preserved in the pseudo-Plutarchan Lives of the Ten Orators, which records that Lycurgus had the plays of Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides copied into the public archive, with actors forbidden to depart from the official text in performance. The notice is late and its details have been debated; its direction is not in doubt. The repertory had become a heritage, to be protected from the actors who performed it. Tragedy's living century was over; its textual millennium — the one that decides what we can read — had begun. Section XIII follows the text from the archive to the schoolroom to the manuscript shelf.

XIII. Survival by Selection: Thirty-Two Plays and a Manuscript Accident

State the arithmetic plainly. Aeschylus wrote, on the ancient catalogues, some seventy to ninety plays; seven survive complete, one of disputed authorship. Sophocles wrote some hundred and twenty; seven survive. Euripides wrote some ninety; eighteen tragedies survive under his name, including the disputed Rhesus, plus Cyclops, the one complete satyr play. The tragedies total thirty-two — roughly a tenth of the three poets' combined output, and a far smaller fraction of the genre's: every other fifth-century tragedian, including poets the ancients ranked high, survives only in fragments. The question is not why so much was lost; in a manuscript culture, loss is the default. It is why these survived — and the answer is not, in the first instance, that they were the best. They were selected, twice over, by institutions with purposes of their own — and then, in Euripides' case, a single manuscript volume escaped the general ruin by chance.

The First Selection: Alexandria and the Schools

The first filter was scholarly. At Alexandria, in the third and second centuries BCE, the Library's scholars — Aristophanes of Byzantium prominent among them, to whom the tradition credits editions and the introductory hypotheses still prefixed to the plays — edited the tragedians whole, and the complete works appear to have circulated through the Hellenistic age. The narrowing came with the schools. In the Roman imperial period — the selection is usually dated to around the second century CE, and is stated here at that strength — a curriculum selection was made for teaching: seven plays of Aeschylus, seven of Sophocles, and ten of Euripides, chosen, so far as the principles can be recovered, for their suitability as school texts. These were copied with their scholia, generation after generation; the unselected plays, for Aeschylus and Sophocles, effectively ceased to be copied and perished. Within the selection, a further narrowing produced the Byzantine triads — the three most-taught plays of each poet, which attracted the fullest commentary and the most copies: for Sophocles, Ajax, Electra, Oedipus the King; for Euripides, Hecuba, Orestes, Phoenician Women — three plays whose medieval manuscript swarms dwarf the rest, and whose prominence in the modern repertory still reflects a Byzantine schoolmaster's syllabus more than any fifth-century verdict. That all three poets' Electra plays stand inside the selections has often been remarked: whether the selectors wanted the comparison possible, as has been suggested, or whether the myth's schoolroom usefulness drove the choice, the effect is the same happy accident for us — one myth, three treatments, the genre's whole development legible in a single dossier.

The Alphabetic Plays: Euripides' Second Survival

Euripides' extra plays survived by a wholly different route, and the route is the purest manuscript accident in classical literature. Alongside the ten school plays, nine further Euripidean works — eight tragedies and the satyr play Cyclops — descend to us through a single line of transmission, traceable to one manuscript tradition (its chief witness the fourteenth-century Laurentian codex, Plut. 32.2, and its apograph). These are the so-called “alphabetic” plays: their Greek titles fall, in sequence, under the letters epsilon, eta, iota, and kappa — Helen, Electra, Heracles, Heraclidae, Ion, the two Iphigenias, Cyclops among them — which is to say, they are one stretch, one volume's worth, of a complete edition of Euripides arranged alphabetically by title. The rest of that edition — the volumes for the earlier and later letters — perished entire. One volume survived, by a chance no source records, through the sackings and library-burnings of Byzantium's last centuries, and because it survived we have the Helen of Section X, the Ion, the Iphigenia among the Taurians, and the Cyclops — plays that no school selected, that carry almost no scholia, and that several modern judges rank among their author's finest. The control case is worth stating coldly: had the surviving volume been the one covering, say, mu to pi, we would possess a different nine plays, lack most of the nine we have, and write a different Euripides. The canon of Greek tragedy, as a modern reader inherits it, is the product of a syllabus and a bookshelf accident, in roughly equal parts.

Two honest corollaries close the account. First, fragments matter: hundreds of lost plays survive in quotations — in Athenaeus, in Stobaeus' anthology, in lexica and scholia, and increasingly in Egyptian papyri, which have returned substantial stretches of lost Euripides and of Sophocles' satyr play the Trackers. The lost bulk is a ruin-field, not a blank. Second, the selection's bias should be faced: the schools chose teachable plays, the alphabet chose at random, and between them they preserved a corpus skewed toward a few great houses, quotable speeches, and arguable morals — and away from connected trilogies, satyr plays, and the genre's whole fourth-century continuation. Every generalisation about “Greek tragedy”, including this article's, is a generalisation about that skewed corpus, and the honest ones say so.

XIV. What the Modern Reader Holds: The Editions

A reader who takes down a Greek tragedy today holds the end of the chain Sections XII and XIII described: a text constituted by an editor from the medieval manuscripts — for the school plays, a tradition of many codices with scholia; for the alphabetic plays, effectively a single codex and its copy — corrected against the fragments, the ancient quotations, and two thousand years of scholarship, and printed with an apparatus recording the choices. Two series dominate the working library, and this article's paraphrases are made from them.
