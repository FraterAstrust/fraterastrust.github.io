I. The Oldest Monster-Slaying Cycle

Perseus is the hero of what is, on the surviving evidence, the oldest fully formed monster-slaying cycle in Greek myth. Heracles, treated in this hub’s Heroes and Epic article, would eventually outgrow him in fame and in the sheer number of his deeds; Theseus would be built up, late and deliberately, as Athens’s answer to both. But the Perseus story was already old, complete, and Panhellenic when our earliest texts were composed. Hesiod’s Theogony (c. 700 BCE) presupposes the whole beheading in a handful of genealogical lines — the Gorgons’ parentage, Medusa’s mortality, her union with Poseidon, and the two beings who spring from her severed neck — without bothering to narrate it, because no listener needed it narrated. The pseudo-Hesiodic Shield of Heracles (early sixth century BCE) can use Perseus in mid-flight, head in bag, Gorgons in pursuit, as a piece of decoration on another hero’s shield: an image inside an image, chosen because every viewer would know it at a glance. A story does not get used as wallpaper until it has been famous for a long time.

The cycle’s shape is worth stating before its parts, because the parts were assembled over centuries and do not all come from the same stratum. A king of Argos, warned that his daughter’s son will kill him, locks the daughter away; a god reaches her anyway, as a shower of gold; mother and infant are cast into the sea in a chest and wash ashore on an island, where a fisherman raises the boy. Grown, the boy is trapped by the island’s king — who wants the mother — into promising an impossible gift: the head of the Gorgon Medusa, whose face turns onlookers to stone. Aided by Hermes and Athena, the hero extorts directions from three ancient sisters who share a single eye and a single tooth, collects a kit of magical equipment from nymphs, flies to the edge of the world, and beheads the one mortal Gorgon while looking only at her reflection. From the wound spring a winged horse and a golden-sworded giant. On the way home the hero petrifies a Titan, rescues a princess chained to a rock for a sea-monster, fights off her former betrothed, returns to petrify the king who sent him, and finally — seeking his grandfather in friendship — kills him by accident with a discus, fulfilling the oracle he never tried to fulfil. He then exchanges kingdoms rather than inherit a throne stained by kin-killing, founds or fortifies Mycenae, and becomes the ancestor of the Perseid dynasty, of the kings of Persia in one Greek telling, and, through his great-grandson’s line, of Heracles himself.

Three features distinguish this cycle from the other great heroic careers, and this article returns to each. First, it is a story about equipment. Perseus performs almost no feat by unaided strength. He wins by information (extorted from the Graeae), by borrowed tools (sandals, wallet, cap, sickle, shield), and by a technique — indirect vision — that is itself a kind of tool. The tale is, in a sense the Greeks themselves seem to have felt, a story about how a mortal handles powers too dangerous to meet directly. Second, its central object outlives its killing. Medusa’s head does not stop working when Medusa dies. It petrifies Atlas, Phineus, Polydectes, and a hall full of men; it ends its career fixed at the centre of Athena’s aegis; and its face, the gorgoneion, becomes the most widely reproduced protective image in Greek art — the monster’s face as a device for warding off monsters. Third, the cycle is a charter. Perseus is Argos’s hero and Mycenae’s founder; his descendants rule the Argolid in the mythic king-lists; Herodotus reports his temple and his two-cubit sandal in Egypt and retails the claim that the Persians take their very name from his son. The story explains who rules, where cities came from, and how Greeks, Egyptians, and Persians are related — which is to say, it does the political and genealogical work that Greek myth existed, in large part, to do.

THE HERO AT A GLANCE

Perseus, son of Zeus and Danaë, daughter of Acrisius, king of Argos. Raised on Seriphos by the fisherman Dictys. Slayer of Medusa; rescuer and husband of Andromeda; founder, in the tradition, of Mycenae; ancestor of the Perseids and, in the line of his son Alcaeus, great-grandfather of Heracles.

A note on method, because this hero needs it more than most. The Perseus story survives in no single ancient telling that is both early and complete. The fullest continuous account, Apollodorus’s Library 2.4.1–5, is a prose handbook of the first or second century CE — some eight centuries after Hesiod — which itself draws heavily on the fifth-century BCE mythographer Pherecydes of Athens, whose account survives only in fragments and in the scholia that preserve it. Between those poles stand lyric allusions (Pindar, Simonides, Bacchylides), a lost tragedy (Aeschylus’s Phorcides), a Roman epic retelling that rearranges and embroiders freely (Ovid, Metamorphoses 4–5), historians with their own agendas (Herodotus), a periegete cataloguing cult places (Pausanias), and an enormous body of images — vases, pediments, coins, shield devices — that sometimes preserve episodes the texts have lost and sometimes contradict the texts we have. This article therefore keeps three layers visibly apart throughout, as this hub’s method requires: (a) the myth as told, source by source, with the telling dated; (b) the cultic and historical footing — what was actually worshipped, built, or claimed, and on what evidence; and (c) the later readings, ancient and modern, that have been laid over the tale. Where a famous detail belongs to a late layer — Medusa’s rape and transformation in Ovid, Bellerophon’s fall from Pegasus in Euripides — the article says so at the point where the detail appears, not in a footnote afterwards.

II. The Sources and Their Layers: How the Tale Survives

The epic stratum (c. 700–550 BCE). Homer barely touches Perseus, and the near-silence is itself evidence: the Iliad knows the Gorgon as a terror fixed on Athena’s aegis and on Agamemnon’s shield, and knows Perseus himself only as an ancestor in genealogies, while the Odyssey fears the Gorgon’s head in the underworld without naming Medusa at all. Homer’s one full heroic inset of this cycle’s world is not Perseus but Bellerophon, told by Glaucus in Iliad 6 — and treated in Section XI of this article, because the tradition itself attached Bellerophon’s career to Perseus’s as its sequel. Hesiod’s Theogony (c. 700 BCE) gives the Gorgons their genealogy and Medusa her death in eleven lines (270–294, treated in Sections VIII and X). The pseudo-Hesiodic Shield of Heracles, a poem of the Hesiodic tradition now dated to the first half of the sixth century BCE — it is not by Hesiod, and was already suspected in antiquity — gives the earliest extended picture of the hero, in its description of the shield Heracles carries into battle (lines 216–237, quoted in Section VII). The lost Hesiodic Astronomy (Astronomia), a star-poem attributed to Hesiod in antiquity and surviving only in fragments and citations — among them citations preserved in the Catasterismi tradition discussed in Section XVII — belongs to the same Hesiodic penumbra: it matters to this article not for any Perseus narrative, of which no fragment survives, but as the earliest named Greek attempt to organise the sky the Perseus family would later fill, and as a warning about how much of the Hesiodic corpus is a name attached to scraps.

The lyric and tragic stratum (sixth–fifth centuries BCE). The lyric poets use Perseus the way lyric uses myth: as a paradigm, compressed to the episode the occasion needs. Simonides of Ceos (c. 556–468 BCE) composed a lyric treatment of Danaë in the chest at sea — the fragment, preserved by Dionysius of Halicarnassus, in which Danaë laments over the sleeping infant, is among the most famous scraps of Greek lyric; it is paraphrased, not quoted, in Section IV, since this article did not consult a complete published translation of it. Pindar gives the cycle two of its sharpest early attestations. In Pythian 10 (498 BCE, for Hippocleas of Thessaly) Perseus feasts among the Hyperboreans, the blessed people beyond the north wind, in a passage quoted in Section II’s table below and discussed in Section VI. In Pythian 12 (490 BCE, for Midas of Acragas, victor in the flute contest) Pindar tells how Athena invented the flute’s “many-headed tune” by imitating the Gorgons’ lament over their beheaded sister — the aetiology that ties Medusa’s death to a living Greek musical practice (Section VIII). Aeschylus staged the Graeae episode in his lost Phorcides — the title is the other name of the Graeae, the Phorcides, daughters of Phorcys — of which only fragments and testimonia survive; what can responsibly be said about it is said in Section VI, and it is handled there strictly as fragments, not as a play we can summarise. Aeschylus’s surviving Prometheus Bound (authorship and date both debated in modern scholarship) gives, in Io’s itinerary, a set-piece description of the Graeae and Gorgons in the far east — three swan-shaped ancient maids with one eye and one tooth between them, and near them their winged, snake-haired sisters, whom no mortal may look on and live (paraphrased from Prometheus Bound 791–800). Sophocles and Euripides both wrote plays on the Andromeda episode — Euripides’ Andromeda (412 BCE) was famous enough for Aristophanes to parody it the next year — and both plays are lost except for fragments.

The mythographic stratum. The fifth-century BCE logographer Pherecydes of Athens gave the Perseus story its first surviving systematic prose form (fragments 10–12, preserved chiefly in the scholia to Apollonius Rhodius), and modern scholarship — the case is set out in Ulrike Kenens’s study of the mythographical tradition from Pherecydes to Tzetzes — treats Apollodorus’s account as a skilled adaptation of Pherecydes, modernised, abridged, and contaminated with other sources. That lineage matters: the connected narrative modern readers meet in handbooks is not a late invention but a classical prose telling, twice epitomised. Apollodorus — properly Pseudo-Apollodorus, since the Library is not by the Athenian scholar of that name — wrote in the first or second century CE; his Library 2.4.1–5, read for this article in J. G. Frazer’s Loeb translation (1921), is the backbone of Sections III–XV, and is cited as Apollodorus throughout, with the dating caveat stated here once and holding everywhere.

The Roman stratum. Ovid’s Metamorphoses (c. 8 CE) retells Perseus across the end of Book 4 and the opening of Book 5 — out of chronological order, beginning with Atlas and Andromeda and giving the Medusa expedition as a flashback told by Perseus himself at his own wedding feast. Ovid’s version is the source, direct or indirect, of most of what the later world “knows” about Perseus: the Atlas metamorphosis as a set piece, the coral aetiology, the Libyan snakes born from dripping blood, the wedding battle expanded into a small epic of slaughter, and above all the story of Medusa herself as a beautiful maiden raped by Neptune in Minerva’s temple and punished by the goddess with serpent hair. None of that last story is in Hesiod, Pindar, Pherecydes, or Apollodorus. This article follows Ovid closely where he is the source and flags him every time; Sections XII and XVIII keep his layer distinct. Hyginus’s Latin handbooks (Fabulae and De astronomia, second century CE) preserve further variants and the fullest surviving star-lore (Section XVII).

The modern scholarship. Two works organise the modern study of this cycle, and this article leans on both, as the brief for it required. Timothy Gantz, Early Greek Myth: A Guide to Literary and Artistic Sources (Johns Hopkins University Press, 1993), chapter 10, “Perseus and Bellerophontes”, assembles every literary and artistic attestation in chronological order; the source-pages for that chapter, maintained in the University of Georgia’s Gantz Myth Sources project, were read for this article and supply several of its fragment references, including the Aeschylus Phorcides testimony. Daniel Ogden, Perseus (Routledge, 2008), in the Gods and Heroes of the Ancient World series, is the first scholarly monograph in English devoted to the whole Perseus myth in over a century; its publisher’s description and the published review literature were consulted for this article, and its organisation of the material — family saga, Medusa and the Gorgons, Andromeda, reception — stands behind the present article’s own. Ogden’s two 2013 volumes on dragons, Drakōn and the sourcebook Dragons, Serpents, and Slayers, treat Medusa and the Ethiopian sea-serpent as dragon-slayings, a framing discussed in Section XVIII. Where this article reports a scholarly position, it names its holder; where the sources disagree, it says so.

III. Acrisius, the Oracle, and Danaë in the Bronze Chamber

The story begins, as so many Greek heroic stories begin, with an oracle a king tries to evade. Acrisius, king of Argos, son of Abas and descendant — through Lynceus and Danaus — of Io’s line, had a daughter, Danaë, and no sons. Apollodorus opens the account (2.4.1, in Frazer’s translation) with the consultation itself: Acrisius inquired of the oracle how he should get male children, and, in Frazer’s wording, the god answered that “his daughter would give birth to a son who would kill him”. The answer converts a dynastic disappointment into a death sentence: the grandson he might have welcomed as an heir is announced, before conception, as his killer. Acrisius’s response is architectural. Still in Frazer’s account, paraphrased here except where quoted: he built a brazen chamber beneath the ground and there guarded Danaë — a prison of bronze, underground, designed to make the prophecy physically impossible by making access to the girl impossible.

The tradition preserves two accounts of how the chamber was breached, and Apollodorus, characteristically, reports both and lets them stand. In the first, Danaë was seduced by Proetus, Acrisius’s own twin brother — whence, Apollodorus notes, arose the brothers’ famous quarrel, the feud that split the Argolid between Argos and Tiryns and that frames the whole Argive heroic age (Bellerophon, too, will be exiled from Proetus’s court; see Section XI). In the second account, the one that conquered the world’s imagination, Zeus came to Danaë in the form of a stream of gold poured through the roof into her lap. Frazer’s Apollodorus gives it in one clause — Zeus “had intercourse with her in the shape of a stream of gold which poured through the roof into Danae’s lap” — and the economy is the point: bronze walls and an underground room are a defence against men, and the story is precisely about the futility of defending against a god. Pindar already knows the golden conception as a byword: in Pythian 12 he calls Perseus, in Diane Arnson Svarlien’s translation, the son of Danaë “who they say was conceived in a spontaneous shower of gold”. The image — wealth, divinity, and irresistible penetration in a single emblem — became one of the most painted scenes in Western art, a later life noted in Section XVIII; in the Greek sources it is stated plainly and passed over quickly, one marvel among many.

// THE BROTHERS’ FEUD //

Acrisius and Proetus quarrelled, in one strand of the tradition, from the womb, and their feud divided the Argolid: Acrisius held Argos, Proetus took Tiryns, Midea, and the coast. The Perseus cycle is unintelligible without that division — it explains Proetus’s alternative paternity for Perseus, Bellerophon’s exile, and the exchange of kingdoms with which Perseus’s own story ends (Sections XI and XIV).

What Acrisius does next defines him. Learning that Danaë has borne a child, Perseus, he refuses to believe Zeus is the father — Apollodorus is explicit that the refusal, not the birth, drives the act — and, unwilling or unable to kill daughter and grandson outright under his own roof, he puts them in a wooden chest (larnax) and casts it into the sea. The distinction matters to the story’s moral machinery. Exposure — abandoning the unwanted to the elements, the sea, the mountainside — is the standard Greek mythic device by which a killer delegates the killing to fortune and keeps his own hands formally clean; Oedipus on Cithaeron and Paris on Ida are the famous parallels. The sea, in these stories, regularly refuses the commission. The chest becomes a cradle, the intended drowning a voyage, and the oracle’s machinery — which no precaution has ever yet slowed — moves mother and child out of Argos altogether, to the small island where the next act of the king’s avoidance will be played out by other hands. Danaë’s own voice in the chest survives, in Simonides’ fragment, as the tradition’s emotional centre for this episode; it is treated with the voyage itself in the next section.

// TWO PATERNITIES, KEPT APART //

Apollodorus reports the Proetus seduction and the shower of gold as alternatives (“as some say” / “but some say”), and this article keeps them so. The divine paternity is the version the rest of the cycle assumes — Perseus acts throughout as Zeus’s son, and Herodotus 6.53 notes that the Greek king-lists run back to Perseus precisely because no mortal father is named for him, as Amphitryon is named for Heracles — but the Proetus version is old, consequential for the Argive feud, and not a late rationalisation to be tidied away.

IV. The Chest, the Sea, Seriphos, and Dictys

The chest was washed ashore, Apollodorus says, on Seriphos — a small, rocky Cycladic island, proverbially poor, whose inhabitants later Greek comedy treated as a byword for insignificance; the choice of landfall is part of the story’s design, since the hero of Argos must grow up where no one knows his name. There Dictys — his name is the Greek word for a net, and Apollodorus presents him at his work — took up the boy and reared him. Dictys is a fisherman, and in most tellings the chest comes up in his nets: the sea that was meant to kill the child delivers him instead to the humblest possible protector, a working man whose only resources are kindness and obscurity. The pattern is the exposure-story’s standard reversal, but the Seriphos setting gives it a particular edge. Perseus grows up not merely hidden but provincial — an Argive prince and a god’s son raised as a fisherman’s fosterling on an island the great world ignored — and everything that follows, Polydectes’s contempt, the boast at the feast, the impossible gift demanded of a young man with nothing, depends on that social fact.

The emotional register of the voyage is supplied by Simonides. His Danaë fragment (fragment 543 in Page’s Poetae Melici Graeci), preserved because Dionysius of Halicarnassus quoted it as a model of pathetic composition, gives Danaë a lament over the sleeping child in the dark, pitching chest: she speaks to the infant who sleeps through the wind and the bronze-nailed box, contrasting his untroubled rest with her own waking terror, in lines that ancient critics already treated as the supreme example of a mother’s voice in lyric. This article paraphrases the fragment rather than quoting it — no complete published translation of it was opened in the research for this article, and the hub’s rule is that quotation marks are earned, not approximated — but its existence and its fame belong in the record: by the early fifth century BCE, the chest episode was already being treated not as adventure but as pathos, and the cycle’s centre of feeling was the mother, not the hero. Gantz’s source-collection (chapter 10) places the fragment at the head of the Danaë testimonia, and Ogden’s monograph likewise gives the Simonides passage its weight as the earliest surviving interior telling of any part of the Perseus story.

Dictys had a brother: Polydectes, king of Seriphos. The two brothers are the island’s whole moral geography — the fisherman who takes in, and the king who takes. Apollodorus’s arrangement is spare: Dictys rears Perseus; Polydectes rules; Danaë lives, in effect, under her rescuer’s protection in her rescuer’s brother’s kingdom. For as long as Perseus is a child, the arrangement holds. The story’s second act begins at the moment it cannot hold any longer — when the fosterling is grown, and stands between the king and the king’s desire. Before turning to that act, the geography deserves one note, because later sections depend on it. Seriphos is real, small, and near; the Gorgons’ country, to which Perseus will be sent, is at the edge of the world, beyond Ocean, in the neighbourhood of Night. The quest’s geography is a geography of increasing remoteness — island, then the Graeae in the twilight, then the nymphs, then Ocean’s shore — and the hero’s equipment (Section VII) is precisely the kit required to traverse that gradient and come back.

V. Polydectes’s Trap and the Impossible Task

Apollodorus tells the trap’s mechanism with a mythographer’s economy (2.4.2, paraphrased from Frazer’s translation). Polydectes fell in love with Danaë but could not get access to her, because Perseus was grown to man’s estate. The son is the obstacle; the king’s problem is therefore not how to woo but how to remove — honourably, publicly, and at no risk to himself. His device is a feast with a fiction attached. He called together his friends, Perseus among them, under the pretext of collecting contributions toward a wedding gift for Hippodamia, daughter of Oenomaus — the princess of Pisa whose suitors raced her father for her hand and died for losing; the pretext is pointed, since a gift-collection for that wedding is a demand no guest can refuse without shame, and the company will be expected to contribute horses, the standard prestige-gift of heroic society. Perseus, who has no horses and no wealth — the fisherman’s fosterling at the king’s table — covers his poverty with the one currency he has, a boast: he declared, in Frazer’s rendering, that he would not stick even at the Gorgon’s head. It is the rash promise of a young man cornered in public, and Polydectes had designed the corner. The king required the others to furnish horses; from Perseus, who had offered more, he required exactly what had been offered. Bring the Gorgon’s head.

The task’s impossibility deserves to be stated flatly, because the cycle’s later wonders can make it sound merely difficult. The Gorgons live beyond Ocean at the world’s edge; their whereabouts are unknown to mortals; they can fly; and their faces turn anyone who looks at them to stone — which is to say, the ordinary heroic method, walk up and fight, is suicide by definition, and even finding them requires information no human source possesses. Polydectes is not sending Perseus on a hard errand. He is sending him to a death that will look like the consequence of his own boast, leaving Danaë unprotected and the king blameless. Pindar, in Pythian 12, compresses the whole Seriphos situation into the deliverance it produced: Perseus, in Svarlien’s translation, repaid Polydectes with a deadly wedding-gift “for the long slavery of his mother and her forced bridal bed” — a phrase that confirms, from the early fifth century BCE, both the marriage-pressure on Danaë and the story’s own sense of the head as a gift answering a gift. The eranos, the contribution-feast, is answered by an anti-eranos: the one contribution that destroys the collector.

// THE BOAST AS BOND //

Greek heroic narrative treats a public boast as a binding act: what a man says he will do, at a feast, before witnesses, he must do or be shamed past recovery. Polydectes’s trap works because the obligation is real. Perseus cannot withdraw the offer without ceasing to be the person the story needs him to be — and the king, who understands the code perfectly, simply holds him to his word.

What saves the enterprise is that Perseus is not, in fact, without resources: he is Zeus’s son, and the Olympian order has its own interest in the Gorgon. Apollodorus states the guidance in a clause — under the guidance of Hermes and Athena he made his way to the daughters of Phorcys — and the pairing is exact. Hermes is the god of roads, thresholds, theft, and clever acquisition, the divine escort who knows the way to places mortals cannot find; Athena is the goddess of craft, technique, and the disciplined use of tools, whose own emblem the Gorgon’s head will become. Between them they embody the quest’s whole method: find out, equip, indirection. Neither god fights Medusa for him. What they provide is a route and, at the kill itself, a guided hand (Section IX). The hero’s part is to execute — and, first, to interrogate the only beings who know the way onward: the Gorgons’ own sisters, the ancient ones of the twilight, the Graeae.

VI. The Graeae: One Eye, One Tooth

The Graeae — Greek Graiai, the Grey Ones, also called the Phorcides — are, in Hesiod’s genealogy (Theogony 270–273), daughters of the sea-god Phorcys and Ceto, grey from birth: Pemphredo and Enyo are the two Hesiod names, and a third, Dino, joins them in Apollodorus and later sources. They are the Gorgons’ sisters and, in the tradition Aeschylus staged, their appointed lookouts — the outer defence of the Gorgon country, posted where the twilight begins. Their defining feature is the one every retelling keeps: the three sisters possess between them one eye and one tooth, which they pass from hand to hand in turn. Apollodorus, in Frazer’s translation, states it without ornament: the three “had but one eye and one tooth, and these they passed to each other in turn”. Aeschylus’s Prometheus Bound gives the same economy a geography — the ancient maids dwell where neither sun nor moon ever looks on them (paraphrased) — and the detail is more than grotesque decoration. Beings who share a single organ of sight are beings whose perception itself has a gap in it: a moment, at every handover, when the eye is in transit and no one is seeing. Perseus’s first exploit is to find that gap and put his hand in it.

Apollodorus’s account of the exploit (2.4.2) is the canonical one, and this article follows it in paraphrase with its key terms quoted from Frazer. Perseus got possession of the eye and the tooth — seized them, the fuller tradition in Pherecydes and the scholia makes clear, in the instant of passing — and when the sisters demanded them back, he named his price: he would return them if they would show him the way to the nymphs who held the quest’s equipment. The extortion is worth pausing on, because it sets the cycle’s ethical tone and Ogden’s monograph remarks on the pattern: Perseus’s characteristic act is not the frontal blow but the seizure of leverage. He takes hostage the two things his victims cannot live without — their sight and their eating — and trades them for information. The Graeae comply; he returns eye and tooth, as agreed; and the bargain, faithfully kept on both sides, moves him one station further out: from the sisters who know the way, to the nymphs who hold the tools. The tale’s logic is a chain of custodians, each guarding the next link, and the hero’s skill is the skill of working the chain.

Aeschylus’s Phorcides: Handling a Lost Play

Aeschylus made a tragedy of this episode — the Phorcides — and the play is lost. What survives is testimony: fragments and reports preserved by later authors, collected as fragment 262 in Stefan Radt’s edition of the tragic fragments (Tragicorum Graecorum Fragmenta, vol. 3) and assembled in Gantz’s source-pages for the Perseus chapter, which were read for this article. The fullest testimony comes through Eratosthenes’ star-lore (the Catasterismi tradition, Section XVII), and in the translation given in Gantz’s sources it reports, in substance, this: that Perseus took the curved blade of adamant from Hephaestus; that, as Aeschylus says in the Phorcides, the Gorgons had the Graeae as their lookouts, who had one eye between them and handed it round as each went on guard; that Perseus, watching carefully, stole the eye as it was handed over and threw it into the Tritonian lake; and that, coming upon the Gorgons asleep, he took Medusa’s head. Two differences from Apollodorus stand out and are recorded here as differences, not harmonised: in the Aeschylean version as reported, Perseus does not bargain — he destroys the eye, throwing it into Lake Tritonis (in Libya, in the far west), blinding the lookouts permanently; and the adamantine blade comes from Hephaestus, not Hermes. The vase-painting record studied by J. H. Oakley in his 1988 article “Perseus, the Graiai and Aeschylus’ Phorkides” (American Journal of Archaeology 92.3) — an Attic red-figure pyxis lid in Athens (National Museum 1291) and related images of Perseus with the Graeae — has been connected with the play’s staging and influence. Beyond this, honesty requires the archive’s standard formula: the play’s plot, structure, and treatment are not recoverable, and any account that summarises the Phorcides as if it could be read is inventing. What the fragments establish is narrower and still valuable: that by the mid-fifth century BCE the Graeae episode was important enough to carry a tragedy, and that its tragic form was harsher than the mythographic one — theft and blinding, not a bargain kept.

Pindar’s Pythian 10 adds one further station that the prose accounts omit, and its omission from Apollodorus should be noted rather than explained away. In the ode for Hippocleas (498 BCE), Perseus, guided — the poem stresses Athena’s favour — journeys to the Hyperboreans, the people beyond Boreas, the north wind, who live in feasting and freedom from disease and old age. In the translation preserved in Gantz’s source-pages, Pindar says that neither by ship nor on foot could one find the marvellous road to the Hyperboreans’ gathering, but that Perseus, the leader of his people, once entered their homes and feasted among them, finding them sacrificing hecatombs of donkeys to Apollo. Where the episode belongs in the quest’s itinerary — before the Gorgons, as a stage on the outward road, is the usual reading — Pindar does not say, and the prose tradition’s silence may mean the visit belonged to a fuller epic telling that the epitomes dropped. This article records it as Pindar’s attestation: the hero’s road to the monster ran, in the earliest lyric telling we have, through the land of the blessed — the far north answering to the far west, both at the rim of a world whose centre is Seriphos and Argos.

// HOW TO READ THE GRAEAE EPISODE //

Three versions, three morals. Apollodorus (after Pherecydes): a bargain — information bought with a hostage and the hostage returned; the hero as negotiator. Aeschylus, as reported: a theft and a blinding; the hero as saboteur of the enemy’s watch. The vases: pursuit and flight, the old women groping. The archive keeps all three, dated and attributed, and does not blend them into a single “real” version.

VII. The Equipment as a System: Tools for Seeing Indirectly

From the Graeae, Perseus comes to the nymphs — unnamed, unplaced except that they lie further along the road the Graeae guarded — and receives the quest’s famous kit. Apollodorus lists its core items as the nymphs’ property: the winged sandals, the kibisis, and the cap of Hades. To these the tradition adds two divine gifts: from Hermes, an adamantine sickle — the harpē, a curved blade of the unbreakable metal adamas — and from Athena, in the beheading scene itself, the polished bronze shield used as a mirror (Section IX). Each item answers a specific term of the problem stated in Section V, and the kit is best understood, as this article’s brief directs, as a system: a matched set of solutions to the problems of distance, containment, concealment, cutting, and sight.

WINGED SANDALS — DISTANCE

The Gorgons live beyond Ocean, unreachable by ship or road in any reasonable time, and they themselves fly. The sandals (Greek pterenta pedila in the epic diction) put the hero in their element: he arrives by air, strikes from above, and escapes by air. In the Shield he is already wearing them in flight, “swift as thought”.

THE CAP OF HADES — CONCEALMENT

The cap (kuneē) of Hades, the god whose name the tradition itself connected with invisibility (a-idēs, the unseen). Apollodorus, in Frazer’s rendering: wearing it, Perseus “saw whom he pleased, but was not seen by others”. Its function is escape: the surviving Gorgons wake and pursue, and cannot find what they cannot see.

THE KIBISIS — CONTAINMENT

A wallet or pouch, worn slung at the side. Its problem is the trophy itself: a head that petrifies anyone who looks at it must be carried without being seen — by enemies, by bystanders, or by the carrier. The kibisis is the quest’s safety case. Apollodorus pauses to etymologise it: it is called kibisis, he says, because clothing and food are deposited in it — a traveller’s provision-bag, repurposed as a container for the most dangerous object in the world.

THE ADAMANTINE HARPĒ — CUTTING

The Gorgons’ bodies, in Apollodorus’s description, are scaled, tusked, and brazen-handed; the neck must be severed in a single operation, performed blind, at a sleeping target guarded by two immortal sisters. The blade is therefore of adamas — the unbreakable substance — and curved, a reaping tool: the beheading is imagined as a harvest. Hermes gives it in Apollodorus; the Aeschylean testimony gives Hephaestus as its maker (Section VI); Ovid’s Perseus carries a curved sword throughout.

The fifth element is not carried to the Gorgons but supplied at the moment of use, and it is the system’s heart: the mirrored shield. Athena’s polished bronze shield — in Apollodorus it is a brazen shield into which Perseus looks with averted gaze while Athena guides his hand — converts an unlookable object into a lookable image. The whole kit can be read as an essay on mediation. The sandals mediate distance, the cap mediates presence, the wallet mediates a dangerous content, the sickle mediates force — and the shield mediates sight itself, interposing a reflection between the hero and a face that kills by being seen. Later ages would make much of this, philosophically and alchemically (Section XVIII); the Greek story itself makes the point by structure rather than by commentary, but the structure is unmistakable: Perseus is the hero who survives by not meeting his enemy directly, in any sense of “directly”, at all.

The earliest surviving picture of the equipped hero is also the earliest proof that the kit, as a set, is archaic. The Shield of Heracles (lines 216–237) describes, among the figures Hephaestus works on Heracles’ shield, Perseus in flight at the moment after the beheading. In H. G. Evelyn-White’s Loeb translation (1914), the passage runs, in excerpt:

“There, too, was the son of rich-haired Danae, the horseman Perseus: his feet did not touch the shield and yet were not far from it — very marvellous to remark, since he was not supported anywhere… On his feet he had winged sandals, and his black-sheathed sword was slung across his shoulders by a cross-belt of bronze. He was flying swift as thought. The head of a dreadful monster, the Gorgon, covered the broad of his back, and a bag of silver — a marvel to see — contained it… Upon the head of the hero lay the dread cap of Hades which had the awful gloom of night.” — [HESIOD], SHIELD OF HERACLES 216–227, TRANS. H. G. EVELYN-WHITE (LOEB, 1914)

Every element of the canonical kit is already there, in the early sixth century BCE: sandals, sword, the Gorgon’s head in its bag — the kibisis, here of silver with golden tassels — and the cap. Apollodorus himself, six centuries later, quotes this very poem (through Pindar and the Shield together) as his authority for the kibisis, embedding the couplet about the head on Perseus’s back and the wallet running round him directly into his prose (2.4.2). The equipment list, in other words, is not a late accumulation of fairy-tale motifs around a simple original. As far back as our evidence reaches, the hero is his equipment: the tradition defines Perseus by the tools in his hands at least as much as by the blood in his veins.

// WHO GAVE WHAT: THE VARIANTS //

The sources do not assign the gifts identically, and this article does not harmonise them. Apollodorus: sandals, kibisis, and cap from the nymphs; sickle from Hermes; the shield-mirror improvised with Athena’s guidance at the kill. Pherecydes’ fragments distribute some items differently (Hermes is prominent as guide and giver). Ovid’s Perseus (paraphrased) flies with winged sandals and a curved sword and uses his shield’s reflection, without Apollodorus’s careful inventory. Later handbooks tidy the list into “Hermes gave the sandals and sword, Athena the shield, the nymphs the rest” — a classroom summary, not an ancient text. The variation is itself the evidence: the set is constant, the attributions float.

VIII. Medusa and the Gorgons

The Gorgons are three sisters, daughters of the ancient sea-god Phorcys and the sea-monster Ceto, and sisters in turn of the Graeae, of the dragon Ladon’s kin, and of Echidna — the whole brood of primordial sea-monsters that Hesiod’s Theogony catalogues in the line of Phorcys (270–336; see this hub’s Theogony article, Section VII, for the catalogue in its place). Their names are Stheno (the Mighty), Euryale (the Far-Leaper), and Medusa (Greek Medousa, the Guardian or Queen — from medō, to rule over and protect; the name will matter when her face becomes a protective device). They dwell, in Hesiod, beyond glorious Ocean in the frontier land toward Night, where the Hesperides sing; the Cypria tradition placed them on the rocky island Sarpedon in Ocean; Aeschylus’s Prometheus Bound moves them to the far east, to the Gorgonean plains of Cisthene across the sea. The geography floats because the meaning is fixed: they live where the map runs out, in whatever direction the teller’s world has its edge.

Hesiod’s account, the earliest we have, is eleven lines of genealogy, and its facts are these (Theogony 270–283, in Evelyn-White’s translation, paraphrased except where quoted): Ceto bore to Phorcys the Graeae, grey from birth, and the Gorgons who dwell beyond Ocean; of the three, “Medusa who suffered a woeful fate: she was mortal, but the two were undying and grew not old”. With Medusa lay “the Dark-haired One” — Poseidon — “in a soft meadow amid spring flowers”. That is the whole of Hesiod’s Medusa: a mortal among immortals, a god’s lover, and a beheading presupposed. There is no transformation, no temple, no punishment, no serpent hair in Hesiod’s lines at all — the snakes belong to the iconography and to later description — and there is no hint that Medusa was ever other than what she is: a Gorgon, born of monsters, distinguished from her sisters by the single, fatal attribute of mortality. The pathos of the later tradition, in which Medusa is a victim made monstrous, is a re-reading, and its source is Ovid (Sections XII and XVIII). The archaic Medusa needs no fall to explain her: she is the one killable member of an unkillable family, and that is why the quest is possible and why it is aimed at her.

What the Gorgons are, physically, the fullest early prose description gives: Apollodorus (2.4.2, in Frazer’s translation, paraphrased) describes heads twined about with the scales of dragons, great tusks like swine’s, brazen hands, and golden wings by which they flew — and the power that defines them, that they turned to stone such as beheld them. The vase-painters’ Gorgon matches the text and precedes its fullest form: a frontal face, round and staring, with glaring eyes, a wide grinning mouth with tusks and lolling tongue, snakes in the hair and at the girdle, wings, and a running, kneeling-run pose (Knielauf) that signifies flight. The frontality is the iconographic point. Greek art almost never shows faces full-front; the Gorgon is the great exception, because her image is her weapon — the picture does to the viewer what the creature did, at one remove, safely. Section IX takes up what that remove made possible.

Athena’s Flute: Pindar, Pythian 12

One early text gives the Gorgons an afterlife stranger than petrification, and it is the earliest surviving account of the beheading’s emotional register. Pindar’s Pythian 12 (490 BCE) honours Midas of Acragas, victor in the aulos — the double-pipe, conventionally “flute” — contest at Delphi, and explains the instrument’s most famous tune as Athena’s invention in the aftermath of Medusa’s death. In Svarlien’s translation, the ode says that Pallas Athena discovered the art “when she wove into music the dire dirge of the reckless Gorgons” — the lament that Perseus heard pouring from the surviving sisters, from beneath their snakey hair, as he carried off the third sister’s head and brought doom to Seriphos. The goddess, Pindar continues (paraphrased), fashioned the “many-headed tune” (nomos polykephalos) to imitate the wailing that burst from Euryale’s ravening jaws, and gave the music to mortals for their contests. The aetiology is doing several things at once: it dignifies a contest-piece with a divine inventor; it explains a tune’s name — many-headed, for the many snake-heads whose hissing and the sisters’ many-voiced grief it imitates; and it preserves, inside a victory ode, the tradition’s own acknowledgement that the beheading had a sound, and the sound was mourning. Medusa’s sisters are not scenery. In the earliest lyric telling, their grief is literally the music of the games.

// MEDUSA ALONE MORTAL //

The mortality is the quest’s enabling condition and the tradition never varies it: Hesiod states it, Apollodorus builds the plot on it (“Medusa alone was mortal; for that reason Perseus was sent to fetch her head”), and the art agrees — it is always Medusa who is beheaded, Medusa whose children spring from the wound, Medusa whose head is carried. Stheno and Euryale pursue, lament, and survive into the world’s furniture of terrors; only the mortal sister can be killed, and only she, therefore, can become a trophy.

IX. The Beheading and the Gorgoneion

Equipped, Perseus flew to Ocean and found the Gorgons asleep. Apollodorus’s account of the kill (2.4.3, in Frazer’s translation) is the canonical text, and it is quoted here at the length its importance earns:

“So Perseus stood over them as they slept, and while Athena guided his hand and he looked with averted gaze on a brazen shield, in which he beheld the image of the Gorgon, he beheaded her. When her head was cut off, there sprang from the Gorgon the winged horse Pegasus and Chrysaor, the father of Geryon; these she had by Poseidon.” — APOLLODORUS, LIBRARY 2.4.2–3, TRANS. J. G. FRAZER (LOEB, 1921)

Every element of the system of Section VII converges in those two sentences. The target is asleep — the hero does not duel the Gorgon awake, because there is no duel to be had with a face that kills by sight. The gaze is averted and mediated: the shield’s polished bronze holds the image, and the image, unlike the face, can be looked at. The hand is guided: Athena’s presence at the stroke is the tradition’s way of saying that the technique itself is divine craft, technē personified, steadying the mortal executor. And the cut is made with the adamantine harpē — a single severing, after which the head goes straight into the kibisis, unseen, and the cap makes the carrier unseen too, while the immortal sisters wake to a pursuit they cannot complete. Scholarship on the early sources (Gantz, chapter 10, collecting the Pherecydes fragments and the early vases) notes that the reflection-device in its fully explicit form — looking into the shield at the image — is clearest in Apollodorus and in the later tradition; earlier tellings stress the averted gaze and the backwards approach, and the art of the sixth and fifth centuries shows Perseus characteristically hurrying away, head averted, while the bag does its work. The mirror is therefore best stated as the tradition’s own progressive clarification of its central technique: first, do not look; then, look only at the image. The clarification is faithful to the story’s logic, and this article reports it as a development, not as a detail present from the start.

The Gorgoneion: The Monster’s Face as Protective Device

What happened to the head after the quest is an art-historical fact, and this article states it as such, in the terms the evidence supports. The severed face — the gorgoneion — became the most widely diffused apotropaic image of the Greek world: a frontal, staring, grinning mask, set up to turn back harm by confronting it. It appears on temple pediments and antefixes, where it guards the building; on shields and breastplates, where it guards the warrior — Homer already knows the Gorgon’s head blazoned on Agamemnon’s shield and on Athena’s aegis, a thing of fear and horror at the centre of the divine armour; on the bottoms of drinking cups, where it meets the drinker’s eye as he drains the wine; on coins, doors, ovens, and kilns — anywhere a threshold wanted a guardian whose look was worse than whatever might approach. The mechanism the Greeks themselves imply is the one the story supplies: the face that petrifies is dangerous to look at, and an image of it, fixed and frontal, directs that danger outward, at the intruder, the evil eye, the ill-wisher. The monster becomes the ward against monsters. It is, so far as this archive is aware, the earliest fully documented case in Greek culture of a weaponised image retained permanently as a protective emblem — and its final placement completes the logic: Perseus gives the head to Athena (Section XIV), and Athena sets it in the middle of her shield and wears it on her aegis ever after. The goddess of craft takes custody of the dangerous image, mounts it, and carries it into every battle. The hero’s trophy becomes the god’s standard equipment.

The monumental proof of the image’s archaic power is the west pediment of the Temple of Artemis at Corcyra (modern Corfu), dated to c. 590–580 BCE — among the earliest stone temple pediments in Greece, now in the Archaeological Museum of Corfu. At its centre, over nine feet high, runs the Gorgon in the kneeling-run pose, snakes in her hair and girdle, flanked by her two children, Pegasus and Chrysaor, shown small beside her, and by two great panthers at the pediment’s narrowing ends. The composition is a heraldic emblem before it is a narrative: Medusa is not shown being beheaded — Perseus is absent — but displayed, frontal and entire, with the offspring who identify her story placed at her sides like supporters on a shield. A temple of Artemis is guarded by the Gorgon’s face for the same reason a shield is: the image protects the place. Section XVII returns to the pediment in the art survey; here it stands as the beheading’s most important consequence stated in stone — within a century and a half of Hesiod, the monster’s face was already architecture.

// APOTROPAIC: A WORD WITH A JOB //

Apotropaic (from Greek apotrepein, to turn away) names an image or object whose function is to avert evil by presentation — to meet the harmful look, demon, or influence with a counter-display. The term is modern scholarship’s, but the function is ancient and is how the sources treat the gorgoneion’s placements: on the aegis that routs armies, on the shield that faces the enemy, on the pediment that faces the world. This article uses the word in that functional sense only, and does not build a psychology of the Gorgon on top of it; the later readings that do are separated in Section XVIII.

X. From the Blood: Pegasus and Chrysaor

The beheading is the only birth-scene in Greek myth performed with a sword. Medusa was pregnant by Poseidon — Hesiod’s “with her lay the Dark-haired One in a soft meadow amid spring flowers” — and when the head was cut off, the pregnancy issued from the wound in the neck: two beings, utterly unlike, spring forth together. In Evelyn-White’s translation of Theogony 280–283: “And when Perseus cut off her head, there sprang forth great Chrysaor and the horse Pegasus who is so called because he was born near the springs of Ocean; and that other, because he held a golden blade in his hands.” Hesiod’s etymologies are part of the text: Pēgasos from the pēgai, the springs of Ocean beside which he was born; Chrysāōr from chrysos, gold, and aor, a blade — the giant who came into the world already armed with a golden sword.

Their careers diverge at once, and Hesiod follows both in the same breath. Pegasus flew away and left the earth, came to the deathless gods, and dwells in the house of Zeus, bringing to wise Zeus the thunder and lightning (Theogony 284–286, paraphrased from Evelyn-White) — the monster’s son as the storm-god’s attendant, carrying the weapons of heaven. His second career, as the mount of Bellerophon, belongs to Section XI; his third, as a constellation, to Section XVII. Chrysaor remained in the genealogy of monsters: joined with the Oceanid Callirrhoë, he fathered three-bodied Geryon, the herdsman of the far-western red cattle beyond Ocean, whom Heracles would kill on his tenth labour — so that the Perseus cycle generates, two generations on, one of the central episodes of the Heracles cycle, and the two great monster-slaying careers are knitted together by blood. The Corfu pediment (Section IX) shows the pair as the Gorgon’s identifying children, small figures at her flanks: by c. 590 BCE the birth from the wound was already the standard shorthand for which Gorgon this is and what her death released.

The blood itself works wonders in the later tradition, and the layers must be kept distinct. In the archaic stratum, the offspring are the wonder: nothing is said of dripping blood creating life. Ovid (paraphrased throughout this article) adds the aetiologies that made the blood fertile in transit: drops falling on the Libyan desert as Perseus flies home breed the snakes with which Libya has teemed ever since, and the head, laid down on a bed of seaweed on the Ethiopian shore, hardens the weed into coral — the origin of coral, which is soft in the sea and stone in the air, explained by contact with the petrifying face. Apollonius’s Argonautica (4.1515 and following) already knows the Libyan snake-aetiology in the third century BCE, so the motif is Hellenistic at latest; the coral is Ovid’s most famous contribution. A further blood-lore — that Medusa’s blood, drawn from her two sides, could kill from one vein and heal, even raise the dead, from the other, and that Athena gave such blood to Asclepius — belongs to later mythographic elaboration (it is attached to the Asclepius tradition in Apollodorus 3.10.3 and its scholia) and is recorded here as a later development of the head’s pharmacology, not as part of the archaic cycle. The pattern across all three layers is consistent, and worth naming: in this story, what comes out of the Gorgon keeps working — children, snakes, coral, cures. The beheading is not an ending but a release.

PEGASUS

Winged horse, son of Poseidon and Medusa, born from the severed neck at the springs of Ocean. In Hesiod, he ascends at once to Zeus’s house and carries the thunder and lightning. Later: Bellerophon’s mount (Section XI) and a constellation (Section XVII). His name is tied by Hesiod to the pēgai, the springs.

CHRYSAOR

“Golden-blade”: the giant who sprang forth holding a golden sword. By Callirrhoë, father of three-bodied Geryon of Erytheia, whose cattle were Heracles’ tenth labour. Chrysaor has almost no story of his own; his function in the tradition is genealogical — he is the hinge between the Perseus cycle and the Heracles cycle.

XI. Bellerophon: The Sequel the Tradition Itself Attached

Pegasus’s rider was not Perseus. In no early source does Perseus mount the horse he released; the winged sandals are his transport, and Pegasus flies straight to Olympus. The horse’s heroic career belongs to Bellerophon (Greek Bellerophontēs), the Corinthian hero whose story the tradition attached to Perseus’s as its sequel — attached literally, in the mythographers’ ordering: Apollodorus tells Bellerophon (Library 2.3) immediately before Perseus (2.4), Gantz’s handbook treats the two in a single chapter, and the logic of the attachment is Pegasus himself, the living link between the Argive cycle and the Corinthian one. Bellerophon is the second monster-slayer of the generation before Heracles, and his story is the Perseus pattern run again with a darker ending: a young hero falsely accused, exiled, sent to die by a monster, saved by divine equipment and a divine patroness — and then, unlike Perseus, destroyed not by an enemy but by his own success.

The Early Source: Glaucus in Iliad 6

The earliest substantial account is also the finest, and it is a speech. In Iliad 6 (lines 152–210), on the battlefield before Troy, the Lycian captain Glaucus answers Diomedes’ challenge by reciting his own ancestry, and the ancestry is Bellerophon’s whole career, told by his grandson as a family’s proudest and saddest possession. In A. T. Murray’s Loeb translation, paraphrased here except where quoted: Bellerophon, son of Glaucus, grandson of Sisyphus of Ephyre (Corinth), was granted by the gods beauty and lovely manliness; but Proetus, king in Argos, plotted against him and drove him out — because Anteia, Proetus’s wife, had lusted for him, been refused, and lied that he had tried to force her. Proetus, unwilling to kill a suppliant and guest outright, sent him to Lycia carrying a folded tablet inscribed with “baneful signs” — the famous Bellerophontic letter, the only reference to writing in Homer — instructing his father-in-law, the Lycian king, to kill the bearer. The king feasted his guest nine days before reading the message — hospitality first, even here — and then, instead of murder, imposed tasks. First, the Chimera. Murray’s rendering of lines 179–183:

“First he bade him slay the raging Chimera. She was of divine stock, not of men, in the fore part a lion, in the hinder a serpent, and in the midst a goat, breathing forth in terrible wise the might of blazing fire. And Bellerophon slew her, trusting in the signs of the gods.” — HOMER, ILIAD 6.179–183, TRANS. A. T. MURRAY (LOEB)

Then the Solymi, the fiercest battle, Glaucus says, that Bellerophon ever entered; then the Amazons; then, on the road home, an ambush of Lycia’s bravest men, whom he killed to a man. The king, recognising “the valiant offspring of a god”, gave him his daughter, half his kingly honour, and a choice estate; she bore Isander, Hippolochus (Glaucus’s father), and Laodameia, who by Zeus bore Sarpedon. And then the speech turns, in Murray’s translation, quoted verbatim because no paraphrase carries its weight:

“But when even Bellerophon came to be hated of all the gods, then verily he wandered alone over the Aleian plain, devouring his own soul, and shunning the paths of men.” — HOMER, ILIAD 6.200–202, TRANS. A. T. MURRAY (LOEB)

Homer gives no reason for the hatred of the gods, no fall, and — the point on which everything in this section turns — no Pegasus. In the Iliad Bellerophon kills the Chimera “trusting in the signs of the gods”, on foot for all the poem says, and his catastrophe is unexplained and inward: a wandering, a self-devouring solitude on the Aleian (the “Wandering”) plain, while his son is killed by Ares and his daughter by Artemis. The magnificent desolation of the ending is Homer’s, and later tradition could not leave it unexplained.

Pegasus, the Fall, and the Later Fillings-In

The fillings-in came in stages, and this article dates them as stages. Pindar, in Olympian 13 (464 BCE, for Xenophon of Corinth), gives the taming: Bellerophon, longing to harness Pegasus at the spring of Peirene, failed again and again until Athena came to him in sleep and gave him a golden bridle — in the Perseus translation of the ode, paraphrased here, the goddess of the dark aegis tells the sleeping hero to take the horse-charm and sacrifice a white bull to Poseidon the Horse-Tamer; he wakes, finds the bridle beside him, bridles the winged horse, and from his back shoots down the Chimera, the Solymi, and the Amazons. Pindar ends the episode with the horse’s reception into the stalls of Zeus on Olympus and pointedly declines to sing Bellerophon’s death. Euripides, in his lost Bellerophontes and Stheneboea (fifth century BCE; both survive in fragments, and Aristophanes parodies the flight in Peace, 421 BCE), supplied the catastrophe Homer withheld: Bellerophon, in the later tradition’s full form, attempted to fly on Pegasus to Olympus itself, to confront or to join the gods; Zeus sent a gadfly that stung the horse; Pegasus threw his rider and ascended alone; Bellerophon fell to earth, lamed and blinded in some versions, and wandered out his days — the fall retroactively explaining the Aleian plain. Apollodorus (2.3) and the later handbooks complete the synthesis: the lead-tipped spear whose molten lead chokes the fire-breathing Chimera (a device absent from Homer and Pindar alike), the punishment of Stheneboea, the fall, the wandering. Each layer is intelligible — the horse explains the victories, the attempted ascent explains the fall, the fall explains the wandering — and the whole composite is the “Bellerophon” of modern retelling. The archive’s duty is the one performed here: to keep the strata visible, so that a reader can see Homer’s unexplained ruin standing, gaunt and complete, underneath the machinery later ages built to explain it. The sequel’s moral, as the tradition finally shaped it, is the Perseus story’s negative: Perseus, who never reached beyond his task, ends as a founder and an ancestor; Bellerophon, who flew one flight too far, ends alone on the plain, shunning the paths of men.

XII. The Return I: Atlas, Libya, and Ovid’s Additions

Perseus’s homeward road, in the prose tradition, is nearly bare: Apollodorus takes him from the Gorgons directly to Aethiopia and Andromeda. The embroidery of the return — its episodes, its aetiologies, its monsters of landscape — is overwhelmingly Ovid’s, and this section treats the return in Ovid’s order while flagging, episode by episode, what is his. Ovid’s Perseus narrative (Metamorphoses 4.604–803 and 5.1–249, paraphrased throughout this article; Ovid is never quoted here) is told backwards: the poem takes up Perseus already victorious, flying home with the head, and reserves the Medusa expedition itself for a flashback at the wedding feast, where the hero narrates it to Cepheus’s court. The structure is a storyteller’s, and it makes the return, not the quest, the Roman poem’s centre of gravity.

Atlas Turned to Stone

Flying west-to-east across North Africa at nightfall, Ovid’s Perseus seeks rest in the kingdom of Atlas — the Titan who holds up the sky at the world’s western edge, owner of vast flocks and of trees with golden leaves and golden fruit (paraphrased from Metamorphoses 4.617–662). Perseus asks hospitality as a guest and names himself: a son of Jupiter, and the slayer of the Gorgon. Atlas remembers an oracle — Themis’s prophecy, in Ovid’s telling, that a son of Jupiter would one day strip his golden tree (the theft Heracles will in fact perform, of the apples of the Hesperides) — and, taking this son of Jupiter for that one, refuses him roughly and tries to drive him out by force. Perseus, no match for the Titan’s bulk, asks him to accept a gift instead, turns his own face away, and holds up Medusa’s head. Atlas becomes a mountain: his beard and hair turn to forests, his shoulders and hands to ridges, his head to the peak, his bones to stone — the Atlas range of northwest Africa, explained, and the sky thereafter rests, with all its stars, on a summit of petrified Titan. The episode is Ovid’s version, and this article flags it in those exact terms, as the brief requires: no Greek source for the petrification of Atlas survives, Apollodorus knows nothing of it, and its logic is Ovidian — a metamorphosis that explains a landscape, worked by the poem’s presiding device, the head that turns living things to stone. Its afterlife has been enormous (the Atlas Mountains carry the story in their name to this day), which is precisely why the flag matters: fame is not antiquity.

The Libyan crossing gives Ovid his second aetiology, already noted in Section X: drops of the Gorgon’s blood, falling from the kibisis as Perseus flies over the desert, breed the snakes of Libya where they touch the sand (paraphrased from Metamorphoses 4.617 and following, with the motif attested earlier in Apollonius, Argonautica 4.1515 and following). Between Atlas and Aethiopia, then, Ovid’s return is a chain of explanations — a mountain range, a desert’s snakes, and presently a sea’s coral — hung on the flight-path of a hero carrying a head whose influence leaks. The Greek prose tradition’s silence about all of it is not a defect to be repaired but a difference to be recorded: Apollodorus’s Perseus does his wonders in inhabited places, against named antagonists, for stated reasons; Ovid’s sheds wonders on the landscape as he passes. Both are “the myth”. They are not the same telling, and they are not the same century.

// FLAGGED: OVID’S ATLAS //

The petrification of Atlas is attested in Ovid, Metamorphoses 4, and in sources dependent on Ovid. It is absent from Hesiod, Pindar, Pherecydes (as preserved), and Apollodorus. Readers meeting the episode in modern handbooks without an attribution are meeting Ovid without knowing it. This article attributes it, here and in the Sources, to Ovid alone.

XIII. The Return II: Andromeda, the Sea-Monster, and the Wedding Fight

In Aethiopia — the Greek world’s name for the lands of the far south, here a kingdom on the sea ruled by Cepheus and his queen Cassiopeia (Cassiepea in Apollodorus’s spelling) — Perseus found the king’s daughter Andromeda set out as prey for a sea-monster. Apollodorus (2.4.3, paraphrased from Frazer) gives the cause with his usual compression: Cassiopeia vied with the Nereids in beauty and boasted she was better than them all; the Nereids were angry, and Poseidon, sharing their wrath, sent a flood and a monster (kētos) against the land; the oracle of Ammon promised deliverance if Andromeda were exposed to the monster; and Cepheus, compelled by the Aethiopians, bound his daughter to a rock. The pattern is the exposure-pattern of Section III inverted and darkened: there, a king exposed a child to escape an oracle; here, a kingdom exposes a child to obey one — and in both cases the intended victim is a daughter, and the fault is not hers. Cassiopeia’s boast is the mother’s; the price is the daughter’s. Greek myth returns to that injustice often enough — Iphigenia is its most terrible instance — that the archive notes it as structure, not accident.

Perseus beheld her, loved her — Apollodorus’s sequence is that brisk — and promised Cepheus he would kill the monster if the rescued girl were given him to wife. On sworn terms, he withstood and slew the monster and released Andromeda. The combat itself the prose tradition leaves almost undescribed; Ovid expands it into a set-piece aerial duel (paraphrased): the hero striking from the air, bracing on a rock as the beast charges, driving his curved sword into its vitals as the surf reddens — and it is Ovid, too, who gives the episode its coral aetiology, Perseus laying the Gorgon’s head on seaweed while he washes and builds altars to Mercury, Minerva, and Jupiter, and the weed hardening at the touch into the first coral, whose seeds the sea-nymphs then scatter (paraphrased from Metamorphoses 4.740–764). The dragon-slaying reading of the episode — the kētos as a drakōn, a great serpent of the sea, and Perseus as its slayer in the sequence that runs through Cadmus, Heracles, and Jason — is Daniel Ogden’s framing in Drakōn (2013) and in his sourcebook Dragons, Serpents, and Slayers (2013), where the Ethiopian sea-serpent slain by Perseus stands as a named chapter; the framing is reported here as Ogden’s, and it has the merit of explaining why the episode clings to the cycle: Perseus is the dragon-slayer twice over, of the Gorgon by indirection and of the sea-beast in open fight.

Phineus and the Wedding Fight

Andromeda had been betrothed before — to Phineus, Cepheus’s brother, her uncle. Apollodorus disposes of the consequence in two clauses: Phineus plotted against Perseus; Perseus discovered the plot and, by showing the Gorgon, turned him and his fellow-conspirators at once into stone (paraphrased from Frazer). Ovid makes of it the whole opening movement of Metamorphoses 5 (paraphrased): the wedding feast in Cepheus’s hall, Perseus’s own narration of the Medusa expedition as the feast’s entertainment — within which Ovid places, as an answer to a guest’s question, the story of Medusa’s beauty, her rape by Neptune in Minerva’s temple, and Minerva’s punishment of the victim by transforming her hair to serpents and setting the Gorgon on her own aegis (paraphrased from Metamorphoses 4.770–803) — and then Phineus bursting in with an armed band to claim the bride by force. The battle that follows is Ovid’s longest sustained combat: a hall full of named combatants killed in catalogued ways, altars and feast-gear turned to weapons, until Perseus, overwhelmed by numbers, warns his friends to avert their eyes and raises the head, petrifying his enemies in batches, each frozen in the attitude of the moment — and Phineus last, begging, averting his face, and petrified anyway, left as a stone monument in his brother’s house (paraphrased). The contrast between the two tellings is the section’s lesson: Apollodorus’s clause and Ovid’s two hundred and fifty lines are the same plot-point at two different scales of art, and a reader who knows only the Ovidian battle knows the Roman Perseus, not the Greek one. Both are given here, labelled.

// ANDROMEDA’S AFTERLIFE IN ART //

The chained Andromeda became one of antiquity’s most reproduced images — on vases from the sixth century BCE, in the lost Andromeda plays of Sophocles and Euripides, and in Roman wall-painting — and travellers in antiquity were shown her rock and the marks of her chains at Joppa (Jaffa) on the Levantine coast, where ancient writers report the display; the localisation moved the Aethiopian princess to a harbour Greek and Roman tourists could actually visit. The reports are recorded here as ancient tourism-traditions, not as evidence for any event.

XIV. Seriphos Avenged and the Discus at Larissa: The Oracle Fulfilled

The return to Seriphos is the cycle’s reckoning, and Apollodorus stages it as a rescue arriving at the last possible hour (2.4.3, paraphrased from Frazer): Perseus found that his mother and Dictys had taken refuge at the altars, fleeing the violence of Polydectes — the marriage the king had wanted, now to be taken by force, from a woman protected only by sanctuary and by her brother-in-law’s roof. Perseus entered the palace, where Polydectes had gathered his friends — the same company, the story invites us to see, that had witnessed the boast and the demand — and with averted face showed the Gorgon’s head. All who beheld it were turned to stone, each in the attitude he happened to have struck. Pindar had already made the petrification of Seriphos the cycle’s byword: in Pythian 10, Perseus brings the Gorgon’s head to the islanders and turns Seriphos to stone (the ode’s phrase, in the translations consulted, makes the head a fatal gift to the whole island), and in Pythian 12 the head is the “deadly wedding-present” that repays Polydectes for Danaë’s slavery (Section V). The averted face is the detail to keep: even in vengeance, even at the moment of triumph, the hero does not look at his own weapon. The discipline of indirection holds to the end.

Then the equipment is returned and the account closed, in a passage whose tidiness is itself a statement about this hero (Apollodorus 2.4.3, paraphrased): Perseus appointed Dictys king of Seriphos — the fisherman succeeds the tyrant, the island’s moral geography made political — and gave back the sandals, the wallet, and the cap to Hermes, who restored them to the nymphs; but the Gorgon’s head he gave to Athena, and Athena inserted it in the middle of her shield. The borrowed tools go home; the trophy goes to the goddess whose craft guided the stroke, to be mounted as the aegis-device of Section IX. Apollodorus adds, and this article preserves, the dissenting note he himself appends: it is alleged by some, he says, that Medusa was beheaded for Athena’s sake, the Gorgon having dared to rival the goddess in beauty — an alternative motivation, goddess against Gorgon, that later tradition (and Ovid’s temple-story, differently shaped) would develop. The mythographer reports it as an allegation. So does this article.

Larissa: The Discus

What remains is the oracle. Perseus hastened with Danaë and Andromeda to Argos, Apollodorus says (2.4.4), in order that he might behold Acrisius — the visit, in every version, is one of reconciliation: the grandson seeks the grandfather who tried to drown him, in friendship. Acrisius, learning of his approach and dreading the oracle still, forsook Argos and fled to the Pelasgian land, to Larissa in Thessaly. There Teutamides, king of Larissa, was holding funeral games for his father, and Perseus came to compete. In Frazer’s translation, the end takes three clauses: he engaged in the pentathlon, “but in throwing the quoit he struck Acrisius on the foot and killed him instantly”. Pausanias’s Argive version (2.16.2) adds the colour the mythographer omits: Perseus, in the prime of life and proud of his invention of the quoit — the discus itself is his invention, in the local tradition — was giving displays, and Acrisius, as luck would have it, stepped unnoticed into the discus’s path. The oracle is fulfilled by accident, in sport, at a funeral, by a grandson who came in peace and a grandfather who fled into the one place in Greece where the fatal throw would be made. No one in the story wills the killing except the prophecy itself; and the tradition is unanimous that Perseus’s response was grief and shame, not triumph. He buried Acrisius outside the city, and, ashamed to return to Argos to claim the inheritance of the man who had died by his hand, went to his cousin Megapenthes, son of Proetus, at Tiryns, and exchanged kingdoms with him: Megapenthes took Argos, and Perseus took Tiryns — and fortified, in Apollodorus’s phrase, Midea and Mycenae besides. The exchange closes the brothers’ feud of Section III in the grandsons’ generation, by contract instead of violence; and it moves Perseus to the citadel from which his dynasty takes its seat, and its name in the next section.

XV. Founder and King: Mycenae, the Perseids, and Cult Traces

Perseus’s last mythic act is a foundation, and the tradition is specific about it in two registers that must be kept apart: what the myth claims, and what a visitor could be shown. The claim, in Apollodorus, is fortification — Perseus reigned at Tiryns “after fortifying also Midea and Mycenae”. In Pausanias it is foundation outright: ascending toward Argos, he writes (2.15.4, in W. H. S. Jones’s translation, paraphrased), you see on the left the ruins of Mycenae, and “the Greeks are aware that the founder of Mycenae was Perseus”. Pausanias then retails the two foundation-aetiologies he knew (2.16.3, paraphrased from Jones): Perseus founded the city on the spot where the cap — mykēs — of his scabbard fell, reading the accident as a sign; or, in the other account Pausanias says he has also heard, thirsty, he picked up a mushroom — mykēs again — from the ground and drank with joy the water that flowed from it, and named the place Mycenae for the mushroom. Both stories are folk-etymologies built on the resemblance of Mykēnai to mykēs, and Pausanias reports them as what they are — local explanations of a famous name — while also recording the rival derivation from Mycene, a daughter of Inachus named in the Odyssey. The archive’s ruling is the one Pausanias’s own practice models: the attribution of Mycenae’s founding to Perseus is a genuine and universal Greek tradition; the etymologies are ancient guesses; and the Bronze Age citadel itself — whose Lion Gate and tholos tombs Pausanias also describes, attributing its walls to the Cyclopes — is older by centuries than any historical kernel a founder-hero could represent. Mythic foundation and archaeological origin are different kinds of fact, and this hub keeps them in different sentences.

The dynasty is the foundation’s continuation. By Andromeda, Apollodorus lists (2.4.5, paraphrased from Frazer): Perses, born before the return to Greece and left with Cepheus — from him, Apollodorus says, the kings of Persia are said to descend (Section XVI takes up the claim’s history) — and, born at Mycenae, Alcaeus, Sthenelus, Heleus, Mestor, and Electryon, and a daughter, Gorgophone. These are the Perseids: Alcaeus fathers Amphitryon; Electryon fathers Alcmene; Amphitryon and Alcmene are Heracles’ mortal parents — so that Heracles is Perseus’s great-grandson, and the second great monster-slayer is the first one’s heir in the direct line. Sthenelus fathers Eurystheus, the king who will impose the labours on Heracles — the Perseid house thus supplies both the hero of the next cycle and his persecutor, uncle and great-nephew in one. The Argive king-lists that Herodotus discusses (Section XVI) run the Dorian royal houses back to Perseus as their Greek starting point: beyond Danaë, he notes, the line goes Egyptian; at Perseus it becomes, for Greek purposes, Greek. A charter hero is a hinge of exactly that kind — the point where a genealogy changes its country.

Cult Traces: What Was Actually Worshipped

Layer (b) — the cultic footing — is thinner for Perseus than for Heracles or Theseus, and this article reports it at its true weight rather than inflating it. Perseus received hero-cult in the Argolid, and the evidence is Pausanias’s itinerary. On the road from Mycenae to Argos there stood a shrine of Perseus on the left-hand side (Pausanias 2.18.1, reported as his testimony): a hero-shrine at a roadside, of the kind this hub’s Heroes and Epic article anatomises — a fixed place where the dead hero could be honoured by the community that claimed him. At Mycenae itself, Pausanias records the fountain called Perseia, the Perseus-spring (2.16.6), attaching the founder’s name to the citadel’s water — the kind of minor, durable, local attachment by which hero cult actually lived, in springs and roadside shrines rather than in Panhellenic festivals. No comparably major independent Perseus cult is attested for Athens or for Seriphos in the sources read for this article; his worship, where the record shows it, is Argive. The honest summary: Perseus’s cult was real, Argive, local, and modest. His fame was Panhellenic, but fame ran through epic, lyric, tragedy, and images — through kleos, in the terms of Section I of the Heroes article — far more than through altars. He is the clearest case in this hub of a hero whose afterlife was literary and visual first, and cultic second.

// HOW TO WEIGH A FOUNDER //

Three questions, applied above. What does the tradition claim? (Perseus founded or fortified Mycenae and seated the Perseids there.) What could be shown? (A roadside shrine, a named spring, ruins already ancient when Pausanias saw them.) What does the ground say independently? (A Bronze Age citadel, centuries older than the heroic age the Greeks imagined.) A founder-hero is a community’s title-deed to its own past; the deed is a historical fact about the community, whatever one concludes about the founder.

XVI. Herodotus Abroad: Perseus at Chemmis and the Persian Name

Twice in the Histories, Herodotus takes Perseus out of Greece, and both passages are reported in this article as what they are: Herodotus’s claims, resting on the informants he names, valuable as evidence for what a fifth-century Greek investigator could be told in Egypt and could retail about Persia — not as verified facts about Egyptian cult or Persian ethnogenesis. The hub’s Egyptology division treats the Egyptian material from the Egyptian side; this section keeps to Herodotus’s text.

Chemmis: The Temple, the Sandal, and the Games (Herodotus 2.91)

Herodotus’s Egyptian book insists that the Egyptians, as a rule, shun foreign customs entirely — and then makes a single, elaborate exception. In G. C. Macaulay’s translation of Histories 2.91, read for this article: there is a large city named Chemmis in the Theban district, near Neapolis (the city is Panopolis, modern Akhmim, in Upper Egypt; its god was Min, whom the Greeks identified with Pan), and in it a temple of Perseus son of Danaë, square in plan, with palm trees around it, a great stone gateway, and two great stone statues at the entrance; within the enclosure, a shrine housing an image of Perseus. The people of Chemmis said, Herodotus reports, that Perseus often appeared in their land and in the temple, and that a sandal worn by him, two cubits long, was sometimes found — and whenever it appeared, all Egypt prospered. The Chemmitae held, in Perseus’s honour and “after Hellenic fashion”, an athletic contest with the full programme of games, offering cattle, cloaks, and skins as prizes — a Greek festival on Egyptian soil, which Herodotus flags as the anomaly it is. Asked why Perseus appeared to them alone, they answered with a genealogy: Danaus and Lynceus were men of Chemmis who had sailed to Greece, and from them the descent ran down to Perseus; Perseus himself had come to Egypt — for the reason the Greeks also give, to fetch the Gorgon’s head from Libya — had visited Chemmis, recognised all his kinsfolk, and ordered the games himself. The commentary tradition (How and Wells, ad loc.) treats the identification of Perseus with the god of Panopolis as a Greek construction — perhaps assisted by a resemblance of names — resting on a Greek or Hellenised community at Chemmis; the organised games, the anthropomorphic apparitions, and the relic-sandal are, as that commentary notes, Greek in character through and through. What Herodotus establishes, at his strongest, is that in the fifth century BCE a city in Upper Egypt maintained a Perseus cult with a foundation-story tying it to the Argive line, and explained its own Greek-style games by the hero’s personal command. That is a remarkable datum about Greek-Egyptian cultural traffic — the world from which this hub’s alchemy wing will later emerge (see the Egyptology division’s Alchemy article for the Egyptian craft tradition in its own terms) — and it is reported here at exactly that strength.

The Persian Name: Herodotus 6.53–54 and 7.61

The second abroad-passage is really two, and the brief for this article named the first of them; both are given here, distinguished. In Histories 6.53–54, tracing the Spartan royal houses, Herodotus reports that the Greek king-lists run rightly back to Perseus son of Danaë — no mortal father being named for him, as Amphitryon is for Heracles — and that reckoned upward from Danaë the Dorian kings prove Egyptian by descent; but then, in Macaulay’s rendering of 6.54, he adds the Persian counter-account: “as the story is reported which the Persians tell, Perseus himself was an Assyrian and became a Hellene, whereas the ancestors of Perseus were not Hellenes”. The claim at 6.54 is thus about Perseus’s own nationality in Persian telling — an Assyrian who became Greek — not yet about the Persian name. The name-derivation stands at 7.61, in the catalogue of Xerxes’ army, and it is quoted here in A. D. Godley’s Loeb translation: the Persians, Herodotus says, were formerly called Cephenes by the Greeks and Artaei by themselves and their neighbours; “when Perseus son of Danae and Zeus had come to Cepheus son of Belus and married his daughter Andromeda, a son was born to him whom he called Perses, and he left him there; for Cepheus had no male offspring; it was from this Perses that the Persians took their name”. Godley’s own footnote, in the edition read, remarks drily that Herodotus is always prone to base ethnological conclusions on Greek legends and the similarity of names — as in the next chapter Medea supplies the name of the Medes. Modern philology agrees with the footnote: the Persian self-designation derives from Pārsa, the Persis region, and the Perses story is a Greek folk-etymology, built on the resemblance of Persēs to Persai and on the myth’s own geography — a Greek hero who marries into Cepheus’s eastern kingdom and leaves a son behind is a ready-made ancestor for an eastern empire. Herodotus also reports (7.150) that Xerxes, invading Greece, sent a herald to Argos invoking precisely this kinship — the Persians, the Argives were told, traced descent from Perseus through Perses — and inviting Argive neutrality on the strength of it: the folk-etymology at work as wartime diplomacy, which is the most historically concrete thing about it. Both passages, 6.54 and 7.61, are therefore carried in this archive as Herodotus’s reports of Greek and Persian claims — evidence for the fifth century’s habits of genealogical diplomacy, not for the origin of a people’s name.

// TWO PASSAGES, NOT ONE //

The Persian material is often cited loosely as “Herodotus says the Persians are named from Perseus”. Precision: the name-derivation, from the son Perses, is 7.61.3. The passage at 6.54 reports a different Persian claim — that Perseus himself was an Assyrian who became a Greek. The passage at 6.53 makes the Greek lists stop at Perseus because he has no mortal father. This article cites each for what it says.

XVII. Art and Sky: The Gorgon’s Face and the Constellation Family

Perseus’s afterlife ran through two media that need no literacy: images and the night sky. The images came first, and they are abundant beyond any other archaic hero’s. The Gorgon’s face, as Section IX set out, was everywhere — pediments, antefixes, shields, coins, cups — and the full figure of the running Gorgon, and of Perseus in flight with the bag, fill archaic and classical vase-painting: the beheading (Perseus averting his face, Athena attending), the pursuit (the sisters, winged and snake-girdled, reaching for a hero already gone), the Graeae (groping old women, the eye in transit), and Andromeda at the rock (increasingly, from the later fifth century, a study in pathos that the lost Euripidean play did much to shape). The metope from Temple C at Selinus in Sicily (c. 540 BCE, now in Palermo) shows the beheading in stone at monumental scale — Perseus, Athena beside him, cutting while looking away, Pegasus springing from the neck — within two generations of the Shield’s poem-picture of the same moment. The Corfu pediment (c. 590–580 BCE, Section IX) remains the corpus’s archaic summit: not a scene but an emblem, the Gorgon displayed with her children as a temple’s guardian. Across the whole corpus the iconographic constants are the story’s constants — frontality for the Gorgon, aversion for the hero, the bag, the cap, the wings at the ankles — and the art is therefore not illustration of the texts but an independent, parallel transmission of the myth, occasionally preserving (as with the Graeae vases and Aeschylus, Section VI) what the texts have lost.

The Constellation Family: The Tale as Star-Map

The sky received the whole cast. In the northern autumn sky, a single connected region of constellations preserves the Andromeda episode as a star-map: Perseus, the hero, shown in the traditional figures with the Gorgon’s head in his hand — its star Algol, the Ghoul, marking Medusa’s eye in the later Arabic-influenced nomenclature; Andromeda, the chained princess, her figure stretched along a line of stars as if still bound; Cassiopeia, her mother, in her distinctive W of bright stars, circumpolar and never setting from Greek latitudes — and, in the later moralising tradition, swinging upside down for half her circuit as a perpetual punishment for vanity; Cepheus, the king, a fainter house-shaped figure near the pole; Pegasus, the winged horse, known by the Great Square; and Cetus, the sea-monster, sprawling in the southern part of the region below Pisces and Aries. Modern astronomy still groups these, with Auriga, Lacerta, and Triangulum, as the Perseus family of constellations — the largest of the traditional families, and the only one that preserves a complete narrative episode, with hero, victim, parents, mount, and monster all in frame. A Greek sailor of the classical period, watching that whole quarter of the sky wheel overhead in autumn, was looking at the story’s dramatis personae set in their places: the tale had become a chart.

The star-lore has its own textual history, and it should be dated like any other layer. Aratus’s Phaenomena (third century BCE), the versified handbook of the constellations that all later antiquity learned, already treats Andromeda, Perseus, Cepheus, Cassiopeia, and Cetus as neighbouring figures with their stories attached. The systematic mythological catalogue is the Catasterismi (“Placings Among the Stars”) transmitted under the name of Eratosthenes of Cyrene (third century BCE) and surviving in an epitome of the first century BCE or CE by an unknown hand — Pseudo-Eratosthenes, in the scholarship’s careful naming. Its chapters treat the family in sequence: Cepheus (ch. 15), Cassiopeia (16), Andromeda (17), the Horse (18), Perseus (22), and Cetus (36) — Andromeda placed among the stars, the epitome says, by Athena’s favour, in memory of Perseus’s deeds, her figure still with arms outstretched as if bound (paraphrased). Hyginus’s De astronomia (Book 2) retails the same catasterisms in Latin with variants. Behind Eratosthenes stands the older Greek star-poetry of which the Hesiodic Astronomy (Section II) was the archaic exemplar: the Catasterismi cites the lost Astronomia attributed to Hesiod among its authorities for star-myths, so that the tradition that fixed Perseus in the sky consciously drew on the same Hesiodic penumbra that first fixed Medusa in the genealogy. Ptolemy’s Almagest (second century CE) canonised the forty-eight constellations, Perseus and his family among them, and the International Astronomical Union’s eighty-eight retain them all. The catasterism — the placing of a figure among the stars — is, for this cycle, the last metamorphosis and the most durable: Ovid’s Atlas turned to a mountain that men can climb; the whole family turned to stars that men still navigate by.

XVIII. Later Readings: What Perseus Has Been Made to Mean

Layer (c) — the readings laid over the tale — begins inside antiquity itself. Ovid’s Medusa is the first great re-reading, and the most consequential: by giving the Gorgon a human past — a beautiful maiden, famed for her hair, raped by Neptune in Minerva’s temple, and punished by the goddess with the serpents and the petrifying face (paraphrased from Metamorphoses 4.790–803, where Perseus himself narrates it and calls the punishment, in Ovid’s presentation, deserved) — Ovid converts an archaic monster into a wronged woman, and the beheading into the last act of her wronging. Every later sympathetic Medusa descends from this passage: the Renaissance painters’ beautiful, suffering Gorgon; the Romantic Medusa of Shelley’s poem on the Uffizi painting; and the modern feminist reclamation, from Hélène Cixous’s “The Laugh of the Medusa” (1975) onward, in which the Gorgon’s face is re-read as female power that the heroic tradition petrified because it feared it. This article records that lineage as reception — powerful, legitimate as art and as criticism, and not a recovery of the archaic myth, in which (Section VIII) Medusa was never human and never transformed. The distinction is the hub’s standing one: a reading’s value does not backdate it.

The allegorical tradition ran the other way, from the hero’s side. Late antique and medieval mythography — Fulgentius, the Vatican Mythographers, and their Renaissance heirs — read Perseus as virtue armed with divine gifts against vice, the mirrored shield as prudence or self-knowledge, the Graeae as the stages of counsel, in the euhemerising and moralising modes this archive meets throughout the Greco-Roman material. The alchemical tradition, which this hub’s second wing documents for other figures, likewise adopted the cycle’s machinery — the dangerous face that must be approached only by reflection, the volatile spirit fixed and carried in a sealed vessel, the monster’s issue transformed into the winged bearer of heaven’s fire — as a storehouse of images for processes its practitioners recognised; Perseus enters the mytho-hermetic reading of the myths that Pernety and his predecessors practised, in which the Argive cycle, like the labours of Heracles and the voyage of the Argo, is decoded as veiled laboratory doctrine. Those decodings are recorded here as what they demonstrably are — early modern and late-antique readings, evidence for the readers, not for the archaic tellers — in accordance with the rule this hub applies to the alchemical interpretation of myth wherever it appears (compare the treatment of the Atlantis tradition in the Before Kemet hub at Plato’s Atlantis: a text’s later esoteric career is a subject in its own right, and is not evidence about its origin). The structuralist and psychoanalytic readings of the twentieth century — the Gorgon’s head as the type of the terrifying female face in Freud’s short essay “Medusa’s Head” (written 1922, published 1940), the mirror-episode in Vernant’s analyses of the Gorgon as the face of death and otherness in La mort dans les yeux (1985) — belong to the same layer and are labelled with it: serious readings, by serious readers, of a story older than every framework brought to it.

What survives every reading is the story’s skeleton, and the skeleton is a piece of engineering. A danger that cannot be faced directly is faced through an image. A power too dangerous to hold is carried in a container, used sparingly, with averted eyes, and finally surrendered to the goddess of craft, who mounts it where it can guard others. A hero who begins as a castaway in a box ends as a founder whose name is on a spring, a shrine, a dynasty, a people’s claimed ancestry, and a quarter of the autumn sky. The Greeks told the story for a thousand years without needing it to mean any one of the things later ages found in it — which is not to say it meant nothing to them, but that its meaning for them is recoverable only where they left it: in the genealogy, the cult place, the pediment, the tune played at the games, and the stars. This article has tried to leave it there, labelled, and to label everything else as what it is. For the heroic institution within which Perseus’s fame and cult operated, see Heroes and Epic; for the divine order his parents and patrons belonged to, Theogony and The Olympians; for the rites by which Greeks approached powers too dangerous for direct contact, compare The Mysteries.
