THE HOMERIC EPICS: THE ILIAD AND ODYSSEY AS TEXTS

Ionia · Athens · Alexandria · c. 750–650 BCE and after

// TWO POEMS :: THREE OBJECTS :: EVENT — TEXT — INSTITUTION //

The Iliad and the Odyssey are the oldest large poems in Greek to survive entire — and the strangest objects in this archive, because each of them is three things at once: a performance that happened in voices, a text that was written, edited, and argued over for a thousand years, and an institution that taught the Greek world how to read. This article treats them as texts: how they are built, how they were made, and how the objects a modern reader buys came to exist.

I. Two Poems, Three Kinds of Object

Every other article in this hub treats a poem as a window: through the Theogony we look at the gods' generations, through the epics we look at heroes, their cults, and their cities. This article turns the glass around and looks at the window itself. Its subjects are two poems — the Iliad, 15,693 lines in twenty-four books, and the Odyssey, 12,110 lines in twenty-four books, both in dactylic hexameter, both attributed by antiquity to a singer named Homer — considered not as stories but as texts: physical and institutional objects with a manufacture, a transmission, and an editorial history longer than that of any other works in European literature. The tales themselves are told elsewhere in this hub — the war and its aftermath in The Trojan Cycle, the homecoming hero in Odysseus — and the Homeric Question is sketched, from the heroes' side, in Heroes and Epic, Sections XI–XIII, to which this article is the full companion. What follows is the textual treatment that sketch promised.

The organising distinction of the article should be stated at once, because every confusion in the two-hundred-year argument about Homer comes from collapsing it. A Homeric poem is three different kinds of object, and a fact true of one is not automatically true of the others.

1 · THE POEM AS EVENT

A performance: a singer, an audience, an occasion — a feast, a festival, a competition. In this mode the poem has no fixed wording. It exists the way a jazz standard exists, as a story and a technique, realised afresh each time. Most of the poems' prehistory, and the whole of their formulaic technique (Sections IV–VI), belongs to this mode.

2 · THE POEM AS TEXT

A written object: letters on papyrus, then parchment, then paper, then print. In this mode the poem has a wording that can be copied, miscopied, compared, edited, and argued about line by line. The fixation of the poems in writing, the Alexandrian editions, and the medieval manuscripts (Sections X–XIII) belong to this mode.

3 · THE POEM AS INSTITUTION

A social fact: the schoolbook of the Greek world, the syllabus of rhetoric, the quarry of moral examples, the text that had to be explained — by allegory when its gods embarrassed its readers. Canonisation, schooling, and the Roman answer to Homer (Sections XIV–XV) belong to this mode.

WHY THE DISTINCTION MATTERS

“Who wrote the Iliad?” has three different answers depending on the mode: a tradition of singers (event), an unknown chain of scribes and editors (text), and the Greek educational system that decided this poem, above all others, would be preserved (institution). Scholarship goes wrong when it demands one answer do the work of all three.

A note on dates, since this article will give many. The dating debate is a debate, and it is reported as one throughout. The conventional position places the monumental crystallisation of the two poems in the later eighth century BCE — the Iliad first, commonly somewhere in the range c. 750–700 BCE, the Odyssey somewhat later, with responsible estimates for its final shaping running into the early seventh century. The margins matter: linguistic arguments have placed the Iliad as early as the mid-eighth century and its fixation as late as the sixth; M. L. West argued for a single Iliad-poet working around the middle of the seventh century (Section IX). What is being dated, in any of these proposals, is a crystallisation — the moment when a fluid tradition hardened into something like our poems — not the tradition's materials, which are demonstrably older, in places centuries older, than any date proposed for the poems themselves. Herodotus, our earliest external witness, placed Homer and Hesiod “not more than four hundred years” before his own time — around 850 BCE by his arithmetic — which tells us that by the fifth century the poets were already figures of a founding past whose date had to be guessed. We are still guessing, with better instruments and the same absence of documents.

One more preliminary. The two poems were not, in antiquity, the whole of “Homer.” The name attracted attributions the way a magnet attracts filings: the poems of the Epic Cycle, the Homeric Hymns, the comic Margites, the mock-epic Battle of Frogs and Mice all travelled under it at one time or another, and ancient scholars already disputed the attributions. The Iliad and Odyssey are the two poems whose Homeric authorship no ancient critic seriously withdrew — though a small Hellenistic school, the chorizontes, the “separators”, argued they were by different poets, a position whose ghost still walks in Section IX. When this article says “the Homeric epics”, it means these two, and the question of what their shared name is worth is part of its subject.

II. The Iliad as Architecture: A Few Weeks Around a Wrath

The Iliad is not the story of the Trojan War. It is the story of a few weeks in the war's tenth year, cut out of the whole with a precision that is itself the poem's first lesson in what kind of object it is. The war's beginning — the judgement of Paris, the abduction of Helen, the gathering at Aulis — is never narrated; it is presupposed, recalled in speeches, present as the audience's prior knowledge. The war's end — the wooden horse, the sack of Troy, the deaths of Achilles and Paris — is never narrated either; it is foreshadowed, prophesied inside the poem by dying men and knowing gods, and left to other poems of the Cycle to tell. What the Iliad narrates is an episode, and it announces the episode, not the war, as its subject in its first word: mēnin, wrath.

“The wrath sing, goddess, of Peleus' son, Achilles, that destructive wrath which brought countless woes upon the Achaeans, and sent forth to Hades many valiant souls of heroes, and made them themselves spoil for dogs and every bird; thus the plan of Zeus came to fulfillment, from the time when first they parted in strife Atreus' son, king of men, and brilliant Achilles.” — HOMER, ILIAD 1.1–7, TRANS. A. T. MURRAY (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY, 1924)

The proem is a table of contents compressed into seven lines. The poem's subject is a single emotion and its consequences: Achilles, dishonoured by Agamemnon's seizure of his prize Briseis, withdraws from the fighting and asks his mother Thetis to persuade Zeus to let the Trojans win until the Achaeans learn his worth. The “plan of Zeus” that the proem names is that arrangement, and the poem is the working-out of its cost. The narrated time is short — some fifty days by the usual reckoning of the poem's internal chronology, with the great mass of the action, the battles in which the Achaeans are driven back to their ships, Patroclus enters the fight and is killed, and Achilles returns in fury to kill Hector, concentrated into a handful of days in the middle. Around that narrow spine the poem builds the widest possible frame: because the fighting pauses, advances, and wheels across the whole plain, and because gods, councils, catalogues, embassies, games, and funerals fill the intervals, the episode comes to contain a whole world — a society at war, viewed entire, inside a story about one man's anger.

The Shape in Books

The twenty-four books (on the division itself, an editorial artefact, see Section XII) fall into movements a reader can hold in the mind. Books 1–9: the quarrel and the withdrawal; the great Catalogue of Ships in Book 2, which musters the whole Achaean host and the Trojan allies as if the war were beginning, though it is ending; the duel of Paris and Menelaus and the meeting of Hector and Andromache (Books 3 and 6), which show what the war is about and what it costs at home; the long first day of battle; and in Book 9 the embassy to Achilles — Odysseus, Phoenix, and Ajax pleading with him to accept Agamemnon's gifts and return — which he refuses, in the poem's most famous speech, on grounds that go beyond the quarrel: no wealth, he says, is worth his life. Books 11–17: the second great battle, in which the Achaean champions fall wounded one by one, the wall is breached, the ships themselves are fired, and Patroclus — Achilles' companion, wearing Achilles' armour — turns the battle, kills Sarpedon, and is killed by Hector with Apollo's help. Books 18–22: Achilles, grief displacing wrath at the Achaeans with wrath at Hector, receives new armour from Hephaestus — its shield, described at length in Book 18, carries an image of the whole world of peace and war — is reconciled with Agamemnon, and drives the Trojans to their walls, where Hector, alone outside them, faces him and dies. Books 23–24: the funeral games for Patroclus, in which the Achaean host reassembles as a society of contests and prizes; and Book 24, in which Priam, guided by Hermes, crosses the battlefield by night to ransom his son's body, and the two enemies — the killer and the father — weep together in Achilles' tent. The poem ends not with a death but with a funeral: Hector's burial, and a truce.

THE WRATH, IN FOUR PHASES

Wrath at Agamemnon (Books 1–9) · wrath displaced and refused, while the cost mounts (9–16) · wrath at Hector, grief turned outward (18–22) · wrath spent, in the ransom scene where Achilles recognises his own father in Priam (24). The emotion announced in line 1 is the plot's engine and its subject of study.

RING COMPOSITION, AT THE LARGEST SCALE

Book 1 opens with a father, Chryses, coming to ransom his child and being refused; Book 24 closes with a father, Priam, coming to ransom his child and being received. The frame is exact, and it is the Unitarians' oldest exhibit (Section VIII): whoever or whatever made this poem, its first and last movements were designed against each other.

WHAT THE POEM EXCLUDES

No judgement of Paris, no horse, no sack, no death of Achilles — the war's famous furniture is present only as memory and prophecy. The exclusions are the design: the poem takes the war's whole meaning and declines to narrate the war.

THE CATALOGUE OF SHIPS (2.484–877)

A muster-list of contingents, commanders, and ship-counts for both armies — the poem's most overtly traditional, least narrative block, and for the Analysts a prime candidate for an originally independent piece. Its geography has been argued over for a century as evidence for the tradition's Bronze Age memories; its presence, whatever its origin, shows the monumental poem absorbing a list the way a cathedral absorbs a Roman wall.

The architecture matters for the textual question because it is the strongest evidence on one side of it. A poem of 15,693 lines whose first and last books mirror each other, whose central refusal (Book 9) is prepared in Book 1 and answered in Book 19, whose hero's death is everywhere prophesied and nowhere shown, does not look like an accidental accumulation. How that design is compatible with the poem's equally demonstrable making out of inherited, formulaic materials is the problem Sections V–IX work through.

III. The Odyssey as Architecture: A Return Organised Around Recognition

The Odyssey is built on the opposite plan. Where the Iliad narrows ten years of war into weeks around one emotion, the Odyssey takes a ten-year homecoming — the nostos of Odysseus from Troy to Ithaca — and narrates only its final phase directly, some forty days of story-time, folding the ten years of wandering into a tale the hero tells about himself, in the middle of the poem, to hosts who have asked who he is. And where the Iliad's first word names its subject, the Odyssey's first word names its man — and then withholds his name for a hundred and fifty lines more of story, as if the poem were already practising its governing device: this is a poem about identity, and identity in it is something disclosed, tested, and recognised, never simply stated.

“Tell me, O Muse, of the man of many devices, who wandered full many ways after he had sacked the sacred citadel of Troy. Many were the men whose cities he saw and whose mind he learned, aye, and many the woes he suffered in his heart upon the sea, seeking to win his own life and the return of his comrades. Yet even so he saved not his comrades, though he desired it sore, for through their own blind folly they perished — fools, who devoured the kine of Helios Hyperion; but he took from them the day of their returning. Of these things, goddess, daughter of Zeus, beginning where thou wilt, tell thou even unto us.” — HOMER, ODYSSEY 1.1–10, TRANS. A. T. MURRAY (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY, 1919)

The epithet Murray renders “of many devices” is polytropos — of many turns, many ways, many selves — and modern translators' divergent renderings of that one word (Lattimore's “man of many ways”, Fagles's “man of twists and turns”, Emily Wilson's “complicated man”, in their well-known versions) are a first, miniature lesson in this article's closing theme: even the most stable text in the world arrives in a modern language only through an editor's and a translator's judgement. The proem, like the Iliad's, is a programme. The hero is defined by two activities — seeing cities and learning minds, suffering and seeking to save — and by one failure, the loss of every companion, which the poem assigns at once to the companions' own folly. The whole poem is the expansion of those ten lines.

The Shape in Books

The Odyssey runs two plots in parallel and joins them late. Books 1–4, the Telemachy: Ithaca without its king — Penelope besieged by suitors who feast in the hall and court her on the assumption that Odysseus is dead; Telemachus, the son who was an infant when his father sailed, sent by Athena on a journey to Pylos and Sparta to seek news, a journey that is also his education into his father's world; the suitors plot to ambush him on his return. Odysseus does not appear. Books 5–8: the hero at last, released from Calypso's island by divine order, shipwrecked by Poseidon, washed ashore among the Phaeacians, where — still unnamed — he hears a singer sing of Troy, and weeps, and is asked who he is. Books 9–12, the Apologoi: Odysseus answers with the wanderings told in the first person — the Lotus-Eaters, the Cyclops, Circe, the descent to the dead, the Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, the cattle of the Sun — the poem's most famous episodes, all of them a tale within the tale, whose teller is the tradition's most accomplished liar, a fact the poem itself underlines. Books 13–24: Ithaca, in disguise. Athena lands him home; the second half of the poem is a long, tightening sequence of recognitions, each one earned and each one different: by the swineherd Eumaeus he is not recognised but sheltered; by Telemachus he reveals himself, by design; by his old dog Argus he is recognised instantly, and the dog dies; by the nurse Eurycleia he is recognised by the scar on his thigh, and silenced; by Penelope he is tested — the trial of the bow, the slaughter of the suitors, and then her own counter-test, the secret of the immovable bed built around a living olive tree, by which she makes him prove that he is himself. The poem ends with the recognition by his father Laertes and Athena's imposition of peace on the suitors' kin.

The wanderings in the hero's own voice: Cyclops, Circe, the dead, the Sirens, the cattle of the Sun. Ten years folded into four books of first-person tale — told, the poem reminds us, by a man famed for tales that are not true.

BOOKS 13–24

ITHACA: THE RECOGNITIONS

Disguise, testing, and disclosure, person by person: Telemachus, Argus, Eurycleia, the bow contest, the suitors' slaughter, Penelope's test of the bed, Laertes. The poem's second half answers its first word: the man of many turns is known, at last, by the one turn that cannot be counterfeited.

Set the two architectures side by side and the pairing that antiquity already felt becomes visible. The Iliad is centripetal: everything is drawn into the gravity of one emotion in one place. The Odyssey is centrifugal and then homebound: its hero is scattered across the whole sea of the world and gathered back, person by person, token by token. One poem is organised around a wrath; the other around a recognition — around the question of how you know that the stranger at the door is who he claims to be. That second question, the reader will notice, is also the question this article asks about the poems themselves. The Odyssey is, among other things, a poem about identifying a much-travelled, much-retold, disguised original. Its scholars have been doing to it, for two thousand years, exactly what Penelope does to Odysseus.

IV. The Language No One Spoke: The Epic Kunstsprache

Open either poem at any line and you are reading a language that was never anyone's mother tongue. Homeric Greek — the epic dialect — is an artificial poetic language, a Kunstsprache (the German term, “art-language”, is standard in the scholarship), whose base is the Ionic Greek of the eastern Aegean, but which preserves, layered into it, forms from Aeolic Greek, archaisms older than either dialect, and occasional traces reaching back toward the Greek of the Bronze Age. No city spoke it. No audience, even in the eighth century BCE, used it to buy bread. It existed for one purpose: to sing hexameters in. Deborah Steiner's Cambridge introduction to the Odyssey, consulted for this article, puts the point in the form this archive adopts: no Greek ever spoke the language that Homer and his characters use; the poet composed in an artificial idiom, an amalgam reflecting the successive stages through which the oral tradition had passed, from the late Bronze Age down to about 700 BCE.

The amalgam was not a defect or an accident. It did three kinds of work at once, and Steiner's summary, paraphrased here, names all three. First, metrical utility: a singer composing in the strict dactylic hexameter needs, for many ideas, more than one word-shape — a long form and a short form, a form beginning with a vowel and one beginning with a consonant — and the mixed dialect supplied doubles and triples for the same notion, each fitting a different slot in the line. Where the spoken language had levelled two forms into one, the epic language kept both, because the metre needed both. Second, Panhellenic portability: a language belonging to no one city could be sung in every city without sounding foreign or parochial; the Kunstsprache is the linguistic form of the Panhellenism described in Heroes and Epic — epic shedding local peculiarity as it travels. Third, elevation: the very artificiality and archaic colouring of the diction set the heroic world at a distance from the audience's everyday speech, the way a liturgical language sets worship at a distance from conversation. The heroes sound older than the listeners because, linguistically, they are.

The Ghost Letter: Digamma

The single most telling fossil in the language is a letter that is not there. Early Greek possessed a consonant, the w-sound written with the letter digamma (ϝ), which Ionic Greek lost early and which the transmitted text of Homer nowhere writes. Yet in a great many lines the metre behaves as if the sound were still present: a vowel that should shorten before a following vowel does not shorten, a syllable that should be light scans heavy, precisely where a word — wanax, “lord”; woinos, “wine”; wergon, “work” — once began with the lost consonant. The eighteenth-century scholar Richard Bentley first argued systematically that the missing letter explained the anomalies, and the argument has held: the tradition preserved, in the rhythm of its phrases, the ghost of a sound its singers' own dialect had ceased to pronounce. A written composition by a single eighth-century author, inventing freely, would have no reason to respect a consonant nobody could hear. A tradition of phrases handed down, fitted to the metre, and conserved because they worked, has every reason. The digamma is the Kunstsprache's certificate of age: proof, inside the language itself, that parts of the poems' verbal fabric are older — much older — than the poems.

This is why the language is evidence and not merely colour. A literary dialect that no one spoke, stocked with doublets no single speaker would maintain, haunted by a letter no copyist wrote, is not the sort of thing an author invents at a desk. It is the sort of thing a profession accumulates over centuries — a singers' technology, refined generation by generation for a single task. The next section takes that technology apart at the level of the individual line.

V. Formula and Type-Scene: How the Line Is Built

“Oral-formulaic” is the most used and least pictured term in Homeric studies. This section pictures it. It names the two mechanisms — the formula at the scale of the phrase, and the type-scene (Lord's theme) at the scale of the episode — and shows each at work in passages any reader can check.

The Formula, and the Epithet System

Milman Parry's definition of the formula, as standardly cited from his published work and repeated in every account since, is: a group of words which is regularly employed under the same metrical conditions to express a given essential idea. The clearest instance is the noun-epithet combination. Achilles is “swift-footed” even when he is sitting in his tent refusing to move — which, for nine books, he is. Odysseus is “much-enduring” and “of many devices”; Dawn is “rosy-fingered” at every arrival; the sea is “wine-dark”; Hera is “white-armed” and “ox-eyed” in different metrical positions. A reader meeting these phrases for the first time in a modern novel would call them repetitive. Parry's demonstration — the argument of his Paris theses of 1928 — was that they form a system, and that the system has two properties no individual stylist's habits explain.

The first property is economy, or thrift. For a given character, in a given grammatical case, in a given metrical slot in the line, the tradition normally keeps exactly one formula. If a singer needs Achilles as the subject of a sentence in the last part of the hexameter, one phrase is ready; if he needs him in another case or another slot, a different single phrase is ready for that. The stock is a fitted toolkit: near-complete coverage of the common needs, with almost no duplication. No poet composing in writing rations himself this way — a writer can pause, revise, and vary. A tradition of singers composing at performance speed must ration itself this way, because a choice between two equivalent phrases is a hesitation the metre does not forgive. The second property is extension: the system is too large, too consistent, and too interlocking to be one man's stock. It covers gods, heroes, ships, sea, earth, speaking, dying, arming, feasting — the whole furniture of the heroic world — in thousands of phrases, many of them demonstrably old (the digamma's ghost haunts the formulae above all). Parry's conclusion, in its careful form, was not that Homer was unoriginal. It was that the medium is traditional: Homer composes the way a jazz musician improvises — in a language of ready phrases whose readiness is the precondition of the performance's speed, and whose traditional character says nothing, by itself, about the quality of what a master does with it.

The Type-Scene: Arming, Feasting, Sacrifice

What the formula is to the phrase, the type-scene is to the episode: a recurrent action with a fixed sequence of elements, which the singer expands or contracts to fit the occasion. Walter Arend's 1933 study of the “typical scenes” in Homer catalogued them — arrivals, departures, sacrifices, feasts, dreams, assemblies, armings — and Lord, developing the same observation from the South Slavic material, called the unit a theme. The textbook case is the arming scene. Four times in the Iliad a hero arms in full, and the sequence does not vary: greaves first, then the corselet, then sword, shield, helmet, and last the spear — Paris in Book 3 (3.328–338), Agamemnon in Book 11 (11.15–55), Patroclus in Book 16 (16.130–154), Achilles in Book 19 (19.364–424). What varies is the weight the scene is given, and the variation is the meaning. Paris arms briefly, in the basic sequence — he is dressing for a duel he will not finish. Agamemnon's arming swells to three times the length, each piece described, because the king is arming for his day of glory. Patroclus arms in Achilles' armour through the standard sequence — and then reaches the spear, the Pelian ash spear that only Achilles can wield, and leaves it behind: the one deviation from the pattern, placed exactly where a practised audience would feel it, tells them the impersonation will fail before a blow is struck. Achilles' own arming, finally, is interrupted and expanded by the description of the new shield itself, the poem's largest single object. The type-scene is thus not a shortcut that flattens the poetry. It is an instrument of emphasis: the pattern creates the expectation, and the singer plays meaning off the pattern — exactly as a congregation hears the one changed word in a familiar liturgy.

The feast works the same way at smaller scale. Again and again the poems mark a meal with the same sequence — the animal slaughtered and butchered, the thigh-pieces wrapped in fat and burned for the gods with wine poured over them, the rest roasted on spits, the company eating “until desire for food and drink is satisfied”, in the recurring formula — and the sequence does social work each time: a feast is how this world ratifies an arrival, seals a truce, honours a guest, buries a dead man. When the suitors feast in Odysseus' hall, the same elements recur in a house where every obligation of the feast — the host's right, the guest's duty — is inverted, and the audience that knows the pattern knows the verdict before the bow is strung. A lay reader who has grasped these two mechanisms — the formula filling the line, the type-scene filling the episode, both inherited, both variable under a master's hand — has grasped what “oral-formulaic” means concretely. The next question is how anyone knows that living singers actually work this way. That knowledge has a date, a place, and a recording machine.

VI. Parry and Lord: The Singers of Tales

The argument so far rests on inference from the texts. Milman Parry (1902–1935) refused to leave it there. Having demonstrated, in his 1928 Paris theses on the traditional epithet and in the papers that followed, that Homeric diction is a formulaic system of a scale and thrift implying generations of traditional singers, he set out to test the implication against a living tradition: if the theory was right, somewhere in the modern world singers should still be composing long heroic songs in performance, by formula and theme, without writing — and their practice should illuminate Homer's at every point where the texts are ambiguous. The place he chose, following the earlier work of the Slovene scholar Matija Murko, was the mountain country of Yugoslavia — Bosnia, Herzegovina, Montenegro, and the Sanjak — where Muslim and Christian singers called guslari, accompanying themselves on the one-stringed gusle, still sang heroic songs of the frontier wars in coffee houses and at weddings and Ramadan evenings.

In the field seasons of 1933–1935, Parry, with his assistant Albert B. Lord (1912–1991) and the local singer and fixer Nikola Vujnović, recorded hundreds of songs on aluminium discs with a specially built recording machine, and took down many more by dictation — building the collection now held at Harvard as the Milman Parry Collection of Oral Literature. The fieldwork was designed as an experiment with controls. The same singer was recorded singing the same song on different occasions, and the versions compared. Singers were asked whether they repeated a song “word for word, without changing anything” — the claim oral singers characteristically make — and the recordings showed what that claim means in practice: the story is held constant with great fidelity, the themes recur in their order, the formulae recur by the thousand, and the wording, the length, and the degree of ornament vary with the audience, the occasion, and the singer's form on the day. A song sung in fifteen minutes for a restless coffee house could be sung in an hour for a patient patron. The song is the same song. It is never the same text. That distinction — identity at the level of story and technique, multiformity at the level of wording — is the fieldwork's central finding, and everything in the modern Homeric settlement is built on it.

// AVDO MEĐEDOVIĆ //

The tradition's master, a farmer and butcher from Bijelo Polje in Montenegro, was recorded and dictated to in 1935. His Wedding of Smailagić Meho runs to over 12,000 lines — Odyssey length — and his dictated songs are longer and more ornate than his sung ones, because dictation gave him time. Lord treated Avdo as the fieldwork's “Homer”: proof that a single non-literate singer, working in a mature tradition, can sustain monumental scale.

Parry died in December 1935, in a hotel accident in Los Angeles, at thirty-three, with the great comparative book unwritten. Lord returned to Yugoslavia in 1935 and again in the 1950s, re-recording singers Parry had recorded — some of them singing the same songs twenty years on, to the same claim of word-for-word fidelity and the same measurable variation — and in 1960 published The Singer of Tales, the book that set out the theory whole: how a singer learns (first listening and absorbing, then applying the technique to songs of his own, then singing before a critical audience); what the formula and the theme are and how they interlock; how composition-in-performance actually proceeds, neither memorisation nor free improvisation but fluency in a specialised grammar of song; and what follows for Homer. The account in this section follows Lord's book at summary level — the archive's standard practice for a long scholarly work, stated here as the brief for this article requires: the summary is Lord's argument as the subsequent literature standardly reports it, checked against that literature, not a substitute for the book.

What followed for Homer, in Lord's own application, was specific. The Iliad and the Odyssey as we have them are, each, the work of a single monumental singer — the tradition's technique in one master's hands — and they became texts by dictation: the singer, in the era when writing had newly become available, performed his song slowly, over days, to a scribe, and the unnatural slowness of dictation — as with Avdo's dictated songs, longer and richer than his sung ones — is itself part of the explanation of the poems' extraordinary scale and finish. The hypothesis is elegant, it fits the fieldwork's one directly comparable case, and it is not provable. Section VII sorts, carefully, what the Parry–Lord work settles from what it leaves open — because the sorting is where the current state of the question actually lives.

VII. What the Theory Settles, and What It Does Not

Oral-formulaic theory is sometimes popularised as having “solved the Homeric Question”. It solved a different and more fundamental thing: it showed that the Question, as posed for two centuries, contained a false premise. But the theory's own claims divide sharply into those the evidence compels and those it merely permits, and an honest archive keeps the line bright.

SETTLED: THE MEDIUM IS TRADITIONAL

The formulaic system, its economy and extension, the Kunstsprache with its fossil layers and its ghost digamma, and the type-scene structure jointly establish that the poems' technique is that of an oral tradition of composition in performance, developed over generations. This is as close to consensus as Homeric scholarship produces, and no serious account now dispenses with it.

SETTLED: LENGTH IS NO OBJECTION

Before the fieldwork, the poems' scale was itself an argument for writing: surely no one composes 15,000 lines without a desk. Avdo Međedović's 12,000-line songs end that argument. Monumental oral composition is a documented human capability, given a mature tradition and a master singer.

NOT SETTLED: THE MOMENT OF WRITING

The theory describes how such poems are composed. It is silent — the South Slavic evidence is silent — about how these poems became these texts. Dictation by a master singer, gradual crystallisation through performance and transcription, and literate composition in the traditional style are all compatible with everything Parry and Lord proved. Choosing among them requires other evidence, and the other evidence underdetermines the choice (Section IX).

NOT SETTLED: ONE SINGER OR A TRADITION'S CONVERGENCE

Lord held that each poem is one singer's dictated performance. The architecture of the poems (Section II) is compatible with that. It is also compatible with a tradition converging, over generations of Panhellenic re-performance, toward monumental fixed forms. The formulaic evidence cannot distinguish a great singer from a great tradition's gradual self-editing, because a great singer is the tradition, concentrated.

The Dictation Hypothesis and the Scribe Question

The dictation hypothesis deserves its own paragraph because it is the point where the poem-as-event and the poem-as-text meet, and because its details are often blurred. In Lord's version: at some moment — Lord himself placed it in the eighth century BCE, when the alphabet was new in Greece — a singer of the first rank was induced to dictate his two great songs (or, in the version that assigns the poems to different singers, each song by its own singer) to a scribe writing them down. The scribe is the hypothesis's shadowy second figure, and the questions multiply around him. Who employed him, and why — a patron, a city, the singer himself? How long did the work take, and in how many sessions? Did the scribe merely record, or did he prompt, arrange, and in any degree edit? Did the singer ever see, or could he read, what was written? On none of these does any evidence survive, and the hypothesis's defenders and critics divide over how much weight an elegant mechanism with no attested instance can bear. Two things can be said fairly. First, dictated oral texts are not a fantasy: the Parry collection itself contains them by the hundred, Avdo's among them, and their characteristics — expansion, ornament, a finish beyond sung performance — match suggestively the characteristics of the Homeric texts. Second, the date proposed for the dictation has proved mobile in a way that should discipline confidence: Lord argued for the eighth century; Richard Janko's linguistic case supports an eighth-century dictation; Minna Skafte Jensen argued, from modern fieldwork of her own, that the dictation happened in Athens in 522 BCE, to a team of scribes, under the Peisistratids — a proposal Gregory Nagy's review of her book sets out and contests. A mechanism that can be dated anywhere across two and a half centuries is a real possibility, not yet a fact. This article therefore treats dictation as the leading candidate mechanism of fixation, and says so in those words.

// ANALOGY IS NOT IDENTITY //

The South Slavic comparison demonstrates a possibility, powerfully: long heroic epic can be composed orally, by formulaic technique, without writing. It does not by itself prove that the Iliad was so composed — the Greek case rests on the Greek evidence, the formulaic system and the language, with Yugoslavia as the control that shows what such evidence means. John Miles Foley, who developed oral-tradition studies after Lord and coined the term oral-derived text for a text whose features descend from oral tradition whatever its exact writing-down, insisted on exactly this discipline: comparison must always be tempered by contrast. The gusle songs are decasyllabic, not hexametric; the Balkan tradition's history, patronage, and relation to writing differ from Archaic Greece's at every point. The analogy tells us what kind of thing Homer could be. Only Homer can tell us what Homer is.

VIII. The Homeric Question Before Parry: Wolf, Analysts, Unitarians

The modern argument begins, by convention and by merit, in 1795, with the Prolegomena ad Homerum of Friedrich August Wolf (1759–1824), professor at Halle. The Prolegomena was meant as the introduction to a critical edition of Homer that never appeared; it became, instead, the founding document of the Homeric Question and, in the judgement of many historians of scholarship, of classical philology as a modern discipline. Wolf's argument, as the modern summaries consulted for this article set it out, ran in steps. First, a historical claim about writing: in Homer's age, alphabetic writing was either unknown in Greece or too new and too cumbersome to serve for works of this scale — and the poems themselves, across nearly 28,000 lines, show a world in which nothing is read, no letter is written, no treaty is inscribed, no tomb bears an inscription; everything is arranged for hearing, and the only repositories of the past are memory and song, which is why the poems keep invoking the Muses, Memory's daughters. Second, a claim about transmission: poems composed without writing could only have been preserved by the professional reciters, the rhapsodes, whose performances over centuries would inevitably alter, expand, and rearrange them. Third, the conclusion: the Iliad and Odyssey we read cannot be the integral works of one poet as he composed them; they must have originated as shorter songs, composed by Homer and sung by his successors, and reached something like their present form only when they were collected and edited — an event Wolf associated, following the ancient tradition, with sixth-century Athens (Section X). The unity of the poems, on this account, is real but secondary: an editor's unity, imposed on materials of different ages.

Wolf did not invent every element of this — the abbé d'Aubignac had argued in the seventeenth century that “Homer” never existed and the poems were a compilation, and Giambattista Vico had made Homer the personified voice of the Greek people — but Wolf gave the position scholarly form, evidence, and institutional force, and German scholarship spent the next century working out its consequences. The consequences split into the two camps whose names still organise the history, already introduced in Heroes and Epic and given here in their full, pre-Parry form.

The Analysts took Wolf's programme literally and applied the scalpel. Karl Lachmann, in his studies of the Iliad (1837–1841), argued that the poem resolves into a set of originally independent lays — he counted first sixteen, then eighteen — each a complete short song, stitched together by a compiler whose seams show in contradictions of fact, doublets (the same event told twice in incompatible forms), linguistic strata, and episodes that can be lifted out without disturbing what surrounds them. The Analyst method produced real discoveries: the Embassy to Achilles in Book 9 does use dual grammatical forms for what the narrative presents as three envoys, a genuine fossil of some earlier state of the story; the Doloneia, Book 10's night raid, is absent from some ancient accounts of the poem's structure and reads, in language and matter, unlike its surroundings; whole speeches recur, word for word, in contexts of different emotional logic. Analysis taught scholarship to see the poems as stratified, and that vision has never been unlearned.

The Unitarians answered from the other end: from the poems as wholes. The Iliad's wrath-plot is announced in its first line and discharged, through preparation and reversal, in its last book; the frame of Chryses and Priam (Section II) is exact; foreshadowing runs forward across thousands of lines and is honoured when it lands. A compilation of lays, they argued, does not produce architecture of this order; only a single designing intelligence does. The inconsistencies the Analysts catalogued are the ordinary tolerances of a very long work composed by a man, not a committee — and in any case, the Unitarians asked, where are these supposed lays attested, except in the Analysts' own dissections? By the early twentieth century the two camps had reached positions the other could not refute, because both were describing the same poems with a shared false assumption: that composition meant writing, so that the only alternatives were one author with a pen or many authors with scissors. Parry's work (Sections V–VI) dissolved the assumption, and with it the Question's original form. What replaced it is the subject of the next section.

IX. The Modern Settlement-in-Tension: Nagy's Evolution and West's Poet

The present state of the question is not a consensus but a structured disagreement — a settlement-in-tension — in which the parties share the oral-traditional premise and divide over what crystallised, when, and by whose agency. Its two poles are named positions, held by named scholars, on stated evidence, and this article presents them as such. Between and around them stand mediating positions — dictation theories of various dates, accounts of literate poets working in the traditional style — but the poles define the field, because they disagree about the most basic thing an account can say: whether there ever was an original text of the Iliad at all.

Nagy: The Evolutionary Model

Gregory Nagy, of Harvard and the Center for Hellenic Studies, has developed across a series of books from Poetry as Performance (1996) onward what he calls an evolutionary model of the Homeric tradition. Its core claim, in Nagy's own summary in his article on the “Panathenaic bottleneck” (Classical Philology, 2001, consulted for this article), is that the Iliad and Odyssey were relatively more multiform in earlier phases and relatively less so in later phases — that is, the tradition moved gradually from fluidity toward rigidity, and there was never a single moment at which an original text came into being and from which all later variation is corruption. Nagy divides the process into five periods of progressively less fluidity: (1) a most fluid period, with no written texts, from the early second millennium BCE to the middle of the eighth century; (2) a more formative, Panhellenic period, still without written texts, from the mid-eighth to the mid-sixth century, in which performance before audiences drawn from many cities progressively shed local variants; (3) a definitive period centred on Athens, from the mid-sixth to the later fourth century, in which texts exist as transcripts — records of performances — beginning with the Peisistratid-era regulation of Homeric performance; (4) a standardising period, from the later fourth to the mid-second century, with texts as scripts, shaping performance; and (5) a most rigid period, from the mid-second century onward, with the text as scripture, beginning with the completion of Aristarchus' editorial work — the point after which the eccentric papyri disappear (Section XI). The narrowing from period 2 to period 3 Nagy calls the Panathenaic bottleneck: the passage of a Panhellenic tradition through a single city's festival regime, which drastically reduced the range of authorised multiforms. On this model, “Homer” names the tradition's own myth of its origin — the culture hero of the guild of Homeridae, the “sons of Homer” who claimed descent from him — as much as any historical singer, and the proper object of a modern edition is not an original but the tradition's documented history, multiforms included: the premise of the Homer Multitext project, which edits the poems from the medieval witnesses and the papyri together rather than reconstructing a single archetype behind them.

West: One Poet, Writing, Over a Lifetime

M. L. West (1937–2015), in The Making of the Iliad: Disquisition and Analytical Commentary (Oxford University Press, 2011) — the capstone of the editorial career that produced the standard modern critical texts of both poems — argued the opposite pole, and argued it as a deliberate revival of analysis in a Unitarian cause. West's five claims, as the reviews and summaries of the book consulted for this article state them, are: the Iliad is almost entirely the work of one poet; he was not the poet of the Odyssey; he was not called Homer; he composed with the aid of writing, over a long period — West placed his activity around the middle of the seventh century BCE, roughly c. 680–640; and he did not compose the poem in one continuous progression from Book 1 to Book 24. The mechanism West proposes will be recognised at once: it is the Analysts' stratigraphy, reassigned to a single biography. The anomalies, discontinuities, and inconsistencies that Lachmann distributed among many lays, West distributes among the stages of one poet's working life: the poet progressively amplified his poem, not only by adding at the end but by making insertions into parts already composed, revising his conception as he went, so that the seams in the text are the traces of one author's decades of reworking — visible, West argued in his preface (paraphrased here), precisely because his hypothesis is founded on the poem's anomalies, which the oral-sequential hypothesis must ignore or deny. The poet, on West's account, knew the traditional technique from inside — his materials are the tradition's materials — but he used writing the way later authors use it: as a memory outside the head, permitting a scale of cross-reference, insertion, and revision that performance alone does not explain.

// HOW TO HOLD THE TWO POLES //

Notice what Nagy and West share, because it is most of the field: the tradition is oral and formulaic; the materials are stratified and of many ages; the poems' fixation is a process with an Athenian chapter and an Alexandrian conclusion. They divide on agency and on ontology. For West, there was a desk, a man, and a manuscript that grew; the editor's task is to recover that manuscript's final state, and his editions print a single reconstructed text. For Nagy, there was a tradition narrowing through a bottleneck; the editor's task is to document the narrowing, and a single reconstructed “original” is a category error. Both positions are held, in print, by scholars who know all of the other's evidence. That is what a settlement-in-tension looks like from inside, and it is the honest answer to “who wrote the Iliad?” at the level the evidence currently allows.

A word on dating, to close the section, since the two poles carry the debate's margins with them. The conventional crystallisation date — later eighth century, Iliad before Odyssey — rests chiefly on the language: Richard Janko's statistical study of the epic dialect's development measured the rate at which archaic forms are replaced by later ones across the early hexameter corpus and placed the Iliad earliest in the sequence, the Odyssey after it, Hesiod after that — the relative order is more widely accepted than any absolute figures hung on it. West's mid-seventh-century poet stands at the late margin of the responsible range; dictation proposals run from the eighth century (Lord, Janko) to 522 BCE (Jensen); Nagy's model dissolves the question of a single date into a five-period process. The margins given in Section I — crystallisation conventionally later-eighth-century, with responsible positions extending a century in either direction depending on what exactly is being dated — are the margins the field itself works with, and this article invents no precision beyond them.

X. Writing and Fixation I: Rhapsodes, the Panathenaic Rule, and the Peisistratean Legend

Between the poems' composition and their editing stands a profession. The rhapsodes (Greek rhapsōidoi — plausibly “song-stitchers”, from rhaptein, to stitch, and ōidē, song, though an ancient rival derivation connected the name with the rhabdos, the staff a reciter carried) were the professional performers of Homer in the centuries after the poems' formation. They were not, in the developed sense, composers: a rhapsode performed the Iliad and Odyssey as given works, in competition, at festivals, holding the staff, often in distinctive dress, rewarded with prizes and fame — Plato's little dialogue Ion preserves the type in its title character, a prize-winning rhapsode from Ephesus who can perform Homer magnificently and, Socrates establishes, understands almost nothing of what he performs. The rhapsodes are the transmission mechanism Wolf's argument requires: a guild of memory and voice, carrying two enormous poems across two centuries in which writing existed but was not yet the poems' primary home. Whether their performances preserved a fixed wording learned by heart or re-composed within narrowing limits is precisely the point on which the modern models divide (Section IX) — the rhapsode stands exactly at the hinge between poem-as-event and poem-as-text.

The Panathenaic Rule

The one institutional act in this story that most scholars accept as historical, in some form, is Athenian. At the Panathenaia — the great festival of Athena, celebrated with special splendour every fourth year — the Homeric poems were performed by rhapsodes in competition, and a rule governed the performance: the rhapsodes had to perform the poems in sequence, each taking up where his predecessor left off, so that the whole poem was delivered, in order, as a single civic act. The rule is attested in the pseudo-Platonic Hipparchus (228b), which credits Hipparchus, son of the tyrant Peisistratus, with being the first to bring the poems of Homer to Attica and with compelling the rhapsodes at the Panathenaea to go through them in relay, in their proper order, “as they still do”; a rival tradition, preserved in Diogenes Laertius, credits the lawgiver Solon, a generation earlier, with a law requiring rhapsodes to perform Homer by relay from a written text. The attributions conflict — Solon or Hipparchus, early or late sixth century — and the conflict itself is data: Athens remembered that someone in its archaic past had regulated Homeric performance, and competed internally over whose ancestor it was. The regulation, whoever issued it, is the kind of act that fixes texts without anyone writing an edition: a rule that the poem be performed whole, in order, in public, on a recurring civic occasion, creates a powerful selective pressure toward a standard sequence and a standard extent. It is the historical core inside Nagy's Panathenaic bottleneck.

The Peisistratean Recension: A Legend About a Real Process

Around that core, antiquity grew a larger story: the so-called Peisistratean recension. In its full form — the form in which Wolf inherited it and Bentley repeated it — the tyrant Peisistratus (in power, with interruptions, c. 561–527 BCE) appointed a commission of scholars who collected the scattered songs of Homer from across the Greek world, where they survived only in fragments and confusion, and arranged them into the two poems as we have them. The sources for the story are late and mutually inconsistent, and their lateness is the first fact about them. Cicero (De oratore 3.137) says that Peisistratus is said to have arranged the books of Homer, previously disordered, in their present order — a Roman of the first century BCE reporting a tradition already five centuries old. Pausanias, in the second century CE, repeats the collection story. Later sources still expand it with the commission, the named editors, and the detail. No sixth-, fifth-, or fourth-century source describes an edition; the fourth-century Megarian historian Dieuchidas already knew a version of the story as a claim to be argued about, in connection with a specific suspicion — that Solon or Peisistratus had interpolated lines into the Catalogue of Ships to support Athens' claim to Salamis.

Modern scholarship's verdict, following J. A. Davison's classic examination of the evidence in 1955, is disciplined suspicion, and this article adopts Davison's terms. A sixth-century Athenian edition that created our text is not demonstrated: the sources are too late, too inconsistent, and too interested — the story does too much work for Athenian prestige to be read as a neutral record. But the legend is not therefore worthless. Legends of this shape — a great collector, a scattered inheritance, an authorised arrangement — are how cultures narrate a real process whose actual mechanism was too gradual and too anonymous to remember. The real process is visible behind the story: Athens in the sixth century did become the poems' most important performance home; the Panathenaic rule did impose sequence and wholeness; Athenian copies of Homer did acquire authority in the centuries that followed (the Attic overlay in the language, Section IV, is the fossil of that chapter). The recension is therefore presented in this archive as what the evidence supports: a legend about a real process — the Athenian regulation and gradual standardisation of the Homeric performance tradition — mistaken by later ages, and by Wolf, for a single editorial event.

XI. Writing and Fixation II: Quotations, Papyri, and the Wild Texts

The legend says the text was fixed by an act. The surviving physical evidence tells a slower and more interesting story, in three kinds of witness: quotations, papyri, and the shape of the tradition itself.

Quotations. From the sixth century BCE onward, Greek authors quote Homer, and their quotations are the earliest datable samples of the text. Xenophanes of Colophon, in the later sixth century, already attacks Homer and Hesiod for attributing to the gods “all that is reproach and blame among men” (paraphrase of his famous fragment) — an attack that presupposes a Homer familiar enough to be worth resisting, in something like the poems' received contents. Simonides quotes a line of the Iliad — the generations of leaves and men, Iliad 6.146 — and attributes it to “the man of Chios”: the earliest surviving quotation of Homer by name of place, and recognisably our line. Herodotus quotes and discusses Homeric passages, weighs the authorship of a Cycle poem against internal evidence, and treats the poems as documents. By the fourth century, Plato and Aristotle quote Homer constantly, at length, and — the significant point — in a text whose wording, where it can be checked, is already close to ours, though not identical to it: ancient quotations regularly preserve small variants, a line more or less, a phrase otherwise turned. Quotation evidence thus shows a text broadly stable in substance from the Classical period and locally fluid in wording — exactly the profile of a tradition in Nagy's periods 3 and 4, standardising but not yet scripture.

Papyri. Egypt's dry soil preserves what the rest of the ancient world lost: actual copies. Homer is by far the most attested author in the papyrus record — hundreds of fragments, from school exercises to handsome book-rolls — which is itself the papyrological form of Section XIV's institutional fact. The earliest substantial witnesses, the Ptolemaic papyri of the third and second centuries BCE, are the famous problem. Many of them are, in the term of art, “wild” or “eccentric”: they contain plus-verses — lines absent from our text, often formulaic expansions of lines present in it — omit lines we have, and vary in wording far beyond the range of the medieval tradition. Graeme Bird's study of these papyri, Multitextuality in the Homeric Iliad, in the account of it given in his Center for Hellenic Studies interview consulted for this article, makes the interpretive point this archive adopts: the “wildness” is wild only by the standard of the later vulgate; read in the light of oral poetics, the Ptolemaic papyri are witnesses to a state of the tradition in which multiformity — alternative lines, expansions, compressions — was still a living feature of what “the text” was. They are not corrupt copies of our poem. They are copies of a poem that had not finished becoming ours.

Then, across the second century BCE, the wildness stops. Papyri after about 150 BCE conform, in extent and wording, to the text the medieval manuscripts transmit — the vulgate. The coincidence of dates is the single most consequential fact in the poems' textual history: the eccentric papyri disappear at just the moment when Aristarchus of Samothrace completes his editorial work at Alexandria. Two explanations of the coincidence have been argued for a century, and the argument is not closed. On the older view, Aristarchus' edition caused the standardisation: his text drove out rivals, as a great edition can. On the revisionist view — pressed by Arthur Ludwich in the 1890s from the observation that the vulgate often does not follow Aristarchus' own preferred readings — the vulgate was already the common text before Alexandria, the Alexandrians worked on it without displacing it, and the disappearance of the wild papyri reflects the general standardisation of the book trade rather than one scholar's authority. The truth the evidence best supports lies between: a common text of great antiquity, an Alexandrian scholarship that pruned, marked, and argued without ever fully imposing its own choices, and a tradition that emerged from the Hellenistic centuries narrower than it entered them. What the Alexandrians actually did, concretely, is recoverable in unusual detail, and it is the next section's subject.

// THE EVIDENCE OF ABSENCE //

No copy of Homer survives from the centuries in which the poems were composed and first fixed — none from the eighth, seventh, or sixth centuries BCE, and only scraps from the fifth. Everything asserted in Sections X–XI about that period is inference from later witnesses: the language, the quotations, the legends, the papyri's behaviour. The inference is disciplined and, in its main lines, strong. But the archive notes the shape of the record honestly: for the decisive centuries, we have the consequences of the text's history, not the text.

XII. The Alexandrian Edition: Zenodotus, Aristophanes, Aristarchus

In the third century BCE, Homer acquired editors — the first in the poems' history whose work can be described rather than inferred. The setting was Alexandria: the Museum and its great Library, founded under the first Ptolemies, which collected Greek literature as an instrument of state and, in collecting it, invented literary scholarship as a profession. The Library bought, seized, and copied texts from across the Greek world — including the so-called city editions of Homer, copies associated with Massalia, Chios, Argos, Cyprus, Sinope, and Crete, whose variant readings the later scholia still cite — and its first heads of the Library took Homer as their first problem. What they produced was not a single edition but a practice: diorthōsis, recension — the comparison of copies, the judgement of variants, the marking of suspect lines — together with its companion genre, the hypomnēma, the running commentary in which the judgements were explained. Modern textual criticism descends from this room.

The Three Editors

Zenodotus of Ephesus (c. 325–c. 260 BCE), the Library's first head and tutor to the royal children, produced the first known recension of both poems, around 274 BCE by the traditional dating, together with a glossary of Homeric words. Zenodotus edited boldly, in the manner of a man founding a discipline without precedents to restrain him: he omitted lines he judged spurious, transposed passages, and substituted readings of his own where the transmitted text offended his sense of the sense. His most durable invention was a mark: the obelos, a short horizontal stroke placed in the margin beside a line he suspected — the first critical sign, and the ancestor of every apparatus since. To mark a line as suspect without deleting it was a new idea in the world: the text and the judgement upon the text, kept visibly distinct on the same page.

Aristophanes of Byzantium (c. 257–c. 180 BCE), head of the Library a generation later and by ancient consent one of the greatest philologists of antiquity, extended the work in every direction. He produced his own recension of Homer; he is credited with refining the system of critical signs, adding the asteriskos for lines repeated elsewhere; and the scholarly tradition assigns to him, or to his circle, the system of accentuation and punctuation by which Greek texts were read for the rest of antiquity — together with, in the usual account, the division of each poem into twenty-four books, lettered by the twenty-four letters of the Greek alphabet, which every modern edition still uses. The twenty-four-book shape of the poems (Sections II–III) is thus, on the standard account, an Alexandrian editorial artefact laid over the poems' own movements — a shelving system that became, by two millennia of use, indistinguishable from the architecture.

Aristarchus of Samothrace (c. 216–c. 144 BCE), Aristophanes' pupil and the sixth head of the Library, brought the practice to its ancient perfection. His recension of the Iliad — he worked on the Odyssey as well — was accompanied by commentaries of a scale antiquity had not seen, and by a developed system of signs, each with a defined function, explained in the previous section's sources and tabulated below. His characteristic operation was athetesis: marking a line with the obelos as spurious — composed, in his judgement, not by Homer but by the tradition — while leaving it in the text, judgement recorded and text preserved, for the reader to weigh. His critical principle, as the scholia report it, was to clarify Homer from Homer: to judge a passage by the poet's own usage elsewhere, a principle whose circularity he managed by an unmatched knowledge of the whole corpus. Aristarchus athetised hundreds of lines; the medieval vulgate kept nearly all of them, obelised in the great manuscripts but present — a fact (Section XI) that measures the real, limited power of even the greatest ancient editor over a tradition larger than any library.

Aristarchus' own works are lost. What survives is their reflection in the scholia — the marginal commentaries in the medieval manuscripts — through a chain that can be named: in the first century BCE and the century following, four scholars excerpted and preserved the Alexandrian inheritance — Aristonicus, on the critical signs; Didymus, on Aristarchus' recension and the variant readings of the city and individual editions; Herodian, on prosody; Nicanor, on punctuation. Their works were combined, probably in the fifth or sixth century CE, into the compilation modern scholarship calls the Viermännerkommentar, the Four-Man Commentary, and it is excerpts of that compilation, copied into the margins of a tenth-century manuscript, that carry Aristarchus' judgements to us. The manuscript is the subject of the next section; it is the most important single object in this article.

XIII. Venetus A and the Medieval Transmission

In the Biblioteca Marciana in Venice sits a large parchment codex of the tenth century CE, catalogued as Marcianus Graecus Z. 454 (now 822) and known to every Homerist as the Venetus A. It contains the complete Iliad in 327 folios — a few leaves are later replacements — written in a fine minuscule hand, and around the text, in the wide margins the scribe left for the purpose, it carries three further cargoes: the critical signs of the Alexandrian system, placed against the lines they judge; the A scholia, the great body of marginal commentary derived, through the Four-Man Commentary, from Aristarchus and his successors; and, at the front, excerpts of the handbook of Proclus preserving a Life of Homer and summaries of the lost poems of the Epic Cycle — for which this manuscript is, to this day, our most important source. At the end of each book, a subscription in the manuscript names its scholarly sources: the signs of Aristonicus and Didymus' work on Aristarchus' recension are “placed alongside”, with material from Herodian and Nicanor — the scribe copying out, book by book, the table of contents of a thousand years of scholarship. The Venetus A is not merely a copy of the Iliad. It is a copy of the Iliad together with the ancient world's judgement upon it, text and commentary in one object: the Alexandrian page, preserved by Byzantine hands.

Its later history is the history of Homeric scholarship in miniature. The codex reached Venice among the manuscripts Cardinal Bessarion, the Byzantine scholar who became a prince of the Roman church, gave to the Republic in 1468 — part of the great transfer of Greek learning westward as the Byzantine world contracted. There it slept, famous and unread, until 1788, when the French scholar Jean-Baptiste Gaspard d'Ansse de Villoison published its scholia in a magnificent edition. The effect was electric, and it closes a circle this article opened in Section VIII: it was the Venetus A — its signs, its scholia, its visible proof that the ancients themselves had doubted, marked, and argued over hundreds of Homeric lines — that stood behind Wolf's Prolegomena seven years later. The Homeric Question in its modern form was born from a manuscript: from the discovery that the text had always had an apparatus. The codex has since been published in full facsimile (1901) and digitised to the highest standard by the Homer Multitext project, whose editors work from its images — a tenth-century object, made to carry a second-century scholar's judgements on an eighth-century poem, now consulted on screens. The transmission chain has not ended; it has changed medium again.

Around the Venetus A stands the rest of the medieval tradition, and its shape differs instructively between the poems. The Iliad survives in over two hundred medieval manuscripts, many with scholia of one family or another — the bT scholia, a second great commentary tradition of more exegetical character, and the D scholia, the elementary glossaries and mythological notes that served the schoolroom (Section XIV). The tradition is rich enough that editors can compare independent branches and reason about their common ancestors. The Odyssey's tradition is thinner and later: fewer manuscripts, sparser scholia, and a text in places demonstrably more disturbed — one consequence of a simple institutional fact, that the Iliad was always the more taught, more copied, more central poem. Both poems passed, in the ninth and tenth centuries, through the great Byzantine transliteration — the re-copying of the ancient book stock from uncial into the new minuscule hand, the bottleneck through which nearly all surviving Greek literature passed, and at which works not chosen for re-copying were lost for good. Homer was chosen first and copied most: the schoolbook's privilege (Section XIV) is written into the manuscript record itself. The medieval tradition, for all its variants and its handful of suspected interpolations — editors still debate individual lines, and one famous Odyssey passage, the continuation of the poem past the reunion in Book 23, was already marked by Aristarchus' successors as the point where the poem properly ends — delivers a text whose overall stability, across a thousand years of hand-copying, is the empirical foundation for Section XVI's closing claim.

XIV. Homer the Schoolbook: Canonisation, Education, and Allegoresis

Why did these two poems, among the dozens of epics the Greek tradition produced, survive entire — while the Cypria, the Aethiopis, the Little Iliad, and the rest of the Cycle survive in summaries and scraps? Part of the answer is quality, as antiquity already judged: Aristotle's Poetics singles out the Iliad and Odyssey for their unity of action against the Cycle's episodic sprawl. But the larger part of the answer is institutional, and it is the third of this article's three objects: the poems survived because they were taught. From the Classical period onward, Homer stood at the centre of Greek education — paideia — at every level. A boy learned his letters from Homer; the papyri preserve the school exercises, the copied lines, the glossed passages, in numbers no other author approaches. The student of rhetoric learned invention, arrangement, and style from Homeric speeches; the orators quote Homer as an authority on everything from seamanship to kingship; the moralist mined the poems for examples of every virtue and vice. To be educated, for a thousand years of Greek and then Greco-Roman civilisation, was in large part to have Homer by heart — and a text that every schoolboy copies is a text that does not die.

“Whenever you meet with any of the eulogists of Homer declaring that he has been the educator of Hellas, and that he is profitable for education and for the ordering of human things, and that you should take him up again and again and get to know him and regulate your whole life according to him, we may love and honour those who say these things… and we are ready to acknowledge that Homer is the greatest of poets and first of tragedy writers.” — PLATO, REPUBLIC 10.606e–607a, TRANS. BENJAMIN JOWETT

Plato's sentence — quoted here from Jowett's translation, consulted for this article — is the institution's certificate and its indictment in one. Socrates reports the eulogists' claim in order to refuse its consequence: even granting that Homer is the educator of Hellas, the greatest of poets, his imitative poetry must still be excluded from the just city, because what it teaches is false and its pleasures corrupt. The argument only makes sense against the fact it concedes: by the fourth century BCE, Homer was the educator of Hellas, the curriculum against which a philosopher had to define himself. Plato's own dialogues are saturated with Homer — quoted, parodied, argued with on every third page — and the tradition that Plato expelled the poets has always had to add that he expelled them as one expels a reigning sovereign, not a stranger. Xenophanes' attack two centuries earlier (Section XI) makes the same point from the other side: one does not bother to denounce the theology of a text nobody is teaching the children.

Allegoresis: Reading Through the Poem

An institution that teaches a text for a thousand years must answer for everything in it, including the parts that embarrass — and Homer's gods, who lie, steal, commit adultery, and brawl on Olympus, embarrassed philosophical readers from the sixth century BCE onward. The answer the tradition developed was allegoresis: the interpretation of the poem as allēgoria, a saying of one thing by means of another, in which the scandalous surface conceals a true, physical or moral sense. The first named practitioner is Theagenes of Rhegium, in the later sixth century BCE — the earliest allegorist, in the judgement Porphyry reports and modern accounts standardly repeat — who defended the battle of the gods in Iliad 20 as a conflict not of persons but of elements and qualities: Apollo against Poseidon is fire against water, Athena against Ares is wisdom against folly. The method never left the poems again. Stoic readers found their physics and their ethics encoded in Homer; Neoplatonists found their metaphysics, and Odysseus' wanderings became the soul's journey through the material world toward its home; Porphyry's essay on the Cave of the Nymphs in Odyssey 13 is the method's surviving masterpiece. Allegoresis is treated as a subject in its own right in this hub's Alchemical Allegory article, where the same instrument, inherited by the alchemical tradition, is turned on myth wholesale — the reader is directed there for the full account, and for Pernety's mytho-hermetic key. What belongs to this article is the institutional point: allegoresis is what a culture does with a text it cannot put down and cannot take literally. It is the surest evidence that Homer had ceased to be a poem among poems. Homer had become the text through which educated Greeks learned to read everything else — the medium of interpretation itself, applied for two millennia to scripture, to nature, and to the alchemists' myths, by readers trained first on him.

// THE CYCLE'S FATE, BY CONTRAST //

The Epic Cycle's poems were read, summarised by Proclus, and quoted — but they were not the schoolbook, and when the Byzantine transliteration came, they were not recopied in bulk. They survive as summaries in the Venetus A and fragments in quotation. The difference between the Cycle's fate and the Iliad's is the difference between a text a culture enjoys and a text a culture teaches. Canonisation is not a judgement delivered once; it is a copying decision renewed by every generation of teachers.

XV. Rome Answers: Livius Andronicus and Virgil

The institution crossed languages exactly as it had been built: through the schoolroom. Latin literature begins, by the ancient reckoning, with a Homer translation made for teaching. Livius Andronicus (c. 284–c. 204 BCE), a Greek from Tarentum brought to Rome as a prisoner of war, freed, and employed as a tutor, rendered the Odyssey into Latin Saturnian verse — the Odusia — around the middle of the third century BCE, in the years after his first staged play of 240 BCE conventionally opens Roman literary history. The translation romanised as it went: Odysseus became Ulixes, the Muse became the Italian Camena, Kronos became Saturnus — the Greek poem naturalised into Rome's own gods and names. Fewer than fifty lines survive, quoted by later grammarians; Cicero thought little of them as poetry, and Horace remembered being beaten through the Odusia at school by his teacher Orbilius — which is precisely the point. For two centuries, Roman boys met Homer first in Livius' Saturnians. The first work of Latin literature is a schoolbook translation of the Greek schoolbook: the institution reproducing itself in a new language.

Rome's second answer was not translation but rivalry. Virgil (70–19 BCE) built the Aeneid — 9,896 lines, unfinished at his death — as a deliberate Roman counterpart to the two poems together: its first half, Aeneas' wanderings from Troy to Italy, answers the Odyssey; its second half, the war for Latium, answers the Iliad; and the whole re-founds the Homeric world on Roman destiny, with Aeneas carrying out of burning Troy the household gods from which Rome will come. Virgil's relation to Homer is the most studied act of imitation in Western literature — line by line, scene by scene, the Latin poem quotes, inverts, and corrects the Greek, expecting a reader who knows both. That expectation is itself the institutional fact: by the first century BCE, the educated Roman reader was bilingual in the classroom sense, formed on Homer in Greek and ready to measure Rome's national epic against him. The Aeneid then took Homer's place in the Latin schoolroom for the next fifteen hundred years, and the pair of them — the Greek original and the Roman answer — became the twin pillars of the European curriculum. What began as two singers' songs in Ionia ends this phase of its history as the syllabus of two civilisations.

XVI. What the Modern Reader Holds: The Critical Edition

A reader who buys the Iliad today — in Greek, or in the translations of Lattimore, Fagles, or Wilson that this hub recommends — holds an object whose nature this article can now state exactly. It is a critical edition: a text constructed by an editor — for the Greek, in the standard modern editions, by editors such as T. W. Allen, Helmut van Thiel, and M. L. West, whose Teubner Iliad (1998–2000) prints a text argued line by line from the whole tradition — out of the medieval manuscripts, the papyri, the ancient quotations, and the scholia, under rules of evidence that descend from Alexandria. Beneath the text, in small print, stands the apparatus criticus: the record of the variants — the readings the editor rejected, the lines Zenodotus omitted or Aristarchus athetised, the places where the manuscripts divide and judgement must choose. The apparatus is not scaffolding to be removed. It is the honest part of the book: the editor's confession, at every disputed line, that the text above it is a decision.

Two things about that object are true at once, and the article's closing duty is to hold them together without letting either swallow the other. The first is stability, and it is astonishing. Between the vulgate that emerges from the second century BCE and the text in a modern edition, the differences amount to a small fraction of the poems' 27,803 lines: disputed readings, a few hundred athetised lines retained or bracketed, local variants of wording. A line chosen at random from a modern Iliad is, with overwhelming probability, the line a reader in Roman Alexandria read, in the form in which the tradition had carried it since the Classical period at the latest. Few human artefacts of anything like this age can say as much; the poems crossed two millennia of hand-copying with their fabric essentially intact, because at every stage an institution — festival, library, school — was paying for their accurate preservation.

The second truth is reconstruction, and it is irreducible. Behind the vulgate, the trail runs out into multiformity: wild papyri, a performance tradition whose wording was never single, a Kunstsprache layered over centuries, a composition process — dictation, crystallisation, a writing poet's lifetime of revision — on which the field's two greatest living traditions of scholarship have not agreed. There is no autograph at the end of the chain, and on Nagy's model there never was one: not a lost original but a category error, the demand for a first text of a thing that was, for most of its life, not a text at all but a way of singing. The modern reader's Iliad is therefore best described in the terms this article began with. As an institution, it is the most successful text in the ancient world. As a text, it is astonishingly stable and irreducibly reconstructed, both, in the same volume, on the same page — the Greek above, the apparatus below, like the Venetus A's text and scholia in modern dress. And as an event, it is the one thing it has always been: a voice asking a goddess to sing a wrath, and a man of many turns — waiting, still, for the next reader to recognise him.

// HOW TO READ HOMER AFTER THIS ARTICLE //

Choose a translation that keeps the lineation and the epithets — Lattimore for closeness to the Greek line, Fagles for momentum in performance, Wilson (the Odyssey, 2017; the Iliad, 2023) for a line-for-line contemporary English — and read the repeated phrases as features, not faults: when Achilles is “swift-footed” in his tent, you are hearing the tradition's grammar, not the poet nodding. Then, once, look at a page of a critical edition and read the apparatus under a famous passage. The poem does not shrink. It acquires a depth of field: twenty-seven centuries of singers, scribes, schoolmasters, and editors, all holding the same lines up to the light.
