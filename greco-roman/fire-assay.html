I. Why Testing Exists: Value You Cannot See

This article belongs to the Foundations cluster of the Alchemy wing, alongside the hub's treatments of Aristotle and matter theory and the four elements. Those articles treat what Greek thinkers believed metals are. This one treats the prior, more urgent question that every buyer, banker, temple treasurer, and king had to answer before theory mattered at all: is this piece of metal what it claims to be? A gold stater, a silver tetradrachm, a votive crown, and an ingot fresh from the smelter are all, in the end, promises. The metal's value lies inside it, where no eye can reach. Testing — dokimasia in Greek, the word that also names the scrutiny of a magistrate before he takes office — is the set of procedures by which the ancient world forced the inside of a piece of metal to give evidence about itself.

The pressure that built the procedures was fraud, and fraud was not an aberration in the system. It was a standing condition of it. Gold and silver are valuable precisely because they are scarce, soft, fusible, and — the decisive property — mixable. Gold accepts silver and copper into itself in any proportion and stays handsome. Silver accepts copper and lead and stays bright. A plated core wears a skin of the real thing and passes every test of the eye. From the moment Lydia struck the first coins in the seventh century BCE, every handler of precious metal worked in an environment where some fraction of what passed through his hands was lying to him, and where the profit on a successful lie was the difference between the face value of the piece and the value of the cheap metal inside it. The tests in this article are the counter-technology, developed over roughly a thousand years, and they are best read as an arms race: each procedure answers a fraud, and each fraud that survives is a map of the procedure's blind spot.

The Greek language preserves the whole story in one word. Basanos (βάσανος) is, first, the touchstone itself: the dark stone on which gold is rubbed so that its streak can be judged. From the stone the word spreads outward — to a test or trial of genuineness in general, to an examination under torture (the stone's interrogation of the metal transferred to the interrogation of a witness), and in poetry to any ordeal that reveals what a person is made of. Bacchylides, writing in the fifth century BCE, already uses the figure in paraphrase: as the Lydian stone tries gold, so truth tries the excellent man. Plato, in the Gorgias (486d, in paraphrase), has Callicles wish for just such a stone for souls: if his soul were gold, he says, he would be delighted to find one of the stones with which gold is tested, and to have it confirm that his soul had been well tended. The metaphor only works because the procedure was familiar, quick, and trusted. Nobody builds a moral philosophy on an instrument his audience has never seen used.

DOKIMAZEIN — TO TEST, TO APPROVE

The verb dokimazein means to put to the proof and, on proof passed, to approve. Athens used the same word-family for the scrutiny of incoming officials (dokimasia), for the vetting of new citizens, and for the assaying of coin. The dokimastēs of Section IX is literally “the Approver”: a man whose verdict turned a lump of stamped silver into money that had to be accepted.

KIBDĒLOS — THE COUNTERFEIT

The standing Greek word for debased or forged coin, and by extension for anything spurious or untrustworthy in a person. The coinage law of 375/4 BCE (Section IX) uses it as a legal category: a coin that is kibdēlon is cut in half on the spot and forfeited to a goddess. Fraud was common enough to need a noun, a law, and a salaried official.

Reconstruction. Ancient texts rarely give a complete bench procedure; they were written for men who already knew the work. Where this article assembles a full sequence of operations from an ancient hint plus the later assay literature and modern replication, the assembled steps are labelled reconstruction in the text. What an ancient author states, what archaeology shows, and what a modern assayer supplies are three different kinds of evidence, and they are not interchangeable. The article keeps them apart — which is, in the end, what an assay is.

The article's route is the route of a piece of suspect metal through a well-run ancient city. First the quick, non-destructive screen: the touchstone (Sections II–III). Then the quick destructive-adjacent screens: fire, breath, and the hot shovel (Section IV). Then the heavy procedures of the refinery, which consume a sample in order to tell the truth about the whole: cupellation (Section V) and parting (Sections VI–VII). Then the one famous test that is not a workshop procedure at all but a piece of natural philosophy — Archimedes and the crown — examined as rigorously as the workshops examined metal (Section VIII). Then the state steps in: Athens institutionalises testing in law, with a public tester, set hours, set penalties, and a chisel (Sections IX–X). And finally the loop closes where this hub began, in the workshops of Roman Egypt, whose recipe books — the Leyden and Stockholm papyri — write the whole tradition down as bench instructions, tests included (Section XI). The reader who wants the workshop world in its own right should read this article beside The Workshop Root: Bolos of Mendes and the Physika kai Mystika, where the recipe tradition that the papyri inherit is treated in full.

II. The Anchor Text: Theophrastus, On Stones §§4, 45–47

The earliest surviving technical account of precious-metal testing in Greek is three short sections of a lecture treatise: Theophrastus of Eresus (c. 371–c. 287 BCE), Aristotle's successor as head of the Lyceum, in his On Stones (Peri lithōn, De lapidibus), written toward the end of the fourth century BCE. The treatise — in form closer to lecture notes than to a finished book, as both of its modern editions note — is the earliest scientific work on minerals to survive, and its testing passages (§§45–47 in the standard numbering, prepared for by §4) are the anchor text for everything in Sections III and IV of this article. They repay slow reading, because Theophrastus is not merely reporting a trick. He is doing something recognisably philosophical with it: asking how the stone knows.

§4 — The Stone Enters the Classification

Theophrastus classifies stones first by their ordinary differences — colour, hardness, smoothness, density — and then by their powers (dynameis): what a stone can do to other things, or suffer from them. In Caley and Richards's translation, the powers include stones that colour water, stones that petrify what is placed in them, stones with the power of attraction, and — in the sentence this article exists to unpack — stones that, as he puts it, “can test gold and silver, such as the stone called the Heraclean and the one called the Lydian”. Two cautions belong to that sentence. First, the pairing is slightly loose: the Heraclean stone is elsewhere in the treatise the lodestone, the magnetic stone whose power is attraction, and the commentators — Eichholz's commentary discusses the passage at length — have long noted that Theophrastus here runs the attracting stones and the testing stone together in one breath. The testing stone proper is the Lydian stone. Second, the classification itself is the point: for Theophrastus, testing gold is a natural power of a kind of stone, on the same list as magnetism. Assaying begins, in the surviving literature, as natural history.

§45 — Two Ways of Knowing: Fire and Friction

Section 45 opens the dedicated discussion, and it opens with a puzzle Theophrastus immediately dissolves. The stone that tests gold seems, he says in paraphrase, to have the same power as fire, since fire tests gold too — and some people are puzzled that two such different things should do the same work. They should not be, he answers, because the two do not do the same work in the same way. In Caley and Richards's wording: “Fire works by changing and altering the colors, and the stone works by friction” — and the stone, he goes on in paraphrase, seems to have the power of picking out, of discriminating, the essential nature of each metal rubbed on it. The distinction is exact, and this article is organised around it. Fire-testing (Section IV) is a reaction test: heat the metal and read what the heat does to its surface. Stone-testing (Section III) is a sampling test: abrade a trace of the metal onto a standard surface and read the trace. Theophrastus, in three sentences, has already separated the two great families of quick assay that the next two thousand years would elaborate.

§46 — The Improved Stone and the Ladder of Small Weights

Section 46 is the most technically dense passage on testing to survive from the classical Greek world, and it deserves to be followed clause by clause, in paraphrase. A much better stone, Theophrastus reports, has now been found than the one used before. The improvement is specified in three stages:

STAGE ONE — PURIFIED GOLD

The older stone, in paraphrase of §46, detected purified gold: it could tell refined gold from what was not gold. A yes-or-no instrument.

STAGE TWO — TERNARY ALLOYS

The new stone also detects gold and silver alloyed with copper: it works on the three-metal mixtures of real commerce, not only on the refiner's pure product.

STAGE THREE — QUANTITY PER COIN

It shows, in Theophrastus's phrase as usually rendered, how much is mixed in each stater. The verdict is no longer genuine or false but a proportion, read off against a ladder of very small weights.

The ladder is the passage's famous crux, and it must be handled carefully. In paraphrase: indications are obtained from the smallest possible weight; the smallest unit is the krithē (a barleycorn, as a weight), then the kollybos (a tiny coin and weight), then the quarter-obol, then the half-obol — and from these the precise proportion is determined. On the Attic standard an obol weighed about 0.72 grams, so the ladder's upper rungs are roughly a third and a sixth of a gram, and its lowest rungs are fractions of those. What the ladder is, physically, Theophrastus does not say: he does not describe a set of comparison needles, and any account that pictures him handling a modern assayer's needle-case is importing later equipment into his text. What the passage establishes — and it is enough — is that by his lifetime the streak test was being read quantitatively, against graded standards fine enough to argue about fractions of an obol in a stater. Eichholz's commentary treats the passage as evidence for comparison against standard alloys; Caley, himself a chemist, discusses in his commentary how the later, fully documented needle method (Section III) achieves exactly the discrimination Theophrastus claims, and where its real limits lie. The honest summary is: the text proves graded comparison existed; the surviving text does not describe the grading hardware.

// THE CLAIM AND ITS LIMITS //

Theophrastus's §46 claims a great deal for the improved stone — discrimination down to the smallest weights in the ladder. Pliny, three and a half centuries later, repeats the tradition in superlatives (Section III). Modern assayers who have worked the touchstone method under controlled conditions find it genuinely discriminating in trained hands — the later literature commonly credits it with about a carat (one twenty-fourth part) on gold under good conditions — but not infallible, and not independent of the operator. Read the ancient claims as the testimony of men impressed by a real skill, stated without error bars. The skill was real. The error bars are this article's addition, and they are labelled as such wherever they appear.

§47 — Where the Stones Come From, and Why One Face Is Better

Section 47 grounds the instrument in a place and a material, in paraphrase. All such stones are found in the river Tmolos — the mountain stream of Lydia, in the Sardis region, the same Tmolus whose slopes fed the gold-bearing Pactolus (Section VI). They are smooth by nature, like pebbles, but flat rather than round, and in size about twice the largest pebble. Then comes the detail that proves Theophrastus is reporting workshop practice and not book-learning: the upper surface of the pebble, the face that has lain toward the sun, tests better than the lower face. His explanation, in paraphrase, is physical and testable: the upper surface is drier, and moisture prevents the stone from picking out the metal; in hot weather, too, the stone tests worse, because it gives out moisture and the metal slips. An instrument whose performance varies with which face is up, with damp, and with the weather is not a philosopher's invention. It is a bench tool, described by someone who had watched it misbehave. Section III builds the full procedure on this foundation — and keeps Theophrastus's failure modes in it, where they belong.

One last framing point. Theophrastus wrote around 315–300 BCE. Bacchylides had used the Lydian stone as a poetic commonplace a century and a half earlier; the Sardis refinery (Section VI) had been rubbing gold on touchstones two and a half centuries before that. Theophrastus is therefore not an inventor reporting a novelty. He is a systematiser reporting a mature craft — mature enough to have already produced an improved instrument, a quantitative reading, and a literature of complaints about damp stones. The Greek world's testing tradition is older than its earliest surviving description of it, and every date in this article should be read with that lag in mind.

III. The Touchstone at Work: Drawing and Reading the Streak

The touchstone test is the most durable assay ever devised. It is attested in Lydia by the sixth century BCE, described by Theophrastus in the fourth, repeated by Pliny in the first century CE, presupposed by the recipe papyri in the fourth century CE (Section XI), and still sold today, in essentially its ancient form, as a jeweller's kit: a black stone, a set of graded needles, and a trained eye. This section gives the procedure in full — first what the stone is, then the operation step by step, then who used it and where, then how it fails. Steps that the ancient sources state are marked as stated; the connective bench detail that the ancient sources omit and the later assay literature supplies is marked reconstruction, per the rule set out in Section I.

The Stone Itself

The ideal touchstone, as the tradition from Theophrastus to the modern manuals describes it, must satisfy four requirements at once, and the geology follows from them. It must be hard — harder than gold and silver, so that the metal, not the stone, is what wears away in the rubbing. It must be fine-grained and very slightly abrasive, with a matte, even texture that takes a clean streak the way fine sandpaper takes a mark, without gouging or polishing. It must be black, or nearly so, because the evidence is a film of metal a few thousandths of a millimetre thick, and gold, silver, and copper can only be told apart by colour if the background swallows all reflected light. And it must be chemically inert, indifferent to the metal, to handling, and to heat. The rocks that meet the specification are dense, black, siliceous stones: the material the mineralogical tradition calls basanite or Lydian stone — a velvety-black, flinty jasper-like rock, in modern terms a fine chert or lydite — with dense basalt and black slate as serviceable seconds. Pliny, in Rackham's translation of Natural History 33.126, gives the stone's dossier in paraphrase: the touchstone (Latin coticula) was formerly, on Theophrastus's authority, found only in the river Tmolus, but in Pliny's day was found in many places; some call it the Heraclian stone, others the Lydian; the pieces are of moderate size, not exceeding about four inches long by two broad; and the face that has lain toward the sun is better than the face that lay on the ground — Theophrastus §47, faithfully transmitted. The name Lydian is itself the provenance record: the stone is named for the kingdom that invented coinage, as if the map remembered what the texts forget to say — that the test and the coin were born in the same place, of the same need.

The Procedure, Step by Step

Step 1 — Prepare the stone (partly reconstruction). The working face must be clean, dry, and free of old streaks. Theophrastus §47 states the governing facts: use the drier, sun-facing surface; expect worse performance from a damp stone and in damp, hot weather, when the stone sweats and the metal slips instead of biting. The later bench practice (reconstruction) adds the maintenance the texts assume: old streaks are scoured off — pumice and water in the later manuals — the face is wiped dry, and the stone is kept out of the tester's sweating hand as much as the work allows. A greasy or wet stone gives a pale, broken streak, and a pale streak reads as poor metal: the commonest false fail in the method is a dirty instrument, not a bad coin.

Step 2 — Draw the streak (stated in substance). Take the piece to be tested — coin, ingot, ornament, or a droplet of metal melted down for the purpose, as at Sardis (Section VI) — and rub a corner or edge of it firmly across the stone in one steady stroke, with even pressure, so as to lay down a continuous band of metal. Pliny's image, in Rackham's translation, is that the experts use the stone “like a file”, taking a scraping from the piece with it. The later manuals (reconstruction) standardise the mark — a streak on the order of a centimetre or two long and a few millimetres wide is the working size in the modern literature — and insist on what any practitioner learns in a day: pressure and speed must match between the unknown streak and the comparison streaks, because a hard rub lays down more metal and a richer colour than a light one, and the reading is a comparison, not a measurement of the streak alone. Only a few milligrams of metal are transferred. The piece is, for practical purposes, undamaged — which is why this test, alone among the accurate ones, could be applied to a finished coin or a temple crown without destroying the thing tested. That single property explains its thousand-year career.

Step 3 — Draw the comparison streaks (the principle stated by Theophrastus §46; the hardware reconstruction). Beside the unknown streak, rub streaks from standards of known composition — in the fully documented later practice, the touch-needles: slim rods or star-mounted points, each tipped with an alloy of certified fineness. A working needle set is a ladder. For gold, the later sets run in carats (twenty-fourths): needles alloyed with silver, needles alloyed with copper, and needles alloyed with both, because a gold–silver alloy and a gold–copper alloy of the same fineness lay down different colours — silver pales the streak toward a greenish white, copper warms it toward red — so a one-dimensional ladder cannot identify a three-metal mixture. The tester picks the needle whose fresh streak looks nearest the unknown, lays it down alongside, and then brackets: a finer needle, a poorer needle, until the unknown is pinned between two standards or matched outright. Theophrastus §46 proves that graded comparison against very small weight-steps was the ancient aim; the Sardis evidence (Section VI) shows a gold needle of known purity in actual refinery use; the organised needle-case itself, with its carat numbering, is documented only in the later assay literature, and this article does not antedate it.

Step 4 — Read the streaks (stated). The reading is colour, judged side by side on the same stone, in the same light, at the same moment. Pure gold lays a deep, rich yellow. Silver in the alloy bleaches it, step by step, through pale yellow toward the greenish-white of electrum and the white of silver itself. Copper reddens it. A streak that matches a standard in hue and in the way it catches the light is read as that standard's fineness; a streak between two standards is read as between them. Pliny, in Rackham's translation (NH 33.126), states the experts' claim without flinching: “When experts using this touchstone, like a file, have taken with it a scraping from an ore, they can say at once how much gold it contains and how much silver or copper, to a difference of a scruple” — a scruple being the Roman scrupulum, a twenty-fourth of an ounce, about 1.1 grams. Read soberly, the claim is that a master could call a ternary alloy's recipe to within a small weight-step, on sight. The modern verdict (Section II's caution) is that the method in practised hands is good to roughly a carat on gold — remarkable for a test that takes ten seconds and consumes nothing — and that Pliny's “never mistaken” is the voice of the tradition admiring its masters, not a laboratory error estimate.

// WHAT THE ANCIENT TEST DID NOT HAVE: THE ACID DROP //

Every modern touchstone kit includes acid — a drop of nitric acid (or, for higher carats, an aqua regia mixture) laid across the streaks, which eats the poorer streak faster and turns a colour judgement into a survival race. That step is not ancient. The Greek and Roman world had no mineral acids to drop (Section VII): nitric acid is a medieval preparation, unambiguous only in the Latin pseudo-Geber writings of the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries. The ancient reading was colour alone, unaided — which is precisely why it demanded a master, why Theophrastus marvels at it, and why its verdicts were argued about. Any picture of a Greek assayer with an acid bottle is an anachronism of about fifteen hundred years.

Who Used It, and Where

The touchstone's users can be placed, from the evidence, at every level of the metal economy. At the top, the refinery: at Sardis, after cementation, the parted gold was assayed on the stone against a needle of known purity before it was collected and sent into the city (Section VI) — the test as quality control on a state-grade product. In the middle, the mint and the banker's table: the Athenian public tester sat among the bankers' tables in the Agora (Section IX), and while the law that placed him there specifies his cut, not his stone, the streak is the only rapid, non-destructive screen a man examining coin after coin all day could have used to decide which pieces deserved the chisel; that sentence is an inference from the workflow, and is labelled as one. At the bench level, the goldsmith: the recipe papyri presuppose the stone throughout — most nakedly in a recipe (Leyden X, No. 38, Section XI) for a gilding that, in Caley's translation, neither touch nor rubbing on the touchstone will detect, a fraud specified precisely by the test it is engineered to beat. And in the culture at large, the poet and the philosopher: Bacchylides and Plato (Section I) reach for the stone when they need an image of a verdict that cannot be flattered, bribed, or argued with — only read. An instrument does not acquire that metaphorical career by being rare.

How the Touchstone Fails

The failure modes define the rest of this article, because every later procedure exists to cover one of them.

FAILURE ONE — IT READS ONLY THE SKIN

A streak is a sample of the surface, a few milligrams deep at most. A plated coin, a gilded copper ring, a surface-enriched alloy (Section VI) all present honest metal to the stone and base metal to the world. The stone cannot see a core. Only the cut can (Section X).

FAILURE TWO — IT CANNOT COUNT THREE METALS AT ONCE

Colour is one channel of information; a gold–silver–copper mixture has two independent variables. Different recipes can lay down near-identical streaks. The needle ladder narrows the possibilities; it does not close them. Pliny's scruple is a master's boast, not a method's guarantee.

FAILURE THREE — THE INSTRUMENT DRIFTS

Moisture (Theophrastus §47), grease, worn or clogged stone, mismatched rubbing pressure, poor light: each shifts the reading. The test's accuracy lives in the operator's discipline, and dies with it.

FAILURE FOUR — IT SAYS NOTHING ABOUT WEIGHT

A clipped, sweated, or underweight coin of honest metal passes the stone perfectly. Fineness and weight are separate promises, kept by separate instruments: the stone for the first, the balance for the second. The dokimasia needed both (Section IX).

Hold those four failures in mind. Cupellation answers the second (it counts, by weighing what survives). Parting answers it completely (it separates the metals so each can be weighed alone). The cut answers the first. The balance answers the fourth. The Greek testing system is not a collection of tricks; it is a set of instruments whose blind spots interlock — and the men who ran it knew the interlocking, because the frauds that beat one test and died on another are recorded in their own books.

IV. Fire as the Second Witness: Colour, Breath, and the Hot Shovel

Theophrastus §45 set the terms: fire tests by changing and altering the colours. Fire-testing is the touchstone's elder sibling — cruder, partly destructive, and available to anyone with a charcoal fire and an eye. Its principle is differential behaviour under heat. Pure gold is, among common metals, uniquely indifferent to fire: it melts (at 1,064 °C, in modern measurement) without oxidising, without tarnishing, and comes out of the fire the colour it went in. Its usual companions are not indifferent. Silver stays bright at a red heat but its copper content oxidises and darkens; copper reddens and then blackens with scale; lead oxidises, softens, and slumps far below the others' melting points; tin sweats out early. Heat a suspect piece strongly and watch it, and the alloy confesses in colour and in texture what the cold eye could not see. Pliny gives the doctrine its Roman form in Natural History 33.59–60, in paraphrase of Rackham's translation: gold is the one substance that loses nothing by fire, even in conflagrations and on funeral pyres; indeed it improves the more often it is fired, and fire serves as a test of its goodness, the gold taking on a red hue like fire itself — and this process, Pliny says, is called assaying. He adds the refiner's detail that gold, for purification, is roasted with lead — a sentence that points straight at cupellation (Section V).

The Procedure, as the Recipe Papyri Write It Down

The fullest surviving bench statement of the fire test is not in a philosopher or an encyclopaedist but in the workshop book this article ends with: Leyden Papyrus X, Nos. 43 and 44, in Caley's translation, here paraphrased closely because the recipes are short and their logic is the whole content. To test gold (No. 43): remelt the metal and heat it strongly. If it is pure, it keeps its colour after heating and stays bright like a coin. If it turns white, it contains silver. If it becomes rougher and harder, it contains copper or tin. If it blackens and softens, it contains lead. To test silver (No. 44): heat or melt it as with gold. If it stays white and brilliant, it is pure. If it turns black, it contains lead. If it turns hard and yellow, it contains copper. That is the complete procedure: one furnace, one heating, four or three colour-and-texture verdicts. Note what it is and is not. It is a screen for gross adulteration — Caley's assessment, carried in the Egyptology division's treatment, is that such recipes detect gross adulteration only — and it is qualitative: it names the adulterant but cannot weigh it. Note also its cost: the piece must be remelted. The fire test in this form can be applied to bullion, to scrap, to a sample — never to a finished coin whose stamp is its value. The touchstone screens the coin; the fire judges the melt. The division of labour between Sections III and IV is a division between objects, not just between techniques.

Pliny's Silver Tests: The Shovel and the Breath

For silver, Pliny (NH 33.127, in paraphrase of Rackham) preserves two further tests, and — characteristically — the fraud that defeats one of them. The shovel test: lay a shaving of the silver on a white-hot iron shovel. A shaving that stays perfectly white is passed as good silver; if it turns red, it is of the next quality down; if black, it is worthless. The chemistry, in modern terms, is the oxidation behaviour of the alloy's copper on the hot iron: the whiter the residue, the less oxidisable metal was in the shaving. Then Pliny's warning, in paraphrase: fraud has found its way even into this test — if the shovels are kept in urine, the shaving is stained during the burning and a base shaving counterfeits whiteness. The detail is precious evidence, worth more than the test itself: by the first century CE the testing of silver was an adversarial practice with a literature of counter-measures, in which even the instrument could be prepared in advance by the cheat. The breath test follows in the same section, in paraphrase: breathe on polished silver; genuine silver at once films with moisture and then dissipates the vapour. It is a test of surface and polish more than of composition — the weakest instrument in this article, recorded here because Pliny records it, and because its presence in his list shows the truth about the whole family: ancient testing was a bundle of screens of very unequal power, and the skill lay in knowing which screen a given suspicion required.

// THE SAME LOGIC, APPLIED TO A PIGMENT //

Pliny's fire logic was general-purpose. In NH 33.121 (in paraphrase of Rackham) he reports that cinnabar from the Sisapo mines is assayed by means of gold made red-hot: adulterated cinnabar blackens against the hot gold, genuine cinnabar keeps its colour — and, if no gold is available, a sheet of red-hot iron serves the same turn, detecting lime adulteration. A standard hot surface, a suspect sample, a colour verdict: the shovel test with the roles reversed. The Greek world's testing habit was not confined to coin metal; it was how a material culture without chemistry interrogated any substance whose value hid inside it.

Fire's verdicts, then, are fast, legible, and shallow. They sort the grossly false from the plausible in minutes, at the price of the sample's form. To go further — to turn this metal contains copper into this metal contains eleven parts of copper in the hundred — the assayer must stop watching surfaces and start destroying samples methodically, weighing what the destruction leaves behind. That is fire assay proper, and its engine is the subject of the next section: a bath of molten lead, a porous cup of bone ash, and a bead that brightens at the end like a signal lamp.

V. Cupellation: The Lead That Carries Everything Away

Cupellation is the quantitative heart of ancient assaying, and the oldest of its heavy procedures: archaeology carries it back to the third millennium BCE in Asia Minor, where it was invented to win silver from lead ores, and it remained, with remarkably little change, the standard fire assay for gold and silver into the modern laboratory. Its principle is a piece of chemical luck so convenient that it looks designed. Melt lead in a stream of air at a bright red heat and it oxidises, steadily and completely, into molten lead oxide — litharge (PbO). Melt gold or silver in the same air at the same heat and they do not oxidise at all. And molten litharge is a solvent and a vehicle: as it forms, it dissolves the oxides of the base metals present — copper, tin, iron, antimony, bismuth — and carries them away with itself. So: alloy the precious metal with a generous excess of lead, hold the melt hot in moving air, and the lead becomes a river that flows out of the vessel taking every base metal with it, until nothing is left in the vessel but the precious metal, alone, in a bead that can be weighed. What the bead weighs, against what the sample weighed, is the fineness. The touchstone estimates; cupellation counts.

The Cupel: A Vessel That Drinks Lead Oxide and Nothing Else

The process turns on its vessel. The cupel is a small, thick-walled, shallow cup whose one job is selective absorption: it must drink the molten litharge as fast as the litharge forms, by capillary action, while refusing the bead of precious metal, whose surface tension keeps it balled up and unabsorbed — the later assay literature's comparison, in paraphrase, is blotting paper and a globule of mercury. The material that does this is bone ash: bone calcined to a white ash (calcium phosphate, in modern terms), ground, moistened, pressed in a mould to a cup with a smooth concave depression, and dried hard. The chemistry of the choice matters and the ancients knew it empirically: the cupel must be calcareous or magnesian, never siliceous, because lead oxide attacks silica and forms a viscous lead silicate that seals the pores and stops the absorption dead; a clay cupel drowns its own bead. Where bone ash was not to hand, the large-scale version of the same idea served: a hearth or shallow bowl lined with crushed bone, shell, lime, or wood ash. The Sardis cupels of the sixth century BCE were, on the excavators' description, saucer-shaped hollows in the ground surface with porous walls — bowl hearths rather than the neat portable cups of the later literature — and as many as two hundred of them were exposed on one refinery floor (Section VI). The form varies; the specification does not. Porous, calcareous, thirsty for litharge, indifferent to silver: that is a cupel, in 550 BCE and in 1850 CE alike.

The Procedure, Step by Step

What follows is the small-scale assay form of cupellation — the form that tests, as distinct from the hearth form that produces silver in bulk, though the two are one operation at two scales. The sequence is assembled from the ancient attestations noted at each stage and from the later fire-assay literature, in which the process is described in full working detail (Agricola's De re metallica, Book VII, in the Hoovers' translation, is the classic full account); stages the ancient sources do not themselves spell out are marked reconstruction, per Section I's rule.

Step 1 — Weigh the sample (stated by the logic of every ancient attestation; the weighing itself is the assay). Cut or file a representative sample of the metal to be tested and weigh it exactly on a fine balance. The whole verdict of the procedure is a ratio between this weight and the bead's weight, so the weighing is not bookkeeping around the test; it is the test. A sample that is not representative — a filing from the enriched skin of a parted ingot, a corner of a segregated casting — yields an exact answer about the wrong metal, the standing failure mode of every sampling assay from Sardis to the present.

Step 2 — Add the lead charge. Wrap the sample in, or melt it with, a weighed excess of pure lead — the collector and the fuel of the process in one. The ancient recipe that states a proportion is Leyden X, No. 26 (in paraphrase of Caley): to purify silver, take one part of silver and an equal weight of lead, and repeat until the silver is brilliant — a production recipe, with the lead renewed across repetitions. The assay literature (reconstruction detail) proportions the lead to the baseness expected: the poorer the sample, the more lead is needed, because the lead must not merely oxidise itself away but must keep enough molten litharge flowing, for long enough, to sweep out every base oxide; too little lead leaves base metal stranded in the bead, too much lengthens the operation and increases the precious-metal loss discussed below. If the sample is an ore or a crude smelt product rather than a metal, a prior crucible fusion with fluxes (reconstruction, from the later literature) first gathers its precious content into a lead button, and it is the button that goes to the cupel.

Step 3 — Charge the cupel and bring up the heat (reconstruction in its bench detail; the conditions are fixed by the physics in the table above). Preheat the cupel in the furnace until it is thoroughly hot and dry — a cold or damp cupel cracks, spits its charge, or freezes the first litharge. Set the lead alloy in the cupel's depression and raise the furnace to a bright red heat, in the working band the modern literature puts at roughly 900–1,100 °C: hot enough that litharge (melting at 888 °C) stays fluid and the silver stays molten (961 °C), with a strong draught or bellows blast moving air across the melt's surface. Air is a reagent in this process, not a condition of the room: starve the draught and oxidation stalls, whatever the temperature.

Step 4 — The drive: watch the litharge (the behaviour stated in the later literature; the Sardis and Laurion archaeologies show it at production scale). The lead melts, skins over with a dark scum, and then — as oxidation takes hold — clears and brightens: the assay has “opened”, in the later assayers' term. From here the operation has a visible physiology, and the operator reads it continuously. Molten litharge forms at the surface and streams off the convex face of the shrinking bead in bright films, down into the cupel, which drinks it; a ring of absorbed litharge spreads in the cupel wall around the bead; a fraction of the litharge leaves as fume. Feather-like litharge crystals at the cupel's cooler rim are the later literature's thermometer: they form when the temperature is right, and if they begin to close in around the bead itself, the furnace is too cold and the litharge is about to freeze where it stands. The bead meanwhile rounds up as its lead leaves it — precious metal has the higher surface tension — and shrinks, steadily, toward its final size. Base-metal oxides ride the litharge out and stain the cupel: the absorbed ring's colour is a rough diary of what was removed.

Step 5 — The brightening: the end-point (phenomenon described in the later assay literature under the names brightening, blick, and play of colours; the physics is the ancient process's own). As the last of the lead leaves, the films of litharge on the bead thin to interference thickness and the bead's surface runs with swiftly moving rainbow bands — the play of colours. Then the film breaks. The colours vanish, the bead dulls for an instant, and then flashes to a clean, bright metallic lustre: the brightening. It is the most dramatic end-point in all pre-modern analysis, and it is information, not theatre: the flash announces that the oxidisable fraction is gone and what remains is precious metal only. The operator's work at this instant is delicate. The bead must be cooled slowly — drawn gradually toward the furnace mouth before it is taken out, in the later manuals' instruction — because molten silver holds dissolved oxygen and, if chilled suddenly, ejects it violently as it solidifies: the bead spits or sprouts, throwing off droplets of silver, and a spat assay is a lost assay, its bead no longer the whole of what survived.

Step 6 — Weigh the bead and reckon the fineness. Lift the cooled bead from the cupel, brush or flatten it clean of adhering ash, and weigh it on the same fine balance as the sample. Bead weight over sample weight is the fineness in precious metal — gold and silver together, if both were present. A worked example, with modern numbers for clarity: a 10.00-gram sample of suspect silver that returns a bead of 9.31 grams was 931 parts per thousand precious — and, since the bead of a silver assay is silver (any gold present reports with it, and must be parted out, Section VI), the verdict is: silver, about 93 per cent fine, the missing 0.69 grams having left as base oxides in the litharge. Pass and fail are commercial, not chemical: the bead either meets the fineness the coinage, the contract, or the temple inventory demands, or it does not.

Cupellation is quantitative, not perfect, and its errors are systematic — which is why the later assay offices ran proofs: control samples of known fineness cupelled alongside the unknowns, so the day's loss could be measured and corrected for (the surcharge of the later literature; the practice is medieval and early modern in its documented form, and is labelled here as the later refinement of an ancient problem). The losses: a small fraction of the silver is absorbed into the cupel with the litharge and a smaller fraction volatilises, the loss growing with temperature and with time — run the furnace too hot or too long and the assay steals from the bead. Run it too cold and litharge freezes over the bead (“drowning” it), trapping lead inside a bead that then weighs heavy and lies in the opposite direction. Add too little lead and base metals survive into the bead. Every one of these failure modes is a direction of error a dishonest or careless operator could lean on — which is one more reason the state, in Section IX, preferred its tester watched, seated in public, and flogged if he absented himself.

Cupellation as Production: Laurion and the Silver of Athens

The assay's parent was not the testing bench but the silver works. The great classical source of silver was argentiferous lead ore — galena and its kin — and the only road from that ore to silver ran through the cupel: smelt the ore to a lead rich in silver, then cupel the lead away and keep the bead, at the scale of hearths rather than cups. Pliny (NH 33.95, in paraphrase of Rackham) describes the smelting behaviour on which it rests: silver ore cannot be smelted except with lead or galena, and in the firing the lead precipitates while the silver floats on the surface like oil on water — a smelter's-eye view of the separation the cupellation hearth then completes by oxidising the lead out from under the silver entirely. At Laurion in Attica, whose mines financed the Athenian fleet and whose silver struck the owls of Section IX, the archaeological record of washeries, furnaces, and litharge is the record of this process run as an industry; Greenland ice-core lead pollution preserves its smoke at continental scale. The point for this article is the continuity of the instrument: the same operation that filled the Athenian treasury also, at bench scale, audited it. When the dokimastēs's verdict was challenged, the appeal court of last resort was a cupel, a lead charge, and a balance.

And its limit must be stated as plainly as its power, because the limit dictates the next two sections. Cupellation separates the precious from the base. It does not — cannot, by its own chemistry — separate the precious metals from each other. A bead of gold-and-silver comes off the cupel as mixed as it went on. For a silver coinage that is enough. For gold — whose commonest companion is silver, whose price turns on the exact proportion, and whose natural alloy, electrum, is precisely gold-with-silver — it is not enough. To know a gold, you must part it. The Greek world had a way to do that. It also, for most of its history, lacked the way a modern reader instinctively imagines. The next two sections take the two halves of that sentence in turn.

VI. Parting I — Cementation: What the Greek World Had

Parting is the separation of gold from silver. The problem is built into the geology: native gold almost always carries silver — the Pactolus electrum of Lydia ran, on the analyses discussed by the Sardis excavators, with a wide and variable silver content — and until the two are separated, neither a pure gold nor a pure silver coinage is possible, and no assay of gold can be completed. The Greek world's solution was cementation: a solid-state, dry process, slow, hot, and — on the Sardis evidence — fully operational by the first half of the sixth century BCE, making it the earliest securely archaeologically attested parting process anywhere. Ramage and Craddock, the Sardis refinery's publishers, argued that the process and the coinage grew up together: coins demand metal of consistent, guaranteeable purity, alluvial gold cannot supply it, and cementation is the technology that closes the gap. Shortly before the middle of the sixth century, under Croesus, Lydia began issuing coins in pure gold and pure silver separately — the bimetallic coinage that replaced electrum. The refinery outside the walls is where that monetary revolution was physically performed.

The Sardis Procedure, from the Archaeology

The installation at sector PN (Pactolus North) at Sardis, excavated in 1968–69 and published by Andrew Ramage and Paul Craddock in King Croesus' Gold (2000), lets the process be given as a procedure rather than a conjecture. The steps below follow the Sardis expedition's own synthesis of the remains; temperatures and times are the excavators' estimates from the materials, and are labelled as such.

Step 1 — Make foil. Larger pieces of electrum are hammered into thin sheets. The reason is geometric and it governs the whole process: cementation attacks a surface, and only a surface — the parting agent cannot reach silver locked in the middle of a thick lump. Thin sheet maximises the surface per unit of metal. Small grains of alluvial gold go in as they are; nature has already done their subdivision. Fragments of gold sheet and drops of gold were recovered across the refinery floor.

Step 2 — Pack the pot. The sheets and grains are sandwiched between layers of the parting agent — common salt, on the excavators' identification, possibly with sulfurous materials such as alum among the additions — mixed with carriers: clay or brick dust, whose job is to absorb the reaction product. The sandwich is packed into coarse pottery containers, in form ordinary Lydian cooking pots. Fragments of these parting vessels survive in quantity, some still bearing traces of gold.

Step 3 — Heat, long and below melting. The sealed pots go into small brick ovens and are held at a relatively low temperature — the excavators estimate about 800 °C — for many hours, perhaps days. Nothing melts: gold melts at 1,064 °C and the charge never approaches it. What happens is slower and stranger. At that heat, in the presence of the clay and the air trapped in the pot, the salt generates chlorinating vapours; the vapours attack the silver in the alloy's surface — and, as fresh silver diffuses outward from within the thin sheet, attack that too — converting it to silver chloride. The chloride is wicked away into the surrounding carrier clay, the pot wall, and the oven brick. Gold, which forms no such chloride under these conditions, is untouched. When the pot is opened, the sheets come out matte, porous, and fragile — a sponge of nearly pure gold, silver-depleted through their whole thickness because they were thin enough for the depletion to reach the middle. The chemistry is stated here in modern terms; the Sardis men had the procedure without the terms, which did not prevent it from working.

Step 4 — Assay the product. The parted gold is tested before it leaves the refinery: pieces are melted on fragments of coarse vessel — many such fragments, bearing traces of melted gold, were found — and the metal is rubbed on a touchstone and its streak compared with the streak of a gold needle of known purity (the Sardis synthesis's account, following the refinery's own finds, which include a touchstone from a Lydian house context). Only then is the gold collected and sent into the city for consolidation and coining. Note the architecture of the whole installation: production process and testing procedure are not two activities but one loop — part, streak, compare, release.

Step 5 — Recover the silver. The silver is not discarded with the pots; it is sitting in the carriers, the pot walls, and the furnace bricks as silver chloride, and it is worth a second industry to get it back. The silver-laden clays are smelted with lead: the lead collects the silver into a lead–silver alloy. That alloy is then cupelled in the bowl hearths of Step V's process — as many as two hundred of them on the PN floor, with bellows nozzles (tuyères) and an iron blowpipe tip among the finds — heated in an oxidising atmosphere to about 1,100 °C on the excavators' estimate, until the lead has gone to litharge and a button of metallic silver remains in the hollow, ready to be lifted out. A litharge cake from the site preserves the very shape of the operation: a disc of lead oxide with the hollow in its top where the silver button sat. Cementation and cupellation, the subjects of Sections V and VI, are at Sardis a single production line with a testing station in the middle of it.

The Literary Trail: Agatharchides, Strabo, Pliny, and the Papyri

The texts catch up with the archaeology centuries later, and say less than the pots do — a pattern worth naming, because it recurs throughout this hub: the workshop literature is always older than the literature about the workshop. Diodorus Siculus, in the first century BCE, preserves (3.12–14, in paraphrase) a description of Egyptian gold refining drawn from Agatharchides of Cnidus's second-century BCE account of the Red Sea gold mines, in which the gold is sealed in earthen pots with lead, salt, a little tin, and barley bran and fired for five days — a cementation-family process, described by a geographer at several removes, whose proportions modern experimenters have found workable in substance. Strabo (in paraphrase) knows of gold refined by cementing with an aluminous earth that destroys the silver. And Pliny gives the tradition's most nearly quantitative recipe, in a passage of NH 33 on gold's purification (in paraphrase of Rackham's translation, with the proportions as the Loeb text gives them): the gold is roasted with twice its weight of salt and three times its weight of misy — misy being an iron-sulfate mineral of the mines — and again with two parts of salt and one of the stone called schiston; the gold itself, Pliny says, comes out pure and incorrupt, the impurities drawn off into the charge burned with it. Set Pliny's salt-and-sulfate charge beside the Sardis pots and the family resemblance is exact: a chloride- and sulfate-bearing cement, an earthen vessel, a long roast, pure gold. The papyri close the trail at bench level: Leyden X, No. 25 (in paraphrase of Caley) coats gold with a pulverised mixture of misy, alum, and salt, four parts each, seals it in an earthenware vessel luted with clay in a furnace, heats until the coating has fused, and scours the gold clean — cementation refining written as a goldsmith's recipe, proportions and all, eight centuries after Sardis.

// SURFACE ENRICHMENT: CEMENTATION'S QUIET TWIN //

Run the same chemistry briefly, on a finished object, and a different product results: only the skin is depleted of silver, leaving a surface of pure, deep-yellow gold over an electrum heart. Surface enrichment by cementation-family treatments is archaeologically attested on early goldwork, and it is legitimate finishing — it is also, structurally, a deception kit: the enriched object streaks pure on the touchstone (Section III's first failure mode) and colours pure in the fire test, while its bulk is nothing of the kind. The ancients' own counter was the one the next sections develop: distrust the skin, and where the value justifies it, cut, cupel a true sample, or weigh the thing in water. Every test in this article exists because some surface, somewhere, was lying.

Cementation's limits are the obverse of its virtues. It is slow — hours to days per charge. It works only on thin stock or small grains, so bulky objects must be hammered out or granulated first, and the parted gold comes back as a fragile sponge that must be consolidated by melting. Its silver is not recovered in the pot but only by the smelt-and-cupel second act. And it leaves the assayer still needing a test of the product — hence the touchstone station on the refinery floor. What it never requires is a corrosive liquid. That absence is not a detail. It is the defining boundary of Greek-world parting, and the next section patrols it.

VII. Parting II — What the Greek World Did Not Have: Acid Parting

Ask a modern jeweller how gold is parted from silver and the answer is a bottle: nitric acid dissolves the silver and leaves the gold standing as a brown sponge, which is washed, dried, and melted. The procedure is so simple, so quick, and so complete that it is hard to hold in mind that the entire classical world did without it. But it did, and the honesty of this article's chronology depends on saying exactly where the bottle comes from — because a large popular literature silently equips Greek and Egyptian workshops with acids they never possessed, and then reads their recipes through equipment that postdates them by a millennium.

The Acid Procedure, Stated So Its Absence Can Be Felt

Acid parting, in its mature (early modern and modern) form, runs as follows — given here in full procedural detail precisely so that its every step can be checked against the ancient record, and labelled throughout as not ancient. First, inquartation (also called quartation): nitric acid cannot attack a gold-rich alloy, because the gold forms a protective skin; the alloy must first be diluted to at most one part gold in four — three parts of fine silver melted in for every part of gold (whence the name: the gold is brought to a quarter). The quartated alloy is granulated by pouring the melt in a thin stream into water, so the acid gets surface to work on. The granules are then boiled in nitric acid of moderate strength; the acid dissolves the silver (and any copper) as nitrates, evolving red fumes, while the gold remains undissolved as a coherent brown sponge that holds the granules' shape. The liquor is decanted, the gold washed repeatedly, dried, annealed, and melted; the silver is recovered from the liquor, classically by precipitating it as chloride with salt or by cementing it out on copper. The whole operation takes hours, works on any quantity, and parts to a completeness cementation never reaches. It is, by every operational measure, the better process. It was unavailable to Theophrastus, to the Sardis refiners, to Pliny, and to Zosimos, for one reason: there was no nitric acid.

Two distinctions keep the chronology clean, and both are commonly blurred. The first is between having corrosive substances and having mineral acids. The Greek world was rich in the first: vinegar sharp enough to be a reagent, urine, brine, sulfur waters, the sulfates of the mine earths, natron, lime. Its recipes use them constantly — to clean, to pickle, to colour, to assist a cement. None of them parts gold from silver in the liquid state, and no ancient text describes distilling a mineral acid or dissolving silver out of gold in a liquor. The second distinction is between antimony and sulfur parting — dry processes, in which sulfur or stibnite converts the silver to a sulfide matte — and acid parting proper. Sulfur-based dry parting belongs to the post-medieval literature's repertoire; it is not the Greek world's documented method either, and this article does not backdate it. What the Greek world had, it had completely: cementation for parting, cupellation for gathering and proving, the stone and the fire for screening. What it lacked, it lacked completely: a liquid that eats one noble metal and spares the other. The recipe papyri's ingenuity (Section XI) is the ingenuity of men working, brilliantly, inside that lack.

The cover-name literature treated in the Decknamen glossary is full of “sharp waters”, “divine water”, and solvents that later readers have wanted to read as acids. Some of those waters are sulfur liquors; some are allegories; none, in the Greek stratum, is a distilled mineral acid, and importing nitric acid into a reading of Zosimos or the papyri falsifies both the recipe and the reader's sense of what the workshop could do. The apparatus tradition of Maria the Jewess — stills, reflux, the water bath — is real, and it is the seed from which medieval distillation, and with it the acids, eventually grew. But a seed is not a tree, and the Greek assayers of this article tested their gold without a single drop of aqua fortis among them.

VIII. Weight and Water: Archimedes, the Crown, and the Limits of Displacement

Every other test in this article comes out of a workshop. This one comes out of a bath-house, two centuries after the fact, in a book about architecture — and it is by far the most famous assay in the world. It must therefore be handled with more care, not less: the story is an anecdote, the method it describes is not the method its hero's own science recommends, and the honest treatment — anecdote labelled as anecdote, principle stated correctly, limits computed — is more interesting than the legend in every particular.

Vitruvius's Telling, Labelled as What It Is

The sole ancient source for the crown story is Vitruvius, On Architecture, Book 9, preface, §§9–12, written in the late first century BCE — roughly two hundred years after Archimedes' death in 212 BCE. No work of Archimedes mentions a crown. What follows is Vitruvius's account, in close paraphrase of Morgan's translation, with its status stated once and plainly: this is a story Vitruvius tells about Archimedes, offered by him as an exemplum of ingenuity, and it is treated here as anecdote, not as a laboratory report.

Hiero, having come to power in Syracuse, vowed a golden crown to the gods, contracted for its making at a fixed price, and weighed out a precise amount of gold to the contractor. The finished crown delighted him, and its weight matched the gold's weight exactly. Then came the charge: gold had been abstracted, and an equivalent weight of silver worked in in its place. Hiero, outraged and without a method, asked Archimedes to consider the matter — the constraint, implicit throughout and explicit in the tradition, being that the votive crown must not be damaged, cut, or melted: the ordinary assays of Sections III–V are all, for this object, forbidden. Archimedes, the case on his mind, went to the bath, got into a full tub, and noticed that the more his body sank, the more water ran out over the side. In Vitruvius's telling he leapt out, ran home naked through the streets, and shouted repeatedly, in Morgan's rendering, “Eureka, eureka” — “I have found (it), I have found (it)”.

The procedure Vitruvius then describes, in paraphrase, is exact enough to be reconstructed step for step. (1) Make two masses, each of the same weight as the crown: one of pure gold, one of pure silver. (2) Fill a large vessel with water to the very brim. Lower in the silver mass; catch or note the water that runs out — it equals the silver's bulk. Take the mass out, and pour the lost water back with a pint measure until the vessel is full to the brim again: the measure of water returned is the measure of the silver's volume, at the crown's weight. (3) Repeat with the gold mass: less water is lost, by exactly as much as a gold mass lacks in bulk against a silver mass of equal weight. (4) Repeat with the crown itself. More water runs over for the crown than for the gold mass of the same weight. (5) Verdict: since the crown displaces more than pure gold of its weight, and less (by the construction of the experiment) than pure silver of its weight, silver is mixed in the gold, and the contractor's theft stands detected. As a piece of reasoning, it is flawless. As a piece of metrology, it has a problem that can be put in numbers.

The Principle, Stated Correctly

The physics the anecdote rides on is sound, and it is Archimedes' own. In his treatise On Floating Bodies stands the proposition modern textbooks call Archimedes' principle, stated here in its standard modern form: a body immersed in a fluid is buoyed up by a force equal to the weight of the fluid it displaces; a fully immersed body displaces its own volume of fluid, and so loses, in apparent weight, exactly the weight of that volume of fluid. Two consequences follow, and the whole of this section is their unfolding. First, density is measurable without damaging the object: weigh the object in air, weigh it immersed (or measure its displaced volume), and the ratio of the weights gives its specific gravity — its density against water's. Second, and decisively: the test measures one number — mean density — and nothing else. Everything the crown test can and cannot prove is contained in that sentence.

A votive crown is an object of, plausibly, a kilogram's order. If the goldsmith stole a tenth of the gold — a rich theft — Vitruvius's Archimedes must detect 4.3 cubic centimetres of extra overflow: about a teaspoonful of water, spread across the rim of a large vessel filled to the brim, in a procedure whose every stage (filling exactly to the brim, lowering a spiky crown without splashing, recovering or re-measuring the overflow with a pint measure) leaks error of the same order as the signal. A fifth stolen gives 2.2 cm³ — a signal no brim-and-pint procedure of the third century BCE could have isolated from its own noise. This is not a modern sneer at ancient skill; it is the objection Galileo made in 1586, in his first scientific work, La Bilancetta (“The Little Balance”), where — in paraphrase of his argument — he calls the overflow method as Vitruvius reports it a crude thing, too coarse for the discrimination required, and proposes that Archimedes must in fact have used the instrument Galileo then describes: a hydrostatic balance.

The Balance in Water: The Method Archimedes' Own Science Recommends

Galileo's proposed procedure — presented here as his conjecture about Archimedes, and as a correct method in itself — converts the tiny volume difference into a weight difference a fine balance can feel. (1) Balance the crown in air against an equal weight of pure gold: beam level, weights equal. (2) Lower both pans into water, crown and gold mass alike fully immersed, disturbing nothing else. (3) If the crown is pure, it displaces exactly what the gold mass displaces, the buoyant forces match, and the beam stays level. (4) If the crown carries silver, its volume is larger (table, above), it displaces more water, it is buoyed up more strongly, and its pan rises: the beam tips toward the gold. The verdict is read off a balance — the most precise instrument the ancient world possessed — instead of off a brim and a pint pot, and the sensitivity is limited by the balance, not by the meniscus. A hydrostatic weighing of this kind is, in modern dress, the standard specific-gravity determination still taught in assay laboratories: specific gravity equals weight in air divided by the loss of weight in water. Whether Archimedes performed it on Hiero's crown is unknowable; that a man who had written On Floating Bodies and On the Equilibrium of Planes owned both halves of the method is a matter of his surviving bibliography. The anecdote preserves the insight and, on Galileo's showing, garbles the instrument.

// THE EQUAL-VOLUME PROBLEM: WHAT EVEN A PERFECT WATER TEST CANNOT PROVE //

Suppose the measurement is perfect. The crown's mean density comes out at, say, nine-tenths of gold's. What has been proved? Exactly one thing: the crown's total volume is larger than the same weight of pure gold by a stated amount. What has not been proved is any particular explanation of that volume. A gold–silver alloy of the right proportion gives it — but so does a gold–copper alloy of a different proportion; so does a hollow crown, honestly or dishonestly made; so does a solid gold crown with a base-metal core (Section X's fraud, wearing a crown's shape); so, in principle, does any composite whose weights and volumes sum to the same totals. Density is a single equation, and the space of adulterations has many unknowns: one equation cannot solve for which light metal, in what amount, in what arrangement. Vitruvius's procedure can convict the crown of not being solid pure gold throughout — which, given that Hiero's question was “was I cheated?”, may have been verdict enough. It cannot return the assayer's verdict of Sections V–VI — this much silver, this much gold — and it cannot distinguish the thief's alloy from the founder's air pocket. The water test is a superb screen and no assay at all. That is not a defect in Archimedes. It is the shape of the physics, and stating it plainly is what separates this article's treatment from the schoolbook's.

The crown therefore takes its place in the toolkit as the limiting case that teaches the system's logic. For an object that may not be scratched, cut, or melted — a votive crown is the type specimen — the Greek world had exactly two instruments: the touchstone, which reads the skin and can be fooled by a skin, and the water test, which reads the mean density and can be fooled by anything with the same mean. Used together, streak and displacement corner a fraud considerably — a plated crown of right weight and wrong volume fails in the water; an enriched solid crown of right volume fails nowhere except the cupel it is forbidden to meet. Testing, at the limit, is negotiation with an object's protections. The Athenians, dealing not with one sacred crown but with a river of anonymous coin, solved the problem differently: they abolished the object's protections by law, and kept a man with a chisel in the middle of the market.

IX. The Dokimasia of Coinage: Athens Puts a Tester in the Agora

Athens struck the most trusted silver coinage of the classical world — the tetradrachm “owls”, about 17.2 grams of Laurion silver (Section V) bearing Athena's head and her owl, accepted from Egypt to Bactria — and trust, in a coinage, is a perishable asset. By the early fourth century BCE the owls had imitators everywhere: foreign mints striking owl-types for the eastern trade, and forgers striking plated ones for any trade at all. A seller handed an owl faced Sections III–V's whole problem in miniature, with a queue forming behind him. Athens's answer was not a better instrument. It was an institution: testing made public, salaried, seated in the open, and armed with legal force. Its charter survives on stone, and it is the single most informative document in this article.

The Law of Nikophon, 375/4 BCE

The inscription (SEG 26.72; Rhodes–Osborne, Greek Historical Inscriptions 25) records a law carried by the lawgivers (nomothetai) in the archonship of Hippodamas, 375/4 BCE, on the proposal of Nikophon. The stele was found in Athens, reused in later building; the text is substantially complete. What follows is a clause-by-clause paraphrase, following the Attic Inscriptions Online translation, with the law's own distinctions kept exact — because the distinctions are the assaying.

The acceptance rule. Attic silver coin is to be accepted — by every seller, as legal tender — when it is determined to be silver and bears the public stamp. Two conditions, and they correspond to this article's two families of fraud: the metal must be silver (the stamp can be copied; the metal is the question), and the stamp must be the public one (the type that guarantees the city's standard). A coin passing both is not merely genuine; by the law's later clauses it becomes unrefusable.

The tester and his seat. The public approver — the dokimastēs — is to sit among the bankers' tables (trapezai) in the Agora and approve on these terms every day, except on days when there is a deposit of public money, when he sits instead in the Council chamber (the Bouleuterion) — the state moves its tester to where its own revenue is being paid in. The dokimastēs was a public slave (dēmosios): the second half of the law, providing a matching tester for the port, directs the Council to appoint one from the public slaves if one is available, or to buy one. The choice is institutionally eloquent. A slave tester has no citizenship to trade on, no clientele to favour, and — the law's next clause — a body that can be flogged for dereliction in a way a citizen official's cannot. Athens bought expertise and then made it answerable with a whip.

The three verdicts. The heart of the law is a decision procedure, and it has three branches, not two. (a) Attic coin that is silver and stamped is approved — and approval has teeth (below). (b) A foreign silver coin bearing the same type as the Attic — the good imitation owls struck abroad, in solid silver, that the eastern trade ran on — is to be test-cut (the inscription's verb at this point is restored and debated; the Attic Inscriptions Online translation renders it “test cut it (?)”, question mark and all, and this article keeps the question mark) and then returned to the man who presented it. Not confiscated, not demonetised by the tester: handed back. The law thereby performs a distinction a modern reader does not expect — between foreign, which is a matter of origin, and false, which is a matter of metal. Good silver in a foreign owl is its owner's property and passes back into his hand, its quality now publicly vouched by the cut and the examination. (c) But a coin that has a bronze core, or a lead core, or is debased (kibdēlon) is to be, in the Attic Inscriptions Online rendering, cut through immediately — “let him cut it through immediately and let it be sacred property of the Mother of the Gods” — and deposited with the Council. The plated coin is not returned, not fined against, not merely refused: it is destroyed as currency on the spot, in public, and its remains are forfeited to a goddess, removing them from any official's pocket as neatly as from circulation.

THE TESTER WHO WILL NOT SIT

In paraphrase of the law: if the approver does not take his seat, or does not approve in accordance with the law, the Conveners of the People are to flog him with fifty lashes. The state's assayer is himself under assay, daily, in the open. A testing system that cannot test its tester is, the law assumes, decoration.

THE SELLER WHO WILL NOT ACCEPT

In paraphrase: anyone who refuses silver that the approver has approved forfeits everything he is selling that day. Approval is not advice. Once the dokimastēs has spoken, the coin is money by law, and refusing it costs the refuser his day's stock. The verdict manufactures the trust it certifies.

Enforcement and the second tester. The law builds its own police procedure around the bench. Denunciations (phaseis) are routed by market: grain-market matters to the grain-guardians (sitophylakes), Agora and city matters to the Conveners of the People, port matters to the managers of the emporion; the denouncer who convicts takes half the penalty; officials who fail to act are themselves liable to report before the Council, loss of office, and a fine of up to 500 drachmas; a slave seller who offends is flogged with fifty lashes. And because the city's silver problem arrived by sea, the law's second half plants a second dokimastēs in the Piraeus, by the stele of Poseidon, for the shipowners, merchants, and everyone else of the port, under the same rules — paid, from the following years, from the same source as the mint workers (argyroko poi), a budget line that files the tester where he belongs: as part of the cost of making money, like the men who strike it. The law is to be inscribed on stone and set up in both places, among the tables and before Poseidon's stele; and any earlier decree contradicting it is to be demolished by the Council's secretary. Procedure, publicity, penalties, payroll, publication: the inscription is a complete assay office in fifty-five lines.

// WHAT THE LAW DOES NOT SAY //

The inscription never specifies the dokimastēs's instruments. It commands verdicts — silver or not, cored or not, debased or not — and prescribes what follows from them, but the bench technique is left to the bench: streak on the stone for the screen (Section III), weight and heft for the standard, the chisel cut for the core, and, behind them all, the refiner's cupellation if a verdict were ever worth a sample's destruction (Section V). That silence is itself evidence. By 375/4 the procedures were so standard that a law could presuppose them the way a modern statute presupposes a breathalyser. The law's novelty is not technical. It is that the verdict was made public, daily, and binding — dokimasia as a utility, like water.

Dokimasia Beyond the Coin Bench

The coin law's word was Athens's general word for vetting, and the parallel is instructive rather than accidental. Incoming magistrates underwent a dokimasia before a court — an examination of their qualifications, citizenship, military service, and treatment of their parents — before they were approved to hold office; cavalry horses were dokimasia-tested; ephebes were enrolled after scrutiny. In every use the structure is identical: a candidate presents itself claiming a status; an authorised examiner applies a standard test; the verdict is public and has legal consequences; the false candidate is rejected and, in the coin's case, cut in half and given to a god. Athens did not borrow a metallurgical metaphor for its politics, or a political metaphor for its metallurgy. It ran one procedure in both materials, because it understood — what this article has been arguing from the bench upward — that a claim of value, in silver or in a citizen, is only as good as the test standing behind it.

A scholarly caution belongs here, because the law's purpose is debated and the debate is live. The straightforward reading — the law defends the quality of the money supply and greases retail trade — has been challenged, most pointedly in an argument (presented here as its author's position, in paraphrase) that the law's real centre of gravity is the enforcement of contracts: that its machinery exists to guarantee that payment in approved coin discharges a debt beyond argument, with the wholesale merchants of the Piraeus, whose contracts turned on unchallengeable tender, as its chief beneficiaries. The two readings are not enemies; a verdict that makes a coin unrefusable serves the shopper and the shipowner at once. What neither reading disputes is the bench itself: a public slave, a daily seat among the tables, a chisel, and three verdicts. On the procedure, the stone is unanimous.

X. The Counterfeiter's Reply: Plated Coins and the Tests That Hunt Them

The dokimastēs's third verdict — the cut — exists because of one specific artefact: the plated coin, modern scholarship's fourrée (French, “stuffed”), in Latin subaeratus, “bronze underneath”. It is the most successful fraud in the ancient monetary world, and its manufacture deserves the same procedural respect as the tests, because the tests are unintelligible except as answers to it.

How a Fourrée Is Made

In paraphrase of the standard numismatic accounts: take a flan — a blank disc — of base metal, copper or bronze (lead cores are also attested, and are named in Nikophon's law). Wrap it in silver foil, or coat it with a silver-rich layer. Heat the wrapped flan: at the interface, silver and copper form a eutectic alloy — in modern measurement, the silver–copper mixture of lowest melting point, about 72 per cent silver — which fuses the skin to the core in a bond that will survive striking and years of handling. Strike the clad flan between dies, genuine or copied. The result is a coin with a silver face, a silver edge (if the wrap was complete), a silver streak on the touchstone, very nearly the right weight — copper at 8.9 g/cm³ against silver's 10.5 leaves the piece somewhat light, a deficit a slightly thicker flan or a leaden core can mask — and perhaps a tenth of its face value in precious metal. Every non-destructive test in this article was designed, in effect, by this object; and this object defeats most of them. It defeats the eye. It defeats the stone, which reads its honest skin (Section III, failure one). It can be made to defeat the balance. What it cannot defeat is a hole.

The Hunting Tests, in Order of Escalation

Heft and weight. The first screen is the hand. A merchant who has handled ten thousand owls feels a light one before he thinks it; the formal version is the balance against the standard weight — a tetradrachm should stand at about 17.2 grams, a drachma at about 4.3. Weight catches the careless fourrée and the clipped genuine coin alike, and misses the careful fourrée entirely. It is a screen, not a verdict.

The ring. A solid silver coin, balanced on a fingertip and tapped, or dropped on stone, sounds with a clear, sustained ring; a plated coin, its skin bonded to a duller core, answers shorter and deader. The test is attested in the later tradition of merchant practice and repeated in the modern literature on fourrées, and it is honestly graded here as the weakest of the hunting tests: real, usable by a practised ear as a reason to escalate, and worthless as proof — ancient silver that has crystallised in the ground rings false even when genuine, a failure mode the modern literature stresses. The ring raises suspicion. It convicts nobody.

The streak. The touchstone's role in the hunt is real but bounded, and its bound is the fourrée's biography: rub the edge, rub a worn high point, rub anywhere the skin is thin or broken, and the stone may pull base metal into the streak and betray the piece. Rub the broad face of a well-made fourrée and the stone certifies the fraud. Worn fourrées betray themselves exactly there — at the high points of the type, where circulation has thinned the skin to the core — which is why the experienced examiner's eye goes to the high points first. The streak, in the hunt, is a test of the coin's damage, not of its substance.

The cut. The verdict test, and the only certain one: drive a chisel into the coin — across the face, or deep into the edge — far enough to open the interior, and look. Solid silver shows silver all the way down, the cut's walls one bright metal. A fourrée shows its biography in section: silver skin, eutectic seam, base core. Surviving ancient silver is full of these gashes — the deep test cuts across Athenian owls that travelled east are the commonest, some coins bearing several, each the fossil of a transaction in which someone refused to trust — alongside the smaller punched banker's marks by which merchants who had verified a coin stamped their verification into it for the next man's benefit. The cut's cost is obvious: it wounds the coin. Its benefit is the one the law of Nikophon institutionalised: it is final. After the cut there is nothing left to argue about, which is why the law prescribes it for the suspect foreign owl (returned, if solid, with the cut as its certificate) and prescribes its completion — cut through — for the cored coin, which is thereby not merely detected but executed.

// THE STATE AS COUNTERFEITER, AND THE COMEDY THAT NOTICED //

The hunter and the hunted were not always on opposite sides of the law. In 406/5 BCE, in the last extremity of the Peloponnesian War, Athens itself melted its gold dedications for coin and issued emergency bronze coins plated with silver — official fourrées, money of necessity, later redeemed in good silver when the city recovered. Aristophanes, in the Frogs of 405 BCE (lines 718–737, in paraphrase), builds the period's most famous joke on exactly this: the city, he complains, treats its best citizens the way it now treats its coinage — the old, sound, full-weight pieces, the only currency trusted abroad, driven out of circulation, while the new base pieces, struck yesterday, pass everywhere at home. The parabasis is political satire, but its metallurgy is this article in miniature: a city that knew precisely what a plated coin was, because it had a tester, a law, and — for one desperate year — a mint striking them.

Rome inherited the hunt and legislated it in its own idiom. Pliny (NH 33.132, in paraphrase of Rackham) records that Antonius alloyed the denarius, that forgers put copper in silver coin and shaved its weight, and that a method of assaying the denarius was consequently devised under a law so popular that the people voted statues, district by district, to the praetor Marius Gratidianus who carried it — Pliny adding, with dry wonder, that in this art alone forgeries themselves become objects of connoisseurship, a choice fake denarius fetching more than a genuine one. The serrated denarii (serrati) of the late Republic — their edges notched with some twenty chisel cuts at the mint itself — are the cut test built into the coin at birth, an official pre-emptive wound to show solid metal at the rim; the survival of plated serrati shows the counterfeiters accepting the challenge and cladding even the notches. The arms race of Section I, in one artefact: test, answer, counter-answer, across four centuries, with the metal itself as the battlefield.

Step back from the hunt and the system's shape is clear. No single ancient test is proof against a first-class fraud. The combination — heft, ring, streak, cut, and behind them the cupel — is proof against every fraud the ancient world is known to have fielded, at a price: time, skill, and, at the end of the escalation, the coin itself. What the dokimasia really sold Athens was not certainty but throughput: a trusted man, applying the cheap tests to everything and the dear test to the suspect, fast enough for a market to clear. That is the form in which testing survives into the modern assay office, the modern mint's trial of the pyx, and the modern jeweller's bench. The last section of the story returns to the place where the whole system was written down as recipes — by the men who stood to lose by it, and beat it, and wrote down how.

XI. Closing the Loop: The Recipe Papyri Write the Tests Down

The tradition this article has followed — from the Sardis refinery floor through Theophrastus's lecture room, Pliny's encyclopaedia, and the Athenian Agora — ends, as a written tradition, in two Greek papyri from a single Theban cache of the late third or early fourth century CE: the Leyden Papyrus X (111 recipes in Caley's numbering) and the Stockholm Papyrus (154 recipes), the workshop books of Greco-Egyptian metalworkers, dyers, and gem-imitators. The Egyptology division of this archive treats both papyri in full procedural depth in its Alchemy article (§XXIV, “The Theban Recipe Papyri”), and the present section complements rather than repeats that treatment: its subject is narrow — the tests in the papyri, and what their presence in a book of imitations proves about the whole testing tradition.

The first thing the papyri prove is that the tests of Sections III–VI were, by the fourth century CE, workshop commonplaces with recipe numbers. Leyden X does not argue for its tests or explain them; it files them between a solder and a cleaning recipe, in the imperative, for a reader who owns a furnace. Walk the file:

NO. 25 — CEMENTATION AS A RECIPE

In paraphrase of Caley: misy, alum, and salt, four parts each, pulverised with water; coat the gold, seal it in an earthenware vessel luted with clay in the furnace, heat until the coating fuses, withdraw and scour. Section VI's refinery process, shrunk to a goldsmith's single charge — with proportions.

NO. 26 — CUPELLATION AS A RECIPE

In paraphrase: silver and an equal weight of lead, melted together in the furnace and kept molten until the lead is just consumed; repeat until the silver is brilliant. Section V's assay, stated as a purification, with its end-point given as a colour — brilliant — the bead's brightening (Section V, Step 5) doing duty as the recipe's full stop.

NO. 32 — THE PAPYRUS TEST FOR TIN

In paraphrase: melt the tin and pour it onto papyrus laid beneath; if the papyrus burns through, the tin contains lead. A melting-point test in a single line — lead drags tin's melting behaviour down toward the eutectic, and the hotter-flowing, lead-bearing melt chars what pure tin, poured at its own temperature, does not. Gross, quick, and — as the Egyptology treatment notes — fooled by some mixtures. A screen, filed as a screen.

NOS. 43–44 — THE FIRE TESTS

Gold and silver judged by remelting and colour — white for silver in gold, roughness for copper or tin, blackening and softening for lead; black for lead in silver, hardness and yellowness for copper. Given in full in Section IV: the papyri are that section's bench source.

NO. 64 — TESTING ASEM

In paraphrase: to recognise false asem — the papyri's family of silver-imitating alloys — lay it in brine and heat; if false, it blackens. Even the imitations have imitations, and the book tests its own products' rivals with the same suspicion it teaches its readers to expect from others.

NO. 70 — “TESTED GOLD” AS AN INGREDIENT

A gold-ink recipe begins, in paraphrase, with a quarter portion of tested gold — gold that has passed — melted in the goldsmith's crucible. The vocabulary of the assay office has become a grade of raw material. Testing is no longer an event in the metal's life; it is an adjective the metal carries to the bench.

The Fraud That Specifies Its Test: No. 38

The single most revealing recipe in either papyrus, for this article's purposes, makes no test at all. It makes a test-proof fraud. Leyden X, No. 38 (in paraphrase of Caley, with its key clause quoted): for giving copper objects the appearance of gold — a ring is the example — grind gold and lead to a flour-fine powder, two parts lead to one of gold, work the powder up with gum, coat the ring, and heat; repeat the coating and heating until the object has taken the colour. The heat burns off the lead and fixes a skin of true gold on the copper. The recipe's boast, in Caley's translation, is that “neither touch nor rubbing against the touchstone will detect them” — and the reasoning is given with an assayer's precision: rubbing the ring draws a streak of genuine gold, because the surface is genuine gold, and the fire that would expose a paint consumes only the lead. Section III's first failure mode — the stone reads the skin — is here not a scholar's inference but a manufacturer's selling point, written by the tradition itself. Whoever compiled Leyden X knew exactly what the touchstone could not do, knew it from the fraud side, and filed the knowledge between a solder recipe and a gold ink. The tests and the frauds are one literature.

“It Will Withstand the Test”: The Formulae of Proof

The papyri's recipes close, again and again, with a warranty clause, and the clauses are the dokimasia's language domesticated to the bench. A product is promised as asem “of the first quality, which will deceive even the artisans” (No. 8, in paraphrase) — the severest jury the writer can name being not the public tester but fellow-craftsmen. A durable amalgam gilding of silver (No. 57) is carried through five applications of the gold-mercury paste, polished, smoothed over embers, and then certified, in Caley's translation, in the sentence this section takes its title from: “It can be submitted to the test for regular gold.” The Stockholm Papyrus, whose metal section is small — nine recipes of its 154, the rest being gems and dyes — applies the same grammar to its own fields: dyeings are judged by fastness, imitation gems by their survival of handling and heat. Read across both books, the pattern is a culture: no recipe is complete until it states the test its product will pass. The proof-formula is the recipe's last ingredient. A tradition that began with a Lydian river pebble and a king's suspicion ends, in its own handbooks, with every manufacture carrying its assay in its final line — the workshop having internalised, completely, the lesson the Agora's law enforced from outside: a thing is worth what it can withstand.

The hub's Workshop Root article traces the recipe tradition from Bolos of Mendes and the Physika kai Mystika — gold, silver, stones, purple, the four books of “dyeing” — down to these Theban survivors, and the Egyptology division's Alchemy article gives the papyri's full dossier, replication record, and hazards. This article's claim is only the complement those treatments invite: that the recipe books are also the testing tradition's archive — the place where the touchstone, the fire verdicts, cupellation, and cementation stop being described about craftsmen and start being written by them, in the imperative mood, with quantities. Zosimos of Panopolis, treated in this hub at Zosimos of Panopolis, worked in the same Upper Egyptian world in the same decades; the distance from his visionary alchemy to Leyden X's “remelt it and heat it: if it is pure it will keep its color” is the distance this whole hub exists to measure — and it is shorter, on the evidence of the tests, than it looks.

One hazard note, carried over from the Egyptology treatment because it governs any modern impulse to replicate: the papyri's tests sit among recipes that use mercury, arsenic compounds, and lead as routine ingredients, and their fire procedures presuppose charcoal, bellows, and enclosed heat. They are documented in this article as history and as procedure-understood, not as bench instructions for modern imitation; the toxicology of the corpus is set out unsparingly in the Egyptology division's treatment, and nothing in the present article should be read as softening it.

XII. The Assayer's Toolkit: What Each Test Could and Could Not Prove

The article closes its argument with the table its whole structure has been building toward. Each instrument of the Greek testing system is entered with its cost, its verdict, and its blind spot — because the system is only intelligible as a set of trade-offs, and because the frauds are only intelligible as the exploitation of the right-hand column.

Read down the table and the Greek achievement comes into focus. It is not any single instrument — the stone is Egyptian-Lydian in its roots, cupellation is Bronze Age, cementation is a Lydian industrial secret before it is a Greek text. The achievement is the system: screens layered by cost, verdicts graded by certainty, the destructive tests held in reserve behind the non-destructive ones, the whole apparatus finally picked up by the state and run as a public utility with a salaried slave, a stone law, and a goddess receiving the corpses of false coins. And it is, at the last, a literature: the system wrote itself down — in a philosopher's classification of powers, in an encyclopaedist's fraud warnings, in an architect's anecdote that a sixteen-year-old Galileo would correct sixteen centuries later, and in two Theban recipe books whose every product carries its proof in its final line. The alchemy this hub exists to trace grew up inside that culture of the test. Its central promise — base metal made truly golden — is a dokimasia claim, and its history is unintelligible without the benches on which such claims were checked. Before the Greek world could dream of transmutation, it had learned, the hard way, how to tell gold from everything that merely shines like it. That learning is the foundation. The rest of the wing is built on it.
