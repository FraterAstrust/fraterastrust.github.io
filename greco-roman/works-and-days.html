HESIOD'S WORKS AND DAYS

Ascra · Boeotia · c. 700 BCE

// WORKS :: DAYS :: JUSTICE — WORK — THE YEAR //

The Works and Days is the other half of Hesiod: 828 lines of hexameter addressed to a brother who cheated him, in a village the poet himself calls good at no season. Out of that quarrel it builds the Greek world's first surviving didactic poem — a myth of why men must labour, a doctrine of justice enforced by Zeus, a working farmer's calendar keyed to the stars, and a catalogue of lucky and unlucky days. The Theogony tells how the world was ordered; this poem tells what it costs to live inside the order, in the iron age, on a small farm.

I. The Poem and Its Occasion: A Quarrel at Ascra

The Works and Days (Erga kai Hēmerai) is a poem of 828 lines in dactylic hexameter, attributed to Hesiod and conventionally dated to around 700 BCE — the same broad horizon as the Theogony, with the relative order of the two poems debated and no external document to settle it. It is not a narrative. Nothing in it happens in sequence the way things happen in the Iliad; its unity is the unity of an address. One man is speaking, throughout, to another man, and everything in the poem — myth, fable, calendar, proverb, curse — is something the speaker is saying to him, for a reason the poem states in its first forty lines and never lets the reader forget.

The addressee is Perses, the poet's brother. The occasion, as the poem itself gives it (lines 27–41, paraphrased here), is a lawsuit that is already over. The two brothers had divided their inheritance — the farm their father left at Ascra — but Perses, Hesiod says, seized the greater share and carried it off, “greatly swelling the glory” of the local lords who judged the case, the “bribe-swallowing” or “gift-devouring” kings (dōrophagoi basileis) who loved to judge such a cause. Perses then squandered what he had taken — the poem's portrait of him is of a man who hangs about the courthouse and the marketplace, living on litigation and on other men's goods, and who has now come back, in want, to the brother he wronged. Hesiod's answer is the poem: no more handouts, no second bite at the estate, but instruction instead — “let us settle our dispute here with true judgement”, and let the settlement be a year's food honestly earned.

// HOW MUCH OF THIS IS BIOGRAPHY? //

The quarrel is known only from the poem, and scholars are divided over its status. One line of reading takes it at face value: the allusiveness of the references — the dispute is mentioned obliquely, never narrated in full — is what a real grievance, known to its first audience, would look like. A second line, associated in modern scholarship with Gregory Nagy's account of Hesiod as a traditional poetic persona, treats “Hesiod” and “Perses” as roles the tradition speaks through, and the quarrel as the poem's enabling fiction rather than a court record. This article reports the quarrel as the poem presents it, and flags the dispute where it matters: nothing in the poem's teaching depends on whether a historical Perses lost a historical lawsuit.

The place matters as much as the persons. Ascra was a village in Boeotia, in the valley below Mount Helicon — the mountain where, in the Theogony's proem, the Muses met the shepherd Hesiod and gave him his song. The Works and Days supplies the family's route there (lines 633–640, paraphrased): the poet's father had been a seafaring trader from Cyme in Aeolis, on the coast of Asia Minor, who fled not riches but “wretched poverty, which Zeus lays upon men”, crossed the sea, and settled near Helicon in Ascra — a place the poem characterises, in its most quoted piece of local colour, as bad in winter, sultry in summer, and good at no time. The family is thus immigrant stock, one generation removed from the sea, farming marginal land under a mountain. The poem's economics are the economics of that holding: a small farm, worked by its owner with a slave or two and a yoke of oxen, one bad season away from begging at neighbours' doors — which is exactly the fate the poem predicts for Perses and labours to prevent for itself.

The third party to the address is the kings themselves. The poem speaks to Perses, but it is built to be overheard: the fable of Section V is told explicitly “for princes who themselves understand”, and the justice teaching of Section VI turns and addresses the gift-devouring judges directly, by name and by crime. The basileis of the poem are not Homer's warrior-kings; they are the local aristocracy of an early Greek village, the men who sit in judgement in the agora and whose verdicts, in a world without written law codes or professional courts, are the law for a smallholder. Hesiod's charge against them is precise: they take gifts — bribes — and give crooked judgements. His counter-weapon is the poem's central doctrine, that judgement is watched: Zeus sees, Justice reports, and a city that harbours crooked verdicts starves for them (Section VI). The Works and Days is therefore a double address from its first lines — a brother warned to work, and a bench warned to judge straight — and its famous mixture of farming instruction and moral thunder is not a mixture at all. Both halves answer the same occasion. Perses must stop living by litigation because litigation, in Ascra, is corrupt; and the kings must stop corrupting it because the poem's god keeps accounts.

A POEM WITHOUT A NARRATOR'S DISTANCE

Where the Homeric narrator effaces himself behind the Muse, the Works and Days is saturated with its speaker: his farm, his father, his lawsuit, his one sea crossing, his prize tripod. Whether that “I” is autobiography or persona (see the note in this section), it is the earliest sustained first-person voice in Greek literature, and the didactic tradition it founds (Section X) inherits the posture: the teacher who has done the work he prescribes.

THE TITLE

Erga kai Hēmerai — “Works and Days” — names the poem's two great practical masses: the works, the tasks of the farming year in their seasons (Section VII), and the days, the lucky and unlucky dates of the month (Section IX). The title is ancient and apt, but a reader who stops at it will miss the poem's first three hundred lines, in which neither works nor days appear: the title describes the manual, not the sermon that authorises it.

II. The Proem and the Two Strifes: Hesiod Correcting Hesiod

The proem is ten lines long — against the Theogony's hundred and fifteen — and it does different work. The Theogony opens on Helicon, with the Muses' gift and the poet's commissioning. The Works and Days opens in Pieria, the Muses' older northern home, and what it asks them to sing is not the poet but their father: Zeus, and Zeus specifically as the power that levels. Through him, the proem says, men are famed or unfamed, spoken of or unspoken of, as Zeus wills; he makes the strong man low and raises the obscure, straightens the crooked and blasts the proud. Then, in a single pivot, the hymn becomes a prayer with an address and a petition: Zeus is asked to hear, to see, and to make judgements straight with righteousness — and the poet turns to his brother: “And I, Perses, would tell of true things.”

“Muses of Pieria who give glory through song, come hither, tell of Zeus your father and chant his praise. Through him mortal men are famed or un-famed, sung or unsung alike, as great Zeus wills. For easily he makes strong, and easily he brings the strong man low; easily he humbles the proud and raises the obscure, and easily he straightens the crooked and blasts the proud — Zeus who thunders aloft and has his dwelling most high. Attend thou with eye and ear, and make judgements straight with righteousness. And I, Perses, would tell of true things.” — HESIOD, WORKS AND DAYS 1–10, TRANS. H. G. EVELYN-WHITE (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY, 1914)

Every element of the poem is already in those ten lines. Zeus the leveller is the god of the justice teaching: the power that raises the obscure is the power a defrauded smallholder invokes against gift-fed judges. The petition for straight judgements names the lawsuit without narrating it. And the closing promise — true things, told to Perses — sets the poem's register: this is not the Muses' ambiguous gift of the Theogony, where the goddesses boast that they can speak lies like the truth as readily as truth itself. The Works and Days claims, for its instruction, plain veracity — the claim every didactic poem after it will repeat, and the reason the proem's Zeus is a god of this world, of verdicts and harvests, rather than of cosmic genealogy.

The Revision: One Eris Becomes Two

What follows is the most remarkable act of self-correction in early Greek poetry. The Theogony knows one Eris — Strife — a daughter of Night, mother of Toil and Famine and Sorrows, of Battles and Murders and Quarrels and Lies, a wholly malign power in the genealogy of the world's evils. The Works and Days opens its argument by taking that back. “So, after all, there was not one kind of Strife alone, but all over the earth there are two” (lines 11–12, Evelyn-White). The correction is performed in the open, in the poem's first movement, by the same poetic voice that had sung the single Eris — whether the two poems are by one historical man revising himself, or by a tradition revising its own inheritance, the text presents the doubling of Eris as a discovery announced to the audience: after all, we were wrong; there are two.

“So, after all, there was not one kind of Strife alone, but all over the earth there are two. As for the one, a man would praise her when he came to understand her; but the other is blameworthy: and they are wholly different in nature. For one fosters evil war and battle, being cruel: her no man loves; but perforce, through the will of the deathless gods, men pay harsh Strife her honour due. But the other is the elder daughter of dark Night, and the son of Cronos who sits above and dwells in the aether, set her in the roots of the earth: and she is far kinder to men. She stirs up even the shiftless to toil; for a man grows eager to work when he considers his neighbour, a rich man who hastens to plough and plant and put his house in good order; and neighbour vies with his neighbour as he hurries after wealth. This Strife is wholesome for men. And potter is angry with potter, and craftsman with craftsman, and beggar is jealous of beggar, and minstrel of minstrel.” — HESIOD, WORKS AND DAYS 11–26, TRANS. H. G. EVELYN-WHITE (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY, 1914)

The doctrine is the poem's engine, and it should be read exactly. The bad Eris is the Strife of the Theogony: war, battle, and — the poem will make the application explicit — the strife of the courthouse, contention over other men's goods. The good Eris is her elder sister, set by Zeus himself in the roots of the earth, and her province is emulation: she works not by hatred but by comparison. A man idles until he sees his neighbour's field ploughed, his neighbour's barn filling; then envy — the poem does not flinch from the word's family — becomes industry. Potter against potter, craftsman against craftsman, beggar against beggar, singer against singer: the same rivalry that, aimed at a lawsuit, ruins a household, aimed at a furrow, feeds it. The two Strifes are the poem's moral physics in miniature. There is one human energy, competition, and it has exactly two lawful and unlawful objects: your neighbour's work, which you may strive to match, and your neighbour's goods, which you may not strive to take. Perses' whole error, and the kings' whole crime, is the confusion of the two.

// A GENEALOGICAL PUZZLE, FLAGGED //

The good Eris is here called “the elder daughter of dark Night” — Night is Eris's mother in the Theogony too. But in the Theogony Night's brood is unrelievedly dark, and no second, beneficent Eris appears. Readers should not harmonise the two poems into a single system the texts themselves do not offer: the Works and Days passage reads, and has been read since antiquity, as a revision of the earlier scheme, not a supplement to it. The hub's Theogony article treats the single Eris in her genealogical place; this article treats the pair. The contradiction is Hesiod's own, and it is evidence — of a poet, or a tradition, willing to correct its theology when the theology failed to describe the moral world.

The application to Perses follows at once (lines 27–41, paraphrased). Let the mischief-loving Strife not hold your heart back from work while you peer and listen at the courthouse. A man who has a year's food stored has little concern with quarrels and courts; a man who has not, has nothing else. Perses has had his verdict and spent it; there will be no second chance to deal so again. What remains is the good Strife's road: store the year's grain first, and then, if he must contend, let him contend with his neighbour's harvest and not with his brother's title deeds. The famous aside that closes the passage — that the gift-swallowing lords do not know “how much more the half is than the whole”, nor the good there is in mallow and asphodel, the poor man's wild greens — compresses the whole ethic into a proverb: half a farm worked is worth more than a whole farm stolen and squandered, and a dinner of foraged greens eaten in independence outranks a fortune held by fraud. The poem then asks the question its myths exist to answer: if work is the human lot and emulation its spur, why is life like this? Why is the means of life hidden, why must bread be sweated for at all? The answer is a story about fire.

III. Prometheus and Pandora: Fire, the Jar, and Hope

The poem's first myth (lines 42–105) is an aetiology — an account of origins that explains a present condition. The condition is stated first, before the story: “the gods keep hidden from men the means of life” (line 42, Evelyn-White's rendering). But for that hiding, a man could work one day and live a year on it, hang up his rudder over the smoke, and let the fields rest. Life is not like that, and the poem says why. Zeus hid the means of life in anger, because Prometheus deceived him — the deception, told in full in the Theogony, is the division of the sacrifice at Mecone, where Prometheus portioned an ox so that men kept the meat and the gods received the bones wrapped in fat. Zeus, undeceived in the long run and furious in the short, hid fire. Prometheus stole it back — in the Works and Days' version, as in the Theogony's, carried down to men hidden in a hollow fennel-stalk, the slow-smouldering pith of the giant fennel that Mediterranean peoples used as a portable ember — and Zeus, seeing the theft, pronounced the price. The full treatment of Prometheus, the sacrifice, and the fire-theft across both poems is in this hub's Makers and Fire article; what belongs here is the form the story takes when its purpose is not cosmic order but the explanation of toil.

“Son of Iapetus, surpassing all in cunning, you are glad that you have outwitted me and stolen fire — a great plague to you yourself and to men that shall be. But I will give men as the price for fire an evil thing in which they may all be glad of heart while they embrace their own destruction.” — HESIOD, WORKS AND DAYS 54–59 (ZEUS TO PROMETHEUS), TRANS. H. G. EVELYN-WHITE (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY, 1914)

The “evil thing” given “as the price for fire” is the first woman. In the Theogony she is never named; the Works and Days names her, and the naming is the poem's addition to the myth. Zeus orders the manufacture as a piece of divine craftsmanship, itemised god by god (lines 60–82, paraphrased from Evelyn-White's text): Hephaestus is to mix earth with water and fashion a maiden's shape, lovely as the goddesses; Athena is to teach her needlework and the weaving of the varied web, and to clothe her; Aphrodite is to shed grace on her head, and with it “cruel longing and cares that weary the limbs”; Hermes is to put in her a shameless mind and a deceitful nature, and to give her speech. The Graces and Persuasion hang gold necklaces on her; the Hours crown her with spring flowers. And Hermes names her: Pandora — “because all they who dwelt on Olympus gave each a gift, a plague to men who eat bread”. The name is a Greek compound, pan + dōron, “all-gifted” — the woman endowed by all the gods — though the poem's gloss already bends it toward the darker sense: the gift that is a plague.

// THE POEM'S SEXISM, STATED PLAINLY //

The Pandora of the Works and Days is presented by the poem itself as a “beautiful evil” — the phrase is the Theogony's (kalon kakon, Theogony 585) for the same figure — a snare manufactured to punish men, endowed by her makers with deceit as a design specification. The misogyny is not an imposition of modern readers; it is the text's own framing, and it runs through the poem's later precepts on wives and women (Section IX). This archive reports it as what it is — the sexual politics of an archaic Greek farming poem, in which woman enters the world as a divinely engineered cost, set against the man's labour as its counterpart — and distinguishes that literary and theological construction from any evidence about real women's lives or cult roles in archaic Boeotia, on which the poem is not a witness but an interested party.

Hermes delivers the finished gift to Epimetheus, Prometheus' brother — Afterthought, as his name says, to his brother's Forethought. Prometheus had warned him never to accept a gift from Olympian Zeus, but to send it back, for fear it might prove harmful to men. Epimetheus took the gift, and afterwards, when the evil thing was already his, he understood. Then comes the act on which the whole aetiology turns (lines 90–105). Before it, the tribes of men lived on earth free from ills, from hard toil, and from the heavy sicknesses that bring men to their deaths. But the woman took the great lid off the jar with her hands, and scattered what was inside: sorrows and mischief for men. The diseases now wander the earth silently, by day and by night, of themselves — Zeus took away their voices, so they give no warning. “So is there no way to escape the will of Zeus.”

The Jar: Pithos, Not Box

The vessel is a pithos: a large clay storage jar, of the kind Greek households used — often sunk into the floor of a storeroom — for grain, oil, and wine, and which could stand as tall as a person. It is not a box, a casket, or a chest, and nothing in Hesiod's Greek suggests one. The universal modern phrase “Pandora's box” is a Renaissance artefact, and its origin can be named with unusual precision. Erasmus of Rotterdam, retelling the story in Latin in his Adagia (1508), in illustration of the proverb Malo accepto stultus sapit — “from experiencing trouble a fool is made wise” — rendered the Greek pithos as pyxis, a small box or casket. The attribution of the mistranslation to Erasmus's 1508 retelling is the standard scholarly account, repeated in the reference literature (the Oxford University Press's own discussion of the myth states it in those terms); a minority tradition has also named the Italian humanist Giglio Gregorio Giraldi (Lilius Giraldus) as a source of the box, and the honest summary is that the pyxis enters the story in sixteenth-century Latin retelling, with Erasmus as its best-documented and most influential carrier. The consequences were not trivial. A jar is a household fixture, too big to carry, part of the furniture of the storeroom — the evils were, so to speak, already in the house. A box is a portable, lockable, openable object, a thing of curiosity; the Renaissance image of Pandora with her casket rewrote the myth's mechanics, shifting its centre toward the woman's curiosity — a motive Hesiod never states. Hesiod does not say why Pandora lifted the lid. He does not say she was curious, or that she had been forbidden to open it. The jar is simply there, in Epimetheus' house, and she opens it. Later tradition supplied the prohibition and the curiosity; the poem supplies neither, and the difference is a standing lesson in this archive's discipline: the famous version of a myth is usually a sediment of retellings, and the earliest text is stranger and barer than all of them.

Elpis Under the Rim: The Poem's Open Question

“Only Hope remained there in an unbreakable home within under the rim of the great jar, and did not fly out at the door; for ere that, the lid of the jar stopped her, by the will of Aegis-holding Zeus who gathers the clouds. But the rest, countless plagues, wander amongst men; for earth is full of evils and the sea is full.” — HESIOD, WORKS AND DAYS 96–101, TRANS. H. G. EVELYN-WHITE (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY, 1914)

One thing did not escape. Elpis — Hope, or Expectation; the Greek word covers both the cheerful and the neutral sense, anticipation of what is coming, good or bad — remained under the rim of the jar, stopped by the lid, “by the will of Zeus”. Hesiod does not explain the detail, and it has been argued over ever since, because the poem's whole theodicy pivots on how it is read. The positions, stated as the scholarship standardly states them, are these. On the consoling reading, hope is preserved for mankind: the evils escaped into the world, but hope stayed in the house, within human reach — the one good thing men keep at home while the plagues wander abroad. On the pessimistic reading, hope is withheld from mankind: it alone of the jar's contents did not get out, shut in by Zeus's express will, so that men suffer their evils without even the mitigation of expectation — or, in a darker variant of the same reading, elpis was itself one of the jar's ambiguous contents, and its retention is a further cruelty, since hope in Hesiod's world is not reliably a blessing: the idle man of line 498 (in Evelyn-White's rendering) “waits on empty hope”, and the poem repeatedly treats hope as the pauper's snare, the substitute for stored grain. A third line of scholarship notes that elpis in early Greek is as often foreboding as comfort, and declines to decide the passage's valence at all. This article flags the question as genuinely open: the text gives the fact — elpis stayed, by Zeus's will — and no interpretation, and any retelling that presents the staying of hope as straightforwardly good news has added something Hesiod withheld.

What the myth explains, taken whole, is the poem's premise. The means of life are hidden; fire was bought at the price of a beautiful evil; diseases walk silently; there is no way to escape the will of Zeus. Therefore — the poem's logic is relentless — a man works, or he starves, and the only choice the world offers is between the good Strife's labour and the bad Strife's litigation. The next myth widens the frame from the household to the whole of human history: if this is the iron present, what were the past ages, and how did the world get from there to here?

IV. The Five Ages: Metal, Heroes, and Iron

The second myth (lines 109–201) is introduced as “another tale”, to be laid up in Perses' heart: how gods and mortal men sprang from one source. It is the famous account of the Five Ages — five successive races of men, each made by the gods, each living its character, each covered by the earth before the next. Four of the races are named for metals, in descending order of worth: gold, silver, bronze, iron. The fifth name on the list is not a metal at all, and its presence is the scheme's most discussed feature. The races are not, strictly, ages in the text — Hesiod's word is genos, a race or generation, each separately created, not one humanity declining through time — though “the Five Ages” is the conventional English title and is used here with that caution attached.

The Heroic Anomaly

The scheme's logic is metallic decline: each race worse than the last, gold to silver to bronze to iron, value falling as the metal cheapens. The heroic race breaks the series twice over. It is not a metal, and it is not worse than what precedes it — it is expressly “nobler and more righteous” than the bronze race before it, an improvement inserted into a decline. The explanation is not far to seek, and scholarship states it plainly: the heroes could not be omitted, because Greek tradition — the whole epic inheritance, the wars at Thebes and Troy that the Theban and Trojan cycles sing — placed a race of demigods immediately before the present age of ordinary men, and Hesiod's audience knew their names. A purely metallic scheme would have had to erase them; the poem instead interrupts its own pattern to accommodate them, and the interruption is the most valuable thing in the myth for a historian of tradition. It shows the scheme being fitted to inherited material that resists it — Hesiod, or the tradition behind him, bending a tidy four-metal decline around the untidy fact of the heroes. The hub's Heroes and Epic article treats the heroic race from the heroes' side; here the point is structural: the anomaly is not a flaw in the poem but a fossil of its making, the seam where an abstract scheme and a concrete tradition were stitched together.

Note, too, what the heroic race's wars are. Thebes and Troy: the two great subjects of the epic cycles, the wars for the flocks of Oedipus and for Helen. The Works and Days, the poem of the small farm and the lawsuit, contains in its second myth a compressed table of contents of heroic epic — and places it one generation back, in a better world, now closed. The heroes are gone to the Isles of the Blessed, at the ends of the earth, where Cronos — the deposed king of the golden race's heaven — rules over them, released from his bonds. The present is defined by their absence.

“Thereafter, would that I were not among the men of the fifth generation, but either had died before or been born afterwards. For now truly is a race of iron, and men never rest from labour and sorrow by day, and from perishing by night; and the gods shall lay sore trouble upon them. But, notwithstanding, even these shall have some good mingled with their evils.” — HESIOD, WORKS AND DAYS 174–179, TRANS. H. G. EVELYN-WHITE (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY, 1914)

The iron race is described twice: first in its present condition — ceaseless labour, sorrow by day, perishing by night, some good mingled with the evils — and then, in the poem's most prophetic passage (lines 180–201, paraphrased), in its future degeneration, the signs by which its end will be known. Zeus will destroy this race also, when children are born already grey at the temples. Father will not agree with children, nor guest with host, nor brother with brother — the poem's audience cannot have missed that the first symptom of the world's end is the poem's own occasion, the broken bond between brothers. Men will dishonour their ageing parents and chide them with bitter words, not knowing the fear of the gods; they will not repay the cost of their nurture. Might will be right; the oath-keeper, the just, and the good will win no favour, while the evil-doer and the violent man are praised. Envy, foul-mouthed and scowling, will walk with all wretched men. And then the end:

“And then Aidos and Nemesis, with their sweet forms wrapped in white robes, will go from the wide-pathed earth and forsake mankind to join the company of the deathless gods: and bitter sorrows will be left for mortal men, and there will be no help against evil.” — HESIOD, WORKS AND DAYS 200–201, TRANS. H. G. EVELYN-WHITE (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY, 1914)

Aidos — Shame, Reverence, the inward check that makes a man regard another's rights — and Nemesis — Indignation, the outward check, the community's anger at wrong — are the last two moral powers left on earth in the iron age, and the poem ends its myth of history with their departure. The personifications belong to the world of the Olympians article in this hub, where the lesser powers around Zeus's throne are set out; here their function is diagnostic. As long as Aidos and Nemesis remain, the iron age, for all its labour and fraud, is still a moral world: men can still be ashamed, and wrong can still provoke. Their leaving is the true end of the race — not a catastrophe of nature but the extinction of the two feelings on which the poem's entire teaching depends. The Works and Days is addressed, from beginning to end, to men presumed still capable of both.

The myth's function in the address is now complete. Pandora explained why the individual man must work; the Five Ages explain where his working life sits in the history of the world — in the last and worst of the races, but not yet at its end, with good still mingled in the evil and Aidos still on earth. What remains is to convert the diagnosis into conduct. The poem does it in two strokes: first a fable, addressed over Perses' head to the kings; then the doctrine of justice, addressed to everyone.

V. The Hawk and the Nightingale: Greek Literature's First Fable

At line 202 the poem changes instrument. “And now I will tell a fable for princes who themselves understand” — the word is ainos, a tale with a point, a story that means something other than what it says and trusts its hearer to draw the meaning. What follows (lines 203–212) is eleven lines long, and it is the earliest animal fable to survive in Greek literature: the ancestor, in the form we have it, of the whole Aesopic tradition that would later attach itself to the name of Aesop. A hawk, carrying a nightingale high up among the clouds, gripped in his talons — the nightingale, the songbird, pierced by the crooked talons, crying pitifully — addresses her, and the speech is the whole fable. There is no narrative beyond the grip and the speech; there is no moral appended. The hawk speaks, and the poem moves on.

“Thus said the hawk to the nightingale with speckled neck, while he carried her high up among the clouds, gripped fast in his talons, and she, pierced by his crooked talons, cried pitifully. To her he spoke disdainfully: 'Miserable thing, why do you cry out? One far stronger than you now holds you fast, and you must go wherever I take you, songstress as you are. And if I please I will make my meal of you, or let you go. He is a fool who tries to withstand the stronger, for he does not get the mastery and suffers pain besides his shame.' So said the swiftly flying hawk, the long-winged bird.” — HESIOD, WORKS AND DAYS 203–212, TRANS. H. G. EVELYN-WHITE (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY, 1914)

The fable's difficulty is its point, and it has troubled readers since antiquity: whose voice is the hawk's, and what is Perses — or the princes — supposed to learn? The hawk's doctrine is naked: the stronger holds, the weaker goes where he is taken, resistance adds shame to pain. Read as the poet's own advice, the fable would counsel submission to power — and would flatly contradict the justice teaching that follows immediately, in which violence is ruin and Justice beats Outrage in the end. The standard scholarly resolutions, stated as positions, are these. On the ventriloquism reading, the hawk speaks the kings' own doctrine back to them: this is what your crooked judgements sound like from underneath, in the talons; the fable is addressed to princes “who themselves understand” precisely so that they will recognise their own voice in the predator's mouth, and recoil. On the warning reading, the fable is for Perses: this is the world as the strong describe it, and the brother who relies on courts and litigation is the nightingale — a singer, like the poet himself, whose art is no defence against talons; the moral is to get out of the strong men's grip and onto the farm, where a man's substance is his own work and not a verdict. The two readings are not exclusive, and the poem's next line supports both at once: “But you, Perses, listen to right and do not foster violence” — the fable is the dark premise, and the justice teaching is the answer to it. What the fable refuses to do is resolve itself. An ainos that explained itself would not need princes who understand.

The fable also marks a formal first. Greek literature before this poem has myth, genealogy, hymn, and proverb, but no surviving beast-fable with a speaking animal delivering a maxim of power. Within a few generations the form is everywhere: Archilochus, in the seventh century, is already working in fable — his fox and eagle ainos turns the same instrument against a faithless partner — and the tradition that crystallises around Aesop makes the beast-fable the Mediterranean's standard vehicle for speaking truth to power at one remove. The line of descent from Hesiod's hawk is not provable in detail — fable is an oral, popular form, and the poem is its first surviving instance, not demonstrably its invention — and this article states the claim in exactly those terms: first in the surviving record, told to kings, and already fully formed in its bleakness.

VI. The Justice Teaching: Dikē Against Hubris

The poem's doctrinal centre (lines 213–285) is a teaching on dikē — justice, right, the straight judgement — against hubris — violence, outrage, the strong man's treatment of the weak as prey. It is addressed in turn to Perses, to the kings, and to the city at large, and it is the archaic Greek world's fullest surviving statement of a doctrine that later Greek thought inherits whole: that justice is not a human convention but a divine institution, watched and enforced by Zeus, with consequences that fall on communities as well as men. The Olympians article treats Dikē among the powers of Zeus's order; this section treats her at work, in the poem that is her first great text.

The teaching opens with a law of consequences, stated as a race (lines 212–218, paraphrased from Evelyn-White): violence is bad for a poor man, and even the prosperous cannot bear its burden when he falls into ruin; the better road runs the other way, toward justice — “for Justice beats Outrage when she comes at length to the end of the race. But only when he has suffered does the fool learn this.” Then the poem personifies its doctrine. Oath runs at the pace of crooked judgements. Justice herself, when she is dragged where the bribe-devourers take her, follows weeping through the city, wrapped in mist, bringing mischief on the men who drove her out. And she has a court of appeal: Dikē is a virgin, daughter of Zeus, honoured among the Olympians, and when men hurt her with lying slander she sits beside her father and tells him of men's wicked hearts, until the people pay for the folly of their princes. The imagery is juridical through and through — a wronged litigant, an appeal to a higher bench, a judgement executed on the community that harboured the corrupt court.

The Two Cities

The teaching's centrepiece is a diptych: the city that judges straight, and the city that practises violence (lines 225–247). It is the earliest surviving statement in Greek of the idea that a community's justice determines its material flourishing — and its terms are entirely material. For the just city, in Evelyn-White's rendering: their city flourishes and the people prosper in it; Peace, the nurse of children, is abroad in their land; Zeus never decrees cruel war against them; neither famine nor disaster haunts men who do true justice; the earth bears them victual in plenty, the oak on the mountain bears acorns at the top and bees in its midst, their sheep are laden with fleeces, their women bear children like their parents; they flourish continually with good things, and they do not travel on ships, for the grain-giving earth bears them fruit. For the unjust, the same ledger reversed: often a whole city suffers for one bad man who sins and devises presumptuous deeds; Zeus lays famine and plague together on the people, so that men perish, women do not bear children, and houses dwindle; or he destroys their army, or their walls, or makes an end of their ships on the sea.

THE JUST CITY (225–237)

Straight judgements to strangers and townsmen alike → the city flourishes; Peace nurses the children; no war decreed by Zeus; no famine, no disaster; earth, oak, and flock bear in plenty; children resemble their parents; no need to sail. Justice is cashed out in crops, children, and safety — the smallholder's whole list of goods.

THE UNJUST CITY (238–247)

Violence and cruel deeds → a whole city suffers for one bad man: famine and plague together; men perish; women bear no children; houses dwindle; or the army, the walls, or the ships are destroyed. The punishment is collective even when the sin is individual — the doctrine's hardest edge, and the reason the kings are addressed as men whose verdicts starve other men's children.

THE WATCHERS (248–255)

The gods are near among men, and mark those who oppress their fellows with crooked judgements. Zeus has set thrice ten thousand spirits on the earth — the golden race become daimones (Section IV) — watchers of mortal men, who keep watch on judgements and deeds of wrong as they roam, clothed in mist, all over the earth. The surveillance is total and invisible: no verdict is given in private.

THE EYE OF ZEUS (267–273)

“The eye of Zeus, seeing all and understanding all, beholds these things too, if so he will, and fails not to mark what sort of justice is this that the city keeps within it.” (Evelyn-White.) The passage darkens into the poem's most honest moment: Hesiod wishes neither himself nor his son to be just among men, if the unjust are to have the greater right — and then recovers: “But I think that all-wise Zeus will not yet bring that to pass.” The faith is stated as a resolve against the evidence, not a deduction from it.

The teaching closes with its foundation laid bare (lines 274–285, paraphrased). Zeus ordained this law for men: that fishes and beasts and winged fowls should devour one another, for right is not in them; but to mankind he gave justice, which proves far the best. The hawk's doctrine, in other words, is true — for hawks. It is the law of the creatures who have no dikē. Man's distinction is not strength or song but the possession of a norm the animals lack, and the whole fable of Section V is retroactively glossed: the king who judges like a hawk has not risen above the nightingale's world but sunk below his own. Whoever knows the right and is ready to speak it, Zeus gives prosperity; the false witness ruins his own generation; the generation of the man who swears truly is better thenceforward. The doctrine is complete: a divine gift, a watching god, a reporting goddess, thirty thousand invisible witnesses, and a ledger kept in harvests and children.

// DOCTRINE, NOT DESCRIPTION //

The justice teaching is a claim about the world, made by an interested party — a man who had lost a lawsuit he believed corrupt — and it should not be read as evidence that archaic Greek justice worked as it describes, or that its audiences believed verdicts moved harvests in any simple way. What it evidences is the availability, by c. 700 BCE, of a fully articulated theology of justice: dikē as Zeus's gift distinguishing man from beast, enforced by surveillance and collective consequence. Later Greek philosophy and Athenian courtroom rhetoric inherit the vocabulary wholesale. The poem's doctrine is treated across this hub as Hesiod's teaching — the earliest great statement of it — and not as a report on Boeotian legal practice, for which the poem is a plaintiff's pamphlet, not a record.

After the doctrine, the poem descends — deliberately — into the gnomic register: a long run of proverbs and precepts (lines 286–382) on work, wealth, neighbours, and speech, including the steep road to Goodness that the gods have set sweat before, and the warning against the woman who coaxes a man with her mind on his barn. These precepts are the bridge to the poem's second half. Doctrine has said why a man works; now the poem says when, and how, and in what order — the works themselves.

VII. The Farming Calendar: The Poem as Procedure

At line 383 the poem becomes what its title promises: a manual. The section that runs to line 617 is the earliest surviving European farming calendar, and this archive — which prizes, above all other contents, the repeatable procedure set down exactly enough to be followed — treats it as the poem's technical core. It is organised not by the civil calendar of months, which in Hesiod's Greece varied from city to city and drifted against the seasons, but by the risings and settings of stars, checked against animal and plant signs: a calendar any farmer could read off the sky and the hedgerow, needing no official almanac. Its master clock is the Pleiades.

“When the Pleiades, daughters of Atlas, are rising, begin your harvest, and your ploughing when they are going to set. Forty nights and days they are hidden and appear again as the year moves round, when first you sharpen your sickle. This is the law of the plains, and of those who live near the sea, and who inhabit rich country, the glens and dingles far from the tossing sea — strip to sow and strip to plough and strip to reap, if you wish to get in all Demeter's fruits in due season, and that each kind may grow in its season.” — HESIOD, WORKS AND DAYS 383–392, TRANS. H. G. EVELYN-WHITE (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY, 1914)

The astronomy, in modern terms, is this. The Pleiades' morning rising — their first visibility before dawn after their period of invisibility — fell in Hesiod's latitude and era in about mid-May: harvest time for grain sown the previous autumn. Their morning setting fell in about early November: the season to plough and sow. Between the two, the cluster is hidden for some forty days around its conjunction with the sun — the poem's “forty nights and days they are hidden” is an accurate observation, and a checkable one. The whole year of works hangs on those two star-events, with subsidiary signs filling the intervals. What follows is the calendar set out as the sequence of operations it is — the poem's order, with its signs, its tools, and its timings, paraphrased from the text (lines 383–617) except where quotation is marked.

STEP 1

PREPARE THE HOLDING (405–413)

First of all, get a house, a woman, and an ox for the plough — the poem specifies a slave woman, not a wife, to follow the oxen — and make everything ready at home, so that you need not ask of another and be refused while the season passes. Do not put work off till tomorrow and the day after: the sluggish worker does not fill his barn, and the man who puts off work is always at hand-grips with ruin.

STEP 2

CUT TIMBER IN AUTUMN (414–436)

When the sun's piercing heat abates and Zeus sends the autumn rains, and Sirius passes over men's heads only briefly by day, the wood cut then is least liable to worm. Hew your timber now, to measure: a mortar three feet wide, a pestle three cubits long, an axle of seven feet (eight, and you can cut a mallet from it as well), a felloe three spans across for a wagon of ten palms' width. Find a plough-tree of holm-oak, the strongest wood for oxen to plough with; poles of laurel or elm are freest from worms; a share-beam of oak.

STEP 3

KEEP TWO PLOUGHS, AND THE RIGHT TEAM (437–447)

Get two ploughs ready at home — one of a single piece, one jointed — for if you break one, you can put the oxen to the other. Get two oxen, bulls of nine years, in their prime, who will not fight in the furrow and break the plough. Let a man of forty follow them, fed on a loaf of four quarters and eight slices, who will drive a straight furrow and keep his mind on his work: no younger man is better at scattering seed without double-sowing.

STEP 4

PLOUGH AT THE CRANE'S CALL (448–492)

Mark when you hear the crane crying year by year from the clouds: she gives the signal for ploughing and shows the season of rainy winter. Feed up the oxen in the byre. When ploughing time is proclaimed, make haste, you and your slaves alike, in wet and in dry, early in the morning. Plough in spring; break fallow in summer; sow fallow while the soil is still light. At the first furrow, pray to Zeus of the Earth and to pure Demeter for sound, heavy grain, one hand on the plough-tail, the stick coming down on the oxen's backs; let a slave follow with a mattock to cover the seed from the birds. Plough late — at the winter solstice — and you will reap sitting, grasping a thin crop; the late plougher's only remedy is the cuckoo's call and three days' rain measured to an ox's hoof.

STEP 5

SURVIVE WINTER (493–563)

Pass by the smithy and its crowded lounge in winter, when the cold keeps men from the fields, lest bitter winter catch you helpless and poor. In midsummer command the slaves: it will not always be summer — build barns. Avoid the month Lenaeon (roughly January), wretched days, all fit to skin an ox, when Boreas blows through an ox's hide and through a goat's hair, though not through the sheep's abundant fleece. Dress for it: a soft cloak and a tunic to the feet, thick woof on thin warp; boots of slaughtered ox-hide lined with felt; firstling kids' skins stitched with ox-sinew against the rain; a felt cap to keep the ears dry. Halve the oxen's fodder in this season, and let the man have more, for the nights are long.

STEP 6

PRUNE AT THE SWALLOW (564–570)

When Zeus has finished sixty wintry days after the solstice, Arcturus leaves the stream of Ocean and rises brilliant at dusk. After him comes the swallow, daughter of Pandion, when spring is just beginning. Before she comes, prune the vines — it is best so.

STEP 7

HARVEST WHEN THE PLEIADES RISE (571–608)

When the snail climbs the plants to escape the Pleiades, stop digging vineyards: whet the sickles and rouse the slaves. Avoid shady seats and sleeping till dawn in harvest season, when the sun scorches the body; dawn takes a third part of a man's work. When the artichoke flowers and the grasshopper pours his song from the tree, goats are plumpest and wine sweetest — rest then, in shade, with wine of Biblis and curds, facing the fresh Zephyr. When Orion first appears, set the slaves to winnow Demeter's holy grain on a smooth threshing-floor in an airy place; measure it and store it in jars. Feed the jagged-toothed dog well, lest the Day-sleeper — the thief — take your stuff.

STEP 8

VINTAGE, AND THE YEAR CLOSES (609–617)

When Orion and Sirius stand in mid-heaven and Dawn sees Arcturus, cut off all the grape-clusters and bring them home. Show them to the sun ten days and ten nights; cover them for five; on the sixth day draw off into vessels the gifts of joyful Dionysus. But when the Pleiades and Hyades and strong Orion begin to set, remember to plough in season — and so the completed year will fitly pass beneath the earth. The calendar ends where it began: at the setting of the Pleiades, with the plough.

Read as procedure, the calendar's character comes clear. It is a smallholder's manual, scaled to one farm: one yoke of oxen, one ploughman, a slave with a mattock, stores kept in jars in the house. Its timings are observational, not calendrical — stars, the crane's migration, the cuckoo, the snail, the artichoke flower — a redundant system of signs, so that a cloudy week at one sign's date does not ruin the year. Its specifications are exact where exactness matters: the age of the oxen, the age and rations of the ploughman, the dimensions of mortar, axle, and felloe, the woods for each part of the plough, the ten-five-six day schedule for the vintage. And it is threaded with the doctrine of the poem's first half: pray at the plough-tail, store against hunger, do not beg, do not idle at the smithy. The calendar is the justice teaching translated into a timetable — the good Strife, given dates.

// READING THE CALENDAR AS A MODERN READER //

The star-dates are epoch- and latitude-specific: precession has shifted the Pleiades' risings and settings since 700 BCE, and they fall differently at other latitudes, so the poem's calendar cannot be lifted directly into a modern almanac. What transfers is the method — keying operations to observed celestial and biological signs rather than to fixed civil dates — and the sequence itself, which remains a sound order of operations for a Mediterranean dry-farming year: autumn timber, autumn ploughing and sowing, winter maintenance and restraint, spring pruning, early-summer harvest, threshing, vintage, and back to the plough.

VIII. The Sailing Section: The Reluctant Sailor

The calendar's year is closed, and the poem turns, almost apologetically, to the sea (lines 618–694). The turn is motivated by the doctrine: a farm that suffices needs no ships — the just city of Section VI “does not travel on ships, for the grain-giving earth bears them fruit” — but Perses' father had sailed for a living, and Perses might. So the poem gives sailing the same treatment as ploughing: a season, a procedure, and a warning — from a teacher who begins by disclaiming the expertise he is about to exercise, in the poem's most famous autobiographical passage.

The procedure first (lines 618–645, paraphrased). When the Pleiades plunge into the misty sea to escape Orion's rude strength — their setting, in autumn — the gales of all kinds rage: keep ships no longer on the sparkling sea. Haul your ship up on the land and pack it closely with stones all round against the damp-blowing winds; draw out the bilge-plug so the rain of heaven may not rot her; put away all the tackle and fittings in the house, stow the ship's “wings” — her sails — neatly, and hang the well-shaped rudder up over the smoke. Then wait for the sailing season. When it comes, haul the swift ship down, stow a convenient cargo, and trade as the poet's father did. Admire a small ship, the poem adds, but put your freight in a large one: the greater the lading, the greater the gain — if the winds keep back their harmful gales. It is winterising instructions for a wooden merchantman, complete in nine lines: haul out, chock with stones, drain, strip, store the gear under cover, smoke the rudder.

The One Crossing: Aulis to Chalcis

Then the disclaimer, which becomes the poem's autobiographical anchor (lines 646–662, paraphrased from Evelyn-White's text). If ever you turn your heart to trading, Hesiod says, I will show you the measures of the loud-roaring sea, though I have no skill in seafaring nor in ships; for never yet have I sailed by ship over the wide sea, but only to Euboea from Aulis — Aulis, where the Achaeans once waited through storm for the gathering against Troy. From Aulis he crossed the strait to Chalcis, to the funeral games of wise Amphidamas, where the dead man's sons had proclaimed and appointed prizes. “And there I boast that I gained the victory with a song and carried off a handled tripod, which I dedicated to the Muses of Helicon, in the place where they first set me in the way of clear song.” Such, he concludes, is all my experience of many-pegged ships; nevertheless I will tell you the will of Zeus — for the Muses have taught me to sing.

// WHY THIS PASSAGE MATTERS //

The Chalcis passage does three kinds of work at once. It is the poem's certificate of its speaker's profession: the farmer is also a prize-winning singer, victor in the agonistic world of funeral games — the good Strife of Section II, in its artistic form, singer against singer. It is a link to the Theogony, whose proem tells the same commissioning from the other side: the Muses of Helicon who received the tripod are the Muses who met the shepherd. And it is the seed of a later legend: the tradition of a singing contest between Homer and Hesiod, preserved in the Contest of Homer and Hesiod, grows from this soil — the two founding singers, matched against each other at Chalcis, with Hesiod awarded the tripod. The legend is later than the poem and is treated in this hub's Homeric Epics article's orbit; the games, the prize, and the dedication are the poem's own testimony, and stand as its one dated, placed, public event.

The sailing advice proper (lines 663–694, paraphrased) is conservative to the point of refusal. Fifty days after the summer solstice, when the season of wearisome heat is ended, is the right time for sailing: then the winds are steady and the sea harmless, and a man may trust the winds without care — but he must make all haste to return, and not wait for the new wine and the autumn rain and the storms of Notus. There is also a spring sailing, when the leaves on the topmost shoot of the fig-tree are as large as a crow's footprint — “for my part I do not praise it, for my heart does not like it. Such a sailing is snatched, and you will hardly avoid mischief.” And the section's closing rule generalises the caution into the poem's ethic of measure: do not put all your goods in hollow ships; leave the greater part behind and put the lesser part on board. Observe due measure: proportion is best in all things.

The reluctance is the point. The sea, in this poem, is what a man turns to when the land has failed him — as it failed the poet's father, who sailed “because he lacked sufficient livelihood” and fled poverty across it to Ascra. Trade is lawful, its seasons are given, its procedure is set out fairly; but it is the recourse of need, not the life the poem recommends, and its patron virtue is not daring but measure. Set beside this hub's sea-poems, the contrast is the point: the Works and Days is the archaic world's great anti-sea poem, written from the shore, by a man who crossed the Euripus once, won a tripod, and came home.

IX. Precepts and Days: The Poem's Social World

The poem's last hundred and thirty lines (695–828) fall into two masses: a run of social and ritual precepts (695–764), and the Days proper (765–828), the catalogue of the month's lucky and unlucky dates that gives the poem the second half of its title. Together they are the fullest surviving picture of the everyday normative world of an archaic Greek village — what a man should do about a wife, a friend, a feast, a river, a libation, and a calendar — and they are presented here as the poem presents them: as the world Perses is to live in, once he is working.

Marriage, Neighbours, and the Conduct of Life

The marriage precepts (lines 695–705, paraphrased) are precise and chilling in equal measure. Bring home a wife when you are not far short of thirty, nor much above; let the wife have been grown up four years, and marry her in the fifth — that is, at about nineteen by the poem's arithmetic. Marry a maiden, so that you can teach her careful ways; marry one who lives near you, but look well about you first, lest your marriage be a joke to your neighbours. For a man wins nothing better than a good wife — and nothing worse than a bad one, a greedy soul who roasts her man without fire and brings him to a raw old age. The passage is of a piece with Section III's Pandora construction, and is flagged with the same caution: this is the poem's ideology of marriage — an economic partnership, contracted late by the man, in which the wife is assessed as a household asset or liability — reported as the text's teaching, not as a neutral record of archaic practice.

The neighbour precepts, which begin earlier in the poem (lines 342–351) and colour this whole section, deserve their standing as the poem's social philosophy in miniature. Call your friend to a feast, and especially call him who lives near you: for if any mischief happens in the place, neighbours come ungirt, but kinsmen stay to gird themselves. A bad neighbour is as great a plague as a good one is a blessing; not even an ox would die but for a bad neighbour. Take fair measure from your neighbour, and pay him back fairly with the same measure, or better if you can — so that if you are in need afterwards, you may find him sure. In a world without insurance, credit markets, or police, the neighbour is all three, and the poem's economics of reciprocal measure — give to one who gives, for Give is a good girl but Take is bad and brings death — is the good Strife domesticated: a competition in reliability, conducted over a lifetime, at the distance of a fence line.

Ritual Purity: The Body and the Gods

Woven through the precepts is a strand of ritual regulation (lines 724–764, paraphrased) that modern readers often skip and the poem plainly does not: it stands in the same imperative register as the ploughing instructions, and for the poem's first audience it belonged to the same category — procedure that works, or fails, with consequences. Never pour a libation of sparkling wine to Zeus after dawn with unwashed hands, nor to any of the immortals, else they do not hear your prayers but spit them back. Do not make water standing upright facing the sun, nor on the road or off it uncovered: the nights belong to the blessed gods; a scrupulous man sits down, or goes to the wall of an enclosed court. Do not beget children when you have come back from an ill-omened burial, but after a festival of the gods. Never cross the sweet-flowing water of ever-rolling rivers afoot until you have prayed, gazing into the flood, and washed your hands in the clear water: whoever crosses with hands unwashed of wickedness, the gods are angry with him and bring trouble on him afterwards. At a cheerful festival, do not cut the withered from the quick on that which has five branches — your nails — with bright steel. Never put the ladle on the mixing-bowl at a wine party, for malignant ill-luck is attached to that. When you build a house, do not leave it rough-hewn, lest a cawing crow settle on it and croak. The strand closes with a personification to match Section VI's: Talk — Phēmē, Rumour — is mischievous, light, easily raised, hard to bear and difficult to be rid of; and Talk never wholly dies when many people voice her: even Talk is in some ways divine.

// POEM, NOT ETHNOGRAPHY //

These precepts are prescriptive verse, not field notes. Some correspond to ritual rules attested elsewhere in Greek practice — hand-washing before libation is universal Greek custom — while others (the ladle on the mixing-bowl, the crow on the unfinished house) survive nowhere else and may be local, proverbial, or the poet's own. The section is evidence for the categories of an archaic villager's religious world — purity, omen, the gods' attention to small acts — and for the poem's claim to govern that world. It is not, and is not used in this hub as, a checklist of what Boeotians in 700 BCE actually did at riversides.

The Days: Hemerology

The poem closes (lines 765–828) with the Days: a day-by-day characterisation of the lunar month, marking which dates are holy, which are good for which works, and which are to be avoided. “Mark the days which come from Zeus, duly telling your slaves of them” — the thirtieth day is best for looking over the work and dealing out supplies. The first, the fourth, and the seventh — the day on which Leto bore Apollo of the golden sword — are holy. The eighth and ninth of the waxing month are specially good for the works of man. The eleventh and twelfth are both excellent, alike for shearing sheep and for reaping; but the twelfth is much better than the eleventh, for on it the airy-swinging spider spins its web in full day, and the wise ant gathers her pile: on that day a woman should set up her loom. Avoid the thirteenth of the waxing month for beginning to sow, though it is the best day for setting plants. The mid-month sixth is very unfavourable for plants, but good for the birth of males, unfavourable for a girl to be born at all or to be married; it is kindly for gelding kids and sheep and for fencing a sheep-cote. On the eighth of the month geld the boar and the loud-bellowing bull, but hard-working mules on the twelfth. On the great twentieth, in full day, a wise man should be born. On the fourth of the month bring home your bride — having first discerned the birds' omens. And so through the month: days for launching a ship, for taming oxen to the hand, for birthdays whose children's characters the date is said to fix, and days fraught with fate, to be avoided for the troubles that eat out the heart.

4th Bring home the bride on the 4th, having discerned the birds; but the 4ths of the month's beginning and ending are also days “very fraught with fate”, to be guarded against for heart-eating troubles — the catalogue's valuations are task-specific, not simple
30th Best day for looking over the work and dealing out supplies

The genre is hemerology — the lore of days — and it is far older and wider than Hesiod: Babylonian hemerologies and Egyptian calendars of lucky and unlucky days precede the poem by centuries, a parallel this hub notes without claiming a channel of transmission for the Greek text. Within Greek literature the Days found the whole genre: the character of a date, fixed by the gods and readable in advance, becomes a standing feature of Greek practical religion, and day-lore of Hesiodic type is still being copied, adapted, and argued over in Byzantine calendars.

// THE DAYS' AUTHENTICITY, FLAGGED //

The authenticity of the Days has been questioned since the nineteenth century: scholars have doubted whether the catalogue (765–828) is Hesiod's own ending or an early accretion to the poem. Wilamowitz omitted the section entirely from his 1928 edition; Solmsen bracketed it in the Oxford Classical Text. Against the doubt, Lardinois (1998) argued that the Days fit the Works — that the catalogue's concerns, and its place as the poem's final movement from works to the days in which works are done, are integral to the poem's design. M. L. West prints the section, with its difficulties noted. This article presents the Days as the transmitted poem's close, and flags the dispute rather than resolving it: the manuscript tradition carries the section, and its excision is an editorial judgement, not a manuscript fact.

The poem thus ends where its title said it would: with days. It began with a god who straightens the crooked; it ends with a calendar in which even time itself is sorted into the favourable and the fraught. Between those poles it has built the complete operating system of a smallholder's world — myth, doctrine, calendar, conduct, and dates. The rest of this article follows what the world did with it.

X. The Didactic Tradition: Aratus, Virgil, and the Commentators

The Works and Days is the founding document of didactic poetry in the West: verse whose declared business is to teach — a body of knowledge, a craft, a way of life — in the metre and diction of epic. The genre it founds is not a marginal one. For a thousand years, when a Greek or Roman poet wanted to put systematic knowledge into verse, the Hesiodic posture was the available instrument: a teacher-persona, an addressee, a divine sanction, and information organised for memory. This section traces the three channels through which the poem's influence runs — the Hellenistic star-poem, the Roman farm-poem, and the commentary tradition — and states, for each, what is documented and what is inference.

Aratus: The Calendar Becomes Astronomy

Aratus of Soli (third century BCE) wrote the Phaenomena, a hexameter poem of just over a thousand lines on the constellations and on weather signs — the most successful didactic poem of antiquity after Hesiod's own, translated into Latin by Cicero, by Germanicus, and by Avienus, and commented on for a millennium. Its relation to the Works and Days is structural and acknowledged in the scholarship: formally, the Phaenomena follows the general plan of Hesiod's poem. Where Hesiod keyed the farmer's year to the risings and settings of a handful of stars and filled the gaps with weather-signs and animal behaviour, Aratus versified the systematic astronomy of Eudoxus of Cnidus — the whole sphere, constellation by constellation — and appended the weather-signs as a second book of the work, the Diosemeiae. The Hesiodic seed is visible in the design: a hymn to Zeus opens the poem, as a hymn to Zeus opens Hesiod's; the sky is a system of signs established for men's benefit, to be read for sailing and for farming; and Aratus even reworks Hesiod's golden race into his own figure of Dikē, Justice, as the constellation Virgo, who once lived among men in the golden age and withdrew from earth as the races worsened — the Five Ages and the departing Aidos of Section IV, fused into a single star-myth. Aratus read Hesiod closely; the Hellenistic poem is, among other things, a commentary on the Works and Days conducted by imitation.

Virgil: The Ascraean Song at Rome

Rome's answer came from Virgil. The Georgics (published 29 BCE), four books of Latin hexameters on the cultivation of fields, trees and vines, livestock, and bees, is the Works and Days' greatest descendant — and Virgil says so, in the poem: at Georgics 2.176 he names his work an Ascraeum carmen, an “Ascraean song”, claiming Hesiod's village as his poem's home. The claim is exact about the affiliation and honest about the transformation. Virgil takes from Hesiod the genre, the teacher's stance, the star-calendar of the farming year (the first book of the Georgics is unthinkable without Section VII's Pleiades), and the weather-sign lore that had passed through Aratus; he adds Lucretius' philosophical scale, Hellenistic polish, and a Roman politics of the land that has no Hesiodic counterpart. The line of descent — Hesiod to Aratus to Virgil — is the standard account in the scholarship of the Georgics' allusive programme, and it is a documented one: Virgil's Hesiodic and Aratean allusions in Georgics 1 are dense and specific, not a vague affinity of genre. From the Georgics the didactic line runs on through the Latin tradition — and the Georgics' author went on to the Aeneid, treated in this hub's Aeneid article, carrying the Ascraean apprenticeship into Rome's national epic.

The Scholia and Proclus: The Poem as School Text

The third channel is commentary. The Works and Days was a school and study text throughout antiquity and Byzantium, and it accumulated the apparatus that school texts accumulate: scholia — marginal and interlinear notes, explanations of words, myths, astronomy, and morals — layered from Hellenistic scholarship through the Byzantine teachers. The ancient scholia to the poem were edited in the modern era by Alois Pertusi (1955), whose edition of the scholia vetera remains the standard collection. The scholia matter beyond their explanations: like the Venetus A scholia for Homer, they are a quarry of lost ancient learning, preserving fragments of commentators whose works have not survived whole.

The greatest of those lost commentators, for this poem, is Proclus (fifth century CE), the Neoplatonist scholarch of Athens, who wrote a full commentary — a hypomnēma — on the Works and Days. The commentary is lost as a continuous work; what survives of it survives because the scholia absorbed it, excerpted and adapted to the margin, and modern scholarship — most fully Patrizia Marzillo's edition of the Proclus fragments (2010) — has extracted the Proclean material from the scholia and reconstituted it as far as the excerpts allow. The result is a double lesson in transmission. We possess a fair portion of Proclus on Hesiod — more extended Neoplatonist exegesis of this poem than of any comparable text — but only such parts of it as later excerptors found suitable for scholia: the commentary as its copyists needed it, not as its author wrote it. Proclus read the poem, on the evidence of the fragments, as a didactic work whose skopos — its aim — was paideutikos, educational: ethical exposition in plain language, though his exegesis could rise to allegory on the two Strifes and on Prometheus and Pandora. There is a live scholarly dispute here, flagged as such: Marzillo argues that Proclus classed the poem with inspired, allegorisable poetry; her reviewers answer that Proclus' own statement of the poem's aim is didactic, and that the scholia contain very little allegory — possibly because the excerptor, not Proclus, was uninterested in Neoplatonic metaphysics. The dispute is unresolvable from excerpts, which is itself the point.

// ALLEGORY, IN ITS PLACE //

Proclus and the Neoplatonic readers after him are the channel through which Hesiod's myths — Pandora, the two Strifes, the races of men — entered the allegorical tradition that the later alchemists inherited. Where those later readers take Pandora or the golden race as figures of the alchemical work, that reading is presented in this hub as the alchemists' reading, an interpretation laid on the poem a thousand years after its making — never as the poem's original meaning. The hub's treatment of the method itself is in Alchemical Allegory. The Works and Days' own meanings are the ones this article has set out: aetiology, doctrine, and procedure, in a Boeotian farmer's voice.

One pairing remains, the oldest of all. Herodotus, in the fifth century BCE, already couples the two founding poets in a single sentence: it was Homer and Hesiod, he says (Histories 2.53), who made the Greeks their theogony, gave the gods their names and honours and forms. The sentence is treated in this hub's Prose Systematizers article, in its Herodotean place; here it stands as the didactic tradition's charter. By Herodotus' time the pairing was already a commonplace — the singer of heroes and the singer of works and days, the two fountains of Greek paideia — and the Works and Days has kept that double standing ever since: half of the founding inheritance, and the half that tells a man not how heroes died but how he is to live.

XI. Editions and Translations: West and the Modern Reader

The modern study of the Works and Days rests on one edition above all others, and this hub names it as its standard, as it does for the Theogony. M. L. West, Hesiod: Works and Days (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1978) is an edition of the Greek text with a full commentary — prolegomena on the poem, its transmission, and its problems; the text with apparatus; and a line-by-line commentary that remains, nearly fifty years on, the first place every question about the poem is taken. West had already edited the Theogony (1966); the two Oxford commentaries together are the hub's Hesiod standard, and the positions attributed to West in this article — the poem as a collection of material that could have ended at several points, the printing of the Days with their difficulties noted — are his edition's. Alongside it stands the Oxford Classical Text of Hesiod's works (Solmsen's edition, in its successive revisions), in which the Days are bracketed — the editorial judgement discussed in Section IX — and the older Teubner tradition behind both.

WEST (1978) — THE STANDARD

M. L. West, Hesiod: Works and Days (Oxford, 1978): Greek text, apparatus, and commentary. The edition this hub cites for the poem's text, numeration, and problems. West's companion Theogony commentary (1966) is the standard for the sister poem.

WEST IN TRANSLATION (1999)

Hesiod: Theogony and Works and Days, translated by M. L. West (Oxford World's Classics, 1999): the editor's own verse translation of both poems, with introduction and notes — the most convenient single volume in which to read the poem with its standard editor as guide.

LATTIMORE (1959)

Richmond Lattimore, Hesiod (University of Michigan Press, 1959): translations of the Works and Days, the Theogony, and the Shield of Heracles, in Lattimore's plain, line-respecting verse — for two generations the standard American classroom Hesiod.

LOMBARDO (1993)

Stanley Lombardo, Works and Days and Theogony (Hackett, 1993), with an introduction by Robert Lamberton: a vigorous contemporary verse translation, paired with Lamberton's account of Hesiod's ancient and modern reception — the version many readers now meet first.

EVELYN-WHITE (1914) — QUOTED HERE

H. G. Evelyn-White, Hesiod, the Homeric Hymns and Homerica (Loeb Classical Library, 1914): the facing- page Loeb translation from which this article's quotations are taken, identified at each point of quotation. Its prose is dated and occasionally softens the Greek; it is used here because it is public, checkable, and was consulted in full.

THE COMMENTARY TRADITION, MODERN

Beyond West: Andrea Ercolani's commentary (2010) and Lilah Grace Canevaro's interpretative commentary (Durham thesis, 2012; developed in her Hesiod's Works and Days: How to Teach Self-Sufficiency, 2018) represent the poem's current scholarly life — Ercolani on the text, Canevaro on its didactic design and its ideal of self-sufficiency.

A reader coming to the poem for the first time is best served, in this hub's judgement, by West's Oxford World's Classics volume for the text with notes, or by Lombardo for the poem as English verse — and by reading the whole 828 lines, in order, at least once. The Works and Days suffers more than most ancient poems from excerpting: its myths are anthologised (Pandora, the Five Ages), its calendar is summarised, its precepts are mined for proverbs, and the address that binds them — the angry, funny, frightened, practical voice talking to its brother across a stolen inheritance — is lost. The poem's unity, such as it is, is that voice. West's caution stands: to anyone who expects an orderly and systematic progression of ideas, the Works and Days is liable to appear a bewildering text. It is not a treatise. It is a speech — read it whole, as Perses was meant to hear it.
