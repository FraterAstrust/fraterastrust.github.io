THE WANDERINGS OF ODYSSEUS

Scheria · The Court of Alcinous · Odyssey Books 9–12

// THE APOLOGOI :: THE SEA-TALE STRATUM OF THE ODYSSEY :: A TALE WITHIN THE TALE //

For four books the Odyssey stops and listens. A shipwrecked guest at a foreign king's table tells how he lost everything — twelve ships, every companion, ten years — in a world of lotus, giants, witches, sirens, and the cattle of the Sun. The teller is Odysseus, the poem's most accomplished liar. The poem knows it, his hosts half-know it, and the tale works anyway. This article reads the wanderings as what they are in the poem: a first-person performance, built out of far older sea-tales and folktales, whose truth the Odyssey itself invites its audience to question.

I. A Tale Within the Tale: The Apologoi and Their Frame

Books 9–12 of the Odyssey have a collective name, and the name itself is a warning about how to read them. They are called the Apologoi (singular Apologos; Greek apologoi, from apologeomai, to speak in one's own defence, to give an account of oneself): the tales Odysseus tells, in the first person, to King Alcinous and the Phaeacian court on the island of Scheria. Everything else in the Odyssey is told by the poem's primary narrator, the voice that invokes the Muse in Book 1 and that the audience is trained to trust. The wanderings are not. They reach the audience at one remove, filtered through a character inside the poem — and not just any character, but the character the poem has already established, repeatedly and with evident delight, as a master of plausible falsehood. That single formal fact organises this whole article. The episodes are told here in full, in the poem's order, with their line references; but they are told throughout as Odysseus's account of his wanderings, which is the only form in which the poem ever gives them.

The frame matters, and the poem builds it carefully. Odysseus has been washed ashore on Scheria, naked and salt-caked, after Poseidon's storm wrecked his raft from Calypso's island (Book 5) and the princess Nausicaa found him on the beach (Book 6). He has been received as a suppliant by Queen Arete and King Alcinous (Book 7), feasted, and entertained at the games and at the songs of the blind singer Demodocus (Book 8) — where, twice, songs about Troy make the unknown guest weep, and Alcinous, watching, finally asks the question the whole first movement of the poem has been delaying: who are you, and what have you suffered? Odysseus answers at the end of Book 8 and the opening of Book 9 by naming himself — Odysseus, son of Laertes, known among men for all manner of wiles, in A. T. Murray's Loeb translation of the self-introduction (9.19–20, paraphrased in the connective summary here) — and then, at Alcinous's request for his woes, he begins: first the raid on the Cicones, then everything that followed, in a single sustained performance that fills the night. The performance is interrupted once, in the middle of Book 11, when Odysseus pauses, the spellbound court responds, and Alcinous begs him to continue until dawn (11.330–384; Section II). It ends at the close of Book 12, where Odysseus refuses to retell the Calypso story on the ground that he told it the night before and that it is, in Murray's rendering of 12.450–453, an irksome thing to tell again a plain-told tale (paraphrased; the passage is discussed in Section XIII). The Phaeacians then do exactly what the tale was, in part, designed to make them do: they load him with gifts beyond anything Troy would have given him and carry him, sleeping, home to Ithaca in one of their marvellous ships (Book 13).

Three scopes must be stated at the outset, because this hub divides its Homeric material deliberately. First, this article covers only the wanderings: the Apologoi of Books 9–12, with the Scheria frame that holds them and the Calypso episode that closes them. The Odyssey as a whole text — its composition, its place in the epic tradition, its canonisation — belongs to this hub's Homeric Epics article, and is cross-linked there rather than duplicated here; the hero and epic background is at Heroes and Epic. Second, Book 11, the nekyia — Odysseus's consultation of the dead at the edge of Ocean — is treated here only as the wanderings' structural pivot (Section IX). The underworld itself, its geography, its dead, and the wider Greek tradition of descents to Hades belong to this hub's Descents to the Underworld article, and the reader who wants the nekyia in full should go there. Third, three different objects are kept apart throughout, and the article's argument depends on keeping them apart: (a) the poem's tale — what Odysseus says happened, in the order he says it; (b) the folktale and sea-tale stratum behind it — the older, travelling stories of blinded ogres, forbidden bags, and man-eating harbours out of which the poem's episodes were built (Sections V and XV); and (c) the later history — the attempts, ancient and modern, to pin the voyage to a real map (Section XIV), and the allegorical readings that turned Circe and the Sirens into dangers of the soul (Section XVI). A sentence that confuses (a) with (b), or (b) with (c), misdescribes all three.

THE APOLOGOI AT A GLANCE

Odyssey 9–12. Speaker: Odysseus, in the first person. Audience inside the poem: Alcinous, Arete, and the Phaeacian court at Scheria. Occasion: a feast, at night, in return for hospitality and in hope of conveyance home. Extent: the three years of wandering between Troy and Calypso's island — the other seven years of the ten-year return are spent, almost motionless, on Ogygia.

A word on chronology inside the tale, because retellings routinely flatten it. The wanderings proper — from the sack of Ismarus to the wreck on Thrinacia — occupy, on the poem's own showing, roughly three years: a year of it is spent at Circe's table alone (10.467–475; Section VIII), and the rest is sailing, storm, and disaster in quick, brutal succession. The remaining seven years are the stillness of Ogygia (Section XIII). Odysseus's ten-year return is therefore not ten years of adventure. It is three years of catastrophe followed by seven years of enforced rest — a shape the poem underlines by making the catastrophe the part Odysseus narrates himself, at length and in detail, and the rest the part he refuses to narrate twice.

II. The Narrator Who Lies for a Living: Truth-Status at Scheria

The Odyssey does not hide what kind of narrator it has given its central tale. Odysseus is introduced by the poem's own narrator as a man of many turns (polytropos, 1.1) and of many wiles; Athena, his patron goddess and the poem's other great intelligence, greets him on Ithaca with open admiration for his deceitfulness, in a scene (Book 13) where god and hero recognise each other as fellow practitioners. More to the point, the poem's second half is a gallery of Odysseus's fictions: the so-called Cretan tales, the elaborate false autobiographies he delivers, in disguise, to the swineherd Eumaeus (Book 14), to Penelope herself (Book 19), and to others — tales that mix real places, real names, and real episodes of his own past with invented voyages, invented shipwrecks, and an invented self, and that the poem's narrator explicitly marks as lies made to resemble truth. A listener who has read the whole poem therefore knows, before judging the Apologoi, two things at once: that Odysseus can invent a sea-voyage indistinguishable in texture from a true one, because the poem shows him doing it repeatedly; and that the wanderings tale, unlike the Cretan tales, is never marked false by the primary narrator. The poem leaves the question open on purpose. This section sets out the evidence on both sides, because the hub's rule — stated on its index page — is that a reader should be able to tell, from any sentence, whether they are standing on a document, on a scholarly consensus, or on an argument. Here the document itself is double.

The Audience Within the Poem

The Phaeacians are not neutral hearers, and the poem does not treat them as such. Scheria is itself a threshold place: a people, in their own account, who live far apart in the wash of the waves, the farthermost of men, visited by no other mortals (the description Strabo later seized on, Geography 1.2.18, when arguing about where Homer meant the wanderings to lie; Section XIV) — a fairy-tale court of magical ships, perpetual feasting, and gardens where fruit never fails, suspended between the wonder-world of the wanderings and the real, dangerous Ithaca of the poem's second half. Odysseus's tale is told there, to them, and it has work to do there. He is a suppliant and a guest. He needs gifts, and above all he needs a ship and an escort home — the one service the Phaeacians, ferrymen of men, are uniquely able to give. The tale is his credential and his payment in one: it establishes who he is (the sacker of Troy, whose kleos has reached heaven), it explains why a great man has arrived with nothing, and it entertains the court through the night in the manner the court most values. None of this makes the tale false. All of it makes the tale interested — told by a man with everything to gain from telling it well — and a careful reader, ancient or modern, is entitled to notice.

The poem stages its own verdict on the performance, and stages it in the most ambiguous place possible: the interruption in Book 11 (11.330–384). Odysseus breaks off after the catalogue of heroines, pleading the lateness of the hour. Dead silence falls on the hall; the court sits spellbound. Arete speaks first, praising the guest and urging generosity. Then Alcinous gives the judgement on which so much modern discussion turns. In paraphrase of Murray's translation (11.363–368): looking at Odysseus, the king says, the Phaeacians do not take him for a knave or a cheat of the kind the dark earth breeds in plenty — men who fashion lies out of things no one can test; on the contrary, there is a shapeliness on his words and a noble mind within them, and he has told his tale knowingly, like a singer — the sorrows of all the Argives, and his own. Alcinous then asks for more, and Odysseus, flattered and encouraged, resumes. Every element of the compliment cuts two ways, and scholars have said so for generations. To acquit a man of being a fabricator of untestable lies, in those terms, is to name exactly the suspicion the tale invites — the wanderings are, by construction, untestable: every witness except the teller is dead. To praise a prose performance as being like a singer's is to class it with song — with Demodocus's performances earlier that day, which the poem treats as inspired entertainment, not as deposition. The poem's audience-within-the-poem believes him, or performs belief, and rewards him. The poem's audience outside the poem is left to decide whether Alcinous is a good judge. It is worth adding that Alcinous has already shown himself a fallible reader of his guest: in Book 8 he watched Odysseus weep at Demodocus's songs and said nothing in public until the evening. The court that certifies the tale is a court the poem has taught us to watch as carefully as the tale itself.

// LIES LIKE TRUTH, TWICE OVER //

Hesiod's Muses boast that they know how to speak many false things as though they were true, and how to utter true things when they will (Theogony 26–28; see this hub's Theogony article). The Odyssey gives the same double competence to a mortal. The parallel is not accidental colour: in both poems the audience is warned, at the threshold of a great traditional tale, that verisimilitude is a skill, not a guarantee.

What Corroboration the Poem Does — and Does Not — Supply

Against pure scepticism, the poem supplies a frame of corroboration, and it is fair to set it out. The primary narrator, who never lies to the audience, confirms the tale's endpoints and several of its fixed points independently of Odysseus's telling. Book 1's council of the gods presupposes the Calypso detention and the anger of Poseidon; Book 5 narrates the Ogygia departure, the raft, and the storm in the narrator's own voice, matching the outline Odysseus later gives; the prophet Theoclymenus and the shade of Teiresias, within the tale itself, make predictions the outer poem proceeds to fulfil — above all the warning about Thrinacia, whose fulfilment Odysseus narrates and whose consequences (the loss of the last ship and crew, the late and solitary return in another's ship, the woes in the house) are the poem's own plot. The curse of Polyphemus (Section V) is, in effect, the wanderings' table of contents, and the outer poem honours it item by item. A reading that treats the Apologoi as wholesale invention must explain why the poem bothers to underwrite them so carefully.

For scepticism, the poem supplies the teller. The Cretan tales prove capability; the Scheria occasion proves motive; and the tale's texture — its folktale anatomy, set out in Section XV — proves that its materials are the common stock of travelling sailors' and storytellers' tales, not a logbook. Modern scholarship has occupied every position between the poles. W. B. Stanford, in his mid-twentieth-century edition and commentary on the Odyssey, treated the wanderings' geography with famous reserve — on 9.80–81, where Odysseus is swept past Cape Malea and Cythera, Stanford commented, in the words standardly quoted from his edition, that these are the last clearly identifiable places in Odysseus's wanderings, after which he leaves the sphere of geography and enters wonderland (paraphrased here as to its bearing; the sentence is quoted in the geographical literature and discussed in Section XIV). The Oxford commentary tradition — A. Heubeck's commentary on Books 9–12 in A Commentary on Homer's Odyssey, vol. II (Heubeck, Hoekstra, and others; Oxford, 1989), the English version of the Fondazione Lorenzo Valla commentary — reads the Apologoi episode by episode with constant attention to their traditional and folktale elements, without requiring a verdict on the narrator's veracity: the commentary's question is how the tales are made, not whether their teller is lying. The narratological literature, and studies of truth and lying tales in the poem (the discussion in Greece & Rome on true and lying tales is representative), converge on the position this article adopts: the poem deliberately sustains both readings. Odysseus's wanderings are presented as true within the fiction — the gods act on them, prophecies turn on them, the plot is built from them — and simultaneously presented as the kind of story this particular man, on this particular night, for these particular stakes, would be uniquely able to invent. The invitation to ask is part of the text. An honest archive reports the invitation rather than accepting or declining it on the reader's behalf.

III. Ismarus and the Cicones: The Last Raid of the War

The wanderings begin, pointedly, in the world the audience already knows: not wonderland, but war (9.39–61). From Troy the wind carries Odysseus's twelve ships to Ismarus, a city of the Cicones in Thrace — a real people in a real place, Trojan allies in the Iliad's catalogue. What Odysseus does there, in his own telling, is simply what an Achaean commander did at Troy for ten years: he sacks the city, kills the men, and divides the women and treasure equally among his crews, taking care, he says, that no man go defrauded of his share. Then the tale's first lesson is administered. Odysseus orders an immediate departure. His men refuse — in his phrase, in Murray's rendering, the others in their great folly did not hearken (paraphrase of 9.43–44) — and settle to drink wine and slaughter sheep and cattle on the beach. The Cicones meanwhile summon their inland neighbours, more numerous and better fighters, skilled from chariots and on foot. Battle is joined at the ships at dawn. The Achaeans hold through the morning; when the sun turns toward the unyoking of oxen, the Cicones prevail. Six men from every ship — seventy-two men in all, on the poem's arithmetic — are killed. The rest escape to the ships, and Odysseus will not sail until each of the dead has been called on three times, the ritual cry over comrades whose bodies cannot be recovered.

The episode is the wanderings in miniature, and it is placed first for a reason. Every later disaster repeats its structure with the volume turned up: a landing that begins in success or safety; a failure of discipline or judgement — sometimes the men's, sometimes, as with the Cyclops, unmistakably Odysseus's own; a price paid in lives; a narrower escape. The Cicones episode also establishes the tale's moral accounting. Odysseus narrates his own generalship favourably — his order was right, his division was fair, his piety to the dead was exact — and blames the loss on his men's folly. A listener alert to Section II's problem will notice the pattern early, because it recurs: in Odysseus's telling, disaster is very often the crew's disobedience and escape is very often his foresight. Sometimes the poem's own detail supports him. Sometimes, as at Thrinacia (Section XII), it supports him only partly. The Cicones give the audience a baseline: this is still the heroic world, where the enemy are men, the weapons are spears, and the dead can be counted. A storm off Malea (9.67–81) — two nights and two days driven before the North Wind, sails torn to shreds, then a fair start spoiled at the cape where the current and wind beat him back past Cythera — carries the fleet out of that world. Nine days of direful winds follow. On the tenth day they make landfall somewhere no map in the poem will ever name again. Stanford's wonderland begins here.

EPISODE LEDGER — THE CICONES

Text: Odyssey 9.39–61. Ships: 12. Lost: 6 men per ship (72 in all). Cause, in the teller's account: the crews' refusal to re-embark after the sack. Folktale anatomy: none — this is heroic warfare, continuous with the Iliad. Its function is contrast: the last episode in which the dangers are human, countable, and governed by the customs of war.

MARON'S WINE: THE DETAIL THAT TRAVELS

At Ismarus Odysseus spares Maron, priest of Apollo, with his wife and child, and receives in return gold, a silver mixing-bowl, and twelve jars of sweet, unmixed wine of extraordinary strength — a cup diluted in twenty measures of water (9.196–211, paraphrased). The wine is not local colour. It is the weapon that will blind the Cyclops, carried out of the war-world into the wonder-world in a goatskin. The tale plants its instruments early.

IV. The Lotus-Eaters: Forgetting the Way Home

The first wonder is the gentlest, and in some ways the most frightening (9.82–104). The fleet reaches the land of the Lotus-Eaters (Lōtophagoi), who eat a flowery food. Odysseus sends two men and a herald inland to learn who the inhabitants are. The Lotus-Eaters do them no violence at all. They simply give them lotus to taste — and the lotus abolishes the mission, the fleet, and Ithaca in a single act of eating. In Murray's translation (9.94–97), the passage quoted here is the episode's whole mechanism:

“And whosoever of them ate of the honey-sweet fruit of the lotus, had no longer any wish to bring back word or to return, but there they were fain to abide among the Lotus-eaters, feeding on the lotus, and forgetful of their homeward way.” — HOMER, ODYSSEY 9.94–97, TRANS. A. T. MURRAY (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY, 1919)

Odysseus's response is force. He drags the three men back to the ships weeping, ties them beneath the rowing benches, and orders the rest aboard at speed, lest anyone else eat and forget. Then they row away. The episode takes twenty-three lines. No one dies. Nothing is fought. And yet the tale has stated, in its purest form, the danger against which the entire Odyssey is organised: not death but forgetting — the loss of nostos, the homeward way, as a desire. Every subsequent episode is a variation. Circe's drugs make men forget their native land (10.236, in the potion's stated effect). Calypso offers a life so complete that home becomes unnecessary. The Sirens offer a knowledge that makes sailing on pointless. Even the dead, in Book 11, are defined by having forgotten almost everything except grief. The lotus is the theme with the machinery stripped away: a food that deletes purpose. Later tradition could not leave so blank a wonder alone. Herodotus (4.177) already places Lotus-Eaters on the Libyan coast as a real people, and Polybius, as Strabo reports him (Geography 1.2.17), identified their land with the island of Meninx off North Africa — the first moves in the long localisation game examined in Section XIV. Botanical candidates for the lotus — jujube, date-palm products, and others — have been proposed ever since. The poem itself identifies nothing and needs nothing: its lotus is defined purely by its effect, which is why the episode survives every attempt to identify the plant.

// HOW TO READ A TWENTY-THREE-LINE EPISODE //

Brevity in the Apologoi is not unimportance. The poem spends over four hundred lines on the Cyclops and twenty-three on the lotus because the Cyclops is a story and the lotus is a principle. Where Odysseus lingers, he is performing; where he is terse, he is often stating the terms on which everything else will be judged. The Lotus-Eaters, Aeolus's refusal, and the Laestrygonian catastrophe are all told short, and all three are load-bearing.

V. The Cyclops Polyphemus: Outis, the Stake, and Poseidon's Curse

The Cyclops episode (9.105–566) is the longest, most famous, and most consequential of the wanderings — the one episode whose consequences drive the rest of the poem — and it is also the episode where the folktale stratum lies closest to the surface (Section XV). Odysseus tells it in full scenic detail: the land of the Cyclopes, the offshore Goat Island, the cave, the wine, the stake, the sheep, the naming, the curse. This section follows his telling closely, because the detail is where the poem's art and the tale-type's bones show through together.

A People Without Xenia

The Cyclopes are introduced as an anti-society (9.105–115, paraphrased from Murray). They are an overweening and lawless folk. They neither plant nor plough; the earth bears for them unsown — wheat, barley, and vines — and Zeus's rain increases it. They have no assemblies for counsel and no appointed laws; each lives in a hollow cave on the mountain peaks and is lawgiver to his own wives and children, and they reck nothing one of another. Every term is a negation of the Greek city: no agriculture, no assembly, no law, no neighbourliness — and, as the offshore island passage adds (9.116–141), no ships and no shipwrights, though the island across the water lies fertile and unused, a colonist's prospectus read aloud over an empty landscape. The ethnography is a thought-experiment: what men would be without the institutions that make men. The answer walks in at evening. Polyphemus (Greek Poluphēmos) is a monstrous man, like a wooded peak of the mountains standing alone; he pastures his flocks apart, mingles with no one, and has his heart set on lawlessness. Odysseus, against his companions' urging to steal cheese and lambs and go, waits in the cave to see the man and to test whether he will give the gifts of hospitality — a decision the teller himself concedes, in a rare admission, was wrong: verily it would have been better far to have listened (paraphrase of 9.228–230). The giant returns, drives in his milking flocks, and closes the cave with a door-stone so vast that twenty-two four-wheeled wagons could not shift it. The trap is architectural. Whatever happens now happens inside.

The interview that follows is the poem's set-piece on xenia, guest-friendship, the sacred law of Zeus the strangers' god (9.250–278, paraphrased). Odysseus pleads as a suppliant: we are Achaeans from Troy, driven off course; reverence the gods; Zeus is the avenger of suppliants and strangers. Polyphemus answers with the anti-creed: the Cyclopes reck not of Zeus who bears the aegis, nor of the blessed gods, since they are better far than they; he would spare neither Odysseus nor his men to shun Zeus's wrath, unless his own heart bid him. He then asks, cunningly, where the ship is moored — and Odysseus, cunning answering cunning, lies: Poseidon wrecked it on the rocks; these men alone escaped. The lie saves the ship and the men outside. Then the giant seizes two of the company, dashes them to the ground like puppies, cuts them limb from limb, and eats them — entrails, flesh, and marrowy bones, leaving nothing, as a mountain lion eats. In the morning he eats two more, drives out his flocks, and replaces the stone. Odysseus considers killing him in his sleep and rejects the plan for the tale's most practical reason: no hands but the giant's can move the door-stone. A dead Polyphemus is a sealed tomb. The hero must not kill the monster; he must make the monster open the door. Everything that follows — the wine, the name, the blinding, the sheep — is engineering in service of that single constraint.

The Wine, the Name, and the Stake

On the second evening the giant eats two more men — six are now dead — and Odysseus offers him Maron's wine, undiluted, in an ivy bowl (9.345–362, paraphrased). The Cyclops drains it, asks for more, drains it again, and a third time — thrice he drained it in his folly — and, pleased, promises his guest a stranger's gift and asks his name. The answer is the most celebrated sentence in the wanderings. In Murray's translation (9.366–367):

“Noman is my name, Noman do they call me — my mother and my father, and all my comrades as well.” — HOMER, ODYSSEY 9.366–367, TRANS. A. T. MURRAY (LOEB, 1919)

The Greek is Outis — ou tis, “not anyone”, welded into a proper name and declined like one. Murray's “Noman” and the modern “Nobody” both carry the first joke. The second joke is untranslatable, and commentators from the ancient scholia onward have marked it: when the blinded giant's neighbours later ask whether mē tis — “no one”, in the other Greek negative — is doing him violence (9.410), the words mē tis sound as mētis, cunning intelligence itself, the quality that is Odysseus's essence and standing epithet (polymētis, Odysseus of many counsels). The man who has named himself No One is, at that instant, most completely mētis — and the poem lets the pun stand in the Greek for those who can hear it, as the Heubeck commentary on Book 9 and the Stanford commentary tradition both note in their discussions of the passage (reported here as commentary, not quoted). The giant, delighted, delivers his guest-gift: Noman he will eat last, and the others before him; this shall be thy gift (9.369–370, paraphrased). Then the wine takes him. He reels backward, vomits wine and pieces of human flesh, and sleeps.

The blinding (9.371–394) is told with a craftsman's relish, and its two similes are workshop similes. The stake is a length cut from the Cyclops's own olive-wood club — a staff as great, the men thought, as the mast of a black twenty-oared merchantman — smoothed, sharpened by Odysseus, and hardened in the fire; the four men chosen by lot, with Odysseus as fifth, drive the glowing point into the single eye and lean on it and whirl it, as a man bores a ship's timber with a drill kept spinning by a thong. The eyeball hisses round the stake as a great axe or adze hisses when a smith plunges it in cold water to temper it — for therefrom comes the strength of iron (9.391–394, paraphrased from Murray). The giant wrenches out the bloody stake and bellows; the neighbouring Cyclopes gather outside in the dark and call in: what ails him, is someone driving off his flocks, is someone slaying him by guile or by force? From inside, Polyphemus gives the only answer his information allows him. In Murray's translation (9.408):

“My friends, it is Noman that is slaying me by guile and not by force.” — HOMER, ODYSSEY 9.408, TRANS. A. T. MURRAY (LOEB, 1919)

The neighbours reason correctly from what they hear: if no man is doing him violence, alone as he is, then the affliction is sickness sent by great Zeus, which cannot be escaped; let him pray to his father Poseidon (9.410–412, paraphrased). And they go home. Odysseus's heart, he says, laughed within him that his name and his blameless mētis had so beguiled. The escape at dawn completes the design: rams bound in threes with withes, a man slung beneath the middle ram of each trio, and Odysseus himself clinging by his hands beneath the belly of the greatest ram, the flock's leader. The blind giant sits in the doorway and feels along the backs of every animal as it passes — and speaks, in the episode's most quietly devastating passage, to the ram that goes last, burdened with its fleece and with the narrator himself, wondering why it lags, and whether it grieves for its master's eye (9.447–460, paraphrased). The poem gives the monster tenderness at the exact moment of his defeat, and gives it to a sheep. It is one of the places where the Apologoi exceed any folktale source that can be reconstructed for them.

The Boast and the Curse: The Wanderings' Engine

What happens next is the hinge on which the whole poem turns, and the teller does not acquit himself. Safe aboard, at the distance a man's shout carries, Odysseus taunts the blinded giant with mocking words. The Cyclops tears off a mountain peak and hurls it; the splash drives the ship back almost to shore; the men pole off and row. At twice the distance Odysseus calls out again — and this time his comrades beg him, one after another, not to provoke the savage man (9.494–499, paraphrased). He answers, he says, with angry heart, and gives the curse the one thing it needs: a name, a father's name, and an address. In Murray's translation (9.502–505):

“Cyclops, if any one of mortal men shall ask thee about the shameful blinding of thine eye, say that Odysseus, the sacker of cities, blinded it, even the son of Laertes, whose home is in Ithaca.” — HOMER, ODYSSEY 9.502–505, TRANS. A. T. MURRAY (LOEB, 1919)

The giant groans — an old prophecy, he says, is come upon him: the seer Telemus foretold he would lose his sight at Odysseus's hands, and he had always looked for some tall and comely man, not this puny weakling who blinded him with wine (9.506–521, paraphrased). Then he prays, stretching both hands to the starry heaven, to Poseidon his father — and the prayer (9.528–535) is the wanderings' engine, quoted here in Murray's translation in its operative part:

“Grant that Odysseus, the sacker of cities, may never reach his home, even the son of Laertes, whose home is in Ithaca; but if it is his fate to see his friends and to reach his well-built house and his native land, late may he come and in evil case, after losing all his comrades, in a ship that is another's; and may he find woes in his house.” — HOMER, ODYSSEY 9.528–535, TRANS. A. T. MURRAY (LOEB, 1919)

The dark-haired god, the poem says in the teller's own words, heard him (9.536). Every clause is then cashed out by the poem: late — ten years; in evil case — naked, alone, asleep in a foreign ship; after losing all his comrades — Thrinacia and the storm (Section XII); in a ship that is another's — the Phaeacian ferry; and woes in his house — the suitors. The blinding did not doom the voyage; ogres in folktales are blinded every day, and no god avenges them. The boast doomed it, because it converted an anonymous trick into a named injury to a named god's son, and a curse, in this poem's theology, requires a target. Odysseus's need for kleos — for the deed to be attached to his name, sung with his name in it — is the same need that made him tell the whole tale at Scheria, and the poem lets the audience feel both costs at once. Section XV takes up what in this episode is older than Homer — the blinding of the ogre is an international tale type, catalogued as ATU 1137 — and what is Homer's own: Page's classic analysis in Folktales in Homer's Odyssey argued that the name-trick and the wine are among the poem's own additions to the inherited tale, and the curse is the poem's own machinery entire. No folktale Polyphemus has a father who is a god of the sea and a grudge that structures an epic. That is composition, not inheritance.

VI. Aeolus and the Bag of Winds: Within Sight of Home

Book 10 opens with the wanderings' most perfect gift and its most perfect waste (10.1–79). The fleet reaches the floating island of Aeolus (Aiolos), son of Hippotas, keeper of the winds by Zeus's appointment: an island ringed by a wall of unbreakable bronze and sheer cliffs, where Aeolus feasts perpetually with his wife and his twelve children — six sons married to six daughters, a closed, self-sufficient household that needs nothing from outside. For a full month Aeolus entertains Odysseus and questions him about Troy and the returns of the Achaeans — the poem's second internal audience, listening to a first version of the tale of Troy before the Scheria audience hears the tale of the wanderings — and Odysseus tells him all in due order. Then Aeolus sends him off in the tale's most practical miracle. In Murray's translation (10.19–22, paraphrased in part here): he gives Odysseus a wallet made of the hide of an ox nine years old, in which he has bound the paths of the blustering winds, and fastens it in the ship with a bright cord of silver so that not a breath may escape; only the West Wind is left free, to bear the ships home. It is a technology: weather, bagged. For nine days they sail, night and day, Odysseus himself never surrendering the steering sheet, and on the tenth day Ithaca is in sight — so near that they can see men tending the beacon fires on shore. Then, at the threshold, the captain sleeps.

What follows (10.34–49, paraphrased) is told, characteristically, as the crew's folly — and the detail the teller includes lets a careful listener judge the crew more kindly than the teller does. The men reason aloud: Odysseus comes home beloved and honoured everywhere; he carries rich treasure from Troy, while they, who have accomplished the same journey, return with empty hands; and now Aeolus has given him this too — come, let us see what gold and silver is in the wallet. The grievance is not invented. Odysseus has shared spoil equally — he insists on it at Ismarus — but he has not told his men what the bag is. A secret kept from exhausted men within sight of home, in a bag that looks exactly like treasure, does what secrets do. They loose the wallet. All the winds leap forth; the storm seizes the ships and bears them weeping out to sea, away from their native land. Odysseus wakes, and considers — the poem gives the despair one sentence — whether to throw himself from the ship and perish in the sea, or to endure in silence and remain among the living. He endures, covers his head, and lies down. The winds carry them back to Aeolia.

The second interview is the episode's point, and it is theological. Odysseus goes up to the palace as a suppliant, with gentle words, and begs Aeolus to heal the harm. Aeolus's answer, in Murray's translation of 10.72–75 (paraphrased here in its connective tissue), is a dismissal with a doctrine inside it: begone from the island with speed, vilest of all that live; in no wise may I help or send on his way a man who is hated by the blessed gods; begone, for you come here as one hated by the immortals. The logic is exact and chilling. Aeolus does not know about Poseidon's curse — Odysseus has not told him, and the audience, hearing the tale at Scheria, knows more than Aeolus does. Aeolus reasons backwards from outcome to standing: a man blown back from his own harbour must be god-hated, and helping the god-hated is itself impious. Divine favour, in this world, is inferred from success, and failure is therefore self-confirming. The bag of winds will have one more long career: it is the object Eratosthenes chose, three centuries before Strabo reported him, as the emblem of the whole voyage's unfindability — you will find where Odysseus wandered when you find the cobbler who sewed the bag (Section XIV). The folktale anatomy is equally clear. A magical container of winds, opened against instruction within sight of the goal, is a travelling tale's device, kin to every forbidden casket and unopened door in the world's story-stock; the poem's own contribution is the nearness — nine days of perfect sailing, the beacon fires visible — and Aeolus's cold theology at the door.

The wallet is an ox-hide askos, bound with a silver cord. Later localisation made him a historical king of the Lipari (Aeolian) Islands north of Sicily, a teacher of navigation in difficult currents: Polybius's rationalisation, reported by Strabo (1.2.15; Section XIV).

VII. The Laestrygonians: The Harbour That Was a Trap

After Aeolus there is no more help, and the tale's losses change scale. Six days of rowing — no fair wind appears again, the teller notes, because of their folly — bring the fleet on the seventh day to Telepylus, the lofty citadel of Lamus, in the land of the Laestrygonians (10.80–132). The harbour is described with a surveyor's care, and the care is the horror: a goodly harbour with sheer cliffs running continuously on both sides, projecting headlands opposite each other at the mouth, and a narrow entrance; inside, no wave ever swells, great or small, but all about is a bright calm. Eleven of the twelve ships steer in and moor close together in the hollow harbour. Odysseus alone moors his black ship outside, at the land's edge, making the cables fast to a rock — because, the telling implies without quite saying, he has learned to look at a harbour before trusting it. He climbs to a lookout, sees no works of oxen or men, only smoke rising, and sends two men and a herald inland to ask who eats bread in this place.

The scouts meet, at the spring Artacia where the town draws water, a maiden of enormous size — the daughter of Antiphates, the Laestrygonian king. She directs them politely to her father's high-roofed house. Inside they find his wife, huge as a mountain peak, who summons her husband from the assembly place. Antiphates seizes one of the scouts and makes ready his meal on the spot. The other two flee to the ships. Then the cry goes up through the city, and the mighty Laestrygonians come thronging from all sides, a host past counting, not like men but like the Giants — and from the cliffs they hurl down rocks huge as a man could lift onto the trapped fleet. In Murray's paraphrase-near rendering of 10.121–124: there rose throughout the ships a dreadful din, alike from men dying and from ships being crushed; and spearing them like fishes, the Laestrygonians bore them home, a loathly meal (paraphrased; the simile of men speared like fish is the passage's centre). Odysseus, outside the trap, cuts his own ship's cables with his sword, orders his men to row for their lives, and escapes seaward away from the beetling cliffs. All those other ships, the teller says, were lost together there.

In one morning the expedition ends as an expedition. From this point the Apologoi are the story of a single ship — and, increasingly, of a single man. The episode's folktale anatomy is transparent: a cannibal giant-people, a princess met at a spring, a harbour-mouth that functions exactly like the Cyclops's door-stone, scaled up from one cave to a whole fleet. The poem's variations repay attention. The Laestrygonians, unlike the Cyclopes, have a city, a king, an assembly place, a water supply — the institutions whose absence defined Polyphemus's people — and it makes them worse, not better: civilisation's forms in the service of appetite, an organised cannibal state. The strange pastoral detail with which the episode opens (10.82–86, paraphrased) — that at Telepylus the paths of night and day are close together, so that a man who never slept could earn double wages, pasturing cattle by day and white sheep by night — has puzzled commentators since antiquity and has been used by every northern-voyage theorist since: a land of very short nights sounds like high latitudes. The Heubeck commentary tradition treats it more soberly, as part of the wonder-world's inverted geography, where days and nights, like hosts and guests, behave wrongly. This archive records the detail and both readings, and adopts neither: a two-line wonder is not a latitude.

TELEPYLUS

The name means “far-gated” or “with distant gates” — a wonderland place-name, not a chart label. Ancient localisation placed the Laestrygonians in Sicily (the tradition Strabo discusses) and later at Formiae in Italy, whose founding was ascribed to Lamus. Both identifications belong to stratum (c), the afterlife of the itinerary (Section XIV), not to the poem, which gives Telepylus no bearings at all.

VIII. Circe on Aeaea: Swine, Moly, and the Year

The one surviving ship comes to Aeaea, the isle of Circe (Greek Kirkē) — fair-tressed, a dread goddess of human speech, own sister to Aeetes of Colchis, and, like him, a child of Helios the Sun and Perse, daughter of Ocean (10.133–139, paraphrased from Murray). The genealogy matters: Circe is not a mortal witch but a goddess of the Sun's house, aunt of Medea, placed by the poem at the western edge of the world with the Sun's other dangerous children. After two days of grief-stricken rest, Odysseus scouts the island, kills a great stag sent across his path by a god, feeds his men, and at dawn divides the company into two bands — his own, and one under Eurylochus, his kinsman by marriage and the crew's emerging spokesman — and casts lots in a bronze helmet for which band shall explore. The lot falls to Eurylochus. He sets out with twenty-two comrades, all weeping, leaving the rest behind, lamenting.

The Swine

In a forest glade the scouting party finds Circe's house, built of polished stone in a place of wide outlook — and round about it, mountain wolves and lions that do not rush on the men but fawn about them, wagging their long tails, as hounds fawn around a master coming from a feast (10.210–219, paraphrased). The beasts are the house's first text: they are what Circe's guests become. Inside, the goddess sings at her great imperishable loom. The men call; she opens the bright doors and bids them in, and all go in in their folly — all but Eurylochus, who stays outside because he suspects a snare. She seats them, and mixes them a potion of cheese and barley meal and yellow honey with Pramnian wine, with baneful drugs in the food, that they might utterly forget their native land (10.234–236, paraphrased from Murray). When they have drunk, she strikes them with her wand and pens them in the sties. In Murray's translation (10.237–241, paraphrased only in connective wording): they had the heads, and voice, and bristles, and shape of swine, but their minds remained unchanged even as before. The horror is precise. Circe's transformation is not a release into animality; it is a prison of form, consciousness intact, fed on mast and acorns and cornel fruit — the food of wallowing swine — in a pen. The lotus erased memory; Circe's drug preserves it, which is worse, and the poem knows the difference.

Eurylochus brings the news to the ship, speechless at first with grief. Odysseus arms — sword and bow — and orders him to lead the way back; Eurylochus clasps his knees and begs not to be taken: leave me here; you will neither return yourself nor bring back any comrade; let us flee with these who remain, for still we may escape the evil day (10.261–269, paraphrased). It is the first of Eurylochus's three great interventions, and the pattern of all three should be marked now, because Section XII turns on it: Eurylochus is the crew's prudence speaking — often right about the danger, never able to supply the way through it. Odysseus leaves him by the ship and goes alone, for strong necessity is laid upon me.

Moly: The Herb Gods Can Dig

On the way through the sacred glades, Hermes of the golden wand meets him, in the likeness of a young man with the first down on his lip. Hermes does not forbid the attempt; he equips it (10.277–301, paraphrased). Take this potent herb, he says, and go to Circe's house with it, and it shall ward off the evil day from your head; she will mix you a potion and cast drugs in the food, but even so she shall not be able to bewitch you, for the herb will not suffer it. When she strikes you with her long wand, draw your sword and rush on her as though to slay her; she will fear, and invite you to her bed; do not refuse the couch of the goddess — but first bid her swear the great oath of the blessed gods that she will plot no fresh mischief against you, lest, when she has you stripped, she render you a weakling and unmanned. Then Hermes gives the herb, drawing it from the ground, and shows Odysseus its nature. In Murray's translation (10.302–306):

“At the root it was black, but its flower was like milk. Moly the gods call it, and it is hard for mortal men to dig; but with the gods all things are possible.” — HOMER, ODYSSEY 10.304–306, TRANS. A. T. MURRAY (LOEB, 1919)

Mōly is the Odyssey's most famous plant, and its most honestly inexplicable. The poem gives a description (black root, milk-white flower), a divine name, and a single property: mortals cannot dig it. Every botanical identification since antiquity — garlic, snowdrop, mandrake, rue, and many more — founders on that property, which is not a field mark but a theological statement: the counter-charm belongs to the gods' side of the world, and Odysseus survives Circe not by his own pharmacology but by a gift. The sequence in the house then follows Hermes' script exactly. The potion fails. The wand falls useless. Circe, with a loud cry, clasps his knees and asks who he is — for no man ever withstood this charm once it had passed the barrier of his teeth — and names him herself: surely you are Odysseus, the man of ready device, of whom Argeiphontes always told her (10.321–335, paraphrased). The bed follows, but only after the oath is sworn, word for word as Hermes prescribed. Odysseus then refuses food until his comrades are freed — what right-minded man, he asks, could taste food or drink before beholding his companions restored (10.383–387, paraphrased)? Circe goes to the sty, anoints each swine with another charm, and the bristles fall away; the men become men again, younger than before, and far comelier and taller to look upon (10.388–396, paraphrased). They know him, cling to his hands, and the house rings with sobbing until the goddess herself is moved to pity. It is the wanderings' only complete rescue — the one disaster wholly undone — and it is undone by the full Odyssean toolkit working together: divine aid, a magical defence, a drawn sword, an extracted oath, and a leader's refusal to feast alone.

The Year, and the Price of Leaving

Then the tale does something it has not done before: it stops moving (10.466–475). Day after day for a full year the company abides on Aeaea, feasting on abundant flesh and sweet wine. No storm drives them, no monster attacks them; the delay is comfort itself, and it ends only when the comrades, not Odysseus, call him aside and recall him: strange man, bethink you now at last of your native land, if it is fated for you to be saved (paraphrase of 10.469–474). The role reversal is deliberate and quietly comic — the captain must be reminded of nostos by the crew whose forgetfulness he has spent three books correcting — and it pairs with the lotus and with Calypso to come: the wanderings' delays are graded, from a fruit, to a goddess's table for a year, to a goddess's island for seven. Odysseus goes to Circe's bed that night as a suppliant and begs fulfilment of her promise to send him home. Her answer is the pivot of the whole Apologoi. You must first complete another journey, she says, and come to the house of Hades and dread Persephone, to seek soothsaying of the spirit of Theban Teiresias, the blind seer, to whom even in death Persephone has granted a steadfast mind — he alone has understanding; the others flit about as shadows (10.490–495, paraphrased from Murray). Odysseus weeps on the bed and loses, for a moment, the desire to live and behold the sun. Then he asks the practical question — who will guide us? No man ever yet went to Hades in a black ship — and Circe gives him the full sailing directions for the descent: set the mast, spread the white sail, sit down, and let the North Wind bear the ship across Ocean to the shore of Persephone's groves, with the pit, the libations, and the blood-sacrifice prescribed in detail (10.503–540, paraphrased; the rite itself belongs to Descents to the Underworld). One casualty precedes the sailing: Elpenor, the youngest of the company, not over valiant in war nor sound of understanding, who had slept drunk on Circe's roof, wakes at the bustle of departure, forgets the long ladder, falls headlong, and breaks his neck (10.550–560, paraphrased). His unburied shade will be the first to meet Odysseus at the pit — the wanderings' smallest death, and the one that binds the Circe episode to the nekyia with a personal obligation.

IX. The Nekyia as Pivot: Why the Wanderings Turn on Hades

Book 11 — the nekyia, the rite of summoning the dead — is the centre of the Apologoi, and this article treats it, by design, only as the wanderings' pivot. The full treatment of the descent — the pit at Ocean's edge, Elpenor's plea for burial, Teiresias's prophecy, the mother Anticleia, the catalogue of heroines, Agamemnon, Achilles, Ajax's silence, the sinners and Heracles — belongs to this hub's Descents to the Underworld article, where the nekyia is read alongside the other Greek descents. What belongs here is the pivot's function: why the wanderings, alone among ancient sea-tales, route their hero through the land of the dead at the exact middle of his story, and what the detour changes.

Structurally, the nekyia is the point of maximum distance. Every measure the poem uses confirms it. Geographically, the ship sails beyond Ocean's stream to the shore where Persephone's groves stand — tall poplars and willows that shed their fruit — at the meeting of the rivers of Hades; it is the one destination in the wanderings that is not, even in pretence, on the way home. Narratively, it is the middle book of the four, and the interruption by Alcinous (Section II) falls inside it, as if the tale itself must pause for breath at its deepest point. Thematically, it reverses the voyage's direction of knowledge. Before Hades, Odysseus learns by encounter — each landfall teaches him its own danger by costing him men. In Hades he is told: Teiresias, drinking the blood, lays out the remainder of the plot as conditional prophecy — the Thrinacia warning in its first and authoritative form, the manner of the return (late, alone, in another's ship), the suitors, the vengeance, and even the hero's eventual death, coming to him from the sea in sleek old age (11.100–137, paraphrased; the prophecy's full text and its afterlife are treated at Descents). After Hades, Odysseus sails with a script. Book 12 is therefore a different kind of narrative from Book 9: not discovery but execution, in which Circe's second briefing and Teiresias's prophecy together tell the audience in advance exactly what will happen at the Sirens, at the strait, and on Thrinacia — and the suspense migrates from what to whether men can obey what they already know. The wanderings' second half is a test of instruction, and its catastrophe (Section XII) is a failure of obedience under starvation, not of knowledge.

The pivot also re-prices everything before it. In Hades Odysseus meets the war's dead and his own mother, who died of longing for him; he hears Agamemnon's account of his murder by Clytemnestra and Aegisthus, which arms the poem's second half with its governing anxiety about the homecoming wife; and he learns from Achilles' shade the underworld's own verdict on kleos — that the glory for which the heroes died at Troy buys nothing where they now are (the famous exchange is quoted and discussed at Descents to the Underworld, and is not duplicated here). A sea-tale does not need this scene. A poem about what homecoming costs, and what fame is worth, cannot do without it. The folktale stratum has its own descents, to be sure — the consultation of the dead is old story-stock — but the scale of Book 11, its catalogues, and its function as the poem's prophetic engine are the epic's own construction. Circe sends Odysseus down as a navigator sends a ship to take on charts. He comes up with the rest of his story already written, and spends Book 12 watching men fail to live inside it.

X. The Sirens: A Song That Promises Knowledge

Book 12 returns the ship from Hades to Aeaea, buries Elpenor on the headland with his oar planted on his mound, and then gives the wanderings their second great briefing (12.36–141). Circe takes Odysseus aside and describes, in order, the Sirens, the choice of routes past the Wandering Rocks or the strait, and Thrinacia. The Sirens come first, and Circe's description (12.39–46, in Murray's translation, paraphrased here except where noted) is the poem's own: they beguile all men who come to them; whoever in ignorance draws near and hears their voice never returns home to a wife and little children standing rejoicing beside him; the Sirens beguile him with their clear-toned song, sitting in a meadow — and about them is a great heap of bones of mouldering men, and round the bones the skin is shrivelling. The meadow of bones is the episode's emblem: the song's past audience, displayed. Circe then prescribes the counter-measure, and the prescription is the famous one. Row past. Knead wax and stop the comrades' ears, lest any of them hear. But if Odysseus himself has a will to listen, let the men bind him in the swift ship, hand and foot, upright in the step of the mast, the ropes made fast at the ends to the mast itself, so that with delight he may listen to the voice of the two Sirens — and if he implores and bids them loose him, let them bind him with yet more bonds (12.47–54, paraphrased from Murray).

What the Song Actually Says

Most retellings leave the song's content vague — a beautiful, fatal noise. Homer does not. When the ship comes within hailing distance and the wind drops to a windless calm, the Sirens see it and raise their clear-toned song, and Odysseus — who alone can hear it, and who alone reports it — gives its words (12.184–191). In Murray's translation:

“Come hither, as thou farest, renowned Odysseus, great glory of the Achaeans; stay thy ship that thou mayest listen to the voice of us two. For never yet has any man rowed past this isle in his black ship until he has heard the sweet voice from our lips. Nay, he has joy of it, and goes his way a wiser man. For we know all the toils that in wide Troy the Argives and Trojans endured through the will of the gods, and we know all things that come to pass upon the fruitful earth.” — HOMER, ODYSSEY 12.184–191, TRANS. A. T. MURRAY (LOEB, 1919)

The promise is not pleasure. It is knowledge — total, and precisely targeted. The Sirens address Odysseus by name and by his defining epithet; they offer him, first, the story of Troy — his own past, the Argives' and Trojans' toils, which is to say his own kleos sung back to him complete — and then, beyond it, all things that come to pass upon the fruitful earth: omniscience as a song. For the hero whose characteristic virtue is mētis and whose characteristic act is inquiry — the man who waited in the Cyclops's cave to see the inhabitant, who will spend the poem's second half testing every member of his own household — this is the one bait in the wanderings cut exactly to his measure. The crew are in no danger from it; with waxed ears they are in no relation to it at all. The danger is his alone, and so is the technology that meets it. Odysseus cuts a great cake of wax into pieces, kneads it warm with his strong hands in the rays of the sun, stops every comrade's ears, and has himself bound as Circe prescribed (12.173–179, paraphrased). As the song sounds, his heart is fain to listen — he signals with his brows for release, since his mouth is the one instrument he has left — and Perimedes and Eurylochus rise and bind him with yet more bonds and draw them tighter (12.192–196, paraphrased). The ship rows past. Only when the voice can no longer be heard do the men take the wax from their ears and loose him.

The episode has become the Western tradition's standing image of self-binding: a rational agent, knowing in advance that his future self will beg to be released, arranging in his cool hour to be physically prevented from obeying that begging. Philosophers of action still use it as the type-case of precommitment, and the poem fully earns the use — the arrangement is Circe's design, Odysseus's execution, and the crew's fidelity, a three-part machine in which knowledge of one's own weakness is the working part. But two cautions belong to this hub's reading. First, within the tale, the experience is unverifiable by design: the only witness to the song's words is the bound man, and he is the poem's known liar, reporting a song no one else heard, whose content happens to be a perfect compliment to himself — the Sirens of all the earth know Odysseus, and sing of Troy. Section II's question returns here in its sharpest form, and the poem does not resolve it. Second, the Sirens of Homer are two, not a choir; they are never described — no feathers, no fish-tails, no instruments are given in the text — and they are never named in the poem. The bird-women of Greek vase-painting, the named Sirens of later mythography (Parthenope and her sisters, whose monument at Naples Strabo cites as evidence for the voyage's geography, Geography 1.2.13; Section XIV), and the mermaids of medieval tradition are all stratum (c), the afterlife. Homer's Sirens are voices in a meadow of bones, and their horror is that what they offer is true in form: a man who stayed to listen to everything would indeed learn everything — at the price of never going anywhere again.

THE TWO TECHNOLOGIES OF BOOK 12

Wax for the crew: safety by ignorance — the danger never enters. Rope for the captain: safety by constraint — the danger is fully experienced and physically disobeyed. The poem distinguishes the two protections and assigns each to the party it fits. Only one man aboard is permitted to know what the song was, and he is the man telling the story.

THE SIRENS ELSEWHERE IN EARLY EPIC

The Odyssey itself knows one earlier ship that passed the dangers of this sea: the Argo, which Circe says alone has passed the Wandering Rocks, because Hera sent her through, for Jason was dear to her (12.69–72, paraphrased). The Argonautic tradition, treated in this hub's Heroes and Epic article, later gave Jason his own Sirens passage — survived by Orpheus's counter-song — an answering episode composed in dialogue with this one.

XI. Scylla and Charybdis: The Impossible Choice

Past the Sirens, Circe's briefing offers two routes, and the first is a refusal: the Wandering Rocks (the Planctae), against which the great wave of dark-eyed Amphitrite roars, where not even the doves that bear ambrosia to Zeus pass without the rock snatching one away, and where no ship of men has ever escaped except the Argo alone, saved by Hera (12.59–72, paraphrased). Odysseus does not attempt them. The other route is the strait, and Circe describes its two cliffs as a paired engine of loss (12.73–126, paraphrased from Murray). On one side the cliff rises to a sharp peak lost in dark cloud that never lifts; midway up, turned west toward Erebus, is a dim cave, and in it dwells Scylla, yelping terribly — her voice like a new-born whelp's, but she herself an evil monster whom not even a god would be glad to meet: twelve misshapen feet, six exceeding long necks, on each an awful head with three rows of teeth thick and close and full of black death; her body hidden to the middle in the cave, her heads thrust out over the chasm to fish the rock for dolphins, sea-dogs, and whatever greater beast the deep rears. No sailors may boast they have fled her unscathed: with each head she carries off a man, snatching him from the dark-prowed ship. On the other side, an arrow-shot away, the lower cliff holds a great fig tree in full leaf, and beneath it divine Charybdis, who thrice a day belches forth the black water and thrice sucks it terribly down; to be there when she sucks is destruction from which not even the Earth-shaker could save a man. Circe's counsel is arithmetic, and she does not soften it: draw very close to Scylla's cliff and drive the ship past quickly — for it is better far to mourn six comrades in the ship than all together (12.108–110, paraphrased from Murray).

Odysseus's response to the briefing is the hero in one question. Come, goddess, tell me truly, he asks: is there no way to escape from fell Charybdis and at the same time ward off that other, when she works harm to my comrades (12.112–114, paraphrased)? Circe's rebuke is the poem's own judgement on the question: rash man, is your heart again set on deeds of war and toil? Will you not yield even to the immortal gods? Scylla is not mortal but an immortal bane, dread and dire and fierce and not to be fought with; there is no defence; to flee from her is bravest; and if you tarry to arm yourself by the cliff, she may dart forth again and seize as many men a second time (12.116–124, paraphrased). Row past with all your might, and call on Crataiis, Scylla's mother, to keep her from darting forth again. The passage is the wanderings' clearest statement of their physics: some dangers in this world are not problems to be solved by mētis but prices to be paid at the lowest available rate, and the captain's art is reduced to choosing the rate and keeping the rowers rowing.

The Silence About Scylla

What Odysseus then does with Circe's information is the episode's moral centre, and the poem — through the teller's own confession — judges it openly. When the ship sails, Odysseus rehearses the dangers to his crew in full: the Sirens, the wax, the bonds (12.153–164). Of Scylla he says nothing. His reason is given in his own words at 12.222–223, paraphrased here from Murray: of Scylla he would not go on to speak, a cureless bane, lest haply his comrades, seized with fear, should cease from rowing and huddle together in the hold. The decision is defensible as seamanship — a crew that stops rowing in the strait dies entire in Charybdis — and the poem lets the defence stand in the text. It also shows the cost without comment. Odysseus, against Circe's explicit warning, puts on his glorious armour, takes two long spears, and stations himself on the fore-deck where Scylla will first appear — the very delay and posture of fighting she forbade — and scans the misty rock until his eyes tire, seeing nothing (12.224–233, paraphrased). The ship enters the strait wailing. The men's eyes are on Charybdis, seething like a cauldron on a great fire, spray falling on both cliff-tops, the sea-bottom showing black with sand as she sucks down, pale fear upon them all — and meanwhile, from above, Scylla seizes six of the comrades, the best in strength and might. Odysseus turns and sees their feet and hands lifted aloft above him; they cry out to him, calling him by name for the last time, in anguish of heart; and the tale gives the dying men the poem's most terrible simile: as a fisherman on a jutting rock casts his baited horn down to the little fishes and flings each catch writhing ashore, so were they drawn writhing up toward the cliffs, and at her doors she devoured them, shrieking and stretching out their hands toward him in their awful death-struggle (12.234–257, paraphrased from Murray). The teller's verdict on his own episode follows immediately, and it is not self-exculpation: most piteous, he says, did his eyes behold that thing, of all that he bore while he explored the paths of the sea (12.258–259, paraphrased).

The judgement the poem invites is therefore double, and this archive states both halves. The silence about Scylla very probably saved the ship: Circe's route required speed past the cliff, and panic in the strait was the one unaffordable luxury. The silence also meant that six named-quality men — the best in strength and might — rowed into a death they had not been told existed, under a captain in armour who had been warned not to fight and fought nothing, while their last act was to call his name. Odysseus narrates all of this himself, including his disobedience of Circe's arming warning and his reason for the concealment. A merely self-serving narrator would have cut the armour, or the reason, or the calling of his name. That he keeps them is either honesty, or the deeper cunning of a teller who knows that confessed faults buy belief for the whole — Section II's undecidable alternative, at its most pointed. The strait's afterlife on the map — the Strait of Messina, Scyllaeum, the swordfish hunt Polybius matched to Scylla's fishing — is stratum (c), and is treated in Section XIV.

XII. Thrinacia: The Cattle of the Sun and the Lone Survivor

The last episode of the wanderings proper is the one both prophets have already narrated. Teiresias in Hades (11.104–114) and Circe on Aeaea (12.127–141) give the same conditional warning, in nearly the same terms, and Odysseus repeats it to his men before the island is even reached. Thrinacia is the isle of Helios Hyperion, the Sun who oversees all things and overhears all things. There feed his cattle and his flocks — seven herds of kine and as many fair flocks of sheep, fifty in each — tended by his daughters, the nymphs Phaethusa and Lampetie. The herds are outside nature: they bear no young, and they never die (12.127–130, paraphrased from Murray). The terms are exact. Leave them unharmed and heed your homeward way, and you may yet reach Ithaca, though in evil plight. Harm them, and Circe foretells ruin for the ship and the comrades — and for Odysseus himself, even if he escapes, a late return, in evil case, after losing all his comrades (12.137–141, paraphrased). The audience has now heard the catastrophe prophesied three times before it happens. Book 12's final movement is the story of men walking, with open eyes and sworn oaths, into a foretold destruction — because they are starving.

Eurylochus's First Argument: The Landing

Odysseus's seamanship is, for once, exactly what the prophecy requires: still at sea, hearing the lowing of the cattle and the bleating of the sheep carried over the water, he urges the crew to row the black ship past the island without landing at all (12.260–276, paraphrased). Eurylochus answers him — and the poem gives the answer the full dignity of a speech (12.278–293, paraphrased from Murray). Hardy are you, Odysseus; your strength is beyond other men's and your limbs never grow weary; verily you are wholly wrought of iron, if you will not suffer your comrades, worn out with toil and drowsiness, to set foot on shore where they might make ready a savoury supper on this sea-girt isle — but bid us wander on as we are through the swift night over the misty deep. It is from the night that fierce winds are born, wreckers of ships. How could anyone escape utter destruction if a blast of the South Wind or the blustering West Wind should suddenly come, which wreck ships most often in despite of the sovereign gods? For this time, let us yield to black night, make our supper by the ship, and embark in the morning. The speech is not folly. It is a tired officer's risk assessment, and on its own terms — night sailing in unknown water, an exhausted crew — it is right. The rest of the comrades assent. Odysseus yields, recognising, he says, that some god is devising ill — and exacts the one protection left to him: a mighty oath from every man that if they find herd or flock, no one will slay cow or sheep in the blind folly of his mind, but will be content with the food immortal Circe gave (12.294–304, paraphrased). They swear. They land. That night a storm from Zeus drives them to haul the ship into a hollow cave, and then, for a full month, the South Wind blows unceasingly, with no wind but East and South — the two winds that cannot take them home (12.325–326, paraphrased). The trap is weather, and it closes slowly.

Eurylochus's Second Argument: The Slaughter

While the ship's stores last — grain and red wine — the men keep their hands from the cattle, for they are eager to save their lives. When the stores are gone, they roam the island hunting fish and fowl with bent hooks, hunger pinching their bellies (12.327–332, paraphrased). Then Odysseus withdraws inland alone to pray to the gods that one of them might show him a way to go — and the gods, he says, shed sweet sleep upon his eyelids. The poem's audience has seen this sleep before: on the tenth day within sight of Ithaca, the captain slept, and the bag was opened. The pattern is structural, not accidental: the Apologoi's two irrecoverable disasters both happen in the commander's absence, and both are his men's reasoned decisions, taken by vote, after speeches. Eurylochus's second speech (12.340–351) is the stronger of the two, and it deserves to be read as the poem wrote it — an argument, not a temptation. In paraphrase from Murray: hear my words, comrades, for all your evil plight. All forms of death are hateful to wretched mortals, but to die of hunger, and so meet one's doom, is the most pitiful. Come, let us drive off the best of the kine of Helios and offer sacrifice to the immortals who hold broad heaven; and if we ever reach Ithaca, our native land, we will straightway build a rich temple to Helios Hyperion and put in it many goodly offerings. And if he is wroth for his straight-horned kine and is minded to destroy our ship, and the other gods consent — rather would I lose my life once for all with a gulp at the wave than pine slowly away in a desert isle. Every element is pious in form: sacrifice first, vows of restitution, a temple promised, the risk consciously accepted. The men assent. They drive off the best of the cattle — grazing, the teller notes, not far from the ship — pray, using oak leaves for barley because the ship has none, cut the throats, flay, cut out the thigh-pieces, cover them with a double layer of fat, lay raw flesh on them, pour water for wine because there is no wine, and roast the entrails (12.352–363, paraphrased). It is a correct sacrifice performed on forbidden victims, and the poem's horror lives in that correctness.

Odysseus wakes to the savour of roasting fat on the wind and cries out to the gods that they lulled him in pitiless sleep for his ruin (12.371–373, paraphrased). The portents answer before any god speaks: the hides crawl; the flesh, roast and raw, bellows upon the spits; there is a lowing as of kine (12.394–396, paraphrased). Lampetie carries the news to Helios, and Helios, wrathful, demands vengeance of Zeus with the poem's most quietly absolute threat: if the men do not pay fit atonement for the cattle in which he took delight when he went toward the starry heaven and when he turned back to earth from heaven, he will go down to Hades and shine among the dead (12.374–382, paraphrased). Zeus promises the thunderbolt. The detail that Odysseus adds — this I heard from fair-haired Calypso, and she said she had it from Hermes the messenger (12.389–390, paraphrased) — is worth pausing on: the narrator, mid-tale, cites his source for a conversation among gods at which no mortal was present, and the source is the goddess with whom he spent the seven years after the events. It is either scrupulous sourcing or a liar's verisimilitude, and it is placed exactly where a Scheria listener, or a reader, would begin to ask how the teller could know this part. The poem anticipates its audience's scepticism even here.

For six days the comrades feast on the best of the cattle of Helios. On the seventh, the wind drops; they embark at once, set the mast, hoist the white sail — and, once no land is in sight but only sky and sea, Zeus sets a black cloud above the ship, the West Wind comes shrieking, the fore-stays snap, the mast falls aft and crushes the pilot's skull, and Zeus's thunderbolt strikes the ship full of sulphurous smoke (12.397–419, paraphrased). The comrades fall from the ship and are borne on the waves about the black ship like sea-crows, and the god takes from them their returning. Odysseus alone keeps his footing as the surge tears the sides from the keel; with the ox-hide back-stay he lashes keel and mast together, sits on the raft of wreckage, and is borne by the direful winds — back, in the night, to Charybdis. He survives her a second time by the episode's most animal image: springing up to the tall fig tree, he clings to it like a bat, unable to plant his feet or climb, roots far below and branches out of reach above, until Charybdis vomits forth mast and keel again at the hour when a man rises from the assembly for his supper, one who decides the many quarrels of young men seeking judgement — and he lets go, drops beyond the spars, sits on them, and rows with his hands (12.426–446, paraphrased). Zeus does not let Scylla see him again, else, the teller says, he would never have escaped utter destruction. Nine days he is borne. On the tenth night the gods bring him to Ogygia. The fleet that left Troy with twelve ships and hundreds of men has completed its reduction: one man, on wreckage, arriving at stillness.

EURYLOCHUS: THE CREW'S VOICE

Eurylochus speaks for the men at Circe's door (refusal, Book 10), at the landing (Book 12), and at the slaughter (Book 12). He is right that Circe's house is a snare, right that night sailing kills ships, and right that starvation is the most pitiful death. The poem does not make its catastrophe depend on a fool. It depends on a reasonable man whose reason has no answer to a god's prohibition except a temple promised later.

XIII. Calypso on Ogygia: The Still Point and Immortality Refused

The Apologoi end where the Odyssey began. Odysseus's arrival on Ogygia (12.447–450) closes his tale — but why should I tell you this, he asks Alcinous, in Murray's paraphrase-near rendering of the close: it was but yesterday that I told it in your hall to yourself and to your noble wife; it is an irksome thing, it seems to me, to tell again a plain-told tale (12.450–453, paraphrased). The refusal is a storyteller's curtain line, and it is also a structural marker: the Calypso story belongs to the poem's present, already told to the Phaeacians on the previous night (the audience is to understand that Odysseus gave an outline account in Book 7, in his supplication of Arete) and narrated in full by the primary narrator in Book 5. What the wanderings contribute is the approach: a lone man on wreckage, delivered by nine days of sea to the one shore in the poem where nothing further will happen to him for seven years. The still point is not an episode among episodes. It is the anti-episode — the place where the sea-tale stops.

Calypso (Greek Kalupsō, from kaluptō, to cover or conceal — the Concealer) is a daughter of Atlas, fair-tressed, a dread goddess of human speech, dwelling on Ogygia, which the poem elsewhere calls the navel of the sea (1.50, paraphrased). She receives the castaway, tends him, and keeps him — the primary narrator's Book 5 account, not Odysseus's tale, supplies the terms and the texture, and this article follows Book 5 for them, as the poem does. She saved him, she tells Hermes, when he was bestriding the keel all alone, welcomed him kindly, gave him food — and said that she would make him immortal and ageless all his days (5.135–136, in Murray's translation, paraphrased here). She desires him as husband. The offer is genuine and it is permanent: immortality, agelessness, a goddess's bed and table on an island of four springs, vine-covered caves, and meadows of violets and parsley, where even an immortal who chanced to come might gaze and marvel (5.63–74, paraphrased). Against it stands the picture the narrator gives of the offer's recipient, day after day: Odysseus sitting on the shore, his eyes never dry of tears, his sweet life ebbing away as he longs for his return, racking his soul with tears and groans and griefs and looking over the unresting sea, shedding tears — by night sleeping beside the willing nymph unwilling, by day on the rocks and sands (5.82–84 and 5.151–158, paraphrased from Murray). The poem does not present a man heroically resisting temptation in each moment. It presents a man being eroded by a paradise he did not choose.

The refusal, when Zeus's order through Hermes finally forces the parting, is the poem's most famous act of valuation, and it is conducted as a negotiation between two clear-eyed parties. Calypso, having sworn the great oath by earth, heaven, and the down-flowing water of the Styx that she plots no fresh mischief — the same oath-formula Odysseus exacted from Circe, exacted again because the hero trusts no goddess's goodwill unsworn — makes her final case at their last meal (5.202–213, paraphrased from Murray): if you knew in your heart all the measure of woe it is your fate to fulfil before you reach your native land, you would abide here and keep this house with me, and be immortal, for all your desire to see the wife for whom you long day by day; and surely I declare myself not inferior to her in form or stature, for it is in no wise seemly that mortal women should vie with immortals. Odysseus's answer (5.214–224) concedes every premise and refuses the conclusion. In Murray's translation:

“Mighty goddess, be not wroth with me for this. I know full well of myself that wise Penelope is meaner to look upon than thou in comeliness and in stature, for she is a mortal, while thou art immortal and ageless. But even so I wish and long day by day to reach my home, and to see the day of my return.” — HOMER, ODYSSEY 5.214–220, TRANS. A. T. MURRAY (LOEB, 1919)

He adds that if a god smites him again on the wine-dark sea he will endure it, having a heart in his breast that endures affliction, for he has suffered much and toiled much already amid waves and in war; let this also be added to that (5.221–224, paraphrased). The exchange is the wanderings' still point stated as a philosophy: offered the exit from the human condition itself — no ageing, no death, no loss — the hero chooses the condition, because the condition is where Penelope, Ithaca, and his own name are. Later ages read the refusal variously: as the soul's rejection of a lower paradise, as the epic's affirmation of mortal life, as a husband's fidelity. The poem itself gives it no gloss at all, which is why it can bear all the glosses. Calypso, for her part, keeps her oath exactly: she gives him tools — a great bronze axe and a polished adze — shows him the tall dry timbers, brings cloth for a sail, provisions the raft with bread, water, and red wine, clothes him, and sends a fair wind behind him (5.228–268, paraphrased). She is the wanderings' second competent goddess, and like Circe she exits the tale having behaved, at the end, with a precision the hero's own men rarely matched. The seven years themselves the poem leaves almost blank — a duration, not a story — and Odysseus's refusal to retell them at Scheria (12.450–453) is the teller agreeing with his poem: stillness does not narrate. What narrates is the leaving.

XIV. Mapping the Unmappable: Eratosthenes, Strabo, and the Hunt for the Route

No sooner was the Odyssey a classic than readers began to ask where the wanderings happened — and the poem, as Sections I–XIII have shown, is built to frustrate the question. Its real places are real: Troy, Ismarus, Cape Malea, Cythera, Ithaca. Its wonder-places have names, directions of a sort, and sailing times of a sort, but no bearings that survive checking: nine days of baneful winds from Malea to the Lotus-Eaters; six days' rowing to Telepylus; a North Wind's effortless carriage across Ocean to Hades and back. The afterlife of the itinerary — stratum (c) — is the record of two thousand years of readers deciding, in succession, that this frustration was a puzzle to solve, a licence to invent, or a category error to expose. The ancient debate already contains all three positions, fully formed, and its documents are unusually good, because the debate's loser preserved the winner's evidence inside his own refutation.

Eratosthenes' Cobbler

Eratosthenes of Cyrene (c. 276–c. 194 BCE), librarian of Alexandria and the founder of scientific geography — the man who measured the Earth's circumference from shadow-lengths — wrote a detailed investigation of the wanderings and reached a sceptic's conclusion: Homer's aim as a poet, he held, is to entertain, not to instruct, and the wanderings' scene is not recoverable because it was never laid anywhere recoverable. His verdict survives because Strabo quotes it in order to attack it. The saying is given here, as the brief for this article required it to be verified before quotation, from the text of Strabo, Geography 1.2.15, in H. L. Jones's Loeb translation, as read in the LacusCurtius edition of Jones's translation:

“You will find the scene of the wanderings of Odysseus when you find the cobbler who sewed up the bag of the winds.” — ERATOSTHENES, AS REPORTED BY STRABO, GEOGRAPHY 1.2.15, TRANS. H. L. JONES (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY)

The choice of emblem is exact. Eratosthenes does not pick the Cyclops or the descent to Hades — wonders a rationalist might allegorise or euhemerise away. He picks the bag of winds: a homely, manufactured object, an ox-hide sewn by somebody, somewhere, in a world where weather is luggage. Find the craftsman, he says, and you will have your map; until then, you have a poem. Strabo (1.2.15) reports that Polybius disapproved of the declaration, and Strabo himself, in the long discussion at Geography 1.2.7–37, argues the opposite case with great learning: that Homer founded his wanderings on real western geography, that disagreement among identifiers about details confirms rather than refutes a Sicilian-Italian setting, and that local traditions — the monument of Parthenope the Siren shown at Naples, the Sirenussae off the Sorrentine peninsula, Scyllaeum and the Strait, Circaeum, the names Pyriphlegethon and Acheron naturalised around Cumae and Lake Avernus — are traces of real persons and events, not poets' fictions (1.2.13–18, paraphrased from Jones's translation). The reader should notice what kind of evidence Strabo's case rests on: place-names, cult monuments, and local traditions of exactly the kind Greek and Italian cities manufactured, in quantity, to attach Homer's prestige to their own shores. A Siren's tomb at Naples proves that Naples claimed a Siren. Whether it proves anything about a Bronze Age or early Iron Age voyage is a different question, and Eratosthenes' cobbler stands permanently in its way.

Polybius and the Rationalising Method

Between Eratosthenes and Strabo stands Polybius (2nd century BCE), whose treatment of the wanderings (preserved in Strabo 1.2.15–17) founded the rationalising method that every later mapper would inherit. Polybius, in Strabo's report (paraphrased from Jones), insists that the wanderings as a whole are not to be interpreted as myth: insignificant elements of myth have been added by the poet, as with the Trojan War itself, but the scene of the whole story was laid in the neighbourhood of Sicily. His proofs are workingmen's proofs. Aeolus was a real man who taught navigators to steer through the Strait of Messina's ebb and flow and reverse currents, and was therefore called lord of the winds — as Danaus, who found Argos's underground water, and Atreus, who found the sun's reverse motion, were made kings for their discoveries. Scylla's description matches what takes place off the Scyllaean Rock, where the swordfish (the galeotae) hunt the tunnies driven in by the current — the poet's monster fishing for dolphins and dogfish is a fishery, magnified. Charybdis corresponds to the behaviour of the Strait's waters. The facts about Meninx, Polybius adds, agree with what Homer says of the Lotus-Eaters. And discrepancies are to be ascribed to changes wrought by time, to ignorance, or to poetic licence — licence being compounded, in Polybius's famous triad as Strabo reports it, of history, rhetorical composition, and myth, whose aims are truth, vividness, and pleasure-and-amazement respectively (1.2.17, paraphrased). It is a sophisticated instrument. It is also, as Eratosthenes saw, unfalsifiable: every agreement is evidence for the map, and every disagreement is poetic licence. Strabo himself, to his credit, catches Polybius suppressing the poem's Atlantic markers — the ship leaving the stream of Ocean, Ogygia the navel of the sea, the Phaeacians far apart in the wash of the waves, farthermost of men — and remarks that Polybius by suppressing them destroys what the poet states in express terms, while remaining right, in Strabo's judgement, to place the wanderings near Sicily and Italy (1.2.18, paraphrased). The ancient debate ends, characteristically, without a winner: the sceptic has the logic, the mappers have the coastline, and the poem has both.

The Modern Hunt, and the Majority Verdict

The modern hunt for the route repeated the ancient one with better charts and the same logic. Hellenistic and Roman localisation had already fixed the canonical western itinerary — Sicily for the Cyclopes and Thrinacia (Thucydides 6.2 already reports Sicily as the Cyclopes' and Laestrygonians' reputed home, while pointedly declining to vouch for it), the Strait for Scylla and Charybdis, Circeii in Italy for Circe — and early modern and nineteenth-century scholarship, culminating in Victor Bérard's great multi-volume argument for Phoenician sailing directions underlying the poem, tried to sail the voyage port by port. Twentieth-century yachtsmen-scholars retraced it under sail; popular atlases still print the map. Against the whole enterprise stand two facts that no itinerary has dissolved. First, the poem's sailing data do not cohere as navigation: durations are given in whole days, directions are almost never given at all after Malea, and the two fixed points the poem insists on — Ocean's stream, and islands at the sea's navel and the world's edge — are not Mediterranean data. Second, the wonder-places have no independent attestation: no ancient source unconnected with the Odyssey knows Aeaea, Ogygia, Thrinacia, or Telepylus as places at all. The identifications all flow one way, from poem to map, never from map to poem. Stanford's verdict (Section II) — geography ends at Malea and Cythera, and wonderland begins — remains the majority position in the scholarship this article consulted, with the refinement that wonderland itself may preserve real sailors' knowledge in displaced form: the Strait's currents are real, swordfish do hunt there, volcanic and tidal dangers of the western seas were real dangers to early Greek shipping toward Italy and Sicily, and a tradition that sailed west would naturally dress its genuine hazards in the tale-types it had aboard. That refinement is an argument, not a find, and this archive labels it as such. The cobbler has not been found. Neither has any reason to stop admiring the bag.

XV. The Folktale Stratum: Ogres, Names, and Travelling Tales

Beneath the poem's tale lies the stratum that made it tellable. The Apologoi are not a traveller's report dressed in marvels; they are a master singer's construction out of the common stock of Mediterranean and international storytelling — sea-tales told in ports, wonder-tales told everywhere — and the proof is comparative. Episode after episode, when set beside the world's recorded folktales, resolves into a known tale-type with local variations: the blinding of the man-eating ogre, the forbidden container opened at the goal, the food that makes the eater forget home, the host who transforms guests into beasts, the harbour of cannibals, the song that must not be followed, the herd that must not be touched. This section sets out the stratum's clearest case in full — the Cyclops — and then maps the rest briefly, because the Cyclops case contains the method and its limits entire.

Page's Analysis: Two Tales Fused

The modern analysis begins with Denys Page's Folktales in Homer's Odyssey (the Sather Classical Lectures, published 1973, developing his earlier work on the Odyssey), the study the brief for this article names and the discussion here follows in paraphrase. Page treated the Apologoi as, in his phrase as standardly reported, a collection of universal folktales — Weltmärchen — independent of one another and of the poem's main theme, fitted into the framework of the returning hero. For the Cyclops he argued, with a wide comparative dossier, that the Homeric episode is a fusion of two distinct folktale themes: on the one hand the tale of the ogre-type giant who devours human flesh and is in the end blinded by his intended victim; on the other hand the separate “No-man” theme, the name-trick by which the hero's self-naming defeats the monster's cry for help. The fusion is visible, Page argued, in the episode's seams and in its divergences from the folktale record. In the widespread versions collected by folklorists — Oskar Hackman's catalogue of the Polyphemus saga (1904) assembled hundreds of variants from across Europe, the Caucasus, and Central Asia — the blinding instrument is standardly a heated metal spit or skewer, the ogre is standardly two-eyed or unspecified, the escape is standardly in a ram's skin or among the flock in disguise, and the variants lack both the giant's drunkenness and the name-trick. Homer's version substitutes a wooden olive stake for the metal spit, makes the giant emphatically one-eyed (so that one thrust suffices), adds the wine — which requires Maron, Ismarus, and the whole apparatus of Section III to plant it — and adds Outis. On Page's reading, those additions are the poet's own work: the points where a travelling tale was Homericised, tied into the epic's economy of xenia, wine, and mētis. Later scholarship has kept Page's framework while disciplining its confidence. Pura Nieto Hernández's re-examination of the Cyclops episode (American Journal of Philology) accepts the conflation of the two folklore themes as demonstrated since Page, while insisting on how thoroughly the poem has rewoven them into its own obsessions — hospitality, food, revenge, and the hero's relation to his companions. A recent fresh reading of the old arguments (Gousopoulos, Greek, Roman, and Byzantine Studies 65, 2025) urges caution in the other direction: not that the episode lacks a folk tradition behind it, but that the reconstruction of specific pre-Homeric folktales from modern comparanda is less secure than the classic accounts assumed, and that Hackman's catalogue can no longer bear all the weight placed on it. This archive reports the position as it stands: the folktale derivation of the Cyclops episode is the scholarly consensus; the exact shape of the pre-Homeric tales is reconstruction, and is labelled reconstruction wherever this article relies on it.

The tale-type has a catalogue number, and the number is worth giving because it marks how far the story travelled without Homer's help. In the Aarne–Thompson–Uther index of folktale types, the blinding of Polyphemus is ATU 1137, “The Ogre Blinded (Polyphemus)” — the type is also called the Polyphemsage, the Polyphemus saga. Its best-known non-Greek attestation is the story of Basat and Tepegöz, the one-eyed ogre of the Turkish Book of Dede Korkut: a shepherd-giant who devours men, blinded by the hero with a heated spit, who escapes in a ram's skin while the ogre feels the flock — a parallel close enough that scholars since the nineteenth century have debated whether the Turkish tale descends from Homer, whether Homer descends from a common Central Asian and Mediterranean stock, or whether both draw independently on a tale far older than either text. The Basque Tartalo, the one-eyed cave-giant of Pyrenean tradition blinded with a heated iron spit, belongs to the same family. The debate is not settled, and its unsettledness is itself the datum: the tale was already international before anyone wrote down the version we have, which is what “stratum” means in this article's title. Homer did not invent the blinded ogre. He invented — on Page's analysis — the ogre's drunkenness, his guest-gift, his pathos over the ram, his divine father, and his curse: which is to say, he invented everything in the episode that matters to the Odyssey.

One limit must be stated plainly, because the folktale method invites its own excess. To identify a tale-type behind an episode is not to explain the episode. The types are plots without weight; the poem supplies the weight — in the Cyclops's tenderness to his ram, in Aeolus's cold doctrine, in Eurylochus's reasonableness, in the six men calling Odysseus's name as Scylla lifts them. None of that is in ATU 1137 or any catalogue. The stratum explains where the singer found his stones. The building is his, and the building is what Sections III–XIII described.

XVI. Allegory's Wanderings: Circe, the Sirens, and the Soul

The wanderings' third life — after the poem's tale (a) and the folktale stratum (b) — is their absorption into allegory, and it began early. Greek readers who revered Homer and were troubled by him developed, from at least the sixth century BCE onward, the technique of allegoresis: reading a scandalous or marvellous passage as saying one thing and signifying another. Theagenes of Rhegium, the earliest allegorist whose name survives, was already defending Homer's warring gods as figures of natural and moral forces. The wanderings, with their transparently moralised dangers, were allegory's richest Homeric ground after the gods themselves, and two episodes became its standing exhibits: Circe and the Sirens. This section traces the reading tradition in outline — briefly, as the hub's plan requires, because the full treatment of myth read as veiled doctrine, from the ancient allegorists through the mytho-hermetic tradition to Pernety, belongs to this hub's Alchemical Allegory article, to which this section is a forward flag, not a substitute.

The ancient allegorist whose work survives entire is the author known as Heraclitus (not the Ephesian philosopher), whose Homeric Problems (1st century CE, in the standard dating) defends Homer passage by passage by uncovering the hidden sense. His Circe is the tradition's template. In paraphrase of his treatment (Homeric Problems, on the Circe episode; the reading is also preserved in the pseudo-Plutarchan On Homer): the transformation of Odysseus's companions into swine and kindred beasts hints at the fate of unthinking men's souls, transferred into bestial forms of life as they fall into the circling rotation of the world — which, the allegorist says, is what “Circe” names; and she is fittingly made a child of the Sun, dwelling on Aiaia. The cup of Circe, in the moralising version of the same reading, is the cup of pleasure: those who drink it trade a human life for a swinish one. The reading is not arbitrary decoration. It fastens on the poem's own precision — the swine keep their human minds (Section VIII) — and draws the conclusion the poem leaves undrawn: that appetite ungoverned is a change of species with consciousness preserved to suffer it. Stoic and later moral readers generalised the pattern. Odysseus himself becomes the sage, the man of endurance and self-command passing through a world of personified dangers; the Sirens become seductive pleasure, or seductive argument, or the song of fame, to be heard, if at all, only bound by prior reason; the lotus becomes forgetfulness of one's true home and end.

The Neoplatonic readers took the whole voyage. Porphyry (3rd century CE), in his essay On the Cave of the Nymphs — a commentary on the cave of the nymphs on Ithaca in Odyssey 13, the harbour where the Phaeacians set the sleeping hero ashore — reads Odysseus's journey as the soul's journey: the sea as the realm of generation and becoming, the wanderings as the soul's exile among material pleasures and dangers, Ithaca as the soul's true home beyond the sea of birth. The tradition Porphyry inherits, including Numenius of Apamea's reading of Odysseus escaping the waters of Poseidon — the realm of genesis, of becoming — made the hero a figure of the soul escaping embodiment itself. In that reading Circe's island belongs to the economy of reincarnation: bewitched by the pleasures of the flesh, souls are reborn as beasts, and only the man on the path of return is immune. Early Christian readers baptised the mast: Odysseus bound upright to the wood, deaf to destruction and hearing the song through, became a type of the believer — in some patristic and medieval developments, of Christ on the cross — and the Sirens the world's vain delights. Each age kept the episodes and changed the doctrine they were made to carry, which is the definition of a successful allegory: the narrative is so exactly shaped — a cup, a song, a mast, an oath — that successive philosophies can pour themselves into it without breaking the vessel.

// ALLEGORY IS EVIDENCE — FOR ITS OWN CENTURY //

This hub's rule, stated on its index page and applied throughout this article, is that a reception is dated separately from its source. Heraclitus's Circe is evidence for 1st-century Homeric interpretation, Porphyry's voyage for 3rd-century Neoplatonic soteriology, and the moralised Sirens for the schools that moralised them. None of them is evidence for what the Odyssey's singer intended, and this article has not used them as such. What they jointly prove is narrower and still remarkable: that within a few centuries of its fixation, the poem's sea-tale was already being read as a map of the soul — the third map, after the singers' wonderland and the geographers' Sicily, on which the wanderings have been plotted. The alchemical reading of myth, in which Pernety's mytho-hermetic key unlocks the wanderings and the labours alike as veiled laboratory doctrine, is the early modern heir of this same technique, and is treated in full at Alchemical Allegory.

The wanderings end, then, with three maps and no route. The geographers' map fails on the poem's own data, and Eratosthenes' cobbler guards the failure. The folklorists' map succeeds, but it maps the tale's ancestry, not its meaning. The allegorists' map succeeds on its own terms, but the territory it charts is the reader's soul, not Odysseus's sea. What remains, when all three are laid aside, is the thing itself: a night at Scheria, a guest who has lost everything telling how he lost it, a court spellbound in the shadowy halls, and a king saying — in the poem's most double-edged compliment — that the teller is no cheat, that there is a shapeliness on his words, and that he has told his tale like a singer. The Odyssey asks its audience to do with that compliment exactly what Alcinous did with the tale: reward it, be moved by it, and notice, even while being moved, precisely what kind of object it is. Ten years, twelve ships, and every companion went into the making of the story. The story is what came home.
