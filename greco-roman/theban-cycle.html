I. The Other Great Cycle: Thebes Against Troy

Greek epic had two great theatres, and the Greeks themselves paired them. When Hesiod, in the Works and Days, came to the fourth of his five races of mankind — the race of heroes, the demigods — he named exactly two wars as the events that destroyed them: some, he says, fell at seven-gated Thebes, fighting for the flocks of Oedipus, and others at Troy, for rich-haired Helen's sake (Works and Days 161–165, paraphrased; the passage is treated in this hub's Theogony article). The pairing stuck. Thebes and Troy are the twin poles of the heroic age: the two cities whose falls closed it, sung in two families of epic poems, and remembered ever after as the measure of what the age of heroes had been.

But the two cycles are opposite in shape, and the opposition is the subject of this article. The Trojan cycle is centrifugal: a coalition gathers from all over Greece, sails outward to a foreign shore, and fights an external enemy for ten years. Its violence, however terrible, is directed at strangers, and its survivors scatter homeward across the whole Greek world. The Theban cycle is centripetal. Almost nothing in it travels. A Phoenician prince arrives from outside and founds a city; after that founding act, the story never really leaves Thebes again — it only descends, from generation to generation of one royal house, each generation's disaster prepared by the one before. The enemy is never finally foreign. The dragon Cadmus kills is the city's own tutelary monster, sacred to Ares. The men who spring from its teeth kill each other before they can kill anyone else. The god who tears the royal house apart in the second generation is the house's own grandson, born in its own palace. The king who saves the city from the Sphinx destroys it by the saving, because the throne and the marriage bed he wins are his father's and his mother's. And the cycle's two wars — the Seven and the Epigoni — are both, at bottom, a quarrel between two brothers over one inheritance, fought the second time by the sons of the first war's dead. Troy is a family destroyed by a war. Thebes is a war generated, twice over, by a family.

The house has a genealogy, and the cycle is unintelligible without it. In outline — the narrative sections below fill in every link — it runs as follows. Cadmus, son of the Phoenician king Agenor, founds Thebes and marries Harmonia, daughter of Ares and Aphrodite. Their children are a son, Polydorus, and four daughters — Autonoe, Ino, Semele, and Agave — every one of whose stories ends in a child destroyed. Polydorus's line continues through Labdacus to Laius, and from Laius to Oedipus. Oedipus's children by Jocasta — Eteocles, Polynices, Antigone, and Ismene — are the cycle's last full generation: the brothers kill each other, Antigone dies for burying one of them, and the kingship passes to the collateral lines, to Creon and to Eteocles' son Laodamas, until Polynices' son Thersander is restored by the Epigoni's victory — a restoration that lasts only until Thersander himself is killed on the road to Troy, handing the Theban story over, at its very end, to the Trojan one. Four generations from Cadmus to Oedipus; a fifth to the Epigoni. The article follows them in order.

GENERATION I — THE FOUNDERS

Cadmus of Tyre and Harmonia, daughter of Ares and Aphrodite. The founding acts: the cow followed, the dragon killed, the teeth sown, the Kadmeia built, the alphabet — in Herodotus's account — brought with the Phoenicians who came with Cadmus (Sections III–VI).

GENERATION II — THE DAUGHTERS

Semele blasted by Zeus's lightning; Ino mad, her son dead in the sea; Autonoe's son Actaeon torn apart by his own hounds; Agave's son Pentheus torn apart by his own mother. The house's second act is a catalogue of children destroyed (Sections VII–VIII).

GENERATION III–IV — THE LABDACIDS

Labdacus, Laius, Oedipus: the oracle defied, the child exposed, the father killed at the crossroads, the mother married, the truth recognised. Then Oedipus's sons, the curse, and the two wars (Sections IX–XVII).

THE FOUR LAYERS OF THIS ARTICLE

Throughout, four things are kept distinct: (a) the myth as told; (b) the lost epic tradition behind the telling; (c) the cult and archaeological footing at real Thebes; and (d) tragedy's remaking of all three in fifth-century Athens. A sentence that confuses the layers is, by this hub's rule, a sentence rewritten.

One more framing point, because it governs the tone of everything below. The Theban cycle is the darker of the two great cycles not because its body count is higher — Troy's is higher — but because its horrors are domestic and hereditary. Its characteristic act is not the duel but the recognition: the moment a character learns what relation they actually stand in to the person they have killed, married, eaten, or torn apart. Its characteristic instrument is not the spear but the inherited object — a necklace, a robe, a silver table, a golden cup — passed down the generations and ruining each new owner in turn. And its characteristic theology is the oracle fulfilled by the very steps taken to escape it. A reader who keeps those three signatures in mind — recognition, inheritance, and the self-fulfilling oracle — holds the key to the whole cycle, from the dragon's teeth to the fall of the city.

II. How the Cycle Reaches Us: Epics, Tragedies, and a Handbook

No Theban epic survives. That single fact shapes everything a modern reader can know about this cycle, and it must be stated before any of the story is told. The Trojan cycle lost most of its poems too, but it kept the Iliad and the Odyssey — two enormous, complete archaic epics around which everything else can be arranged. The Theban cycle kept nothing whole. Its poems — the Oedipodea, the Thebaid, the Epigoni, and the Alcmaeonis — survive as a handful of quoted lines, a few ancient testimonies about their length and authorship, and the use later writers made of them (Section XVIII gives the full inventory). What we possess instead is the cycle at second and third hand, in three great bodies of material, each with its own date and its own agenda.

First Body: The Early Notices — Homer and Hesiod

The earliest surviving Greek literature already treats the Theban stories as old, famous, and available for allusion rather than narration. Homer never tells a Theban story whole, but the poems are salted with references that assume an audience who knows them: the Iliad's Catalogue of Ships lists not Thebes itself but Hypothebai, “Lower Thebes” (Iliad 2.505) — as if, already in the epic present, the great city of the cycle stood diminished beside its own lower town, a detail ancient and modern readers alike have connected with the Epigoni's sack. The Iliad knows Tydeus's embassy to Thebes and his ambush on the way home (4.376–398), Zeus's love for Semele (14.323–324), and funeral games held at Thebes for the dead Oedipus (23.679–680). The Odyssey's catalogue of heroines in the underworld gives the oldest surviving connected account of Oedipus's marriage: Odysseus sees Epicaste — the name the later tradition standardises as Jocasta — who in ignorance married her own son, and hanged herself when the gods made the truth known, leaving Oedipus to rule the Cadmeans in sorrow under his mother's Furies (Odyssey 11.271–280, paraphrased). Note what Homer does not have: no blinding, no exile, no Sphinx, no plague. The archaic Oedipus of the Odyssey remains king after the revelation. Much of what a modern reader thinks of as “the myth of Oedipus” is later than Homer, and this article flags the joins where they matter. Hesiod, for his part, supplies the Sphinx's parentage — she belongs to the monster-brood of Echidna, a bane to the Cadmeans, in the Theogony's catalogue (326–332; see this hub's Theogony article) — and the heroes-at-Thebes passage of the Works and Days quoted in Section I.

Second Body: Tragedy — The Fifth-Century Remaking

The cycle's second life, and the one that fixed its modern shape, is Athenian tragedy. All three great tragedians worked the Theban material repeatedly, and seven Theban plays survive entire — more than for any other cycle. Aeschylus produced a connected Theban trilogy in 467 BCE — Laius, Oedipus, and Seven Against Thebes, with the satyr play Sphinx — of which only the Seven survives. Sophocles returned to the house three times across forty years, in three unconnected productions: Antigone (c. 442/441 BCE), Oedipus the King (conventionally c. 429 BCE; the date is an estimate, not a record), and Oedipus at Colonus (written near his death in 406 BCE and produced posthumously in 401 BCE). Euripides treated the material in the Phoenician Women (c. 411–409 BCE), the most densely populated retelling of the whole saga that survives from antiquity; in the Suppliants (c. 423 BCE), on the recovery of the Argive dead; in the Bacchae (produced posthumously, c. 405 BCE), on Pentheus; and in lost plays including a Chrysippus and an Antigone whose ending differed from Sophocles'. Tragedy is not a transparent window on the epic cycle. Each tragedian selects, compresses, invents, and — above all — re-motivates: motives that epic left to curse and oracle are re-examined as character, law, and civic argument. Where this article follows a tragedian's version against the handbook tradition, it says so.

Third Body: The Handbook — Apollodorus, and the Traveller Pausanias

The fullest continuous prose account of the cycle is the Library (Bibliothēkē) attributed to Apollodorus — in modern usage Pseudo-Apollodorus, since the work, dated to the first or second century CE, cannot be by the second-century BCE Athenian scholar of that name. Book 3, chapters 4–7, tells the Theban story from Cadmus to Alcmaeon in a single unbroken genealogical narrative, drawing on the lost epics, on early prose mythographers such as Pherecydes (whom it names where he differs), and on tragedy, and noting variant traditions as it goes. This article takes Apollodorus, in J. G. Frazer's Loeb translation, as its narrative spine — not because the Library is the oldest source (it is among the latest) but because it is the only source that tells the whole story in order, and because its habit of flagging variants (“some say”) lets a careful reader see the tradition's seams. Alongside it stands Pausanias, the second-century CE traveller whose Description of Greece devotes the first half of Book 9 to Thebes: its mythical history, its seven gates, its springs, tombs, and sanctuaries, described as things a visitor could still be shown. Pausanias is this article's chief witness for layer (c) — what the stories looked like when they had hardened into a landscape — and, in his argument at 9.5.10–11 about Oedipus's children, a rare ancient voice doing source-criticism on the cycle itself.

// THE PROCLUS GAP, STATED PLAINLY //

Readers of the Trojan cycle rely on the prose summaries of the Epic Cycle written by Proclus and preserved, through Photius, for the Trojan poems. No such summary survives for the Theban poems. Proclus's Chrestomathy evidently covered them — his summary of the Cypria begins by succeeding the Epigoni, the last Theban poem — but Photius, excerpting Proclus in the ninth century, dropped the Theban summaries, and they are lost. For the Theban epics we have fragments and testimonies only. Any account that purports to give the contents of the Oedipodea or the Epigoni in detail is reconstructing them from Apollodorus, tragedy, and scholia, and should be read as reconstruction. This article marks that boundary wherever it is crossed.

III. Cadmus: The Search for Europa and the Oracle's Cow

The cycle begins, uniquely among Greek foundation stories, with a failure. Cadmus does not set out to found a city. He sets out to find his sister. The story, as Apollodorus tells it (Library 3.1.1–2 and 3.4.1, in Frazer's translation, paraphrased here except where quotation is marked), begins in Phoenicia. Agenor, king in Phoenicia, has by Telephassa a daughter, Europa, and three sons — Cadmus, Phoenix, and Cilix. Zeus, desiring Europa, takes the shape of a tame bull, breathes saffron from his mouth (the detail is Apollodorus's), tempts her onto his back, and carries her across the sea to Crete, where she bears Minos, Sarpedon, and Rhadamanthys — the Cretan royal house, and with it the whole Cretan cycle treated in this hub's Heroes and Epic article's background, is the Theban cycle's first offshoot. Agenor sends his sons to search for their sister, with orders not to return without her. The search disperses the family across the map, and the dispersals are eponyms: Phoenix gives his name to Phoenicia, Cilix to Cilicia, Thasus — a companion or kinsman in the party — to the island of Thasos. Cadmus and his mother Telephassa settle for a time in Thrace. Europa is never found. She was never findable: a god had taken her, and the search was, from the first, a sentence of permanent exile disguised as an errand.

Telephassa dies in Thrace. Cadmus buries her, is hospitably received by the Thracians, and goes to Delphi to ask the god about Europa. The answer he receives is the cycle's first oracle, and it establishes the pattern every later oracle in the saga will follow: it refuses the question asked and substitutes the answer that matters. In Frazer's translation of Apollodorus 3.4.1, the god told Cadmus not to trouble about Europa, but to be guided by a cow, and to found a city wherever she should fall down for weariness. Cadmus journeys through Phocis, falls in with a cow among the herds of Pelagon, and follows behind her across Boeotia until, at the place where Thebes now is, the cow sinks down. The founder does not choose the site; he is led to it, on foot, behind an animal, at walking pace, by an oracle that has first cancelled his life's errand. Greek foundation stories are full of such guidances — a crow, a goat, a fish — but the Theban version has a characteristic bleakness: the city begins at the exact point where the search for the lost sister is abandoned, and the founder's obedience consists in stopping.

// THE COW AND THE NAME //

Ancient writers themselves connected the cow with the country's name: Boeotia was popularly derived from bous, an ox or cow. The derivation is a folk etymology, not a linguistic fact, and this article reports it as the ancients' own wordplay — the kind of pun by which Greek tradition habitually welded a story to a landscape. Pausanias (9.12.1) adds that the cow Cadmus followed bore on each flank a white mark like the full moon: the guide was signed, for those who needed a sign.

Cadmus's first act on the site is a sacrifice: he wishes to offer the cow to Athena. Sacrifice requires water. He sends companions to draw it from a nearby spring — the spring of Ares — and the companions do not come back. With that errand, told in a sentence by Apollodorus, the founding turns violent, and the cycle's true subject announces itself: at Thebes, even water is guarded, and everything the house takes from the land must be paid for in kind. The next section takes up the spring, the dragon, and the most consequential sowing in Greek myth.

IV. The Dragon of Ares, the Sown Men, and the Founding of the Kadmeia

The spring — later Theban topography knew it as the spring of Ares, and Pausanias's Thebes still pointed out its waters — was guarded by a dragon: a great serpent, which some said was the offspring of Ares (Apollodorus 3.4.1). It killed most of the companions Cadmus had sent for water. Cadmus, in indignation, killed the dragon in his turn. Euripides' Phoenician Women (638–675, paraphrased), in the long ode in which the chorus recalls the founding, gives the killing a single vivid means: Cadmus destroyed the serpent with a stone, showering blows on its head — the founder fights not with a hero's sword but with the weapon of a man caught unprepared at a spring. The killing is the founding act, and it is also the founding debt. The dragon was Ares' creature, in some tellings Ares' child; its death is a homicide within the divine household into which Cadmus is about to marry, and it will be expiated, pursued, and finally — in the founder's own serpent metamorphosis — literally embodied, across the rest of his story (Section VI).

Then comes the sowing. By Athena's advice, Cadmus sowed the dragon's teeth in the earth. From the ground rose armed men — Spartoi, the Sown Men, from speirein, to sow. What the earth of Thebes produces, when seeded with the teeth of its own guardian serpent, is soldiers; and the soldiers' first act is to kill one another. Apollodorus reports two versions of how the slaughter began, and keeps both: in one, the Spartoi fell to fighting of themselves, some in a chance brawl and some in ignorance; in the other, which he attributes to Pherecydes, Cadmus, seeing armed men growing from the ground, flung stones among them, and each, supposing himself pelted by the man beside him, came to blows with his neighbour. Five survived: Echion, Udaeus, Chthonius, Hyperenor, and Pelorus — names that are themselves a programme: Echion is “snake-man”, Chthonius and Udaeus are “earth-men”, Pelorus is “monstrous”. From these five, the tradition held, descended the noble houses of Thebes: the Spartoi are the city's aristocracy, autochthonous in the most literal sense Greek myth affords — not merely born in the land, but grown from it, out of a dragon's jaw.

The Theban aristocrat's pedigree, as the myth frames it, runs: earth, dragon, tooth, soldier. The city's first crop is a battle in which the crop destroys itself; five men are the harvest. Every later Theban war — brothers, Seven, Epigoni — is already present in miniature in the founding furrow: Thebans killing Thebans, on Theban soil, at the moment of the city's birth.

The debt to Ares was paid in service. Apollodorus (3.4.2) says Cadmus atoned for the slaughter by serving Ares for an “eternal year” — and adds the gloss, in Frazer's rendering, that the year was then equivalent to eight years of our reckoning. Only after the servitude does the founding complete itself: Athena procures Cadmus the kingship, and Zeus gives him a wife. The city he builds on the hill above the spring takes his name: the Kadmeia (Cadmea), the citadel of Thebes — a name the real citadel of the real city kept into the historical period and beyond, so that Xenophon, describing the Spartan seizure of the Theban acropolis in 382 BCE, calls it simply the Cadmea (Hellenica 5.2, paraphrased). The walls of the wider city belong, in the tradition, to a later generation — to Amphion's lyre (Section IX) — and the distinction is worth keeping: Cadmus founds the citadel and the royal house; the seven-gated circuit that defines the cycle's geography is a second foundation laid over his.

THE FIVE SURVIVORS

Echion · Udaeus · Chthonius · Hyperenor · Pelorus. Ancestors, in the tradition, of Thebes's Spartoi families. Echion marries into the royal house itself — his wife is Cadmus's daughter Agave, and their son is Pentheus (Section VIII).

THE OTHER SOWING

The dragon's teeth were not all sown at Thebes. In the Argonautic tradition, Athena gave half the teeth to Aeëtes of Colchis, and Jason, at the far end of the world, sows the other half and faces the same crop of armed earthborn men — the Theban founding replayed as a hero's task. See Heroes and Epic, Section VIII.

THE SPRING

The spring of Ares, where the dragon kept guard, remained a named place in the storied topography Pausanias walked (Book 9). Thebes's myths are unusually well-anchored in named waters: Dirce, Ismenus, and the spring of Ares all carry episodes of the cycle.

THE DEBT STRUCTURE

Founding at Thebes is never free. The cow is sacrificed; the companions are killed; the dragon's death is expiated by eight years' servitude; the sown men mostly die. The cycle's accounting begins here: every acquisition in this saga is entered twice, once as gain and once as debt.

V. The Alphabet: Herodotus and the Cadmean Letters

Cadmus brought Thebes one more thing besides a citadel, and it is the only gift in the cycle that is an unmixed good — though the claim for it must be stated with exact care. The claim is Herodotus's. In Histories 5.57–58, tracing the Athenian clan of the Gephyraeans back to Phoenician settlers who, by his enquiry, had come with Cadmus to the land now called Boeotia, Herodotus writes, in A. D. Godley's Loeb translation:

“These Phoenicians who came with Cadmus and of whom the Gephyraeans were a part brought with them to Hellas, among many other kinds of learning, the alphabet, which had been unknown before this, I think, to the Greeks. As time went on the sound and the form of the letters were changed.” — HERODOTUS, HISTORIES 5.58.1, TRANS. A. D. GODLEY (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY)

Herodotus continues (5.58.2, paraphrased) that the Greeks who then lived around the Phoenicians — for the most part Ionians — learned the letters from them, altered a few of their forms, and called the characters Phoenician, “as was quite fair”, in Godley's rendering, seeing that the Phoenicians had brought them into Greece. And he offers evidence of the kind he trusts: autopsy. In 5.59 he reports that he has himself seen Cadmean letters (Kadmēia grammata) in the temple of Ismenian Apollo at Thebes in Boeotia, engraved on certain tripods and for the most part resembling Ionian letters; and he transcribes the tripods' dedications, the last of them ascribed to Laodamas, son of Eteocles — that is, to a king of the cycle's own fifth generation (5.59–61, paraphrased). The historian who is so often accused of credulity is here doing something precise: attributing a technology to a carrier, naming the mechanism of its adaptation, and citing inscribed objects he claims to have inspected, in the very city the myth says the carrier founded.

Now the frame must be set beside the claim, and this hub's own timeline supplies it: the Greek alphabet was adapted from the Phoenician script around c. 800 BCE (see the hub index timeline), when Greek communities took over the Phoenician consonantal signs and, decisively, repurposed some of them as vowels. That is the archaeological and epigraphic record: no Cadmus stands in it, and no single act of importation can be dated or placed. The myth, by contrast, gives the alphabet a Phoenician prince, a name, a route, and a city. The two accounts should be stated together, in exactly these terms and no further: the myth gives the alphabet a Phoenician prince, the record gives it to Phoenician trade, and the two are not as far apart as usual. A technology that the record attributes to sustained contact between Greek speakers and Phoenician carriers is attributed by the tradition to a Phoenician who came and stayed. Herodotus, standing between the two, reports the tradition as tradition — “as I think” is his own hedge, in Godley's translation — and anchors it to letters he says he saw cut in bronze at Thebes. This article leaves the matter exactly there.

// CADMUS OF TYRE //

Herodotus knows Cadmus as a Tyrian: at 2.49 he writes of “Cadmus of Tyre and those who came with him from Phoenicia to the land now called Boeotia” (Godley's translation, paraphrased in the connective wording here) as the channel through which Melampus learned the worship of Dionysus. The alphabet passage is not an isolated fancy; it belongs to Herodotus's settled view that Cadmus's Phoenicians were a real vector of eastern learning into Greece — letters and a god among the cargo.

There is a fittingness the tradition itself surely felt. The cycle that would become the most written of Greek sagas — the saga of the lost epics, of the shield inscriptions Eteocles reads aloud, of the letters on Polynices' shield in Aeschylus, of an oracle culture that ran on inscribed tripods — traces its city's founding to the man Greek tradition credited with bringing writing itself. Whether or not any historical kernel stands behind the attribution, the shape of the claim is the shape of the cycle: at Thebes, everything, even the alphabet, arrives as an import that takes root and turns native.

The Levantine world those Phoenicians came from — its cities, its scribal schools, and its own great mythic corpus, the Baal Cycle of Ugarit — is treated in Ugarit and the Baal Cycle, this hub's account of the tradition on the alphabet's home shore.

VI. Cadmus and Harmonia: The Wedding, the Necklace, and the Serpent End

After the servitude to Ares, the founding is crowned with a marriage that no other Greek hero is granted. Zeus gives Cadmus to wife Harmonia, daughter of Aphrodite and Ares — Hesiod's Theogony (933–937) already lists Harmonia among the children Ares and Aphrodite had together — and Apollodorus (3.4.2) describes the wedding in terms that make it the cycle's one moment of unshadowed splendour, in paraphrase: all the gods quitted the sky, and, feasting in the Kadmeia, celebrated the marriage with hymns. Later tradition amplified the scene into the first wedding on earth attended by the Olympians in person, with the Muses singing the epithalamium and the gods bringing gifts; Diodorus Siculus (4.2 and 5.49, paraphrased) preserves versions of that amplification. The bride was, note, the daughter of the two divinities the founding had most engaged: Ares, whose dragon Cadmus killed and whom Cadmus then served, and Aphrodite, whose line the marriage joins. Harmonia's very name — Fitting-Together, Concord — proclaims what the union was meant to be: the Phoenician stranger fitted into the Greek land, war and love reconciled, the debt to Ares discharged in kinship. The cycle will spend four generations demonstrating what that name was worth.

The Necklace

Among the wedding gifts was the object that outlasts every person in this article. Apollodorus (3.4.2, paraphrased, with the variants he himself records): Cadmus gave Harmonia a robe and the necklace wrought by Hephaestus — which some say was given to Cadmus by Hephaestus, but Pherecydes says was given by Europa, who had received it from Zeus. The Library does not call the necklace cursed; it simply follows it, and the following is the curse. The necklace descends with the royal house. Generations later it is in the treasury of Thebes, and Polynices, exiled, takes it with him — with the robe — as his portable wealth (Apollodorus 3.6.1). At Argos he uses it to bribe Eriphyle, and the bribe sends her husband Amphiaraus to his death and, in the next generation, sends her sons to war and Eriphyle herself to death at her son's hand (Sections XIV and XVII). Alcmaeon gives necklace and robe to his first wife; his second wife demands them; the lie he tells to recover them gets him murdered; his young sons avenge him, and the necklace's last recorded act in the tradition is its dedication — Pausanias (8.24 and 9.31, paraphrased) knew of the necklace of Harmonia shown among the dedications at Delphi, the poisoned gift finally taken out of circulation by being given to a god. Later writers, scholiasts especially, supplied the necklace with an explicit malice — Hephaestus, in those accounts, made it in bitterness at Aphrodite's adultery with Ares, so that her daughter's line would never prosper — but that explanation is a later rationalisation of a pattern the older sources simply display: at Thebes, the wedding gift and the ruin are the same object, and no one who owns it keeps it safely.

The Children, and the End in Illyria

To Cadmus and Harmonia were born, in Apollodorus's list (3.4.2), daughters Autonoe, Ino, Semele, and Agave, and a son, Polydorus. The marriages are a map of the house's alliances: Ino to Athamas, king of Boeotian Orchomenus; Autonoe to Aristaeus, the culture-hero of herding and bees; Agave to Echion, one of the five Spartoi — the founder's daughter wedded to the earthborn, the two foundings of Thebes joined in one bed. Their stories are the next two sections. Of Cadmus and Harmonia themselves, the end is strange and, in its way, gentle. In old age the couple leave Thebes — the throne passes down the line toward Polydorus's descendants and, in the immediate term, to Agave's son Pentheus — and go out to the Encheleans in Illyria, who make Cadmus their king after an oracle promises them victory under his leadership (Apollodorus 3.5.4, paraphrased). There a son, Illyrius, is born to them, eponym of the Illyrians. And there, at the last, the debt of the spring is called in, in the mildest possible form: Cadmus and Harmonia are turned into serpents, and Zeus sends them to the Elysian fields. The founder becomes the animal he killed. Ovid's telling (Metamorphoses 4.563–603, paraphrased) makes the metamorphosis Cadmus's own weary inference — if a serpent's life is so precious to the gods, he muses, let him stretch out into a serpent's length — and gives Harmonia the choice to join him in the shape rather than be left human without him. Euripides, in the Bacchae's closing prophecy (1330–1339, paraphrased), has Dionysus foretell the same end with a harder edge: Cadmus, become a serpent, with Harmonia a serpent beside him, will lead a barbarian host against Greece and sack many cities, until at last he is brought to the land of the blessed. Even the founder's apotheosis is double: a serpent's exile, then blessedness; a barbarian invasion, then rest. The cycle permits no one an unmixed ending — but it permits its founders, alone among its kings, an ending that is not a catastrophe.

ACT I

THE FOUNDING

Europa lost; the cow followed; the dragon killed; the teeth sown; eight years' service to Ares; the Kadmeia founded; the wedding of Cadmus and Harmonia, with the necklace among the gifts.

ACT II

THE DAUGHTERS AND PENTHEUS

Semele, Ino, Autonoe, and Agave each lose a child or a mind to a god; Dionysus, the house's own grandson, returns unrecognised and destroys Pentheus, the house's king.

ACT III

OEDIPUS

Laius defies the oracle; Oedipus is exposed, saved, and returns to kill his father, answer the Sphinx, and marry his mother; the truth is recognised; the blind exile dies a holy death at Colonus.

ACT IV

THE TWO WARS

Oedipus's curse divides his sons; the Seven fail before the seven gates; Antigone dies for a burial; ten years on, the Epigoni — the After-born — succeed, and Thebes falls.

VII. The Daughters' Generation: Semele, Actaeon, Ino, and Agave

The house of Cadmus has no ordinary second generation. Polydorus's line — the male line that will produce Laius and Oedipus — runs on quietly in the background of the sources, while the foreground belongs to the four daughters and to what the gods did to each of them. Apollodorus tells their stories consecutively (3.4.3–3.5.2), and told consecutively they form a single pattern with four variations: in each, a god enters the house from outside the ordinary rules — as lover, as offended majesty, as madness, as the family's own unrecognised child — and in each, the price is paid by a child.

Semele and the Thigh of Zeus

Zeus loved Semele and lay with her unknown to Hera, having promised — the fatal formula — to do for her whatever she asked. Hera, deceiving her, persuaded her to ask that Zeus come to her as he came when he was wooing Hera herself: in his full state, with his lightnings. Bound by his promise, Zeus came to her chamber in a chariot with thunder and lightning and launched a thunderbolt; Semele died of fright; and Zeus snatched the six-month child from the fire and sewed it into his own thigh, from which, at term, Dionysus was born a second time (Apollodorus 3.4.3, paraphrased). The sisters of Semele then spread the report that she had lain with a mortal man and lied about Zeus, and that the thunderbolt was her punishment — a piece of family slander that the tradition never forgets, because Dionysus's whole Theban career (Section VIII) is its refutation. The child was given to Hermes, and by him to Ino and Athamas, to be reared as a girl. Semele's own end has a coda the sources treat as the house's single act of rescue: Dionysus, grown to godhead, descended to Hades and brought his mother up, giving her the name Thyone, and ascended with her to Olympus (Apollodorus 3.5.3, paraphrased). The daughter the lightning killed becomes, alone of Cadmus's children, an immortal — by her son's hand, in the underworld, offstage.

Actaeon, Ino, and the Wages of Rearing a God

Autonoe's son was Actaeon, reared by the centaur Chiron to be a hunter. Apollodorus (3.4.4, paraphrased) gives the cause of his death in the form that became standard: on Cithaeron, Actaeon saw Artemis bathing, and the goddess — lest he take a wife and publish what he had seen, in the logic of the tale — turned him into a stag, and put madness in his fifty hounds, who tore their master apart unknowing, and afterwards searched for him howling, until Chiron made an image of Actaeon to console them. (Apollodorus himself notes a variant: Acusilaus said Artemis punished Actaeon because he wished to marry Semele — the house's disasters interlock even in the variants.) Ino's story is the price of the nursery. Because she and Athamas reared the infant Dionysus, Hera drove them mad: Athamas hunted his elder son Learchus as a deer and killed him, and Ino threw her younger son Melicertes into a boiling cauldron and leapt with the body into the sea (Apollodorus 3.4.3, paraphrased). There the story turns to cult, and the turn is the point: Ino became a goddess of the sea, Leucothea, the White Goddess who aids sailors — it is Leucothea who saves Odysseus with her veil in Odyssey 5 — and Melicertes became Palaemon, a sea-power with honours at the Isthmus of Corinth, where the Isthmian Games were held in his name. The house's catastrophes keep precipitating cults; Section XIII will show the pattern completing itself at Colonus.

Agave's story is reserved for the next section, because it belongs to her son. But its place in the pattern should be marked here: of the four sisters, Agave is the one who survives into old age, and her survival is the worst of the four fates. Semele dies by fire and is raised; Ino dies by water and is deified; Autonoe loses a son to a goddess's anger. Agave loses her son to her own hands, in madness sent by her own nephew, and comes to herself holding his head. The daughters' generation is the cycle's proof that the house does not need an external enemy. Given gods, it suffices.

SEMELE

Loved by Zeus; killed by his lightning at her own request, engineered by Hera; her unborn son sewn into Zeus's thigh. Raised from Hades by Dionysus and installed on Olympus as Thyone.

ACTAEON (SON OF AUTONOE)

Saw Artemis bathing; was made a stag and torn apart by his own fifty hounds, who did not know him — the cycle's purest emblem of recognition arriving one moment too late.

INO

Reared Dionysus; maddened by Hera; her sons Learchus killed by his father and Melicertes dead in her arms in the sea. Becomes Leucothea, rescuer of sailors; Melicertes becomes Palaemon of the Isthmus.

AGAVE

Wife of Echion the Spartos, mother of Pentheus. Her punishment is deferred to her son's reign, and executed by her own hands (Section VIII).

VIII. Pentheus: The God Unrecognised in His Own City

This section is deliberately brief. Dionysian cult — the maenads, the mountain dancing, the raw-flesh rite, the god's whole religious reality — is treated in this hub's Mysteries and Healing Cults article, and is not duplicated here. What belongs to this article is only the episode's place in the cycle's narrative: the moment the house of Cadmus meets the god it produced, and refuses to know him.

Pentheus, son of Agave and Echion, succeeds to the kingship of Thebes — Apollodorus (3.5.2) marks the succession with a dry note that he was “like-minded” with the grandfather whose place he took. Dionysus, having established his worship across Asia, comes home to Thebes to establish it there and to vindicate his mother Semele against her sisters' slander that her lover had been a mortal. The sisters — Agave among them — and Pentheus refuse the god. In Apollodorus's compressed account (3.5.2, paraphrased): Dionysus drove the Theban women from their houses to rave in Bacchic frenzy on Cithaeron; Pentheus, coming to spy on the Bacchanals, was torn limb from limb by his mother Agave in a fit of madness, for she thought he was a wild beast; and Dionysus, having shown the Thebans that he was a god, went on to Argos. Euripides' Bacchae (produced posthumously, c. 405 BCE) unfolds the same events into the most terrible recognition scene in Greek tragedy: the god, disguised as his own priest, dresses the spying king in women's clothes, leads him through his own city, and sets him in a pine tree above the maenads; Agave, possessed, takes her son for a lion, leads the tearing — the sparagmos — and carries his head home in triumph, believing it a beast's, until old Cadmus, gathering the scattered limbs, brings her slowly back to knowledge of what she holds. The founder ends his earthly story reassembling his grandson's body. No summary improves on that fact, and the cycle's logic is complete in it: the house that would not recognise its god is destroyed by the recognition it refused, administered through the hands of its own women. Pentheus is the last king of Cadmus's line to rule Thebes in peace. After him the succession breaks — and the Labdacid line, with its oracle, takes the story over.

IX. The Interregnum: Lycus, Amphion and Zethus, Niobe, and the Crime of Laius

Between Pentheus and Oedipus, Apollodorus's narrative (3.5.5–7) runs through a generation of usurpation and counter-usurpation that most retellings skip — and that matters, because it establishes the cycle's political law: the Theban throne is never simply inherited; it is held by whoever's violence or marriage last took it, and every holder leaves a claimant in exile. Polydorus's son Labdacus — the ancestor who gives the doomed line its modern scholarly name, the Labdacids — dies leaving a one-year-old son, Laius. The regency falls to Lycus, and the regency becomes a feud: Lycus's brother Nycteus, whose daughter Antiope has been seduced by Zeus and then carried off by Epopeus of Sicyon, dies charging Lycus to punish them; Lycus campaigns, kills Epopeus, and brings Antiope home a prisoner, to be tormented for years by Lycus and his wife Dirce. Antiope's twin sons by Zeus, Zethus and Amphion — the herdsman and the musician, Hermes' lyre in the musician's hands — grow up in exile, recognise their mother when her bonds miraculously loosen and she flees to them, kill Lycus, and tie Dirce to a bull, which drags her to death; her body is thrown into the spring that thereafter bears her name, Dirce, one of Thebes's two famous waters. The twins take the sovereignty, expel the young Laius, and give the city its second foundation: they fortify Thebes, and the stones of the wall, in the tradition's most famous image of art ordering matter, follow Amphion's lyre into place — the wall of the seven gates rises to music, the herdsman's strength and the musician's art building together what neither could build alone.

Amphion marries Niobe, daughter of Tantalus, and their house falls by the daughters' generation's pattern in its starkest form. Niobe, proud of her many children, set herself above Leto, who had borne only two. Leto's two — Apollo and Artemis — shot Niobe's children down, sons and daughters both, and Niobe, turned to stone on Mount Sipylus in her Phrygian homeland, wept on in rock (Apollodorus 3.5.6, paraphrased). The sources disagree on the children's number — Homer, in Achilles' speech to Priam, says twelve, six sons and six daughters (Iliad 24.602–617); other traditions count fourteen or more — and the disagreement is itself the honest datum: the story's point is not arithmetic but the completeness of the loss. Pausanias (9.5.8–9, paraphrased) adds the Theban sequel: of Amphion's house a single line survived, through his daughter Chloris, who married into the house of Pylos and became the mother of Nestor — so that the Trojan cycle's oldest counsellor is, by this genealogy, the grandson of Amphion of Thebes, a living bridge between the two cycles. Amphion himself, in Apollodorus's telling, died with his house, shot by the same gods or mad with grief; Thebes keeps a joint tomb of Amphion and Zethus, which Pausanias describes (9.17.4, paraphrased) and which Theban ritual still tended in his day.

With Amphion dead, Laius at last succeeds to his father's kingdom — and commits, in exile's aftermath, the act that the tradition treats as the Labdacid curse's human root. Laius had spent his exile at the court of Pelops in the Peloponnese, hospitably received; employed to teach Pelops's son Chrysippus chariot-driving, he conceived a passion for the boy and carried him off (Apollodorus 3.5.5, paraphrased). The abduction of a host's son — a crime against xenia, guest-friendship, the bond on which the heroic world's peace depended — drew Pelops's curse, in the versions that give it: that Laius's own son should kill him. Euripides devoted a tragedy, the lost Chrysippus, to the episode, and later sources treat it as the fountainhead of the house's sorrows. This article records the tradition with its layers marked: Apollodorus reports the abduction without a curse; the curse of Pelops is explicit in later tellings and in the Chrysippus tradition; and the oracle Laius himself receives — the subject of the next section — needs no father's curse to operate. Thebes's doom, characteristically, is over-determined: oracle, curse, and character all point the same way, and the tradition declines to choose among them.

X. Laius, the Oracle, and the Exposure of Oedipus

Laius marries a daughter of Menoeceus — “some say that she was Jocasta, and some that she was Epicasta”, Apollodorus notes (3.5.7, in Frazer's translation) — and receives the oracle around which the rest of the cycle turns. The oracle had warned him not to beget a son, for the son that should be begotten would kill his father. Aeschylus's chorus, in the Seven Against Thebes (742–749, in Smyth's translation as read for this article, paraphrased here), gives the oracle its fullest archaic form: Apollo at his Pythian oracle, at the centre of the earth, told Laius three times that the king would save his city if he died without offspring. The condition is the cruelty of it. The oracle does not merely predict a parricide; it prices the city's safety at the extinction of the royal line, and leaves Laius to choose between his house and his city. He chooses his house. Apollodorus's wording is brutally plain (3.5.7, paraphrased): nevertheless, flushed with wine, he had intercourse with his wife. The defiance is not defiance in the grand manner — no Titan storming heaven — but a drunken night and a disregarded warning, which is the Labdacid register exactly: this house is ruined not by monsters but by small, comprehensible failures at the moments when comprehension was available.

When the child is born, Laius pierces its ankles with brooches and gives it to a herdsman to expose — to be left on the mountain to die, the ancient world's ordinary machinery for an unwanted infant, here applied as an instrument of state. The herdsman exposes the baby on Cithaeron — the same mountain where Actaeon died and Pentheus will die; Thebes's wild margin collects the house's victims. But the child is found by the herdsmen of Polybus, king of Corinth, and brought to the king's wife — Periboea in Apollodorus; Sophocles calls her Merope — who adopts him, passes him off as her own, heals his ankles, and names him for what the piercing did: Oedipus, Swell-Foot. Every element of the rescue is a deferred disaster. The name records the wound; the adoption conceals the birth; the kindness of Corinth guarantees that the young man will one day flee Corinth in exactly the wrong direction.

Grown, and excelling his fellows in strength, Oedipus is taunted by his companions with being supposititious — a changeling, not the king's true son. He questions Periboea and learns nothing; so he does the one thing the cycle's characters always do with a doubt, and goes to Delphi. The god does not answer his question. Oedipus asks: who are my parents? The oracle answers, as it answered Cadmus: not your question, but your fate — do not go to your native land, because you will murder your father and lie with your mother (Apollodorus 3.5.7, paraphrased). Believing himself Corinthian, Oedipus resolves never to return to Corinth, and leaves the city he thinks is home in order to protect the people he thinks are his parents. The flight from the oracle is the oracle's instrument. It is worth pausing on the symmetry, because the cycle insists on it: Cadmus was told not to ask after Europa and to follow a cow; Oedipus is told not to ask after his birth and flees a throne. Both obey. Both arrive exactly where the god's silence was leading them.

// WHAT THE ORACLE DOES NOT DO //

Nothing in any ancient version has the oracle cause the fulfilment by compulsion. Laius begets in drink; the herdsman exposes but does not kill; the Corinthian herdsmen rescue; Oedipus, warned, chooses flight; at the crossroads, warned men choose violence. The tradition's horror is precisely that the prophecy works through free, intelligible, even well-intentioned choices — a point the tragedians understood completely and built their plays upon. Accounts that flatten the story into “fate made them do it” are describing a cruder story than the one the sources tell.

XI. The Crossroads, the Sphinx, and the Riddle of Man

Riding in a chariot through Phocis, Oedipus falls in with Laius driving in a chariot in a certain narrow road (Apollodorus 3.5.7, paraphrased). Polyphontes, Laius's herald, orders him to make way, and kills one of Oedipus's horses when he disobeys and delays. Oedipus, in a rage, kills Polyphontes — and Laius. The place became one of the most famous spots in the storied landscape: the place where three roads meet, in Phocis, on the way from Delphi; Sophocles' Oedipus the King returns to it obsessively, and Pausanias (10.5.2–4, paraphrased) describes the tomb of Laius and his attendant at the crossroads, still shown in his day, with the local account of the killing attached. Neither man knows the other. The father is travelling — in Sophocles' version, to Delphi itself, to ask about the child he exposed. The son is travelling from Delphi, fleeing the parents he has never met. They meet at the one point where their two journeys cross, over the right of way on a narrow road, and the parricide the oracle promised is accomplished as a traffic killing, in anger, between strangers. Laius was buried where he fell by Damasistratus, king of Plataea (Apollodorus 3.5.8) — a neighbour's piety performing the office his own house could not.

The Sphinx

Creon, son of Menoeceus — Jocasta's brother — succeeds to the kingdom, and in his reign the calamity comes. Hera sent the Sphinx against Thebes: daughter of Echidna and Typhon in Apollodorus (3.5.8); in Hesiod's genealogy a child of Echidna's brood by Orthrus — the variant is noted here because the sources genuinely differ — with the face of a woman, the breast, feet, and tail of a lion, and the wings of a bird. She had learned a riddle from the Muses, and she sat on Mount Phicium and propounded it to the Thebans. In Frazer's translation of Apollodorus 3.5.8, the riddle is given in prose:

“What is that which has one voice and yet becomes four-footed and two-footed and three-footed?” — APOLLODORUS, LIBRARY 3.5.8, TRANS. J. G. FRAZER (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY)

The Thebans held an oracle that they would be rid of the Sphinx when the riddle was read, and they met repeatedly to debate the answer; failing, they lost a citizen each time, snatched away and devoured — last of all, in Apollodorus's account, Creon's own son Haemon. (The lost Oedipodea also made Haemon the Sphinx's victim: its surviving fragment, preserved by a scholiast on Euripides, calls him, in Evelyn-White's translation, the comeliest and loveliest of boys — the epic already grieving where the handbook only lists.) Creon then proclaimed the cycle's most fateful reward: to the man who read the riddle, the kingdom, and the wife of Laius. Oedipus heard, and answered. The riddle of the Sphinx referred to man: four-footed as a babe, crawling on four limbs; two-footed as an adult; three-footed as an old man, taking a staff as a third support. The Sphinx threw herself from the citadel. Oedipus received the kingdom, and unwittingly married his mother, and begat by her Polynices and Eteocles, Ismene and Antigone — though Apollodorus, faithful to his variants, adds that some say the children were borne by a second wife, Euryganeia, daughter of Hyperphas: the Oedipodea's version, as Pausanias saw (Section XVIII).

The riddle deserves its reputation as the cycle's emblem, and not only for its wit. Its answer is the one creature Oedipus does not know: man — himself, in the specific sense that he cannot say what a man is in his own case: whose son, whose husband, whose father. He solves the riddle of the species and misses the riddle of the person, and the solving is what enthrones and ruins him. The staff of the riddle's third age is the blind man's staff he will carry out of Thebes. Even the feet are in it: the answer counts feet — four, two, three — in a story whose hero is named for his feet, whose identity will be proved by the scars on them, and who met his father where three roads' feet of traffic meet. The tragedians did not invent this density; they found it in the tradition and tightened it. But it is Sophocles who makes the tightening into a play, and the play is the next section.

XII. Oedipus the King: Plague, Investigation, and Recognition

Apollodorus disposes of the revelation in one sentence (3.5.9, in Frazer's translation, paraphrased in part): when the secret afterwards came to light, Jocasta hanged herself in a noose, and Oedipus was driven from Thebes, after he had put out his eyes and cursed his sons, who saw him cast out of the city without lifting a hand to help him. Everything in that sentence — the discovery, the suicide, the blinding, the curse — is expanded by Sophocles' Oedipus the King (Oidipous Tyrannos, conventionally dated c. 429 BCE) into the most influential plot in Western literature: the plot Aristotle, in the Poetics, takes as the model of tragedy, where recognition (anagnōrisis) and reversal (peripeteia) arrive in the same blow. What follows is the play's action, in paraphrase; no line of it is quoted, and the reader who wants the words should go to the play.

The play opens on a city dying of plague — blight on crops, cattle, and women in labour — and on a king who has already acted: Oedipus has sent Creon to Delphi before the first scene begins. The oracle's answer is a detective's brief. The plague is pollution (miasma): the killer of Laius is in Thebes, unpunished, and the city will be cleansed when he is killed or exiled. Oedipus proclaims a public curse on the unknown killer, barring him from every hearth in Thebes — cursing, with full civic solemnity, himself. The seer Tiresias, summoned and reluctant, finally names the killer to his face: Oedipus is the man he seeks, and lives in shameful intercourse with his nearest kin. Oedipus reads the accusation as a plot by Creon and Tiresias to seize the throne, and quarrels with both. Jocasta, calming him, offers the rationalist's comfort: oracles fail — why, Laius was told his son would kill him, and the child was exposed with its ankles pinned, and Laius was killed by robbers at a place where three roads meet. The comfort is the trap. The detail of the crossroads fits Oedipus's own memory too exactly, and Jocasta's proof that prophecy is worthless becomes the first proof that it is not.

The investigation then runs on two converging tracks, and the play's machinery is the convergence. A messenger arrives from Corinth: Polybus is dead — of age and sickness, not by any son's hand, which Oedipus hails as the oracle's second failure — and the Corinthians wish to make Oedipus king. But the messenger, easing Oedipus's remaining fear of the mother-half of the prophecy, lets slip that Polybus was no father to him: the messenger himself, a shepherd once, received the infant Oedipus on Cithaeron from another shepherd — a servant of Laius's house — and carried him to Corinth, a baby with pierced ankles. Jocasta understands first, begs Oedipus to inquire no further, and goes in. The Theban herdsman is fetched — the same man who exposed the child and who, Sophocles has established, is also the sole survivor of the crossroads killing, who on returning to find Oedipus enthroned begged to be sent to the far pastures, out of sight. Under threat, he confirms everything: the child he gave away was Laius's son, given him by Jocasta herself, to destroy, because of the prophecies. Oedipus's cry that all has come out true closes the investigation he himself conducted, at every stage against advice, in public, by due process. The detective is the criminal; the judge pronounces his own sentence; the king who opened the play as the city's saviour ends it as its pollution.

What follows is reported, in the Greek manner, not shown. A second messenger describes Jocasta found hanged in the bedchamber, and Oedipus, taking the golden brooches from her dress, striking his own eyes with them again and again — that they might never again see the evils he had suffered and done, in darkness henceforth what they should not see and fail to know what they desired to know (paraphrased from the messenger's speech). The blinded king is led out and displayed to the city, begs for exile to Cithaeron — the mountain that should have been his tomb — and commends his daughters to Creon's care. Creon, now in authority, will neither exile nor retain him without asking the god. The play ends there, in suspension, with the chorus's closing reflection on the swiftness of the fall of the man who had been the most fortunate of mortals.

// WHAT SOPHOCLES CHANGED //

Measured against the older tradition, Sophocles' remakings are visible. Homer's Oedipus rules on after the revelation; the blinding and the plague-investigation are not in Homer or in Apollodorus's summary of the older material in this form. Sophocles concentrates a saga of years into a single day of inquiry, makes the discovery Oedipus's own achievement — his intelligence, persistence, and public honesty are the instruments of his ruin — and leaves the exile undecided at the curtain, the better to take it up, decades later and in a different play, at Colonus.

Two cautions close the section. First, the date: c. 429 BCE is a scholarly convention, argued from the play's plague against the great plague of Athens that began in 430 BCE — an attractive synchronism, but an inference, and this article states it as one. Second, the title: Tyrannos in the play's Greek title means a ruler who holds power without hereditary right — which Oedipus, a supposed Corinthian who won the throne by answering a riddle, exactly is. It carries none of the modern word “tyrant”'s moral charge, and the play's Oedipus is not a tyrant in our sense but something more disturbing: a good king, a solver, a prosecutor of the truth, destroyed by the completeness with which he does everything right.

XIII. Oedipus at Colonus: The Strange, Holy Coda

Apollodorus closes Oedipus's story in a clause (3.5.9, paraphrased): having come with Antigone to Colonus in Attica, where is the precinct of the Eumenides, he sat down there as a suppliant, was kindly received by Theseus, and died not long afterwards. Sophocles' last play — written in his extreme old age, produced posthumously in 401 BCE by his grandson — makes that clause into the cycle's coda, and it is a coda in a different key from everything before it. The Oedipus at Colonus is the strangest panel of the saga: the polluted parricide and incestuous husband of the earlier plays arrives, blind, beggared, led by Antigone, at a grove in Sophocles' own home deme of Colonus, just outside Athens — and the play insists, with mounting solemnity, that this ruined man is now a blessing, that his body is a prize cities will fight over, and that his grave will be a protection to the land that holds it.

The mechanism is hero cult, and the play is unintelligible without the institution this hub's Heroes and Epic article sets out: the powerful dead, anchored at a tomb, exercising protection over the community that keeps the tomb. Oedipus knows — an oracle has told him — that he will die at the seat of the Awful Goddesses, the Erinyes worshipped gently as Eumenides, the Kindly Ones, and that his buried body will bless the land that receives it and curse the land that cast him out. Ismene brings the news that Thebes has learned the same lesson too late: an oracle now says that Thebes's safety depends on Oedipus's grave, and Theban parties are coming to fetch him back — to plant him at their border, controlled, outside the city that exiled him, close enough to work and too polluted to enter. Oedipus refuses, with the full bitterness of the exile who remembers that his sons let him go. Creon comes with guards and seizes Antigone and Ismene to compel him; Theseus, king of Athens, rescues the daughters and shelters the father. Polynices comes last, begging a blessing for his expedition against Thebes, and receives instead his father's renewed curse — that he and his brother shall die by each other's hands — pronounced now not in blind rage but in cold, informed, deliberate judgement. Then the thunder sounds, the signs gather, and Oedipus rises and walks unaided — the blind man leading the sighted, for once — to the place of his death, which he reveals to Theseus alone. A messenger reports the end: after the daughters' lament and the farewells, a voice called Oedipus by name; all withdrew but Theseus; and when they looked back, Oedipus was gone, and Theseus stood shielding his eyes, as at a sight too bright or too terrible to bear. No tomb is shown. The grave's place is a state secret of Athens, to be passed from king to heir, and its protection is conditional on the secrecy (all paraphrased from the play).

The coda's strangeness is its theology. The man whose very presence was plague in Thebes is, in Athens, a talisman — not because he has changed, but because the categories have: pollution and power, in Greek religion, are close neighbours, and the hero's grave concentrates both. Sophocles, an Athenian writing for Athens at the end of the Peloponnesian War, gives his city the Theban cycle's final gift: Athens shelters the suppliant everyone else fears, and is repaid with the one durable asset Thebes produced — a protecting grave. Pausanias, centuries later, records the afterlife of the claim with a traveller's even hand: at Athens he was shown a grave of Oedipus within the precinct of the Erinyes near the Areopagus, and he reports the Athenian account that the bones were brought from Thebes (1.28.7, paraphrased) — a competing tradition to Colonus, in which the talisman was not a secret death on Attic soil but a translated relic. The competition itself is the evidence. By the time the claims are being made, Oedipus's body is worth stealing. The cycle's most polluted figure has become its most contested sacred asset, and the family saga that began with a founder following a cow ends, for Oedipus, with two cities quietly disputing the ownership of his bones.

// HOW TO READ THE CODA //

Do not read the Colonus as a sequel that cancels the King. The two plays were written decades apart for different festivals, and their theologies differ because their questions differ. The King asks what a man is responsible for when he acts in ignorance. The Colonus asks what a community should do with the suffering it cannot undo — and answers, in the language of hero cult: receive it, honour it, keep its grave, and be protected by the keeping. Thebes expelled its pollution and fell. Athens housed its suppliant and, in the play's promise, stands forever. That is Athenian civic doctrine preached through a Theban story — tragedy's remaking at its most transparent, and none the less moving for being visible.

XIV. The Curse at Work: The Brothers' Compact and the Necklace's Second Victim

Oedipus cursed his sons twice over, and the tradition preserves both curses because they work differently. In Apollodorus's compressed version (3.5.9), the curse is a father's response to a slight: the sons saw him cast out of the city and did not lift a hand to help him. In the lost Thebaid, the curse was provoked at table, and its surviving fragments (Section XVIII) give it the cycle's characteristic instrument — an inherited object. Polynices set before his blind father the silver table and golden cup of Cadmus, treasures Oedipus had forbidden; at the sight — at the knowledge — of his father's goods served to him by his son, Oedipus cursed both sons, that they should never divide their inheritance in brotherhood but that war and fighting should be ever the portion of them both; and a second fragment gives the companion scene, the dishonouring portion of meat sent to the old king, and the prayer that each brother fall by the other's hand. The curse, in the epic, is not a metaphor for the brothers' quarrel. It is its efficient cause, spoken aloud, heard by the Erinys, and executed by the machinery of the next two wars.

The quarrel's terms, in Apollodorus (3.6.1, paraphrased): Eteocles and Polynices made a compact concerning the kingdom, that each should rule alternately, a year at a time. Some say Polynices ruled first and handed over the kingdom after a year; others that Eteocles ruled first and refused to hand it over. On the second version — the one tragedy adopts — Polynices is banished, and goes out from Thebes taking with him the necklace and the robe of Harmonia. He comes by night to Argos, to the palace of King Adrastus, and there, in the porch, falls to fighting with another night-arriving exile: Tydeus, son of Oeneus, fled from Calydon for a killing. Adrastus parts them, and remembers an oracle that he should yoke his daughters in marriage to a boar and a lion: the two exiles bear those very beasts — in Apollodorus, as devices on their shields; in Euripides' version, as the skins they wear. Adrastus marries Tydeus to Deipyle and Polynices to Argia, and promises to restore both men to their native lands — Tydeus to Calydon in due course, Polynices first, to Thebes. The war of the Seven begins, therefore, not as an invasion but as a restoration: an Argive king's promise to his son-in-law, to put a wrongfully exiled prince back on his own throne. Every party to it can state a just cause. That is what makes it a Theban war.

Amphiaraus and the Necklace

One man at Argos knows the expedition will fail. Amphiaraus, son of Oicles, is a seer — and, a rarity in this saga, a just man — and foreseeing that all who join the expedition except Adrastus are destined to perish, he refuses to go, and dissuades the rest. Polynices learns from Iphis how Amphiaraus can be compelled: if Eriphyle, Amphiaraus's wife and Adrastus's sister, gets the necklace. The machinery is a sworn settlement. Amphiaraus and Adrastus had once quarrelled and been reconciled on terms that Eriphyle should arbitrate any future dispute between them; Amphiaraus, knowing his wife, had forbidden her to accept gifts from Polynices. Polynices gives her the necklace of Harmonia, and begs her to persuade her husband. The arbitration falls due: Adrastus advocates the war, Amphiaraus opposes it, Eriphyle decides — for the war. Amphiaraus, bound by his oath and his wife's judgement, marches to a death he has foreseen in detail; and before he goes he lays his commands on his young sons: when they are grown, to kill their mother, and to march against Thebes (Apollodorus 3.6.2, paraphrased). The necklace has done its second piece of work. Its first owner was Harmonia; its price, this time, is a seer's life, a wife's life in escrow, and a second war already scheduled before the first has begun. Homer knew the transaction's outline centuries before Apollodorus: in the underworld catalogue of the Odyssey (11.326–327, paraphrased), Odysseus sees Eriphyle, who took precious gold for the life of her own husband. The necklace, in the archaic notice, is already simply “precious gold” — the object's name can fall away; what it bought cannot.

The army musters with seven leaders — Apollodorus's list (3.6.3) is Adrastus, Amphiaraus, Capaneus, and Hippomedon from Argos; Polynices from Thebes; Tydeus the Aetolian; Parthenopaeus the Arcadian — noting that some do not count Tydeus and Polynices among the seven but include Eteoclus and Mecisteus instead: the canonical seven were never quite canonical, and the lists vary by source in a way Section XV's table makes visible. On the march, at Nemea, the army asks for water; Hypsipyle, the Lemnian princess enslaved there as a nurse, sets down her charge, the infant Opheltes, to show them the spring, and in her absence a serpent kills the child. The Seven kill the serpent, bury the boy, and hear Amphiaraus rename him Archemorus, “Beginning of Doom”, reading the omen correctly; and they found funeral games in his honour — the origin, in the tradition, of the Nemean Games, one of the four great Panhellenic festivals, founded (like the cycle's cults generally) on a dead child. Then the army comes to Thebes, and to the seven gates.

XV. The Seven Against Thebes: Aeschylus and the Catalogue of Shields

The war's surviving masterpiece is Aeschylus's Seven Against Thebes (467 BCE), the third play of his Theban trilogy, and its centre is not a battle but a reading. For the play's long middle, a Scout reports, one by one, the seven Argive champions posted at the seven gates, describing each man's shield-device; and Eteocles, hearing each description, interprets the device — usually against its bearer — and assigns a Theban defender to the gate. The scene is a duel of interpretations before it is a duel of spears, and it is the fullest surviving treatment of the war from any ancient source. What follows is Aeschylus's catalogue as the Scout and Eteocles give it (lines 375–676, in Herbert Weir Smyth's Loeb translation, read for this article and paraphrased below except where quotation is marked). The play's gates are the Proetid, Electran, Neistan, the gate by Onca Athena's shrine, the Northern gate, the Homoloid, and the seventh — compare Pausanias's gate-names in Section XIX, which overlap but do not coincide exactly; the seven gates are a fixed number with fluid names, like the seven champions themselves.

Eteocles' interpretations are the scene's engine, and they run on a single principle: a device is a prophecy its bearer has written against himself. Tydeus's moonlit night will fall on his own eyes in death. Capaneus's torch will be answered by the real fire, Zeus's thunderbolt, which is not “midday heat” — Capaneus's own blasphemous comparison, reported by the Scout, of the god's lightning to summer warmth. Hippomedon's Typhon meets Hyperbius's Zeus, and Zeus has never yet been seen conquered. Parthenopaeus's Sphinx, Thebes's old enemy carried as a boast, will be beaten flat beneath the walls of the city she once plagued. Amphiaraus alone defeats interpretation by refusing a device: the Scout's report of him is a speech, not an emblem — the seer at the Homoloid gate publicly rebuking Tydeus as a murderer and counsellor of evil, and Polynices by name, asking what justice there is in draining dry the spring of one's own existence, and prophesying his own burial in foreign earth. Eteocles answers him with the play's bleakest reflection, on the just man ruined by evil partnership: the pious passenger drowns with the impious crew. And at the seventh gate the machinery stops pretending to be about shields at all. The Scout names the seventh champion — your own brother — and Eteocles, hearing the Justice-device and its golden legend, answers that Justice never stood by Polynices in the womb, in childhood, or in manhood, and will not stand by him now; and he takes the seventh gate himself: commander against commander, brother against brother, enemy against enemy. The chorus begs him not to go — blood of strangers can be expiated, they sing, but kindred blood, brother's by brother, is a pollution that never grows old. He goes. The curse, the play insists, is fulfilled not because Eteocles is dragged but because he reasons his way to the gate: honour, justice, and the gods' abandonment all argue, in his own mouth, for the one act that destroys him.

The Deaths of the Seven

Aeschylus ends with the brothers' bodies brought in together and the city saved; the full tally of the deaths belongs to the handbook tradition (Apollodorus 3.6.6–8, paraphrased), cross-lit by Euripides' Phoenician Women, which stages the same war with Jocasta still alive to walk the battlefield. Menoeceus, the young son of Creon, first: Tiresias had declared that Thebes would be saved if Menoeceus — a descendant of the Spartoi of unmixed descent — were sacrificed to Ares, in payment for the dragon of old; the young man, sent away by his father for safety, returned and killed himself upon the walls, at the dragon's own lair, and the city held: the founding debt, called in at last, and paid voluntarily by the Sown Men's purest heir. Capaneus, mounting a ladder against the Electran gate — Pausanias (9.8.7) still knew the spot — was struck by Zeus's thunderbolt in mid-boast, exactly as his shield had promised and Eteocles had read. Tydeus, mortally wounded by Melanippus, was brought the head of his killer by Amphiaraus, and ate the brains; Athena, approaching with a gift of immortality obtained for him from Zeus, saw the act, and in disgust let him die — the war's most savage vignette, and its clearest statement that there are victories that cost the victor his humanity. Amphiaraus, fleeing, was swallowed by the earth with his chariot and horses, Zeus splitting the ground with a thunderbolt to receive him before Periclymenus's spear could reach his back: the seer does not die so much as get taken, and his disappearance becomes a cult — the oracle of Amphiaraus at Oropus, where Pausanias (1.34) describes the sanctuary and the earth-swallowing was still pointed out on the road from Potniae to Thebes (9.8.3). Parthenopaeus and Hippomedon and Eteoclus fell in the fighting at their gates. And at the seventh gate the brothers met, and each killed the other, and the curse of Oedipus completed its work in the only form it had ever specified. Of the seven leaders, Adrastus alone survived, carried off the field by his divine horse Arion — the Thebaid's surviving line on the flight, quoted by Pausanias (8.25.8) in Evelyn-White's translation, has him flee “wearing miserable garments”, taking black-maned Arion with him. Thebes stood. Its king and its claimant lay dead in one another's arms' reach, and the throne fell to Creon — whose first act as king opens the saga's most famous aftermath.

XVI. The Unburied Dead: Antigone, Creon, and the Athenian Intervention

Creon, succeeding to the kingdom of Thebes, cast out the Argive dead unburied and issued an edict against burying them, setting guards on the corpses (Apollodorus 3.7.1, paraphrased). In the play-tradition the edict is narrower and sharper: it is Polynices in particular — the brother who brought a foreign army against his own city — who is denied burial in Theban earth, while Eteocles, the defender, is buried with full honours. Sophocles' Antigone (c. 442/441 BCE), the earliest of his Theban plays to be written and the last in the story's order, takes the edict as its premise and builds from it Greek tragedy's most durable argument. Antigone, daughter of Oedipus, buries her brother — in the play, by scattering the ritual dust with her own hands, twice over, the second time caught in the act. Brought before Creon, she does not deny the deed; she denies the edict's jurisdiction. The defence, paraphrased here from the play and never quoted, is the tradition's most famous piece of legal theology: there are unwritten and unfailing laws of the gods — among them the duty to bury one's kin — that no human proclamation can override, laws not of today or yesterday, whose life is eternal and whose origin no one knows. Creon, for his part, is not a stage tyrant but a statesman with a doctrine — the city before the family, the traitor's corpse as a public lesson — and the play's catastrophe is engineered from the collision of two intelligible orders, each absolute. Antigone is walled up alive in a rock tomb, a bride of Hades; there she hangs herself. Haemon, Creon's son and her betrothed — the second Haemon of the saga, Creon's younger son, the elder having been the Sphinx's last victim — finds her dead, and kills himself beside her when his father arrives too late with a reprieve. Eurydice, Creon's wife, hears of her son's death and kills herself at the altar, cursing her husband. Tiresias had already told Creon the gods were rejecting Thebes's sacrifices because of the unburied corpse and the living entombment; the lesson arrives, as it always does in this cycle, one recognition too late. Apollodorus's handbook version (3.7.1) compresses the play's action into its outcome: Antigone, caught burying Polynices by stealth, was shut up by Creon in the tomb and there killed herself; Haemon killed himself too. Other traditions — Euripides' lost Antigone among them, by its surviving hypothesis — let Antigone live, be detected with Haemon's help, and be given to him in marriage. The variants are recorded here because the hub's rule requires it: the death in the tomb is Sophocles' version, and it is Sophocles' version, not the only ancient one, that the world has kept.

The Argive dead, meanwhile, had their own advocate. Adrastus, the expedition's sole surviving leader, fled to Athens and took refuge at the altar of Mercy, begging the Athenians to recover the bodies of his men. Euripides' Suppliants (c. 423 BCE) stages the embassy: Theseus, at first refusing — the play lets him lecture Adrastus on the folly of the war — is persuaded by his mother Aethra and by the principle the play exists to state, that the burial of the dead is a Panhellenic law no city may suspend. Apollodorus (3.7.1, paraphrased) gives the outcome in a sentence: the Athenians marched with Theseus, captured Thebes, and gave the dead to their kinsfolk to bury. In Euripides the recovery is won first by heraldic argument and then by battle, and the play closes among the pyres — with Evadne, wife of Capaneus, leaping in her festival dress onto her husband's burning pyre to die with him, while her father Iphis watches; and with the sons of the dead, the Epigoni as small boys, carrying their fathers' bones and swearing the oath the next section redeems. Thebes's victory over the Seven thus ends, within a few years of story-time, in a humiliation administered by Athens and in a generation of Argive orphans raised on an unburied debt. The cycle's arithmetic is already running toward the second war.

// THEBES AND ATHENS //

Notice the pattern across Sections XIII and XVI: in the Athenian retellings, Athens is where Theban stories come to be judged and closed. Athens shelters Oedipus, buries the Argives, protects Antigone's principle if not her person, and will shelter Alcmaeon-traditions too. The tragedians were Athenians writing for Athens, and their Thebes is in part a mirror held up to their own city — the anti-city, brilliant and doomed, whose royal house does everything Athens congratulated itself on not doing. The myth is older than the mirror; but the polish on the glass is fifth-century, and this article has tried throughout to let the reader see both.

XVII. The Epigoni: The Second War and the Fall of Thebes

Ten years afterwards — Apollodorus's dating (3.7.2) is the tradition's — the sons of the fallen, called the Epigoni, the After-born, purposed to march against Thebes to avenge their fathers. They consulted the oracle, and the god promised victory under the leadership of Alcmaeon, Amphiaraus's son. Alcmaeon was unwilling to lead the army until he had punished his mother — for Eriphyle had done it again: Thersander, son of Polynices, had given her the robe of Harmonia, as his father had given her the necklace, and she had persuaded her sons also to go to the war. The necklace and the robe, between them, have now purchased three expeditions' worth of deaths: Amphiaraus's, the Seven's, and — by the command Amphiaraus laid on his sons — Eriphyle's own. The Epigoni's muster, in Apollodorus's list, is the war's meaning in catalogue form: Alcmaeon and Amphilochus, sons of Amphiaraus; Aegialeus, son of Adrastus; Diomedes, son of Tydeus; Promachus, son of Parthenopaeus; Sthenelus, son of Capaneus; Thersander, son of Polynices; and Euryalus, son of Mecisteus. Every name is a father's name with “son of” after it. The second Theban war is the first war, inherited.

The campaign, in Apollodorus (3.7.3–4, paraphrased), is everything the first was not: methodical, and successful. The Epigoni first laid waste the surrounding villages. Then, when the Thebans advanced against them led by Laodamas, son of Eteocles — the dead defender's son holding his father's city against the dead attackers' sons — Laodamas killed Aegialeus, the Epigoni's one fatality of note (and, by the tradition's fine print, the only son of Adrastus to fall where his father had survived), and was himself killed by Alcmaeon. The Thebans fled within the walls. Then Tiresias — the seer who spans the whole saga, already old in Laius's reign, still alive at the city's end — advised them to send a herald to treat with the Argives while the city emptied itself in flight; and the Thebans obeyed, and escaped by night. Tiresias died on the road, at the spring of Tilphussa, drinking its cold water (Apollodorus 3.7.4); the Epigoni entered an empty city and sacked it. They sent Manto — Daphne, in Diodorus's naming — the daughter of Tiresias, to Delphi, as the fairest of the spoils, in fulfilment of a vow: the seer's daughter becomes the god's, and the prophetic line of Thebes is exported to Apollo's shrine as war-booty. Thersander, son of Polynices, was installed as king of Thebes — the restoration his father died for, achieved by his father's enemies' sons in his father's name. His reign is the cycle's last page: Thersander married Demonassa, daughter of Amphiaraus — the two houses the necklace ruined, joined at last in marriage — and was killed years later in Mysia, by Telephus, while the Achaean fleet was gathering against Troy and mistook Mysia for the Troad: so the Cypria's tradition, preserved in Proclus's surviving summary of that Trojan poem, hands the Theban succession over to the Trojan war. Thebes's story ends not with a Theban death but with its king dying as a footnote to Troy's first campaign. The two cycles, paired by Hesiod, are in the end one chronology.

Alcmaeon: The Necklace's Last Owner

After the capture of Thebes, Alcmaeon learned that his mother Eriphyle had been bribed to his undoing also, and, in accordance with an oracle of Apollo, killed her (Apollodorus 3.7.5, paraphrased; some say with his brother's help, others alone). The matricide — commanded by a god, laid on him by his father before he could walk — brought the Fury of his mother's murder on him, and madness, and the cycle's last wandering. Purified by Phegeus at Psophis in Arcadia, Alcmaeon married Phegeus's daughter Arsinoe and gave her the necklace and the robe. But the land turned barren on his account, and an oracle sent him to the river Achelous, to be purified again and to settle on land that had not existed when the murder was done — the river's fresh silt at its mouth, earth too new to carry the curse. There he married the river's daughter Callirrhoe. Callirrhoe coveted the necklace and the robe, and said she would not live with him if she did not get them; Alcmaeon went back to Psophis and lied — that his madness would be cured if the necklace and robe were dedicated at Delphi — and Phegeus, believing him, gave them up; and Phegeus's sons, learning the truth from a servant, ambushed and killed him on the road. Callirrhoe's infant sons, grown miraculously to manhood by Zeus at her prayer, avenged their father, killed Phegeus and his sons, and dedicated the necklace and the robe at Delphi — where, Pausanias reports, the necklace of Harmonia was still shown in his own time (Apollodorus 3.7.5–7; Pausanias 8.24, 9.31; all paraphrased). The object that entered the story as a wedding gift exits it as a temple dedication, having outlived Harmonia, Eriphyle, Alcmaeon, and the city that minted its fame. If the cycle has a single protagonist that survives every generation, it is not a person. It is the necklace.

XVIII. The Lost Epics Behind It All

Behind Apollodorus, behind the tragedians, behind even Homer's allusions, stand the poems this section inventories — the Theban books of the Epic Cycle, the larger kyklos into which Hellenistic scholars arranged the archaic epics as a continuous history of the world from the gods' beginnings to the death of Odysseus. Photius, summarising Proclus in the ninth century, describes the Cycle's full sweep as including, before the Trojan poems, a Titanomachy and the Theban group: the Oedipodea, the Thebaid, the Epigoni, and the Alcmaeonis. The poems are lost. What survives of them, in M. L. West's standard modern edition (Greek Epic Fragments, vol. II, Loeb Classical Library, 2003) and in H. G. Evelyn-White's earlier Loeb collection (1914), from which this article's fragment-quotations are taken, is the following — and the poverty of the list is itself the finding.

The Oedipodea

Attributed to Cinaethon of Sparta and recorded, by an inscriptional testimony, at 6,600 verses — a poem on the scale of the Odyssey's half. Of those 6,600 lines, the tradition preserves effectively three data. First, the Sphinx fragment (preserved by a scholiast on Euripides' Phoenician Women): the Sphinx killed Haemon, son of Creon — “noble Haemon, the dear son of blameless Creon, the comeliest and loveliest of boys”, in Evelyn-White's translation. Second, the testimony of Pausanias (9.5.10–11), who argues from the Odyssey that Oedipus's children were not Jocasta's: in Homer, Pausanias reasons, the gods made the truth known at once, and a childless exposure follows; the sons and daughters were born of a second wife, Euryganeia, “as the writer of the epic called the Story of Oedipus clearly shows” (Evelyn-White's rendering of Pausanias's testimony). Third, by inference from its descendants: the poem must have carried the story from Laius's oracle through the Sphinx to Oedipus's marriages and death — Homer's reference to Oedipus's funeral games at Thebes (Iliad 23.679–680) shows an Oedipus who died honoured, as king, in his own city, which fits the epic's shape better than Sophocles' exile. Everything beyond that is reconstruction, and is labelled as such wherever this article has used it.

The Thebaid

The cycle's centrepiece was the Thebaid — the poem of the Seven. The Contest of Homer and Hesiod testifies that it ran to 7,000 verses and was recited by Homer himself as the first of his epics; antiquity widely attributed it to Homer (Callinus, by Pausanias's report at 9.9.5, did so), and modern scholarship treats the attribution as the compliment it is: the poem was good enough, and old enough, to need the greatest name. Its opening line survives, in Evelyn-White's translation:

“Sing, goddess, of parched Argos, whence lords …” — THEBAID, FRAGMENT 1, TRANS. H. G. EVELYN-WHITE (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY, 1914)

The poem begins at Argos, not Thebes — it is the attackers' epic, the story of the expedition told from the expedition's side. Its other verbatim remains are the two curse fragments set out in Section XIV — the silver table and golden cup of Cadmus produced before the blind Oedipus (quoted by Athenaeus), and the dishonouring haunch (preserved by a scholiast on Sophocles) — and the line on Adrastus's flight quoted by Pausanias. A scholiast's note adds that in the Thebaid the Epigoni sent Manto, Tiresias's daughter, to Delphi as first-fruits of the spoil — which suggests the poem's narrative reached beyond the Seven's failure into the second war, or that the scholiast's attribution strays. From such pieces, and from the poem's vast afterlife — Pindar's Theban odes breathe its air; Aeschylus's Seven is its dramatisation; Statius's Latin Thebaid of the first century CE, twelve books, is its Roman re-creation — scholars reconstruct a poem whose spine was: Oedipus's curses, the brothers' compact and breach, the Argive muster, the deaths at the gates, the mutual fratricide. The reconstruction is probable in outline and unverifiable in every detail, and both halves of that sentence must stand.

The Epigoni and the Alcmaeonis

The Epigoni was attributed in antiquity variously to Homer and to Antimachus of Teos. Its one famous datum is its opening, preserved by a scholiast on Aristophanes' Peace: where the Thebaid had sung the fathers, the sequel begins by singing “the younger men” — the sons — in a line that announces the poem's whole conception as a second-generation song (paraphrased; the scholiast quotes the opening words). Herodotus (4.32) already knows the Epigoni and doubts its Homeric authorship — the earliest piece of Cycle-criticism that survives, and a reminder that these poems were texts with reputations, argued over, by the fifth century BCE. The Alcmaeonis, finally, is barely more than a title with a hero: an epic on Alcmaeon — his matricide, madness, and wanderings, and, by its few testimonies, material reaching back to the war of the Epigoni and forward toward the foundations of Acarnania. Its fragments can be counted on one hand and settle almost nothing. The Theban Cycle, as a set of poems, thus reduces to: two poems known by a title, a length, an opening, and a dozen lines between them; one known by a title and a hero; and the certainty, from the whole later tradition, that between them they once contained the story this article has told. The cycle is literature on a lost literature, as its city is literature on a ruin — which is the subject of the final section.

XIX. The Footing: Bronze Age Thebes and the Kadmeia

Underneath the literature there is a hill. Thebes stands on a low ridge in the Boeotian plain, at the meeting of routes between Attica, the Corinthian Gulf, and the north — and on its acropolis hill, the Kadmeia, excavation has recovered one of the great palace centres of Mycenaean Greece. The footing matters to this article for one precise reason: the Theban cycle is not set in a place that later Greeks had to imagine. It is set in a place whose greatness was still legible in the ground — a palace-city of the Late Bronze Age, destroyed by fire around the end of the thirteenth century BCE, whose ruins, fortifications, and rich graves stood in the landscape throughout the centuries when the epics were forming. The cycle is literature on a real ruin, as at Troy — but with a family saga instead of a war as the literature's subject, and with the ruin in the middle of a living Greek city rather than on a deserted shore.

The palace itself — the Kadmeion, as the Thebes Museum's presentation names it — stood near the centre of the Kadmeia, a complex of many rooms and corridors given to work and storage as much as to ceremony, decorated with frescoes and destroyed by fire in around 1200 BCE (the museum's dating; the palace in its excavated form belongs to the thirteenth century BCE). Its storerooms held great stirrup jars of oil imported from Crete. Its workshops produced miniatures and jewellery from lapis lazuli, agate, quartz, and gold — materials drawn, as the museum's account puts it, from as close as Thebes and as far away as Afghanistan, the lapis's ultimate source. And its treasury held the single most evocative find of Mycenaean Thebes: a hoard of imported cylinder seals — some three dozen and more, of Assyrian, Hittite, Hurrian, Babylonian (Kassite), and Cypriot origin, many cut in lapis lazuli — stored in a palace room alongside gold, agate, and ivory. Edith Porada's celebrated suggestion, reported in the literature on the hoard, was that the Kassite seals — whose combined lapis weight is about an ancient mina — reached Thebes as a diplomatic gift, perhaps from the Assyrian king Tukulti-Ninurta I after his capture of Babylon around 1225 BCE. The suggestion is a suggestion, and is labelled as one here; what is not in doubt is the fact beneath it, that the Kadmeia's palace kept a treasury of Near Eastern seals in Near Eastern stone — that Bronze Age Thebes stood in direct, documented contact with the very East from which its foundation myth imports its founder.

The palace kept its accounts in Linear B, the syllabic script of the Mycenaean world, and Thebes has yielded one of the largest tablet archives on the Greek mainland — tablets and sealings recording wool and its processing, livestock, oil, offerings, and personnel, including the so-called Of series from a wool-working unit of the palace, sixteen tablets in one excavated room alone. The archive's contents are, in the usual Mycenaean way, gloriously mundane: no king of the tablets is named Cadmus, no tablet mentions a dragon. What the tablets prove is scale and system — a redistributive palace economy administering a wide Boeotian territory from the Kadmeia hill — and, in their divine names and offering-lists, a religious world already continuous with the classical one. Around the palace stood fortifications of Cyclopean masonry, cemeteries of chamber tombs rich in weapons and imports, and a lower town of real extent. Sarantis Symeonoglou's The Topography of Thebes from the Bronze Age to Modern Times (Princeton, 1985) remains the standard synthesis of the site's archaeology and topography, and the Theban excavations associated with Keramopoullos, Demakopoulou, Aravantinos, and their successors — conducted, of necessity, in rescue plots between the buildings of the modern city that sits exactly on the ancient one — continue to extend the palace's plan.

// WHAT THE FOOTING DOES AND DOES NOT SHOW //

It shows: a great Mycenaean palace at Thebes, rich, literate, fortified, in contact with the Near East, destroyed by fire around 1200 BCE — a city whose later inhabitants lived among its ruins and knew it had been great. It does not show: Cadmus, a dragon, an Oedipus, or a war of seven champions. The Iliad's Catalogue already lists only Hypothebai, Lower Thebes, in the generation after the Epigoni — the epic tradition itself registers a diminished successor settlement beside a great predecessor, which is precisely the archaeological picture of post-palatial Thebes. The honest statement is the one this hub makes for Troy as well: the cycle preserves the memory of a place's greatness, not a record of its events. The family saga is the form the memory took. A palace that kept Near Eastern seals in its treasury got, in its legend, a Phoenician founder; a palace that burned got a house that burned itself down across four generations. The fit is suggestive, and this article leaves it at suggestive.

The classical and later city kept the saga's geography on public display, and Pausanias Book 9 is the guidebook that survives. He walked a Thebes — rebuilt after Alexander the Great destroyed it in 335 BCE for revolt, an event that closes the historical city's ancient greatness as the Epigoni close the mythical one — whose ancient wall still had its seven gates, and he lists their names (9.8.4–7, in W. H. S. Jones's Loeb translation, paraphrased): the Electran gate, named for Electra, Cadmus's sister; the Proetidian; the Neistan, named, they said, for the nete, the last string of the lyre, which Amphion invented at that gate — or for Neis, a son of Zethus; the Crenaean; the Hypsistan, by the sanctuary of Zeus the Most High; the Ogygian, which seemed to Pausanias the most ancient of the names; and the Homoloid, the most recent, named for the Theban exiles' return from Mount Homole in Thessaly under Thersander — the Epigoni's king personally commemorated in his city's gate-list. At the Electran gate, where the road from Plataea enters, they showed the place where Capaneus was struck by lightning on the wall. He saw the spring of Dirce and the Ismenus river, the tomb of Amphion and Zethus, the house-sites of Cadmus and of Oedipus, the sanctuary of Apollo Ismenius where Herodotus had seen the Cadmean tripods six centuries before him. The storied landscape was, by Pausanias's day, over a thousand years old as an object of tourism and local pride, and it had outlasted the palace by longer than the palace's whole world had lasted. That is the cycle's final footing: not the ruin, but the ruin's interpretation, maintained by a living city as its own title-deed — until the city itself, like its saga's kings, was destroyed for reaching too far, and rebuilt smaller on the same hill, under the same name, over the same stones.
