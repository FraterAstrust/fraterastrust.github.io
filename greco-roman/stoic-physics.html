I. Why Wing II Needs a Physics: The Operating Vocabulary

This article belongs to the Foundations cluster of the Alchemy wing — Wing II — of the Greco-Roman hub. It is not an article about an alchemist, an apparatus, or a recipe. It is an article about the physics that the wing’s other articles keep presupposing, usually in a single unexplained word. When the article on Greek alchemy’s origins says that Bolos of Mendes catalogued the sympathies and antipathies of things, when the article on Zosimos of Panopolis says that a sublimed vapour is a pneuma and that a tincture is that spirit fixed in a body, and when the article on alchemical allegory says that the Stoics first taught Greek readers to decode a god into a physical principle, all three are spending a currency they did not mint. The mint was the Stoa.

The claim of this article is a limited and a checkable one. Between roughly 300 and 200 BCE, in Athens, three successive heads of one philosophical school — Zeno of Citium (c. 334–262 BCE), Cleanthes of Assos (c. 331–232 BCE), and Chrysippus of Soli (c. 279–206 BCE) — built, and their successors maintained and argued over, a complete physics of a continuous, living, rational cosmos. In that physics a single material breath, the pneuma, pervades all matter; its tension, tonos, is what makes a stone one stone, a plant alive, an animal ensouled, and a human being rational; bodies can blend wholly through one another without either ceasing to be itself; and because the whole is one animal held together by one breath, what happens in one part can genuinely affect a distant part — the doctrine the Greeks called sympatheia, sympathy, literally a suffering-with or feeling-with. That is the doctrine, and the vocabulary, that the Hellenistic workshop inherited.

The inheritance matters because sympathy, in the alchemical texts, is not a decorative metaphor. In Bolos it is offered as the reason a recipe works. In Zosimos the rising vapour is not like a spirit; in his Greek register it is a pneuma, a body fine enough to leave a body and to carry a quality into another body and fix it there. A reader who meets those claims without the Stoic physics behind them will read them as poetry, or as magic in the loose modern sense of the word. A reader who has the physics can see what their authors saw: a workshop application of the most systematic natural philosophy of the Hellenistic age. Recovering that difference — between a metaphor and a mechanism, as its users understood mechanism — is this article’s whole job.

WHAT THIS ARTICLE IS

The physics beneath Wing II’s operating vocabulary: the two principles, the designing fire, pneuma and tension, total mixture, cosmic sympathy, the conflagration and recurrence, and fate and providence in the brief form the physics requires — followed by the handover of that vocabulary to Bolos, to Zosimos, and to the allegorists.

WHAT THIS ARTICLE IS NOT

Not a life of Zeno, Cleanthes, or Chrysippus; not an account of Stoic logic or of Stoic ethics, except where the physics touches them; and not a claim that every alchemist was a Stoic. The alchemists were borrowers and adapters, and Section XV keeps the borrowing and the adapting distinct.

A note on method, placed here because this subject, like the Hermetica treated in the companion article on the Hermetica, attracts confident quotation of texts that do not survive. No complete work of Zeno, Cleanthes, or Chrysippus on physics survives. Chrysippus alone was credited in antiquity with hundreds of books; not one of them comes down to us whole. What survives is testimony: summaries, paraphrases, and short quotations preserved inside the books of later writers — doxographers, commentators, physicians, church fathers, and, very often, the Stoics’ philosophical opponents. The backbone modern collection of that testimony, in new English translations with commentary, is A. A. Long and D. N. Sedley, The Hellenistic Philosophers (Cambridge, 1987), cited throughout this article as Long & Sedley, or by their section numbers (44 for the principles, 46 for God, fire, and the cosmic cycle, 47 for elements, breath, tenor, and tension, 48 for mixture, 52 for everlasting recurrence, 55 for causation and fate). Accordingly: verbatim quotation in this article is confined to passages whose wording is verified in a named translation, and the translation is named at the quotation. Everything else that reports a Stoic doctrine is paraphrase, and is presented as paraphrase. Where the ancient testimony disagrees with itself — and it does, on points this article is required to cover — the disagreement is stated, not smoothed over (Section XIV).

// THE FOUNDATIONS CLUSTER //

This article assumes, and repeatedly points to, its cluster companions: Aristotle on matter and mixture, for the Peripatetic physics the Stoics were answering; atomism, for the Democritean and Epicurean physics of particles and void that is Stoic continuism’s great rival; and Greek astrology, for the astral art that Stoic sympathy was used to underwrite. Read together, the four give Wing II its floor: what matter was, what the rival accounts of it were, and what followed from choosing this one.

One further framing point, and it governs every section that follows. Stoic physics is not a detachable “science” module inside an otherwise ethical philosophy. The Stoics themselves divided philosophy into logic, physics, and ethics and then insisted, in the comparisons Diogenes Laertius preserves, that the parts are not separate: philosophy is compared, in paraphrase of Diogenes Laertius 7.39–40, to a living animal — logic the bones and sinews, ethics the flesh, physics the soul — and to an egg, and to a fertile field whose wall is logic, whose fruit is ethics, and whose soil and trees are physics. The comparisons are not ornaments. A school that held the cosmos to be one animal held its own philosophy to be one animal too, and it placed physics where the soul goes: innermost, animating, and impossible to remove without killing the whole. The modern popular Stoicism of handbooks and maxims, which keeps the ethics and discards the physics, would have struck the early Stoa as a dissection, not an abridgement. This article restores the discarded part — not because the ethics needs rescuing, but because the alchemists did not discard it. They took the physics, and left the maxims.

II. The Evidence: A System Rebuilt from Other People’s Books

Every statement in Sections III–XIII rests on evidence of a peculiar kind, and the reader is entitled to know its shape before being asked to trust any of it. The shape is this. Of the early Stoa’s own writings, what survives whole can be listed quickly: Cleanthes’ short Hymn to Zeus, preserved inside an anthology, is the famous exception that proves the rule. For the rest — for Zeno’s On Substance and On the Universe, for Cleanthes’ physical writings, for the enormous Chrysippean corpus on which the system chiefly rests — we have titles, summaries, and fragments: passages quoted, paraphrased, mocked, or refuted by later authors who had the books in front of them and had their own reasons for opening them.

The Witnesses, in Rough Order of Usefulness

The single most serviceable witness is Diogenes Laertius, whose Lives of Eminent Philosophers, written in the third century CE, devotes its seventh book to the Stoics. At 7.132–160 Diogenes gives a continuous, school-manual summary of Stoic physics: the division of the subject, the two principles, the elements, the cosmos and its ruling part, fate, divination, primary matter, mixture, the soul and its parts. The summary is compressed, occasionally garbled in transmission, and five centuries younger than Zeno — and it is still, by a wide margin, the closest thing to a Stoic physics textbook that antiquity left us. This article cites it constantly, by book and section, and quotes it, where it quotes it at all, in the Loeb translation of R. D. Hicks (1925), named at the quotation. A second English version, the older translation of C. D. Yonge (1853, reissued 1895), was also read in full for the physics sections in preparing this article, and is useful precisely because its divergences from Hicks mark the places where the Greek is hard. One such place matters and is flagged where it occurs: at 7.134 the tradition is unanimous that both principles are bodies, and renderings that make the principles incorporeal should be treated as a translator’s or copyist’s slip against the whole drift of the system (Sections III and XIV).

Around Diogenes stands a crowd of witnesses, each with an angle. The doxographer conventionally called Aetius preserves placita — school positions in a line or two — including the celebrated definition of the Stoic god as designing fire and breath that Section V quotes from Long & Sedley’s translation. Stobaeus, anthologising in the fifth century CE, preserves long extracts from the Stoic Arius Didymus and others, including the two-kinds-of-fire testimony of Section V. Cicero, in On the Nature of the Gods, On Fate, and On Divination, gives Latin, first-century-BCE expositions and critiques of Stoic theology, providence, and divination that are among our fullest sources for Sections X–XIII — written by a sceptical Academic who understood the system he was attacking. Plutarch, in On Stoic Self-Contradictions and On Common Conceptions Against the Stoics, quotes Chrysippus extensively in order to convict him of inconsistency, and thereby preserves him. Alexander of Aphrodisias, the great Aristotelian commentator of the late second and early third centuries CE, wrote a whole treatise, On Mixture (De mixtione), against the Stoic theory of total blending, and a treatise On Fate against Stoic determinism; hostile, exact, and indispensable, he is the chief source for Section IX. Galen, the physician, preserves Stoic doctrine on breath, soul, and mixture inside medical polemic. Sextus Empiricus, Philo of Alexandria, Nemesius, Origen, Eusebius quoting the otherwise lost Aristocles, Aulus Gellius, Hippolytus — the list in Long & Sedley’s index of sources runs to dozens of names, and Sections III–XIII draw on the testimony under the names given there.

// READING HOSTILE TESTIMONY //

A doctrine reported by its refuter is evidence twice handled. Alexander wants blending to be absurd; Plutarch wants Chrysippus to contradict himself; the church fathers want the conflagration to be a pagan error awaiting correction. The modern method — Long & Sedley’s, and this article’s — is not to discard such witnesses, which would discard most of the evidence, but to read them against one another: where a hostile summary, a neutral doxography, and a sympathetic exposition converge on the same doctrine, the doctrine is as secure as anything in Hellenistic philosophy can be. Where they diverge, this article says so. The two principles, pneuma and tension, total blending, sympathy, and the conflagration all pass the convergence test. Several details inside them do not, and Section XIV collects the failures honestly.

The Modern Reconstruction

The testimony was first collected entire by Hans von Arnim in Stoicorum Veterum Fragmenta (1903–1905), conventionally cited as SVF — the great Greek and Latin quarry from which all later work builds. Long & Sedley (1987) re-selected, re-translated, and commented the principal sources thematically, and their Stoic physics sections — 43, the scope of physics; 44, principles; 45, body; 46, God, fire, and the cosmic cycle; 47, elements, breath, tenor, and tension; 48, mixture; 49, place and void; 50, continuum; 51, time; 52, everlasting recurrence; 53, soul; 54, theology; 55, causation and fate — are the map this article follows. Two further modern works are used where they are apt, and their angles are different enough to be worth naming at the outset. S. Sambursky, The Physics of the Stoics (first published 1959) reads Stoic physics with a physicist’s eye, and is responsible, more than any other modern book, for the influential picture of pneuma as a continuum or field-like medium whose tension is a physical state of the body it informs — a picture Section XIV both uses and warns against taking for an ancient self-description. David E. Hahm, The Origins of Stoic Cosmology (1977) works in the opposite direction: not what Stoic physics resembles in modern science, but where it came from — arguing, in summary, that the Stoics built their cosmology by combining materials drawn from Plato’s Timaeus and receptacle and world-soul doctrines and from Aristotle’s accounts of generation, causation, and the divine, together with Presocratic elemental models, into a system neither predecessor would have owned. Where this article makes a claim about origins, it is Hahm’s kind of claim; where it makes a claim about structure, it is more often Sambursky’s or Long & Sedley’s. The three do not always agree, and the article does not pretend they do.

Finally, a caution about the word “system” itself. Zeno founded, Cleanthes developed, Chrysippus — in the ancient tag, without whom there would have been no Stoa — systematised. But the school then lived for five more centuries, and later Stoics changed their minds in public: about the seat of the cosmos’s ruling part, about whether souls survive and for how long, about divination, and, most consequentially for this article, about whether the cosmos burns at all (Sections XII and XIV). “The Stoics hold” in what follows therefore means, unless a name is given, “the early Stoa as the testimony reconstructs it, with Chrysippus as its weightiest voice”. Where a doctrine belongs to one man against another, the men are named. That discipline — names, not just schools — is the difference between a reconstruction and a legend, and this article observes it throughout.

III. Only Bodies Act: Corporealism and the Two Principles

Stoic physics begins with a criterion of reality, taken over from a famous passage of Plato’s Sophist (247d–e) and then hardened. In paraphrase of the Stoic position as Long & Sedley present it (sections 27 and 45): to be, in the full sense, is to be able to act or to be acted upon; and only a body — a three-dimensional solid with resistance — can act or be acted upon. The conclusion is uncompromising. Only bodies exist. God is a body. The soul is a body. Qualities, virtues, breath, fire — if they do anything, they are bodies. A small class of incorporeals — place, void, time, and lekta, the sayables or meanings of speech — are allowed a lesser status, subsistence rather than existence, precisely because they neither act nor suffer action. Everything that does work in the world is solid, extended, and resistant. This corporealism is the load-bearing wall of the whole physics: remove it, and pneuma becomes a metaphor; keep it, and pneuma must be a stuff.

Within the world of bodies, explanation bottoms out at two principles (archai). Diogenes Laertius states them at 7.134, in the passage on which this whole section rests. In the Loeb translation of R. D. Hicks (1925), quoted verbatim:

They hold that there are two principles in the universe, the active principle and the passive. The passive principle, then, is a substance without quality, i.e. matter, whereas the active is the reason inherent in this substance, that is God. For he is everlasting and is the artificer of each several thing throughout the whole extent of matter. — DIOGENES LAERTIUS 7.134, TRANS. R. D. HICKS (LOEB, 1925) = LONG & SEDLEY 44B

Unpack the sentence, term by term, because everything else in this article is commentary on it. The passive principle (to paschon) is unqualified substance (apoios ousia), matter: that which underlies everything, has of itself no quality, no form, no motion, and no resistance to being shaped, and can neither come to be nor perish. The active principle (to poioun) is the logos — reason — inherent in matter, and it is identified, without embarrassment and without remainder, with God. God, on this account, is not a craftsman standing outside his material, as the demiurge of Plato’s Timaeus is pictured standing outside his. The Stoic God is in the matter, everlasting as it is, and crafts each thing, in Diogenes’ phrase, throughout the whole of it. Diogenes immediately adds the pedigree: the doctrine is laid down, he says in paraphrase, by Zeno in On Substance, by Cleanthes, by Chrysippus towards the end of the first book of his Physics, by Archedemus, and by Posidonius. This is not one man’s speculation. It is the school’s foundation deed, signed by five names across two centuries.

THE PASSIVE PRINCIPLE — TO PASCHON

Unqualified substance; matter (hulē). No quality, form, or motion of its own. Ungenerated and indestructible. That which is acted upon, shaped, divided, and qualified — never, in the actual world, found alone and bare.

THE ACTIVE PRINCIPLE — TO POIOUN

Logos, reason inherent in matter; God; Zeus; designing fire; pneuma. Everlasting as matter is. That which acts: crafts, qualifies, coheres, moves, and orders each thing throughout the whole extent of matter.

Three consequences follow at once, and the article will keep returning to all three. First, both principles are bodies. That is the point on which the testimony, read whole, is firm — Long & Sedley’s section 44 is built on it — and it is what makes the Stoic God a physical doctrine rather than a theology pasted onto a physics. A bodily God can be mixed with bodily matter, can be present in different degrees and tensions in different places, and can literally be the breath in an animal’s chest and the coherence in a stone. An incorporeal God could do none of those things except by metaphor, and the Stoics, who allowed incorporeals to subsist but never to act, had closed the metaphorical door on purpose. Second, the principles are inseparable. They are distinguished in thought; they are never found apart in fact. There is no moment, not even at the conflagration of Section XII, at which bare matter sits waiting to be informed, and no place at which the active principle floats free of a material vehicle. The pair is an analysis of the one world, not an inventory of two worlds. Third, the principles are everlasting, and the elements are not. Diogenes draws exactly this contrast at 7.134, in paraphrase: principles and elements differ, in that the principles are ungenerated and indestructible, whereas the elements are destroyed at the conflagration; and the principles are unformed, whereas the elements have taken form. Keep the contrast in hand. It is what allows the cosmos to be, at once, eternal in its constituents and periodic in its arrangements — the hinge on which Section XII turns.

It is worth pausing on how strange the passive principle is, since later alchemy inherits a cousin of it. Unqualified substance is not any kind of stuff. It is not wet, dry, hot, cold, bright, dark, heavy, or light; each of those is a qualification the active principle has imposed. Nor is it a heap of particles: Stoic matter is continuous, infinitely divisible without ever reaching an indivisible minimum — the doctrine of the continuum in Long & Sedley’s section 50 — and the school’s rejection of atoms is not an oversight but a theorem (Section IX). Diogenes returns to primary matter at 7.150, in paraphrase of the Yonge and Hicks renderings alike: the substance of all things is primary matter; matter as a whole neither increases nor decreases, though the matter of particular things does both; and body is finite, changeable, and divisible without end. A substrate that never appears unqualified, is never exhausted, and has no smallest part: the Stoics were aware that such a thing is known by reason rather than displayed to sense, and they accepted the consequence. What sense displays is always the blend — the peculiarly qualified individual, this stone, this plant, this person — in which the two principles are already, and inseparably, at work.

// NOT PLATO’S CRAFTSMAN, NOT ARISTOTLE’S FORM //

The Stoic pair is easiest to misread as a re-labelling of an older pair: Plato’s demiurge and receptacle, or Aristotle’s form and matter. Hahm’s Origins shows how much material the Stoics genuinely took from both — and the differences are the doctrine. Aristotle’s form is not a body and does not travel through its matter; Plato’s craftsman works from outside, on a model he did not make. The Stoic active principle is a body, is inside, and is its own model: the seminal principles of Section V are not Forms in a separate heaven but reasons carried in the fire itself. See the cluster companion, Aristotle on matter and mixture, for the position being answered.

A last distinction, without which the next two sections blur. Principles explain; elements compose. The two principles are the world analysed into agent and patient. The four elements — fire, air, water, earth — are the first formed bodies into which, and out of which, the world’s furniture is actually made and unmade. Confusing the two levels is the commonest beginner’s error in reading Stoic physics, and the school’s own summary in Diogenes is careful about it in a way popular accounts rarely are. God is not a fifth element, and matter is not a zeroth one. They are what elements, and everything made from elements, are made of — the active breath and the passive body it breathes through, all the way down.

IV. The Four Elements in Stoic Dress

The Stoics kept the canonical four elements of Greek physics — fire, air, water, earth — and redressed every one of them. The redressing, not the list, is the doctrine. Three changes matter: the elements are qualified from the start; they are divided into active and passive pairs; and fire is promoted from first among equals to the element from which the others derive and into which they resolve.

Diogenes states the qualifications at 7.137. In paraphrase, following both the Hicks and the Yonge renderings: the four elements together are unqualified substance — matter — but taken singly, fire is the hot, water the moist (or wet), air the cold, and earth the dry. The sentence has troubled editors — the companion discussion in modern scholarship of how elements can be both matter and qualified is genuine, and is noted in Section XIV — but its physical intent is plain enough. An element, for the Stoics, is not a neutral building block awaiting a quality. It is a primary quality carried by a body: heat, cold, wetness, dryness in their purest available forms. That is why elemental change, for the Stoics, is real transformation rather than rearrangement. When air condenses to water, nothing has been shuffled; a body has changed its tension and its quality, through and through — a claim that only makes sense inside the mixture theory of Section IX, and that the atomist alternative, treated in the atomism article, exists precisely to deny.

ElementPrimary quality (DL 7.137, paraphrase)Role
Fire The hot Active. Pre-eminent: the element from which the others are generated and into which the cosmos resolves at the conflagration. In its designing form, the vehicle of the active principle itself.
Air The cold Active. With fire, constitutes pneuma. Its contractive, condensing work supplies unity and resistance; the medium of sound, of vision’s tensed cone, and of the soul’s messengers.
Water The moist / wet Passive. With earth, the sustained and shaped pair: the material the active elements hold together, nourish, and qualify.
Earth The dry Passive. The densest element, settled at the centre of the spherical cosmos; the sediment, in Diogenes’ image at 7.137, of the rest.

The active–passive division of the elements is the two principles reappearing at the elemental level. The testimony is explicit: a passage of Nemesius, cited in the modern collections and discussed in Long & Sedley’s section 47, reports in paraphrase that the Stoics held some elements to be active and others passive — fire and air active, earth and water passive — the upper, rarer pair doing the sustaining and the lower, denser pair being sustained. The architecture is fractal, and deliberately so: agent and patient at the level of principles; active and passive at the level of elements; and, in Section VIII, tenor, nature, and soul as grades of the same agency at the level of things. A Stoic explanation is recognisable across the centuries by exactly this signature — the same two-handed pattern, applied at every scale, with nothing left over that acts from outside the pattern.

Fire’s promotion needs its own paragraph, because Wing II lives downstream of it. In the cosmogony Diogenes summarises at 7.135–136 and 7.142 — paraphrased in Section V — the world is not assembled from four co-eternal elements. Substance turns, at the beginning of a cosmic cycle, from fire through air into moisture; the thick part of the moisture settles as earth, the thin part is rarefied as air and, rarefied further, kindles again as fire; and from the mixture of the elements so produced come plants, animals, and the rest. Fire is therefore both the first state of the whole and the last: at the conflagration everything resolves into it, and out of it the ordered world condenses again. Diogenes’ arrangement at 7.137, in paraphrase, gives the settled cosmos its onion of spheres: highest, the fire called aether, in which the sphere of the fixed stars is generated, then the planetary sphere, then air, then water, and earth, like a sediment, at the centre of all. Note what this is not. It is not the Aristotelian cosmos of a fifth, celestial element moving naturally in circles above a corruptible sublunary world. The Stoic heaven is fire — the same element that burns in a hearth, in its purest and most designing state — and the stars, in testimony Diogenes and Stobaeus preserve, are intelligent, fiery, and nourished by exhalations from sea and earth. One physics, from the fixed stars to the sediment: that monism, as much as any single doctrine, is what the alchemists bought when they bought the Stoa.

// WHY THE WORKSHOP CARED ABOUT ELEMENTS //

A recipe tradition that heats, condenses, sublimes, and dissolves is, whether it says so or not, a technology of elemental change. Stoic elemental theory told the Hellenistic workshop three usable things: that change of quality is real and total, not a rearrangement of changeless grains; that the active agents in any operation are the hot and the cold, fire and air, working on the wet and the dry; and that the rarest, most fiery fraction of a body is also its most powerful. The dyeing, gilding, and purple recipes of the Physika kai Mystika tradition are not Stoic treatises — but their operations are exactly the operations this physics describes best.

V. The Designing Fire and the Seminal Principles

The active principle has a biography inside the school, and the biography explains the vocabulary Wing II inherited. Zeno’s characteristic name for the active principle, on the testimony, was fire — not the destructive fire of the hearth but a creative, craftsmanlike fire. Cleanthes developed the fiery theology, located the cosmos’s ruling part in the sun, and gave the school its one surviving complete poem, the Hymn to Zeus, in which the universal logos steers all things. Chrysippus then refined fire into the subtler and more versatile instrument the mature system runs on: pneuma, breath, fire blended with air. Section VI takes up the refinement. This section keeps the fire, because two of the system’s central notions — the designing fire and the seminal principles — are stated in its language and never quite lose its accent.

The definition is Aetius’, and it is the second of this article’s two verbatim quotations. In Long & Sedley’s translation (The Hellenistic Philosophers, 1987, section 46A), quoted verbatim:

The Stoics made god out to be intelligent, a designing fire which methodically proceeds towards creation of the world, and encompasses all the seminal principles according to which everything comes about according to fate, and a breath pervading the whole world, which takes on different names owing to the alterations of the matter through which it passes. — AETIUS 1.7.33, TRANS. LONG & SEDLEY (1987) 46A

“Designing fire” renders pyr technikon: fire that works technikōs, with art, as a craftsman works — methodically, the translation says, proceeding by a road towards the production of a world. Diogenes gives the same phrase in his own summary at 7.156, in paraphrase: nature is a designing fire, proceeding methodically to creation, a fiery breath proceeding according to art. The contrast class is stated in a testimony of Zeno preserved by Stobaeus (1.213, in Long & Sedley’s section 46), here in paraphrase: there are two kinds of fire — the undesigning kind, which converts its fuel into itself, and the designing kind, which causes growth and preservation, as in plants, where it is their nature, and in animals, where it is their soul; and such, Zeno is reported as saying, is the fire that constitutes the substance of the stars. Hold the contrast firmly. Stoic fire is not combustion with a compliment attached. It is a defined technical term: the hot, active body in its ordering, preserving, life-giving employment, as distinct from the same element in its consuming employment. When later alchemical writing distinguishes a fire that cooks from a fire that destroys — a distinction the workshop literature makes constantly — it is spending this Stoic coin, whether or not the writer could have named the mint.

The Seminal Principles (Spermatikoi Logoi)

A designing fire that merely heated would explain a warm world, not an ordered one. The ordering is done by the seminal principles — spermatikoi logoi, seminal reasons — which the Aetius definition says the fire encompasses, and according to which everything comes about. The governing image is biological, and Hahm’s Origins is the modern account that takes the biology most seriously: as a seed contains, in miniature and in programme, the whole future organism and the timetable of its unfolding, so the primordial fire contains the logoi of all things — the rational formulae, carried materially in the fire itself, from which each kind of thing, and in the Chrysippean strong version each individual thing and event, develops in its appointed sequence. Diogenes states the cosmogonic form of the image at 7.135–136, in paraphrase: God, being alone at the beginning, turns the whole of substance through air into water; and as the seed is enveloped in the seminal fluid, so God, the seminal principle of the world, remains in the moisture, making matter serviceable to himself for the successive stages of creation, and then creates first the four elements. Eusebius, quoting Aristocles, preserves the conflagration-side statement (Long & Sedley 46G), likewise in paraphrase: at fated times the whole world is conflagrated and reconstituted, and the primary fire is as it were a seed, possessing the principles of all things and the causes of past, present, and future events, whose nexus and succession is fate.

Two warnings about the seminal principles, both necessary. First, they are not Platonic Forms. A Form, in the Platonic scheme the Stoics knew and rejected, is incorporeal, separate, and contemplated by a craftsman who copies it into matter from outside. A spermatikos logos is corporeal, inseparable, and inside: a portion of the designing fire’s own rational structure, differentiated by the matter it is developing. Proclus, in antiquity, already tried the equation of seminal principles with Forms, and modern scholarship — the point is standard in the commentary tradition Long & Sedley represent — rejects it. Second, the principles are not a blueprint consulted once. Because the fire that carries them never leaves the world it made, the logoi remain at work as the world’s continuing natures: the seminal principle of an oak is not exhausted in the acorn, and the seminal principle of the cosmos is not exhausted in the cosmogony. Development, growth, generation, decay — the whole traffic of nature — is the seminal principles still proceeding methodically. That is why one Greek word, physis, can mean both the nature of a single plant and Nature as the governing whole, and why Section VIII’s ladder can place the plant’s physis and the sage’s reason on a single scale of one breath’s tension.

PYR TECHNIKON — DESIGNING FIRE

The active principle under its elemental description: intelligent, methodical, productive. Contrasted with undesigning fire, which only consumes its fuel. The substance of the stars; in plants, their nature; in animals, their soul (Zeno, in Stobaeus, paraphrased above).

SPERMATIKOI LOGOI — SEMINAL PRINCIPLES

The rational programmes carried in the designing fire, according to which each thing develops in sequence. Corporeal and immanent, not separate Forms. At the conflagration, the primary fire as seed holds the principles of all things, past, present, and future.

Diogenes gathers the identifications into a single sentence at 7.135, in paraphrase: God and mind and fate and Zeus are one thing, called by many names. The sentence is the school’s theology in miniature, and Section XIII will need it: the many names are not many gods, and not mere aliases either, but the one active principle named from its several employments — as reason, as craftsman, as the chain of causes, as the ruler of the whole. At 7.147 Diogenes even preserves the school’s etymological exuberance on the point, in paraphrase: the pervading portion of God is called Dia, Zeus, Athena, Hera, Hephaestus, Poseidon, Demeter, and the rest, according to the region and power through which it extends — aether, air, fire, moisture, earth. The allegorists of the companion article on alchemical allegory begin exactly here: if the gods’ names are already, in Stoic hands, compressed physics, then Homer’s gods can be decompressed back into physics by the same method. The designing fire is where that method gets its licence.

VI. Pneuma: Breath Made of Fire and Air

Pneuma is the central word of this article, and it must be slowed down. In ordinary Greek it means a blowing: wind, breath, the breath of life. In the medical writers the Stoics read, it already named the vital breath whose movements in the body carry sensation and motion. The Stoics took the word, gave it a precise composition, and promoted it to the active principle’s working form at every scale below the cosmogony. The definition, as the testimony collected in Long & Sedley’s section 47 presents it, is this: pneuma is a blend of fire and air — of the two active elements — pervading matter, holding it together, qualifying it, and, at the appropriate tensions, making it alive and ensouled. Galen, no friend of the school, preserves the corollary for living things, in paraphrase: the Stoics held the soul to be a kind of pneuma, and nature likewise; the pneuma of nature being more fluid and cool, that of soul drier and hotter, and pneuma in either case a symmetrical blend of airy and fiery substance, since an animal’s body is dominated by neither extreme heat nor extreme cold. A blend, note — not fire alone, which would burn the body up, and not air alone, which would leave it cold and inert. Life, on this physics, lives in the mixture, and the proportions of the mixture are already an explanation of the difference between a stone’s coherence, a plant’s growth, and an animal’s warmth.

Why did the system need pneuma, when it already had designing fire? The honest answer is that fire alone could not do the work the school’s explanations required, and the testimony lets us watch the requirement being met. Fire expands, rarefies, rises, consumes. But bodies also contract, condense, resist, cohere — and a physics whose only active agent expands will explain a world that flies apart, not a world that holds together. Air, the cold active element, supplies the missing hand: contraction, condensation, resistance, unity. Blend the two and the blend can do both at once — which is precisely the two-way tensile movement of Section VII. Modern scholarship marks the development with names: the prominence of pneuma as the school’s master explanatory principle is, on the account David Sedley gives in his survey of Hellenistic matter-theory, probably the trademark of Chrysippus, building on Zeno’s and Cleanthes’ fire. Ian Hensley’s recent study of early Stoic pneuma, read in preparation for this article, argues the complementary caution: that pneuma is a genuine compound of fire and air, that many of its functions can be traced back to the functions of those two elements, and that each of the first three scholarchs did posit a pneuma pervading the cosmos. Both points are kept in what follows: pneuma is a real blend with a real composition, and it is also more than the sum of its ingredients’ separate employments, because the blend, and only the blend, has tension.

Pneuma is, next, everywhere. The cosmos contains no vacuum within itself — the void, for the Stoics, lies outside the spherical world, infinite and incorporeal, waiting, as Section XII will show, to receive the world’s expansion at the conflagration. Inside, all is plenum: body through body, with pneuma as the universal through-body. Cicero’s Stoic spokesman in On the Nature of the Gods (2.19) makes the cosmological form of the claim, in paraphrase: meteorological phenomena, and the mutual harmony of the parts of the cosmos, could not happen as they do unless the parts were held together by one divine and continuously connected pneuma. The sentence is worth dwelling on, because it states the inference pattern of the whole physics. The Stoics did not first posit a breath and then notice a harmony. They observed — or believed they observed — a cosmos whose distant parts behave as parts of one thing, and inferred the continuous bodily medium that alone, on their corporealism, could make one thing of them. Pneuma is an inference to the only kind of cause their first principles allowed: a body, acting by contact, everywhere at once because it is everywhere, full stop.

// BREATH, SPIRIT, OR STUFF? //

English forces a choice Greek does not: “breath” sounds physiological, “spirit” sounds theological, “stuff” sounds crude. The Stoic pneuma is all three registers at once, and the refusal to choose is the doctrine. It is the breath in the chest — Zeno, Antipater, and Posidonius are reported at Diogenes Laertius 7.157, in paraphrase, as holding that the soul is pneuma because by it we breathe and by it we are moved. It is spirit, in that it is God’s own presence in matter. And it is stuff, in that it has a composition (fire and air), a proportion, a temperature, and a tension that can rise, slacken, and fail. Translations that spiritualise pneuma out of the body betray the physics; translations that materialise it out of the theology betray it the other way. This article keeps the Greek word wherever the distinction matters.

Pneuma is also, finally, the soul — and not by analogy. The soul of an animal is a particularly hot, dry, and highly tensioned portion of the same breath that coheres in a stone, differentiated from the cosmic pneuma as a fragment is differentiated from its whole. Diogenes reports the cosmology in one breath with the psychology at 7.143, in paraphrase: the world is an animal, ensouled and rational, and its being alive is plain from the fact that our own soul is a fragment broken off from it. At 7.156–157, again in paraphrase: the soul is an inborn pneuma, a body, which survives death but is perishable; the soul of the universe, of which the souls in animals are parts, is imperishable; and on the survival of individual souls the school split in the open — Cleanthes holding that all souls persist until the conflagration, Chrysippus that only the souls of the wise do. The detail is included here deliberately. A physics in which the teachers disagree, on the record, about how long a soul lasts is a physics being worked, not a creed being recited — and the survival dispute is the small, checkable proof of the larger claim of Section II, that “the Stoics hold” always conceals names, dates, and arguments that the honest account must keep visible.

The soul’s internal architecture is pneumatic too, and one paragraph of it belongs in a physics article because Section XI will need it. The soul, in the standard early-Stoic account Diogenes summarises at 7.157–159, has eight parts: a ruling part, the hēgemonikon, seated in the heart, and seven outgrowths of pneuma reaching from it — to the five senses, to the voice, and to the generative organs. Perception is a physical transmission through tensed pneuma and tensed air: sight, in Chrysippus’s account as Diogenes reports it, works by a cone of luminous, tensed air stretched between eye and object, which reports the object to the eye as the ground is reported to a blind man by his staff; hearing, by air struck into spherical waves, like the ripples when a stone falls in a cistern. Sleep, in the same summary, is a relaxation of the sensory tension around the ruling part. Keep the staff and the cistern in mind. They are the school’s own images for its central conviction: that influence travels, really and bodily, through a continuous tensed medium — within the animal, and, Section X will argue, within the greater animal that contains all animals.

VII. Tension (Tonos): The Two-Way Movement

If pneuma is the Stoa’s master stuff, tonos — tension, tone, the word a Greek used for the tautness of a bowstring or a lyre string — is its master property. The doctrine, in the form Long & Sedley’s section 47 reconstructs it, is this. Pneuma in a body is not at rest. It moves, simultaneously, outwards and inwards: the outward movement produces a body’s quantities and qualities — its size, its colour, its heat, its hardness, everything by which it presents itself — and the inward movement produces its unity and substance, its being one thing rather than a heap. The classic testimony is Nemesius, in paraphrase: there exists in bodies a tensile movement which moves simultaneously inwards and outwards, the outward movement producing quantities and qualities and the inward one unity and substance. Sambursky’s Physics of the Stoics, in the compiled highlights read for this article, draws the systematic moral in his own terms: the tension of the pneuma has a twofold function — besides being a binding force, it is the agent that generates all the physical qualities of matter — and since pneuma pervades the whole universe, its tension makes the cosmos a single cohesive unit. One movement, two directions, and between them everything a body is: held together from within, qualified towards without.

The school knew the doctrine was hard, and the difficulty is itself instructive. Tension is not contact-force of the familiar ancient kind — one billiard body pushing another — because the pneuma does not push the body it informs from outside; it is through the body, wholly blended with it (Section IX), and its opposed movements are the body’s own state. The later, orthodox formulation is generally credited to Chrysippus, against a simpler first draft from Cleanthes, who is reported to have called tension a stroke — or blow — of fire: a phrase the testimony preserves and modern commentators, from Long & Sedley’s section 47 onward, have found genuinely difficult to construe. Three modern construals of the two-way movement are worth distinguishing, because Section XIV returns to them: the pneuma may circulate, part streaming outward while part streams inward, like a fountain’s rise and fall; it may oscillate in place, a vibration rather than a journey; or there may be, strictly, no locomotion at all, but opposed forces in equilibrium, as in a drawn bow that does not move. The ancient texts do not decide among these with the precision a modern reader wants, and this article does not decide for them. What is not in doubt is the function the doctrine performs. Tonos is the Stoa’s answer to the question every continuum physics must answer: in a world with no atoms and no internal void, what makes one region of the plenum this thing, with these qualities, holding this boundary against its neighbours? The answer is not a wall. It is a tone.

THE OUTWARD MOVEMENT

Produces quantity and quality: the body’s extension, and every property by which it is hot or cold, bright or dark, hard or soft, coloured, flavoured, sounding. The body as it presents itself to its neighbours — and acts on them.

THE INWARD MOVEMENT

Produces unity and substance: the body’s cohesion, its resistance to being crushed or split, its return to its form when distorted. The body as one thing to itself — the hexis, the tenor or holding-state, of Section VIII.

Tension admits of degree, and degree is destiny in this physics. The same breath, at a low tension, merely coheres; at a higher, it grows; at a higher still, it perceives and moves itself; at the highest, it reasons. Section VIII sets out that ladder in full. Here, note only the explanatory economy the ladder buys. A physics with atoms must explain the difference between a stone and a soul by arrangement — the same grains, differently shuffled. A physics with forms must explain it by imposition — different moulds on the same clay. The Stoic physics explains it by degree of one continuous quantity: turn the tension up, and coherence becomes life, life becomes soul, soul becomes reason, without at any point introducing a new kind of stuff or a cause from outside the world. Sambursky prized exactly this in the system, and his famous description of pneuma as a physical field — the carrier of all the specific properties of material bodies, whose mixtures in a body together constitute its hexis, its physical state — is a modern physicist’s recognition of the economy. Whether “field” is a legitimate translation of the Stoic intention, or a twentieth- century compliment the Stoics could not have paid themselves, is a real question, and it is reserved, deliberately, for Section XIV. The economy itself is ancient, and it is not in dispute.

One corollary of tension deserves stating before the ladder, because the alchemical sections will lean on it hardest. If a body’s qualities are its pneuma’s tension, then a body’s qualities can be changed by changing its pneuma — by heating it, rarefying it, blending another pneuma through it, or drawing its own pneuma out of it. Nothing in the physics forbids the transmutation of qualities; the physics is, from end to end, a theory of how qualities are produced, maintained, and altered by a breath that can be worked on. The Stoics themselves drew ethical and cosmological conclusions from that theory, not metallurgical ones. But a workshop tradition that adopted the theory’s vocabulary — that spoke of drawing a spirit out of a body, of a vapour carrying a quality, of a tincture fixing a new quality in a body that receives it — was not misusing the physics. It was applying it, at the bench, to the cases the bench supplied. Sections VIII and IX give the theory; Section XV gives the application.

VIII. The Ladder of Tension: Hexis, Physis, Psyche, Logos

The grades of pneuma are the Stoa’s classification of everything there is, stated as a physics. The testimony is unusually convergent — Diogenes, Philo, Origen, Galen, and the doxographers all report versions of the same ladder — and its fullest surviving statement, Philo’s in Allegories of the Laws (2.22–23), is a borrowing from the Stoa by a writer with his own allegorical purposes, which makes its agreement with the school’s other witnesses the more valuable. In paraphrase of Philo’s statement: intelligence has many powers — the tenor kind, the physical, the psychic, the rational, the calculative; tenor is shared by lifeless things, stones and logs, and our own bones participate in it; physique extends to plants, and in us nails and hair are plant-like; physique is tenor in actual motion; soul is physique that has acquired impression and impulse besides, and is shared by the irrational animals. The ladder, rung by rung, is as follows.

Logikē psychē — rational soul Mature human beings; and, at the cosmic scale, the cosmos itself Soul plus reason: impression judged, assent given or withheld, in the ruling part. The grade at which pneuma is not only alive and perceiving but accountable — and the grade the whole cosmos, as a rational animal, possesses entire.

Three features of the ladder must not be missed. First, the grades are cumulative, not exclusive. A human being does not exchange tenor for nature and nature for soul, as a climber leaves rungs behind. The bones still cohere by hexis while the hair grows by physis and the person perceives and reasons by psyche: one body, several pneumata, each grade present where its work is done. Origen’s summary, preserved in On Principles (3.1.2–3) and cited in Long & Sedley’s physics sections, makes the same point from the side of motion, in paraphrase: things held together by hexis alone are moved only from outside, as sticks and stones are; things held together by nature or soul have the cause of motion in themselves; and among the self-moved, plants and soulless growths move from themselves, ensouled things by themselves, when an impression stimulates impulse. Second, the ladder is a continuum with thresholds, not a set of boxes. Tension rises by degree, and the school was untroubled that the degrees shade into one another; what makes a grade a grade is the appearance of a new power — growth, then impression-and-impulse, then assent — not a new substance. Third, the ladder runs in both directions of scale. Downwards, it classifies the parts of one animal: bone, hair, sense, reason. Upwards, it classifies the cosmos: the world too has its coherence, its nature, its soul, and its ruling reason, and Diogenes’ report at 7.138–139 — that intellect pervades the world as soul pervades us, in some parts more, in some less, through the bones and sinews as mere tenor, through the ruling part as mind — is simply the ladder applied to the largest body there is. The disagreement Diogenes records in the same breath, about where the world’s ruling part sits — Cleanthes saying the sun, Chrysippus and Posidonius the heaven or aether, Antipater the aether under another description — is a disagreement within the ladder, about the address of its top rung in the cosmos. Nobody in the school doubted the ladder.

// THE LADDER AT THE BENCH //

Read the ladder once more with a workshop eye, because Section XV will. A metal, on this physics, is a body held by hexis: its qualities — colour, hardness, fusibility — are the tension of the pneuma blended through it. A vapour rising from a heated body is that body’s pneuma loosening, rarefying, departing — and carrying, as Galen’s testimony insists a soul-pneuma carries a blend’s proportions, the quality of its source with it. To tinge a metal, then, is to blend a foreign, quality-bearing pneuma wholly through it and fix it at a new tension. That sentence is not yet alchemy. But it is, word for word, the physics alchemy will speak. The ladder is where the bench learned that a body’s spirit could be graded, drawn, and reset — because the philosophers had already said that every body has one.

A note on the ruling part, to close the section, since fate and freedom both wait on it in Section XIII. In the rational animal, the pneuma of the ruling part — the hēgemonikon, in the heart — receives impressions through the outgrown pneumata of the senses, and action begins when the ruling part gives or withholds assent. Assent is a pneumatic event: a state of the breath in the chest, as physical as a heartbeat, and the school’s moral psychology is unintelligible except as physics at this address. Chrysippus’s famous comparison — preserved in the later testimony and discussed under causation in Long & Sedley’s section 55 — likens the external impression to the push given to a cylinder, and the assent to the cylinder’s rolling by its own roundness: the push is an auxiliary, proximate cause; the rolling is the thing’s own, a principal and perfect cause, and it is the rolling, not the push, that is laid at the agent’s door. The image is usually taught as ethics. It is stated here, in its home, as what it is: a doctrine about grades of tension, and about which tension, in a rational animal, is the animal’s own.

IX. Total Mixture (Krasis di’ Holou): Bodies Through Bodies

Everything so far — the two bodily principles wholly pervading one another, the breath blended through the body it qualifies, the soul interpenetrating its limbs — presupposes a physical possibility that Greek physics, before the Stoa, had unanimously denied: that two bodies can occupy the same place, wholly, at the same time. The Stoics did not presuppose it sheepishly. Chrysippus built a theory of mixture to assert it, defended it against the obvious objections, and made it the school’s signature paradox. The theory survives chiefly because Alexander of Aphrodisias wrote On Mixture to destroy it — the happiest accident in the transmission of Stoic physics, and the reason Long & Sedley’s section 48 can reconstruct the doctrine in detail from its refutation.

Alexander’s report, in paraphrase, distinguishes three ways bodies were said to combine. The first is juxtaposition (parathesis): grains of wheat and barley in one heap, bodies side by side, touching at surfaces, each keeping its own place and its own boundary. There is no unity here at all, only contact — and this, the Stoics agreed with everyone, is not mixture. The second is fusion (synchysis): the ingredients are destroyed into a new body, as, in the medical examples, the ingredients of a compound drug perish into the drug. There is unity here, but it is the unity of a successor: what went in does not survive to be recovered. The third — the Stoic claim — is blending, krasis, and in its full form total blending, krasis di’ holou, blending through the whole. Two (or more) bodies interpenetrate one another entirely, extending through one another whole through whole, so that no part of either fails to participate in the other; the blended bodies nevertheless preserve their own substances and qualities in the blend; the blend occupies no more room than one of its ingredients did alone in the cases that scandalised antiquity; and the ingredients can, in principle, be separated out again. Soul and body are the standing example: the soul is wholly in every part of the body it animates, the body wholly pervaded by it, and neither is diminished by the coextension. God and matter are the cosmological example: the two principles of Section III are not two layers but one total blend, everywhere.

Set the Stoic claim against its two rivals, as the cluster’s design requires, and its audacity comes into focus. Against Aristotelian mixis — the subject of the companion article on Aristotle’s matter theory — the difference is the survival clause. For Aristotle, in paraphrase of On Generation and Corruption I.10, a true mixture is a union of mixables that have altered one another; the ingredients are present in the mixture not actually but potentially, which is why they can be separated out again, and the mixture has a single form of its own that is neither ingredient’s. The Stoics found the potentiality clause an evasion. On their corporealism, a quality that is only potentially present does no work, and the blend’s work — the soul’s perceiving in every limb, the wine’s flavour in every drop of the cup — is actual work. So they paid the price Aristotle refused to pay: two bodies, actually both, in the same place, whole through whole. Against atomist juxtaposition — the subject of the companion article on atomism — the difference is unity itself. For Democritus and Epicurus, every apparent mixture resolves into indivisible grains in contact, with void between; qualities are arrangements, and nothing is ever truly in anything else. The Stoics, whose continuum admits no atoms and no internal void, found juxtaposition an evasion in the opposite direction: a heap is not a body, contact is not cohesion, and a physics that cannot distinguish a stone from a pile of gravel has declined to explain the first fact of experience. Total blending is the Stoic refusal of both evasions at once. It is also, not incidentally, the only one of the three theories under which a quality-bearing vapour can be wholly in the metal it tinges while remaining itself — a point Section XV collects.

The objections came at once, and the testimony keeps the best of them. If a drop of wine blends through the whole sea — the example Diogenes attaches to Chrysippus at 7.151, in paraphrase: a little wine thrown into the sea keeps its distinctness briefly, and is then lost — has the drop been blended or annihilated? If blended bodies are coextended, why does the blend not always double in volume? If the soul is wholly in the foot, is the foot rational? Alexander presses versions of all three, with a Peripatetic’s patience and relish. The Stoic replies, as modern reconstruction — Vanessa de Harven’s rehabilitation of the doctrine, read for this article, states the strongest form of it — begin by refusing the objector’s picture of body. The resistance (antitypia) that makes a body a body is not, for the Stoics, the ricochet of absolutely full, impenetrable solids; it is the resistance of a tensional state, which can coextend with another tensional state as one strain in a chord coexists with another. On that conception, blending is not two billiard balls attempting the impossible, but two qualified continua wholly present to one another, interacting causally at every point — which is, after all, exactly what the two principles had been doing since Section III. Whether the reply succeeds is a live question in the scholarship, and Section XIV leaves it live. That Chrysippus held the doctrine, stated it in its strongest form, and understood its cost, is not in question at all. Diogenes’ one-line summary at 7.151, in the renderings read for this article, has blendings occur through and through, and not by surface contact and juxtaposition — a sentence aimed, across two centuries of school warfare, directly at the atomists.

// A DROP OF WINE, HANDLED CAREFULLY //

The wine-drop-in-the-sea example is routinely quoted in popular accounts as if it were the Stoics’ proud illustration of blending’s power. In Diogenes’ text it appears in a compressed report of Chrysippus on infinite divisibility, and its drift is concessive as much as triumphant: the drop holds its distinctness briefly, then is lost to perception. Whether Chrysippus held that the drop’s substance persists, infinitely attenuated, through the whole sea — the strong reading Alexander attacks — or allowed a limit at which blending gives out, is one of the genuinely disputed details of Section XIV. This article reports the example with its concession attached, and builds nothing on the strong reading that the ladder and the principles do not build without it.

X. Sympatheia: The Cosmos as One Animal

Sympathy is where the physics becomes a doctrine about distance. The word is sympatheia: a being-affected-together, a co-suffering. Its Stoic content follows from the preceding five sections by a chain the school itself drew, and the chain is worth drawing slowly, link by link, because later users of the word — astrologers, physicians, alchemists, and, in the fullness of time, advertisers of crystals — have usually kept the conclusion and discarded the chain.

Link one: the cosmos is one body. It is finite, spherical, continuous, with no void inside it, surrounded by the infinite incorporeal void outside (Diogenes Laertius 7.140, in paraphrase). Link two: it is one body of a specific kind — an animal. Diogenes at 7.142–143, in paraphrase: that the world is an animal, rational, ensouled, and intelligent, is affirmed by Chrysippus in the first book of On Providence, by Apollodorus, and by Posidonius; an animal is an ensouled substance with sensation; an animal is better than a non-animal, and nothing is better than the world, therefore the world is an animal — and Boethus, Diogenes adds without blinking, denies it. Link three: an animal is unified by its pneuma. As the soul-pneuma, wholly blended through its body, makes the animal’s parts parts of one perceiving whole — so that a wound in the foot is suffered by the animal, not by the foot alone — so the cosmic pneuma, wholly blended through the cosmos, makes the world’s parts parts of one whole. Link four: in such a whole, affection is transmissible without remainder of distance. Not by magic, and not by a body leaping a gap — there are no gaps — but by the continuous tensed medium itself, as the staff transmits the ground to the blind man’s hand and the tensed air the seen object to the eye (Section VI). Conclusion: distant parts of the cosmos can genuinely affect, indicate, and answer to one another, because they are organs of one animal, strung on one breath, at one tension. That conclusion is sympatheia.

An animal is better than a non-animal; nothing is better than the cosmos; therefore the cosmos is an animal — and it is ensouled, as is clear from our own soul being a fragment of it. — PARAPHRASE OF DIOGENES LAERTIUS 7.143 (CHRYSIPPUS, APOLLODORUS, POSIDONIUS); NOT A VERBATIM QUOTATION

The school’s own vocabulary for the unity is worth a sentence, because the alchemists inherited the words with the doctrine. Alongside sympatheia the testimony uses sympnoia — a breathing-together, conspiration in the word’s literal, pre-political sense — and syntonia, a being-at-one-tension. The three words are one thought in three registers: one breath, one tension, one suffering-with. Modern discussion of Stoic natural philosophy (Michael White’s account is the one the secondary literature read for this article cites) gathers them as the school’s description of how the logos unifies the manifold of corporeal being into one world. A cosmos that breathes together can be read together: a sign in one organ can signify a state of the whole. Section XI is the history of that reading.

Who made sympathy a doctrine? The testimony distributes the credit in a way this article reports rather than resolves. The physical premises are Chrysippean: total blending, the continuous pneuma, fate as a pneumatic power — Chrysippus is reported in the later testimony as having defined fate itself as a dunamis pneumatikē, a pneumatic power, the causal nexus and the force in one. The systematic exploitation of sympathy — its development into a principle of natural inquiry, connecting tides, weather, animals, plants, and the heavens — is credited by the modern literature, and by the ancient tradition behind it, above all to Posidonius of Apamea (c. 135–51 BCE), the last great systematiser of the Hellenistic Stoa, who travelled, measured, and theorised the connectedness of the whole with an empiricist’s appetite. The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s account of Hellenistic astrology, read in full for this article, puts the received position carefully: cosmic sympathy was already abroad in the medical and natural thought of the age, Posidonius is credited with its development inside the Stoic school, and Posidonius himself is said to have drawn the notion from a line of predecessors — Democritus, Xenophanes, Pythagoras, Socrates are the names the tradition gives. Sympathy, that is, was not invented in the Stoa. It was housed there: given, for the first time, a physics in which it was not a marvel but a theorem. The distinction between inventing a word and housing it will matter again, decisively, in Section XV’s treatment of Bolos — whose floruit, around 200 BCE, falls within Chrysippus’s own lifetime, and whose sympathy-catalogues therefore cannot be a simple borrowing from a finished Stoic system.

// SYMPATHY IS NOT ACTION AT A DISTANCE //

The phrase “action at a distance” is used loosely of Stoic sympathy, and this article’s own commission uses it — but strictly, the Stoics are the ancient school least entitled to it. Nothing, in their physics, acts across a gap, because there is no gap for it to act across. The moon does not reach the tide across empty space; moon, intervening pneuma, sea, and shore are one continuous tensed body, and the affection travels as a strain travels in a string. Sympathy is action through a plenum — distance without separation. Keep the correction in hand whenever the shorter phrase is used below: it is the continuity, not the distance, that does the explanatory work.

XI. What Sympathy Was Made to Explain: Tides, Divination, Astral Influence

A theorem earns its keep by the phenomena it is set to explain. Sympathy was set to explain three, and the three are instructive in ascending order of controversy: the tides, where sympathy guided genuine discovery; divination, where it underwrote the school’s most debated commitment; and astral influence, where it supplied the physical licence for the art treated in the companion article on Greek astrology.

The Tides and the Moon

The Mediterranean is nearly tideless, and the regular rise and fall of the Atlantic at Gades (modern Cádiz), where Posidonius observed, was one of the Hellenistic world’s standing wonders. Posidonius’s treatment — preserved in summary by later geographers and discussed in every modern account of him — was, in paraphrase, the first systematic correlation of the tides with the moon: a daily cycle answering to the moon’s passage, a monthly cycle answering to its phases, and an annual modulation besides, established by observation at Gades over an extended stay. Gravitation being unavailable to him, the physical account he reached for was sympathy: the sea and the moon are parts of one animal, and the sea’s affection answers the moon’s as one organ answers another. The received modern judgement, represented in the reference literature read for this article, is that the tidal researches may themselves have fed the doctrine — that sustained attention to a distant body’s regular governance of a local sea helped make cosmic sympathy, for Posidonius, an empirical conclusion and not only a school dogma. The direction of travel matters less than the pattern it sets: sympathy, in its best ancient employment, is a research programme, not a conversation-stopper. It told its holder where to look for correlations, and at Gades the correlations were really there.

Divination

Diogenes states the school’s commitment without hedging at 7.149, in paraphrase: all things happen by fate, say Chrysippus, Posidonius, Zeno, and Boethus; fate is a connected cause of the things that are, the reason by which the world is administered; and divination is real in all its forms, if providence is real — a position Zeno, Chrysippus in the second book of On Divination, Athenodorus, and Posidonius are named as holding, and Panaetius as denying. The argument connecting the physics to the practice runs through Sections X and XIII together. If the cosmos is one animal, strung on one pneuma, and its history is one connected chain of causes administered by a provident reason, then the future is already woven into the present’s fabric — and a present sign, rightly read, can be a genuine sample of that fabric, as a symptom is a genuine sample of a body’s state. Cicero’s On Divination, Book I, preserves the Posidonian form of the case (in the exposition modern scholarship, following Peter Struck’s study of divination and intuition, locates chiefly at I.118–132): in paraphrase, the universe is permeated by the divine pneuma; every thing can affect every other; the pneuma, as the world’s soul, contains not only the present but the causes of what will be; and divination — technical, from entrails, birds, and lots, and natural, from dreams and inspired utterance alike — is the perception, by a soul that is itself a fragment of that pneuma, of the larger pattern its fragmentary station usually hides. Sleep, on the pneumatic psychology of Section VI, loosens the sensory tension that binds the soul to its immediate impressions, and is therefore the natural divinatory state. None of this requires a god to interrupt nature with a message. That is the point, and the school’s pride in it is audible even through Cicero’s sceptical frame: divination, Stoically construed, is not a suspension of the causal order but one of its consequences.

Astral Influence

From divination to astrology is one step, and the physics takes it without changing its stride. The stars, on Section IV’s cosmology, are designing fire in its purest state — intelligent, divine, nourished by the earth’s and sea’s exhalations, moving in the aether whose sphere is, on Chrysippus’s and Posidonius’s view, the cosmos’s ruling part. Bodies so placed, so constituted, and so central in the one animal’s economy cannot be idle spectators of the sublunary world: their configurations are at once causes within the pneumatic continuum and signs of the whole’s state, and the distinction between causing and signifying — which modern readers press — is one the physics itself softens, since in a totally blended, totally connected whole, the heavens are the ruling organ through which the whole is administered. Augustine, hostile and therefore precise, reports Posidonius as much given to astrology and an asserter of the stars’ fatal influence (City of God 5.2, in paraphrase); the modern assessment, as the IEP account read for this article gives it, is more layered — Posidonius’s astronomy, meteorology, and tidal work are certain, his support for astrology well attested, and the school’s general position is that astral influence is sympathy’s most systematic application, developed, in the mathematical form the art soon took, into the astrology the hub treats separately. The full account — the zodiac, the aspects, the lots, the practitioners, and the critics, Panaetius prominent among the internal ones — belongs to the Greek astrology article. What belongs here is the licence, and it can be stated in one sentence: astrology is physically possible, on Stoic premises, for exactly the reason the tides are explicable — there is one animal, one breath, and no separation. Schools that denied the premises, atomists above all, denied the art its physics and were reduced to denying its phenomena. The Stoa never had to choose.

THE TIDES

Sympathy as research programme. Posidonius at Gades correlates the sea’s cycles with the moon’s — daily, monthly, annual — and explains the correlation by the co-affection of organs in one animal.

DIVINATION

Sympathy as epistemology. A provident, fated, pneumatic whole can be sampled: signs, dreams, and entrails as symptoms of the world-body’s state. Affirmed by Chrysippus and Posidonius; denied, on the record, by Panaetius.

ASTRAL INFLUENCE

Sympathy as licence. The heavens, designing fire and the cosmos’s ruling part, act and signify within the one continuum. The physics underwrites the art whose practice the companion article treats.

XII. Ekpyrosis and Everlasting Recurrence

The Stoic cosmos has a life cycle, and the ladder of Section VIII should have prepared the reader for nothing less: what is an animal is born, flourishes, and dies — and the cosmos, being the animal that contains all animals, dies on the grandest schedule there is. Its death is the ekpyrōsis, the conflagration: at fated intervals, when the planets return to the same configuration in which they stood at the cycle’s beginning — the cosmic period the tradition calls the Great Year — the ordered world resolves wholly into fire. Not into nothing. Into fire: the first element, the designing element, the active principle’s own element, which at the conflagration is all that there is — God alone with his own substance, holding, as the Aristocles testimony of Section V puts it in paraphrase, the seminal principles of all things and the causes of past, present, and future events, as a seed holds a plant. Then, by the cosmogony of Sections IV–V, the whole proceeds again: substance turns from fire through air to moisture, the elements separate, the world condenses, furnishes itself, and runs its course to the next conflagration. The technical name for the restoration is apokatastasis; the doctrine as a whole is Long & Sedley’s section 52, everlasting recurrence.

Recurrence, in the Chrysippean strong form, means identity. Alexander of Aphrodisias, again preserving by attacking, reports the doctrine in paraphrase: after the conflagration, all the same things come to be again in the world, numerically — so that even the same peculiarly qualified individual exists and comes to be again in the succeeding world. Eudemus, Aristotle’s pupil, had already caught the flavour of the Pythagorean ancestor of the idea in the tag the modern literature loves: the teacher will talk again, staff in hand, to the same pupils sitting just so, and everything else will be alike. The Stoic version’s logic is internal and, on its premises, tight. The succeeding world is made by the same God, from the same substance, according to the same seminal principles, by a reason that is perfect and therefore cannot improve on its own best work: the present world is the best possible, any variation would be a variation for the worse, and so the next world is this world, again. The Routledge Encyclopedia’s summary, read for this article, states the consequence without flinching: an endless series of identical worlds, history repeating itself in every minute detail — and adds the school’s own consolation, that the conflagration is not the world’s collapse into ruin but its achievement of perfection, the phase in which what exists is, in effect, pure intelligence, planning the next world-phase in every detail. Fire, which the hearth knows as destruction, is at the cosmic scale the world’s most rational state. The paradox is deliberate, and it is Section V’s two kinds of fire, written across eternity.

A cosmic cycle

THE ORDERED WORLD (DIAKOSMĒSIS)

The cosmos as we inhabit it: elements separated, stars nourished and moving, animals and plants developing by their seminal principles, fate unfolding as the connected chain of causes. Finite, spherical, one animal — and, in its parts, destructible, since its parts change into one another (Diogenes Laertius 7.140–141, paraphrased).

The Great Year ends

EKPYRŌSIS — THE CONFLAGRATION

At the fated return of the heavenly bodies to their starting configuration, the world resolves into designing fire. The elements perish; the principles — God and matter, everlasting both — do not. Souls persist, on Cleanthes’ view all of them, on Chrysippus’s only the wise, until this hour (Diogenes Laertius 7.157, paraphrased).

The seed-phase

GOD AS SEMINAL PRINCIPLE

The primary fire, as a seed, possesses the logoi of all things and the causes of past, present, and future events; their nexus and succession is fate (Aristocles in Eusebius, paraphrased; Long & Sedley 46G).

The next cycle

APOKATASTASIS — RESTORATION

Substance turns from fire through air into moisture; the thick settles as earth, the thin rarefies to air and fire; plants, animals, and persons come to be again — the same, on the Chrysippean doctrine, numerically the same (Diogenes Laertius 7.135–136, 7.142, paraphrased).

The dissent is part of the doctrine’s history and belongs in any honest statement of it. The conflagration was controversial inside the school from an early date. The recent scholarship on Cleanthes and early Stoic cosmogony (Benjamin Harriman’s study, read for this article) shows the first three scholarchs — Zeno, Cleanthes, Chrysippus — in substantial agreement that the present order will be destroyed into fire and an identical order emerge, while already differing over the mechanics of the passage from fiery destruction to renewed cosmogony, and over Cleanthes’ staging of the process from the centre outward. The later defections are blunter. Diogenes records at 7.142, in paraphrase, that Panaetius (c. 185–110 BCE), the great Middle-Stoic who carried the school to Rome, asserted the world to be imperishable — a flat denial of the cycle. Zeno of Tarsus, Chrysippus’s own successor, is reported in the later tradition to have suspended judgement on the conflagration; Boethus of Sidon denied it outright, as he denied (Section X) that the world is an animal. By the Roman period the ekpyrosis had become, for many Stoics, an heirloom doctrine — honoured, allegorised, or quietly shelved — while remaining, in the early testimony, as central as any physical claim the school made. This article states it as the early Stoa’s doctrine, Chrysippus’s above all, and states the defections in the same breath. Christian writers from Hippolytus and Origen onward attacked the cycle as a denial of creation, judgement, and once-for-all salvation — Hippolytus’s summary, in paraphrase, has the Stoics expecting a conflagration and purification of the world, some saying entirely, others in part, and calling the destruction and subsequent generation a purification — and their attacks, like Alexander’s on mixture, preserve the doctrine they bury. The wheel the school built to explain the world’s eternity turned out to be the wheel its critics could least forgive.

For Wing II, one consequence of the cycle matters above the rest, and it is easy to miss. A cosmos that recurs identically, from a seed that contains all causes, is a cosmos in which correspondence is guaranteed by manufacture. Things sympathise not because a magician has linked them, nor because a god has whimsically signed them, but because one reason made them all, from one substance, according to one set of seminal principles, and makes them all again, identically, for ever. The sympathy of Section X is the cycle’s local weather. When the alchemical tradition, centuries later, treats the world as a web of stable, discoverable sympathies — this plant for that organ, this metal for that planet, this vapour for that body — it is assuming exactly the guarantee the ekpyrosis doctrine supplied: that the web was woven once, rationally, and holds in every weaving. Remove the cycle and the guarantee weakens to a habit. Keep it, and sympathy is not a hope. It is a theorem of the world’s manufacture.

XIII. Fate and Providence — Only as Far as the Physics Needs Them

This section is deliberately brief, and its brevity is a matter of scope, not of embarrassment. Fate (heimarmenē) and providence (pronoia) are, in the full Stoic system, enormous subjects, reaching deep into ethics, responsibility, and the problem of evil. This article takes them only as far as the physics needs them — which is far enough to show that they are, in the first instance, physical doctrines, stated in the vocabulary of breath, tension, and cause that the preceding sections built.

Fate, physically stated, is the connectedness of Section X, viewed along the axis of time. The definitions the testimony preserves are all of one family. Aetius, in paraphrase: fate is a string of causes, an ordering and connection which is inescapable. Aulus Gellius preserves Chrysippus’s own rolling image of it, in paraphrase: fate is a sempiternal and unswerving series and chain of things, rolling and unravelling itself through eternal sequences of cause and effect, of which it is composed and compounded. Diogenes, at 7.149, in paraphrase: fate is a connected cause of the things that are, or the reason according to which the world is administered. And the physical reduction underneath the metaphors is the testimony, noted in Section X, that Chrysippus defined fate as a pneumatic power: the chain of causes is not an abstract graph but a state of the one breath, in which each event is a body acting on a body, and the effect — the school’s analysis makes effects, strictly, incorporeal predicates, things said of bodies — follows with the necessity of a tension transmitted through a plenum that has nowhere else for it to go. Universal causation, universal connectedness, universal breath: three statements of one physics.

Providence, physically stated, is the same chain viewed as the work of a reason that intends the whole’s good. The Stoic God does not intervene in the chain from outside — there is no outside for a bodily, immanent God to intervene from — but is the chain, under its description as designing fire. Cicero’s Stoic spokesman gives providence’s priorities, in On the Nature of the Gods (2.58), in paraphrase: that the cosmos be as well fitted as possible to endure; that it lack nothing; and, most of all, that it possess surpassing beauty and every adornment. The priorities are a craftsman’s, and deliberately unglamorous in their order: durability first, completeness second, beauty crowning both. A providence that is a physics has no miracles to offer, and the school offered none. What it offers instead is Section XI’s divination (a provident whole can be read), Section XII’s recurrence (a perfect craftsman repeats his best work), and the ethical corollary this article is scoped to mention only in passing: that a rational animal’s ruling part can assent to the chain or be dragged by it — the image, preserved in Hippolytus, of the dog tied to the cart, who may follow willingly or be dragged, but follows either way; and Chrysippus’s answer to the lazy argument (if recovery is fated, the doctor is pointless) in the doctrine of the co-fated: the calling of the doctor is itself woven into the chain by which the recovery, if it comes, comes. The cylinder of Section VIII does the remaining work: external causes push, but the thing rolls by its own nature, and the rolling is its own. Fate, in the Stoa, is not the enemy of responsibility. It is the physics responsibility is made of.

// ONE THING, MANY NAMES — AGAIN //

God, logos, designing fire, pneuma, nature, fate, providence, Zeus: Diogenes’ sentence at 7.135 — one thing, called by many names — is the key that keeps this section short. The names are not rivals and not synonyms in the flat sense. Each names the one active principle from the standpoint of one of its works: as reason, as craftsman, as breath, as the world’s self-maintenance, as the chain of causes, as the chain’s benevolence, as its ruler. A reader who keeps the physics in view will never mistake the list for a pantheon, nor the pantheon’s etymologies in Diogenes 7.147 for a mythology. The allegorists’ whole art, Section XV will show, begins by reading the list backwards.

XIV. Disputes and Open Questions: How Literally, Whose System?

The preceding sections have stated the system as the convergent testimony supports it. This section collects, in one place, the places where the testimony, the scholarship, or the philosophy itself remains genuinely unsettled — because a Foundations article that hid them would mis-found everything built on it. Six disputes matter most.

1. How Literally Is Pneuma a Breath?

The school’s own statements run in both directions at once. Pneuma has a recipe — fire and air, in proportions that vary by grade — a temperature, a seat in the chest, a cone it stretches to the seen object, waves it strikes into the air of hearing. That is as literal as ancient physics gets. Yet its defining property, tension, is a state or activity more readily pictured as a string’s tone than as a wind’s blowing, and the functions multiply until the stuff threatens to become whatever the explanation needs. Modern scholarship divides accordingly. One line, descending from Sambursky, emphasises the systematic, quasi-field character: pneuma as the continuous carrier of qualities and states. Another, represented in the recent work read for this article by Hensley’s reduction of many pneumatic functions to the functions of fire and air, emphasises the compound’s literal composition and warns against letting the system’s elegance dissolve the stuff. The position of this article is the double one the testimony supports: the early Stoics meant a body, with a composition — and meant it to do work no mere wind could do, work they described in the language of tension because tension, not blowing, was the operative idea. Readers who meet later, looser uses of “spirit” in alchemical and medical writing should not read the looseness back into the Stoa.

2. Fire First, or Breath First? Zeno, Cleanthes, Chrysippus

The system has three authors, and their emphases differ on the record. Zeno’s active principle speaks the language of fire; Cleanthes is the first Stoic we know to have made tonos, tension, a physical term of art — earlier use in the school’s orbit appears ethical and psychological — and called it, obscurely, a stroke of fire, and placed the world’s ruling part in the sun; Chrysippus, probably, gave pneuma its systematic dominance, formulated total blending, and placed the ruling part in the heaven or aether. Sedley’s judgement, quoted in substance in Section VI — fire’s initial leading role a legacy of Heraclitus mediated by Cleanthes, pneuma’s eventual dominance probably Chrysippus’s trademark — is the current scholarly centre of gravity, and this article follows it, flagged as a judgement and not a datum. The older picture, of a single system delivered complete by its founder, is not what the evidence shows, and Hahm’s Origins — which attributes the synthesis of the physical and biological cosmogonical models to Zeno, with Cleanthes and Chrysippus sustaining the main view while contributing new but minor motifs — differs from Sedley’s distribution of credit in detail. The disagreement is noted here because Wing II’s other articles cite “the Stoics” for doctrines whose patents, on a closer reading, belong to named men in sequence.

3. Is Sambursky’s “Field” an Anachronism?

Sambursky generalised the Stoic continuum, in the highlights read for this article, into a field theory: pneuma as the physical field that is the carrier of all specific properties of material bodies, each body’s hexis the superposition of the pneumatic mixtures corresponding to its qualities. The reading is brilliant, and it taught two generations to see the system’s unity. It is also, as its critics observe, a reading the Stoics could not have given of themselves: they had no mathematics of fields, no notion of a property-space, and a stubbornly corporeal imagination in which the carrier of a quality is always another body, never an abstract assignment of values to points. This article’s practice has been to use Sambursky for structure and to refuse him for self-description: the Stoics anticipated the problems field theories solve — continuity, cohesion, quality, transmission — with a material invention of their own, and the invention is more interesting than the anticipation.

4. Where Did It Come From? Hahm and His Critics

Hahm’s Origins of Stoic Cosmology (1977) remains the major modern answer: corporealism developed out of the post-Melissus, atomist, Platonic, and Aristotelian debate about the nature of real things, its premises taken largely from Plato and Aristotle; the two principles built from Aristotle’s biology of generation and his four causes and god, combined with Plato’s receptacle, demiurge, and world-soul; the cosmogony a synthesis of a physical model (elements derived in sequence, with Presocratic and Platonic antecedents) and a biological model (the cosmos generated as an animal is generated, by seed in seminal fluid). The book’s reviewers — the review literature read for this article is representative — judge the case persuasive in its main lines while disputing distributions of emphasis, and later work, Sedley’s included, has pressed an older, dualist lineage for the god-and-matter pair and a stronger Academic mediation. What no serious account now maintains is the nineteenth-century shortcut that Stoic physics is Heraclitus warmed over. Heraclitus is in the system — the ever-living fire, the measures of kindling and going out — as an honoured ancestor and a source of images, not as the system’s sufficient cause.

5. The Hard Places in the Text

Three textual knots deserve naming. At Diogenes 7.134, the principles are bodies — the doctrine the whole system requires — yet the sentence’s tail, in some renderings, appears to make them formless in a sense that slides towards incorporeal; the scholarship treats the corporeality as secure and the slide as a problem of translation and transmission, and this article has done the same, openly. At 7.137, the elements are called, in one breath, unqualified substance and, in the next, the hot, the cold, the wet, and the dry; a recent philological note devoted to the sentence (Ian Hensley’s, in Classical Philology, 2023, read for this article) shows the puzzle is real and proposes retaining the transmitted text under a new interpretation — the article has reported the doctrine and flagged the knot rather than silently choosing. And at 7.151, the wine-drop’s fate in the sea (Section IX) remains a genuinely disputed limit-case of blending. A Foundations article’s duty at such places is not to win them but to mark them, so that no later article in the hub builds a load-bearing claim on a contested line.

6. The Dissenters Inside the School

Finally, the dissent already scattered through the article, gathered: Boethus denied the world is an animal and denied the conflagration; Zeno of Tarsus suspended judgement on the conflagration; Panaetius asserted the world imperishable and denied divination a certain foundation; Cleanthes and Chrysippus disagreed on the seat of the cosmic ruling part and on the survival of souls. Stoicism, across five centuries, was a research tradition with orthodoxies, not a creed with anathemas, and its physics was the most argued part of it — precisely because, unlike the ethics, it made claims the heavens, the tides, and the mixing bowl could talk back to. The alchemists who borrowed the physics borrowed, whether they knew it or not, a going concern.

XV. Closing the Loop: The Stoic Substrate of Alchemical Sympathy

The loop this article was commissioned to close can now be closed precisely, because every term in it has been given its physics. The claim, stated once more in its exact form, is not that the Greek alchemists were Stoics, nor that alchemy is applied Stoicism. It is that the vocabulary in which Greek alchemy first explained itself — sympathy and antipathy, spirit drawn from body and fixed in body, qualities carried by vapours, natures that act on their kin — is Stoic physics in workshop dress: the same words, doing the same explanatory work, at a smaller scale and over a bench. Three stations mark the handover.

First Station: Bolos of Mendes and the Physika kai Mystika

Bolos of Mendes, the Egyptian-Greek compiler the hub’s canonical chronology places at c. 200 BCE, built the work the whole tradition descends from: the Physika — natural properties, organised as sympathies and antipathies — a catalogue of the hidden affinities and aversions among animals, plants, stones, and substances, of what restores what, counteracts what, attracts and repels what. The full dossier — the two Boloses of the Suda, Columella’s unifying testimony, the works on sympathies, on things made by hand, on farming, on stones — is the business of the companion article on Greek alchemy’s origins, and is not repeated here. What belongs here is the physics of Bolos’s organising idea, and that article’s own Section XVII states the point this article exists to underwrite: sympathy, in Bolos, is genuinely explanatory in intent — offered as the reason recipes work, generative of expectations — and it is continuous with respectable Hellenistic physics, Stoic cosmology above all, which ran on cosmic sympathy, the universe as one animal, its parts in mutual affection. A catalogue of sympathies is a field guide to Section X’s theorem, compiled by a man who needed the theorem at the scale of herbs, venoms, stones, and dyes.

The Physika kai Mystika — the Natural and Secret Things, transmitted under the name of Democritus, Bolos’s adopted authority, and surviving, in the epitomised form in which we have it, as a work of the first century CE, the hub’s canonical dating — is where the catalogue becomes a craft. Its four books, on the making of gold, of silver, of stones, and of purple, work a single maxim the tradition never tired of repeating, in paraphrase: nature delights in nature, nature conquers nature, nature masters nature — like acting on like, the oldest clause in the sympathy physics, promoted to a workshop rule. A treatment is repeated until the sympathetic body has wholly taken the tingeing nature into itself; a substance is chosen because its nature is kin to the nature aimed at; an antipathetic substance is chosen to strip, mortify, or fix. Read with Sections VIII–IX in hand, the procedures are legible as physics: graded pneumata, quality-bearing vapours, total blending pursued by iteration. Read without them, they are legible only as ritual. The recipes do not cite Chrysippus. They do not need to. They presuppose his world.

// THE CHRONOLOGY, KEPT HONEST //

Bolos at c. 200 BCE is Chrysippus’s contemporary, not his pupil, and the sympathy idea is older than both — the tradition itself traced it through Democritus, Xenophanes, Pythagoras, and Socrates, and Hippocratic medicine dosed by sympathy and antipathy before the Stoa existed. The honest claim is therefore not a one-way borrowing, Bolos from the Stoa. It is a shared Hellenistic substrate, which the Stoa systematised into a physics — plenum, pneuma, tension, total blending — and which the Bolos tradition simultaneously compiled into a practice. System and catalogue grew up together, in the same cities, in the same decades, and each is evidence for the world the other describes. Where this article has spoken of a “Stoic substrate”, that contemporaneous, systematising sense is the sense intended — substrate as the articulated theory beneath a practice, not as a book on the compiler’s desk.

Second Station: Zosimos’s Vapours and Spirits

Three centuries after Bolos, at the other end of the tradition the hub treats, Zosimos of Panopolis (writing around 300 CE) speaks the physics fluently and at full strength — the subject of the companion article on Zosimos, whose Sections X–XII give the doctrine in his own textual house. In summary here, following that article’s account: Zosimos’s operative pair is sōma and pneuma, body and spirit; a spirit is what is volatile, the vapour that rises from a heated body; and — the equation on which his whole art turns, in that article’s paraphrase of his text — every sublimed vapour is a pneuma, and so are the tingeing qualities themselves. The art’s two master operations are aposōmatōsis, disembodiment, the drawing of spirit out of body, and episōmatōsis, embodiment, the bonding of spirit within body; its agent-medium is the divine water, which loosens a body’s own spirit and delivers another’s; and its success-state is baphē, tincture — pneuma in transit become baphē at the moment of fixation, when the vapour’s quality has passed wholly into the receiving body and holds there, as colour holds in dyed wool.

Set that paragraph beside Sections VI–IX and the correspondence is not impressionistic; it is term-by-term. Zosimos’s spirit is Stoic pneuma at bench scale: a body, fine and fiery-airy, that leaves one body and enters another. His disembodiment and embodiment are the ladder’s tensions loosened and reset. His tincture is total blending with a result clause — the quality-bearing pneuma wholly through the receiving body, its own quality intact, now the blend’s quality too — pursued by the kerotakis’s circulation of rising and falling vapours, in which, as the Zosimos article’s Mertens reading notes, the dream-visions’ dismembered and reconstituted figures move as the vapours in the apparatus move. Even his prime matter has the family features: black lead, in the account the conversion scholarship gives of his usage, as the unworked body from which no spirit has yet been drawn and into which none has been bonded — unqualified substance, waiting at the bench as it waits, in Section III, at the foundation of the world. Zosimos also read the philosophical Hermetica, and his religious register — the pneumatic self escaping the body fate built, ascending past the powers — is the same physics turned to salvation. The hub keeps the two registers in one article because Zosimos did. The present article’s contribution is only to show that both registers are dialects of one language, and that the language was first grammared in the Stoa.

Third Station: The Allegorists’ Sympathy Vocabulary

The third handover is a method, and the companion article on alchemical allegory is its history. The Stoics, needing Homer — the culture’s scripture — to agree with their physics, perfected allegoresis: the decoding of myth into nature. From Zeno through Cleanthes and Chrysippus to Cornutus, whose Compendium of Greek Theology survives to show the method at work, the Stoics read the gods as compressed physics — Hera as air, Hephaestus as designing fire, the whole pantheon as the one pneuma’s names and powers, exactly as Diogenes 7.147’s etymologies (Section V) display. The alchemists took the method over whole, and turned its key one notch further: where the Stoic decoded a god into a physical principle, the alchemist decoded a hero, a marriage, a fleece, a cauldron, into an operation — and the connective tissue of the decoding, in text after text, is the vocabulary of this article. Natures are kin or hostile; substances sympathise and antipathise; a spirit is drawn out of a body as a god was drawn out of a name. The allegory article’s own treatment of the pseudo-Democritean material shows the workshop texts classifying their substances by kinship of qualities, by sympathies and antipathies — Bolos’s catalogue again, now wearing myth’s clothes. Physics supplied the lexicon; allegory supplied the syntax; the workshop supplied the sentences.

BOLOS — THE CATALOGUE

Sympathies and antipathies compiled as natural properties (Physika, c. 200 BCE); the maxim of nature delighting in, conquering, and mastering nature worked as craft in the Physika kai Mystika (surviving form, 1st century CE). See Greek alchemy’s origins.

ZOSIMOS — THE VAPOURS

Sublimed vapours as pneumata; disembodiment and embodiment; the divine water as carrier; tincture as a spirit’s quality fixed in a body. Stoic physics at the bench, c. 300 CE. See Zosimos of Panopolis.

THE ALLEGORISTS — THE METHOD

Stoic allegoresis, gods decoded into pneuma and its powers, inherited by the alchemists and turned from principles to operations, in a vocabulary of kinship, sympathy, and antipathy. See alchemical allegory.

WHAT THE LOOP EXPLAINS

Why alchemical sympathy is stated as fact, not figure; why a vapour can carry a quality; why a tincture can be total; why the same writer can pass from a still to a salvation without changing his physics. The workshop inherited a world in which those sentences were theorems.

A closing sentence for the Foundations cluster, and for Wing II. The alchemists’ world is often described, from outside, as enchanted — a world of occult sympathies, signatures, and spirits, set against the disenchanted world of mechanism. The physics this article has reconstructed suggests the description is backwards. The Stoic cosmos is not an enchanted machine. It is a body — one animal, breathing one breath, at one interlocking set of tensions, made by one reason from one substance and remade identically for ever — and sympathy, spirit, and tincture are not spells cast upon that body but names for how such a body, being the kind of body it is, cannot help but behave. The alchemists did not enchant the Stoics’ world. They inherited it, moved into its workshop, and got to work. What they made there is the rest of Wing II.
