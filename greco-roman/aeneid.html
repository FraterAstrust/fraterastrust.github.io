VIRGIL'S AENEID

Mantua · Rome · Brundisium · 29–19 BCE

// ARMS AND THE MAN :: PIETAS AGAINST KLEOS :: THE UNFINISHED CHARTER //

The Aeneid is the poem Rome read itself in for two thousand years: twelve books, 9,896 lines of Latin hexameter, in which a refugee from burning Troy carries his father, his son, and his household gods across the sea to found the people from whom Rome will come. It is also, by its author's own reported judgement, a failure: Virgil died at Brundisium in 19 BCE with the poem unrevised, asking that it be burned. This article treats the whole poem — its making, its design, its hero, its tragedy, its charter vision, its violent and disputed ending, and its immense afterlife as the school text of the Latin West.

I. A Poem Rome Read Itself In

Every other treatment of this poem in the archive is an excerpt or an answer. The descent of Book VI is treated whole in Descents to the Underworld, Section XII, where it stands as the Roman summa of the nekyia tradition; Virgil's answering of Homer is sketched in The Homeric Epics, Section XV, from the Greek side. What has been missing is the poem itself, entire: the Aeneid of Publius Vergilius Maro (70–19 BCE), twelve books and 9,896 lines of dactylic hexameter, composed in the last eleven years of the poet's life under the patronage of Maecenas and the direct, impatient interest of Augustus himself. This article supplies that treatment. It is an article about a single Latin poem — its author's life, the strange circumstances of its survival, the legend it inherited, the design it announces in its first lines, the value it puts at its centre, the tragedy at its heart, the vision at its midpoint, the charter it carries on a shield, the killing it ends on, and the two millennia in which it stood, after Homer, as the model of what an epic is.

Three cautions govern everything that follows, and they are the archive's standing discipline applied to this poem in particular. First: the biographical tradition about Virgil descends chiefly through the Life transmitted under the name of Aelius Donatus, the fourth-century grammarian, which draws on Suetonius' lost On Poets of the second century CE. It is our fullest source and it is not a contemporary one. Where this article reports the deathbed request, the fainting empress's sister, or the poet's working habits, it reports the tradition, and says so: these stories may preserve fact, and they certainly preserve what the ancient world found it necessary to believe about its greatest poet. Second: the Aeneid is a literary construction built on older legendary strata, Greek and Roman, which it freely rearranges — most famously in the meeting of Aeneas and Dido, which the older chronology makes impossible (Section IX). Literary myth is not cult practice and is not history, and the poem is not evidence for what Romans did in their rites; for Roman religion as practised, see Roman Religion. Third: the poem has been read, since antiquity, as propaganda, as tragedy, and as both at once. The modern form of that argument — the “two voices” debate — is a scholarly dispute of the twentieth century, and it is presented in Section XII as exactly that: named positions, held by named scholars, about a text that has never compelled a single reading.

A word on the name. The poet's third name is spelled Vergilius in Latin and in the scholarly tradition; the familiar English “Virgil” descends from the medieval spelling Virgilius, itself influenced by virga, a wand — one root of the medieval legend of Virgil the magician (Section XIII). This article uses “Virgil” for the man, as English usage does, and Vergilius where the manuscripts themselves are the subject. The hero's name needs no such note: Aeneas, son of Anchises and Venus, Trojan prince, refugee, founder — the man of whom the poem's first word after its theme is not a name but a burden: virum, “the man”.

II. The Poet: Virgil's Life, 70–19 BCE

What can be said of Virgil's life divides, as ancient literary lives always do, into a skeleton of fact that the poems and the historical record support, and a flesh of anecdote that the Life transmitted by Donatus supplies and that no contemporary document checks. The skeleton first. Publius Vergilius Maro was born, by the tradition's consistent account, on 15 October 70 BCE at Andes, a village near Mantua in Cisalpine Gaul — the Po valley of northern Italy, a region Roman by law only within living memory and Celtic and Etruscan in its older strata. His family were small landowners of no distinction; the Life's embroidery — his father a potter or a potter's hired man who married the employer's daughter and rose by buying woodland and keeping bees — is the kind of detail ancient biography manufactures for poets, and is reported here as the tradition's colour, not as record. He was educated at Cremona and Milan, then at Rome, in rhetoric and philosophy; the Life says he pleaded one case in court, found his delivery unequal to the forum, and never tried again. The shy, slow-spoken provincial who failed at the one profession Rome honoured above poetry is, true or not, the portrait the whole tradition keeps: the epic of empire was written by a man who could not make himself heard in a courtroom.

The historical frame of the life is the collapse of the Roman Republic, and the poems are unintelligible without it. Virgil was twenty-six when Julius Caesar crossed the Rubicon; the triumviral land confiscations of 41 BCE, which seized Mantuan territory for distribution to veterans, stand behind the Eclogues (c. 42–39 BCE), the ten pastoral poems with which he entered the circle of Maecenas, Augustus' minister of culture. The Life's story that Virgil's own family farm was among the confiscated lands, and was restored by the young Octavian's favour after an appeal at Rome, is again tradition rather than document — but the Eclogues themselves, with their dispossessed shepherds and their godlike young benefactor, are the primary evidence that the confiscations touched the poet's world directly, and the first Eclogue's grateful Meliboeus-and-Tityrus dialogue is the sound of a poet learning that his safety now depended on one man's patronage. The Georgics, four books on the farming life, followed (published 29 BCE), written at Maecenas' urging and read, the Life says, to Octavian at Atella over four days as he returned from the East after Actium. Then, for the remaining eleven years of his life, the Aeneid.

70 BCE

BIRTH AT ANDES

Born 15 October, by the tradition's account, at Andes near Mantua in Cisalpine Gaul, to a family of small landowners. Educated at Cremona, Milan, and Rome.

c. 42–39 BCE

THE ECLOGUES

Ten pastoral poems, written against the background of the triumviral land confiscations; the poet enters the circle of Maecenas. The fourth Eclogue's child will matter enormously to the poem's afterlife (Section XIII).

29 BCE

THE GEORGICS; THE AENEID BEGUN

The Georgics published and read to Octavian at Atella, per the Life. Virgil begins the Aeneid — first, the Life says, as a prose draft of all twelve books, then versifying passage by passage in no fixed order.

26–25 BCE

AUGUSTUS ASKS FOR THE POEM

The Life preserves the tradition that Augustus, on campaign in Spain, wrote repeatedly — pleading, then jokingly threatening — for a draft or specimen of the poem in progress. The emperor was the poem's first impatient reader.

23 BCE

THE READING; MARCELLUS DIES

Virgil reads Books II, IV, and VI to Augustus and his sister Octavia, per the Life. At the lines on Octavia's son Marcellus — dead that year at nineteen — the tradition says she fainted. The passage stands in the poem still (6.860–886).

19 BCE

DEATH AT BRUNDISIUM

Virgil sails for Greece, intending three years of revision there and in Asia, then a life given to philosophy. Meeting Augustus at Athens, he turns back; falls ill at Megara; dies at Brundisium on 21 September, aged fifty. The poem is unrevised. What happened next is Section III.

Two features of the Life's working portrait deserve record because they explain the physical state of the poem (Sections III–IV). The first is the method: the Aeneid was drafted entire in prose, then versified out of order, passage by passage, as the poet's inclination took him — and where a passage was not yet ready, Virgil left what the Life calls props: light temporary verses, inserted to hold the structure up, in his own joke, “until the solid columns arrived”. The second is the pace: the Life says he composed as a bear licks her cubs into shape — dictating many lines in the morning and spending the day reducing them to very few. A poet who works like that, and dies mid-revision, leaves exactly the kind of text the Aeneid is: architecturally complete, locally unfinished, its scaffolding still visible in the walls. The epitaph the tradition gives him — that Mantua bore him, Calabria snatched him away, and Naples holds him now; that he sang of pastures, farms, and leaders — is the Life's, and is quoted here in paraphrase as the tradition's summary of the three works: Eclogues, Georgics, Aeneid.

III. The Unfinished Poem: The Deathbed, the Fire, and the Editors

The story of the poem's survival is the most famous deathbed scene in literary history, and it must be told the way the archive tells all such stories: as the tradition reports it, labelled as the tradition. The source is the Life of Virgil transmitted by Donatus and descending, in the judgement of most scholarship, from Suetonius' On Poets — that is, from a biographer writing well over a century after the death, in a genre that prized the significant anecdote over the verifiable one. The various ancient accounts of Virgil's instructions for the poem are, moreover, not consistent with one another. What follows is the tradition's account. Its outline is corroborated at one point only — the poem itself, which is demonstrably unfinished (Section IV).

Virgil, the Life says, had intended to spend three years in Greece and Asia revising the Aeneid, and then to give the rest of his life to philosophy. Before leaving Italy he had asked his friend and fellow poet Lucius Varius Rufus to burn the poem if anything should happen to him; Varius had refused. At Athens Virgil met Augustus, returning from the East, and was persuaded to sail home with him. Visiting Megara under a fierce sun, he fell ill; the illness worsened on the voyage, and he died at Brundisium days after landing, on 21 September 19 BCE. Dying, the Life says, he called repeatedly for his scroll-cases, meaning to burn the Aeneid himself; no one brought them; and so the poem survived its author by the simple refusal of everyone around his deathbed to obey him. Augustus then countermanded the poet's wish outright and ordered the poem published. The editors were Varius and Plotius Tucca, both poets of Virgil's circle. Their commission, in the Life's famous formulation, was to publish the text with the lightest possible correction — to remove what was superfluous, in the tradition's phrase as standardly reported, and to add nothing: the half-lines were to stand exactly as the poet had left them.

// HOW MUCH OF THIS IS FACT? //

The honest answer is: the death at Brundisium in 19 BCE and the poem's unfinished state are as certain as anything in Virgil's biography; the burning story is tradition. Suetonius, the ultimate source, wrote more than a century later; the surviving accounts of the will's terms differ among themselves; and the tale has the shape ancient biography loved — the masterpiece saved from its own maker's perfectionism by the ruler it glorified. Modern scholars have doubted nearly every element of it while agreeing that something like an imperially ordered, minimally edited posthumous publication must stand behind the text we have, because the text we have is precisely what such a publication would produce: a poem nobody finished and nobody dared to finish. The story is told in this article as the tradition tells it, and the physical evidence — the half-lines of Section IV — is allowed to carry the weight the anecdote cannot.

One editorial act the tradition does attribute to the posthumous editors deserves separate notice, because it touches the poem's first lines. The Aeneid as we have it opens arma virumque cano — “arms and the man I sing”. But Servius, the great late-antique commentator, reports that the poem originally opened with four other lines, beginning ille ego qui quondam — “I am he who once” — in which the poet surveyed his earlier works, pastures and farms, before turning to arms; and that Varius removed them. The lines survive in the biographical and commentary tradition, not in the poem's manuscripts. Modern editors are divided: the four lines may be Virgil's discarded opening, or a later fabrication stitched from his own phrases (the Georgics open their fourth book with a promise of Maecenas' “commands” in similar words). The judgement matters less than the fact it illustrates: the poem reached its readers already edited, by named men working under orders, and every reader since antiquity has read the Aeneid in the form Varius and Tucca released. There is no unedited Aeneid behind theirs to recover. There is only their restraint — for which, if the tradition is even half right, the poem's jagged, living unfinishedness is the monument.

IV. The Half-Lines: Incompletion You Can See

Most unfinished works are declared unfinished by their catalogues. The Aeneid shows it, line by line, in the metre itself. A dactylic hexameter has six feet and a fixed architecture; a line that stops after two or three feet is as visibly incomplete to a reader of Latin verse as a sentence ending mid-word is to a reader of English. The Aeneid contains fifty-eight such half-lines in the standard modern count — verses that break off before the hexameter completes, most of them syntactically suspended, waiting for the continuation the poet never supplied. They are distributed through all twelve books. They are the physical evidence of incompletion, independent of every anecdote in Section III: whatever one thinks of the deathbed story, the poem's own body testifies that its author died with the revision unfinished, and that his editors kept their orders and left the gaps standing.

The Life explains the mechanism (Section II): drafting out of order, Virgil propped unfinished passages with temporary material and left places where the verse itself was still to come. The half-lines are those places. A few have become famous in their own right. In Book I, Aeneas, recounting his wanderings to Dido's court in prospect, breaks off with hic cursus fuit — “thither lay our course” — a half-line whose sense is complete though its metre is not. In Book IV, Aeneas' cold excuse to the queen he is abandoning ends Italiam non sponte sequor — “it is not of my own will that I pursue Italy” — a broken verse at the poem's most broken moment, which readers have been tempted ever since to call deliberate. In Book III a half-line breaks a sentence in the middle outright: quem tibi iam Troia — “whom now Troy to you…” — and stops, the grammar hanging in the air; the ancient Life itself singles this one out. The glosses given here are plain glosses of the Latin, offered as such.

THE TEMPTATION: EFFECT

Some half-lines fall at moments of such emotional pressure — Aeneas' excuse to Dido above all — that readers from antiquity onward have suspected design: the verse breaks because speech breaks. John Sparrow's Half-Lines and Repetitions in Virgil (1931) remains the standard study of the question.

THE DISCIPLINE: EVIDENCE

Against the temptation stands the distribution: half-lines occur in flat narrative and in catalogue matter where no expressive effect is plausible, and several leave their sentences grammatically incomplete, which no poet designs. The scholarly consensus treats them as what the Life says they are — placeholders in an unrevised draft — while granting that Virgil's readers have made poetry of them anyway.

REPETITIONS AND SEAMS

The half-lines are not the only seams. The poem contains repeated whole lines in contexts where revision would likely have varied them, and a handful of narrative inconsistencies ancient critics already catalogued — most famously Helen's presence in Book II, in a passage (2.567–588) absent from the earliest manuscripts and reported by Servius to have been removed by the editors themselves. The passage is bracketed or relegated to the apparatus in modern editions; its status is a genuine editorial crux, presented here as one.

WHAT WAS NOT LEFT UNDONE

The architecture is complete: twelve books, the halves balanced, the proem's programme fulfilled, the ending's scene in place. What death interrupted was the finish, not the structure — the polish of a poet who reduced a morning's dictation to a day's few lines, working on a poem of nearly ten thousand. The Aeneid is not a fragment. It is a finished building with the scaffolding still up.

The half-lines matter beyond biography. They are the reason the Aeneid's text is a special case among classical poems: its unfinished state is authorial, documented in the verse, and protected by the editors' restraint — so that the modern editor (Section XIV) does not complete them, and the modern reader reads a poem that carries its author's death inside its metre. Every other great epic of antiquity reached us as its tradition smoothed it. This one reached us with the work stopped mid-gesture, fifty-eight times.

V. Aeneas Before Virgil: The Legend in Brief

Virgil invented almost nothing of his story's outline, and the poem's originality — which is immense — lies elsewhere than in invention. The strata beneath him are treated in full in this hub's Roman Founding Legends, and the war from which his hero flees is told in The Trojan Cycle; what belongs here is the brief map of what the poet inherited, so that what he changed can be seen.

The Greek stratum. In Homer's Iliad, Aeneas is a secondary Trojan prince — son of Anchises and Aphrodite, rescued in battle by gods more than once, and the object of the poem's one prophecy of a Trojan future: Poseidon, saving him from Achilles in Book XX, declares that Aeneas and his descendants shall rule the Trojans thereafter. The Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite — treated in this hub's Homeric Hymns — tells how Aphrodite, shamed and exultant, names the unborn child Aeneas and promises Anchises that his line shall rule the Trojans, generation upon generation: the divine charter of the house, already fixed in archaic Greek verse. What Homer and the Hymn promise is rule over the Trojans, at Troy. The westward journey, Italy, and Rome are not in them.

The westward turn. The migration of Aeneas to the West is a construction of the fifth century BCE and after: Greek historians such as Hellanicus of Lesbos already brought Aeneas to Italy, and the legend grew in the telling of Sicilian and Italian Greeks for whom Aeneas' wanderings explained Trojan and Elymian settlements in the West. By the third century BCE Rome's own first historians — Fabius Pictor, and Cato in his Origines — had taken the story up: Aeneas came to Latium, married Lavinia, daughter of King Latinus, founded Lavinium; his son Ascanius (also called Iulus) founded Alba Longa; and from Alba's kings, in a genealogy smoothed over centuries of gap, descended Romulus. The fullest surviving pre-Virgilian telling is the first book of Dionysius of Halicarnassus' Roman Antiquities, written in Virgil's own lifetime; Livy's first chapters give the spare annalistic version. Aeneas carrying his aged father Anchises out of the fire, leading his small son by the hand, is already the legend's emblematic image in art before Virgil — it appears in Greek and Etruscan representations centuries older than the poem.

What Virgil did with it can therefore be stated precisely, and it is not invention of plot. He took a legend whose episodes the tradition had scattered across five centuries of historians, annalists, and local aetiologies, and he compressed it into one man's burden: the wanderings, the landing, the marriage, the war — all of it re-motivated by a single divine machinery (Juno's wrath, Venus' care, Jupiter's fate) and a single human quality, pietas, that the tradition had attached to Aeneas as an epithet and that Virgil made the engine of the whole (Section VIII). He also committed the tradition's boldest act of compression in the Dido episode, collapsing some three and a half centuries of the legend's own chronology to bring two founders face to face (Section IX). And he connected the legend, explicitly and repeatedly, to a living family: Iulus, the son, is the claimed ancestor of the gens Iulia — of Julius Caesar, and so of Augustus. The poem's mythic past terminates, by design, in the principate's present. How much of that connection Romans of Virgil's generation already believed, and how much the poem itself fixed into canonical form, is a question the sources do not let us answer cleanly; the detail is in Roman Founding Legends, and the cultic framework into which such claims fitted is in Roman Religion.

VI. The Design: Twelve Books, Two Homers

The Aeneid's design is the most deliberate in ancient epic, and it announces itself structurally before it announces itself in words. Twelve books divide into two halves of six, and the halves answer the two Homeric poems in turn — the correspondence was already the ancient critics' commonplace, and it remains the soundest first map of the poem. The first half (Books I–VI) is the Odyssean half: a sea-wanderer's story — storm, shipwreck, strange coasts, a royal host's hall, the hero's own narration of his past sufferings in Books II–III (as Odysseus narrates his among the Phaeacians), funeral games in Book V (as in Iliad XXIII, but placed here), and a descent to the dead in Book VI answering the Odyssey's nekyia and exceeding it (Section X). The second half (Books VII–XII) is the Iliadic half: the hero landed, a war for the land itself — catalogues of the Italian peoples answering the Iliad's Catalogue of Ships, single combats, a night raid, a truce broken, a beloved young companion's death avenged, and a final duel between the two champions on which everything turns. Virgil marks the hinge himself: Book VII opens with a second invocation, to Erato, in which the poet declares — paraphrased — that he will tell of kings and battles, and that a greater work now opens before him: maius opus moveo, “a greater task I set in motion”, the gloss being this article's.

The two-halves scheme is a map, not a cage, and the poem's art lives in its violations. The Odyssean half contains the Iliad's fall-of-Troy matter that Homer excluded; the Iliadic half opens with a river-journey and a pastoral locus — Evander's humble Pallanteum, on the site where Rome will stand, shown to Aeneas by a Greek settler-king who keeps the rite of Hercules at the Ara Maxima — that belongs to no battle poem. And the halves are bound by cross-reference at every joint: the Dido of Book IV reappears, silent, among the dead of Book VI; the shield of Book VIII answers the parade of Book VI; the boy Pallas, entrusted to Aeneas in Book VIII, dies in Book X wearing the belt that will decide the poem's last act in Book XII. Virgil's relation to Homer, treated from the Greek side in The Homeric Epics, Section XV, is the most studied imitation in Western literature — scene answering scene, line answering line, in a Latin poem that expects a reader who knows both texts and measures the differences. The differences are the meaning: where Homer narrates a world that is ending, Virgil narrates one that is beginning, and his hero's task is not to win glory in the wreck but to carry the wreck's sacred furniture into the future.

VII. The Proem as Programme

The poem opens with eleven lines that contain, in germ, every subject of this article. The Latin may be quoted as Latin; the English is Fairclough's Loeb translation, consulted for this article and quoted here in full for the proem alone.

Arma virumque cano, Troiae qui primus ab oris
Italiam, fato profugus, Laviniaque venit
litora, multum ille et terris iactatus et alto
vi superum, saevae memorem Iunonis ob iram,
multa quoque et bello passus, dum conderet urbem
inferretque deos Latio, genus unde Latinum
Albanique patres atque altae moenia Romae. — VIRGIL, AENEID 1.1–7 (LATIN)
“Arms and the man I sing, who first from the coasts of Troy, exiled by fate, came to Italy and Lavine shores; much buffeted on sea and land by violence from above, through cruel Juno's unforgiving wrath, and much enduring in war also, till he should build a city and bring his gods to Latium; whence came the Latin race, the lords of Alba, and the lofty walls of Rome.” — VIRGIL, AENEID 1.1–7, TRANS. H. R. FAIRCLOUGH (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY, 1916)

The first two words are the design of Section VI in miniature. Arma, arms, is the Iliad's matter — war; virum, the man, is the Odyssey's first word and matter — the man of many turns, defined by wandering. Virgil fuses the two poems' subjects into a single object of his verb cano, I sing, and then runs the whole story in one sentence: exile by fate, buffeting by sea and land, endurance in war, and the telos — not homecoming, as in the Odyssey, but foundation: a city built, gods carried in, and from that act the Latin race, Alba's lords, Rome's walls. The proem's geography is a genealogy: Troy, Lavinium, Alba, Rome, each name the seed of the next. And its theology is stated as a tension that the poem never resolves cheaply: the man is driven by fatum, fate, and opposed by a god's ira, wrath — Juno's, which is “mindful”, memorem, unforgetting, the precise opposite of the letting-go the poem's ending will require of its hero.

Then the invocation — and it is not Homer's. Homer asks the goddess to sing, the Muse to tell; the singer is a vessel. Virgil sings in his own person for seven lines and only then turns to the Muse with a question no Homeric proem asks:

Musa, mihi causas memora, quo numine laeso
quidve dolens regina deum tot volvere casus
insignem pietate virum, tot adire labores
impulerit. Tantaene animis caelestibus irae? — VIRGIL, AENEID 1.8–11 (LATIN)
“Tell me, O Muse, the cause; wherein thwarted in will or wherefore angered, did the Queen of heaven drive a man, of goodness so wondrous, to traverse so many perils, to face so many toils. Can heavenly spirits cherish resentment so dire?” — VIRGIL, AENEID 1.8–11, TRANS. H. R. FAIRCLOUGH (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY, 1916)

Fairclough's “of goodness so wondrous” renders insignem pietate — “distinguished by pietas” — and the softening is itself a small lesson in translation: the poem's key term (Section VIII) is already present in its eighth line, naming the hero not by strength, cunning, or wrath but by his defining obligation. The question the Muse is asked is aetiological and moral at once: causas, the causes — why did Juno do this? — and the proem ends not with an answer but with the poet's own astonished question, whether anger so great can live in heavenly minds. The whole poem is the working-out of that question, and it is worth noticing that the poem does not end with the anger of heaven. It ends with the anger of the hero (Section XII). The proem's programme — arms, the man, fate, wrath, foundation — is fulfilled to the letter. Whether its fulfilment is a triumph is the question Section XII leaves, deliberately, where the scholarship leaves it: open.

VIII. Pietas Against Kleos: A New Kind of Hero

Homeric heroism runs on kleos — glory, the fame of a man's deeds sung after him, the one compensation the Iliad offers for a short life. Achilles chooses kleos over homecoming with open eyes; Hector fights knowing the city will fall, so that his name will not perish unremembered; Odysseus, the great survivor, is kleos' cunning form, a man whose name and story are his weapon and his prize. The economy is individual: the hero's excellence is his own, its reward is his own name, and its arena is the present generation, even when the song outlives it. The Aeneid keeps the machinery — there are aristeiai, duels, funeral games, and a hero who kills in fury — but it replaces the engine. Aeneas' defining epithet, from the proem's eighth line onward, is pius: the man of pietas.

Pietas is not “piety” in the modern devotional sense, and the difference is the poem. It is the Roman virtue of discharged obligation — to the gods, to one's father and family, to one's dead, to one's country and its future — a duty owed simultaneously upward, backward, and forward in time. The pius man is not the man who feels devotion but the man who carries the load: and Virgil gives the abstraction a single unforgettable image in Book II, in the sack of Troy, when Aeneas lifts his aged father Anchises onto his shoulders, takes his small son Ascanius by the hand, and orders his wife Creusa to follow behind, while the household gods — the penates, Troy's sacred objects, entrusted to him by Hector's ghost and by the priest Panthus — are carried out of the fire in his father's hands, since Aeneas' own hands, fresh from killing, may not touch them until washed. The image is the poem's thesis in one tableau: the hero as bearer. Behind him, the father and the gods of the past; beside him, the child and the future; the man himself is the structure that carries both. Creusa, following behind, is lost in the streets — the first of the poem's many prices, paid by someone else, for the hero's obedience to his task.

KLEOS: THE HOMERIC ECONOMY

The hero's excellence is his own; its arena is his own lifetime; its reward is his name in song. Achilles' choice — a short life with glory against a long one without — is the economy's clearest statement, and the Iliad honours it even while counting its cost.

PIETAS: THE VIRGILIAN ECONOMY

The hero's excellence is discharged obligation — to gods, father, son, dead, and unborn. Its arena is generations: the reward is not Aeneas' name but Rome, which he will never see. Aeneas is promised a few years of rule in Latium and an early death; the glory is entirely posthumous and entirely collective.

THE COST ACCOUNTING

Pietas, in this poem, is never free, and the poem keeps the ledger openly: Creusa lost in the flight, Dido on her pyre (Section IX), Nisus and Euryalus, Pallas, Lausus, and Camilla among the young dead of the Italian war, Anchises himself dying in Sicily before the promised land. The hero's obedience is the engine of the plot and of its casualties alike.

FUROR: THE COUNTER-PRINCIPLE

Against pietas the poem sets furor — rage, madness, the force that possesses Juno, Allecto, the young Turnus, and, at the poem's crisis-points, Aeneas himself: in the sack of Troy he must be restrained by a vision of Venus from killing Helen; at the poem's end, fury is what he acts in. The poem's moral physics is the war of these two forces in one man.

This is why Aeneas has struck so many readers, from antiquity onward, as a colder hero than Achilles or Odysseus — and why the coldness is the design and not a failure of it. Aeneas is the first epic hero whose greatness consists in subordination: he is repeatedly shown wanting something the mission forbids — rest in Carthage, despair in the storm of Book I, where he envies the men who died at Troy — and repeatedly surrendering the want. In the storm he cries out, paraphrased, that the men who fell before their fathers' eyes under Troy's walls were three and four times blessed; then he beaches the ships, feeds his men, and speaks hope he does not feel, hiding his anguish, in the poem's famous psychology of command. He is a hero for an age that had watched a century of charismatic warlords destroy a republic: Rome's founding man is not the most glorious warrior available but the man who can be trusted with a burden — who will carry the gods out of the fire and keep carrying them, at whatever personal price, until the city they are destined for can be founded by his descendants. The Odysseus of the Greek poem fights his way home to his own house. Aeneas has no house. He is carrying it, and everyone in it, on his back.

IX. Dido: The Tragic Construction

Book IV is the most famous book of the Aeneid, the one ancient readers wept over, the one Virgil chose — with II and VI — for his reading before Augustus, and the one that has no Homeric model. It is also, stated plainly at the outset as this article's discipline requires, a chronological impossibility within the legend's own older strata. Aeneas belongs to the generation of Troy's fall, which the Greek chronological tradition placed in 1184 BCE (Eratosthenes' date). Dido — Elissa of Tyre — belongs to the foundation of Carthage, which the historian Timaeus of Tauromenium, our earliest source for her story, placed in 814 or 813 BCE. Between the Trojan refugee and the Tyrian foundress stand some three hundred and seventy years. In the older strata they do not meet, because they cannot: the fullest surviving pre-Virgilian account of Dido — Pompeius Trogus' history, preserved in Justin's epitome — tells her whole story, the flight from her brother Pygmalion, the murder of her husband, the ox-hide stratagem by which she bought Carthage's site, her death on a pyre to escape a forced marriage to the Moorish king Iarbas, with no Aeneas in it at all. Her pyre, in that account, is a monument of fidelity to her dead husband and of refusal — the opposite pole from Virgil's.

Who first brought the two founders together cannot be said with certainty. The usual candidate is Naevius, the third-century BCE Roman epic poet whose Bellum Punicum, on the First Punic War, included Aeneas' wanderings in its retrospective frame: a fragment of Naevius survives that may belong to a conversation between Aeneas and Dido, and Servius' commentary shows that the conjunction was known to the tradition before or beside Virgil — Servius also cites Varro for a version in which it was Dido's sister Anna, not Dido herself, who died for love of Aeneas. The honest statement is the hedged one: the meeting is a Roman literary construction, possibly Naevian, certainly pre-Virgilian in some form, which Virgil took up and made canonical. What is wholly Virgil's is the tragedy: the Dido of Book IV is his construction in the way Medea is Euripides' — built from inherited material into a figure the inheritance did not contain.

The Construction, Book by Book

Dido enters in Book I as a founder in mid-foundation: Aeneas, invisible in Venus' mist, watches Carthage rising — walls, citadel, theatre, harbour — and watches its queen come to the temple of Juno like Diana among her nymphs, a simile that makes her a virgin goddess of the wild at the very moment the plot begins to undo her. She is a widow: her husband Sychaeus was murdered by her brother; she has sworn fidelity to his ashes; she is, when Aeneas arrives, what he is — an exile who carried her people's gods out of a doomed city and is building them a home. The poem insists on the symmetry. She is Aeneas' mirror: the founder who succeeds by stopping, as he will succeed by going. At the banquet she asks him for his story — Books II and III are told to her — and by Book IV's opening she is, in the poem's pathology of love, wounded: she feeds the wound with her lifeblood, in Fairclough's image as commonly cited, and burns with a fire no one sees. The machinery is divine and is presented as machinery: Venus, fearing Juno's city and Juno's queen, sends Cupid in Ascanius' shape to infect Dido with love at her own feast; Juno, hoping to detain Aeneas in Africa forever, proposes to Venus a marriage; the storm drives the hunting party into a cave, and what happens there the poem calls, through Dido's own conscience, a marriage — coniugium vocat, she calls it marriage, and with that name, the poem says, she covered her fault. Whether a marriage occurred is a question the poem deliberately leaves between the gods' stage management, Dido's naming, and Aeneas' later denial that he ever entered a marriage pact. The ambiguity is not a gap in the poem. It is the poem's point about how such things happen.

The end is engineered by the mission itself. Jupiter, reminded by Iarbas' complaint, sends Mercury: the man who is to found the Roman race is building another woman's city. Aeneas obeys — in secret, ordering the fleet prepared silently, in the cowardice of a good man who cannot face the conversation — and is caught, and faces it. His defence is the proem's programme spoken as an excuse: he never claimed the marriage; his own kingdom is Italy; Italiam non sponte sequor, the half-line of Section IV — not of my own will do I pursue Italy. Dido's speeches in reply are the poem's summit of rhetoric: plea, reproach, contempt, and finally the curse, in which the private catastrophe is converted into history. She prays — paraphrased — for an avenger to rise from her bones, exoriare aliquis nostris ex ossibus ultor, and for her people and his to be enemies shore against shore, wave against wave, arms against arms, in their descendants forever. The Roman reader knew the avenger's name: Hannibal. The Punic Wars are aetiologised in a dying woman's curse, and Carthage's destruction in 146 BCE is written backward into its foundress' broken heart. Then the pyre, built as if for a rite to burn away her love, on which she falls upon Aeneas' sword — a gift she had given him — and dies slowly, while Juno, in the poem's bleakest act of mercy, sends Iris to cut the lock of hair that binds her soul, since she dies neither by fate nor by desert but before her day. Aeneas, looking back from his ship, sees the flames on the walls and does not know their cause. He will meet her once more, among the dead (Section X), and she will not speak to him.

// READING BOOK IV HONESTLY //

Read it as what it is: a tragedy Virgil constructed by collapsing his sources' chronology, and one whose power depends on the reader holding both truths at once — that Dido is the poem's most fully realised human being, and that the poem's design requires her destruction. The ancient world felt no strain in admiring both the mission and the queen; Augustine confesses in his Confessions that as a schoolboy he wept for Dido, dead for love of Aeneas, and counts the tears among his sins — which is to say that the school text of Section XIII taught its readers to grieve for the woman its hero abandoned, and that the grief, not the doctrine, is what the schoolboy remembered. The “two voices” of Section XII are heard in Book IV first, and loudest.

X. Book VI: The Descent as Charter Vision

This article gives Book VI one section, by design. The descent itself — the Sibyl of Cumae and her cave, the golden bough, the unburied Palinurus, Charon and Cerberus, the fields of mourning where Dido turns away in silence to Sychaeus, Tartarus heard from its threshold, Elysium, the river Lethe and the souls massed like bees for rebirth — receives its full treatment in this hub's Descents to the Underworld, Section XII, where the book is set line by line within the whole nekyia tradition from Odyssey XI forward, and the reader is directed there for the geography, the ritual footing, and the dead. What belongs to the present article is the one thing Book VI is in the Aeneid's design that it is in no other descent: a charter vision. Odysseus goes down to learn how to get home. Aeneas goes down to be shown what home is for — and is shown the future.

The structural placement is the argument. Book VI stands at the hinge of the two halves (Section VI): the last book of the wanderings, the threshold of the war. Aeneas has buried his nurse's namesake landfall, performed his father's funeral games, lost his helmsman; he lands in Italy at Cumae and his first act on Italian soil is to go underground. There Anchises — dead in Sicily in Book III, met now in Elysium — explains the cosmos to his son (the world-soul, the purgation of souls, the wheel of rebirth: philosophy the poem puts in a father's mouth, on which see this hub's Wing II treatments of the traditions it draws on) and then does the thing no Homeric ghost does: he points. Along the banks of Lethe stand the souls of Romans not yet born, and Anchises conducts his son past them in review — the parade of heroes, treated with the shield in Section XI, where its charter function is analysed. Aeneas returns to the upper world through the gate of ivory — the gate of false dreams, in Homer's distinction, which Virgil adopts and applies, scandalously and deliberately, to his hero's exit. Why the bearer of Rome's true future leaves by the gate of deceptive dreams is one of the poem's celebrated cruxes; commentators from Servius onward have proposed solutions — chronological, symbolic, textual — and the honest report is that none has carried the field. The crux is registered here, as the scholarship registers it: unsolved, and possibly meant to be felt rather than solved, at the exact midpoint where the poem's vision of glory is at its most splendid.

One further point belongs here, because it is the descent's human price and the poem does not hide it. Aeneas meets Dido among the mourning dead, and speaks — paraphrased — in extenuation: unwilling was he, he swears it by the gods, to leave her shore; the commands of heaven drove him then as they drive him now. She does not answer. She looks at the ground, turns, and flees into the shadowy wood to Sychaeus, as Ajax in Odyssey XI turns from Odysseus in silence — the Homeric model inverted, for there the silent one is the wronged warrior and here it is the wronged queen, and the speech of excuse is given to the hero himself. Aeneas follows her with tears and pity, the poem says, and goes on to meet his father and receive the vision of Rome. The sequence is the poem in miniature: the apology made, the dead unanswering, the mission resumed. The full nekyia, again, is in Descents to the Underworld, Section XII; nothing in this section should be read as duplicating it.

XI. The Shield and the Parade: The Poem as Foundation Charter

Twice, in its second half's approach, the poem stops the action and displays the whole of Roman history as a pageant: once in prophecy, in the underworld of Book VI, and once in metal, on the shield Vulcan makes for Aeneas in Book VIII. Together they are the poem's charter — the instruments by which a refugee's errand is certified as the founding act of an empire — and they are the passages on which every reading of the poem, Augustan or pessimist, ultimately stands or falls (Section XII).

The Parade of Heroes (Book VI)

Anchises' review of the unborn (6.756–886) proceeds, in paraphrase, from the Alban kings through Romulus — singled out, under whose auspices Rome will extend her empire to the ends of the earth — to the kings and the Republic's great names: Brutus the avenger, the Decii, the Drusi, Torquatus, Camillus, the Gracchi, the Scipios, Fabricius, Cato, and then the two great figures of the poem's own century. Julius Caesar and Pompey appear together, as father-in-law and son-in-law, and Anchises begs them in advance — paraphrased — not to turn their strength against the vitals of their country: the civil wars are in the parade, mourned inside the triumph. Then Augustus himself, named and exalted: Augustus Caesar, divi genus — “Augustus Caesar, offspring of a god”, the gloss being this article's — who will found again a golden age in the fields where Saturn once ruled, and extend empire beyond the Garamantes and the Indians, to lands beyond the stars. Anchises caps the review with the poem's most quoted sentence, Rome's mission statement, set against the arts of Greece: others — Greeks — will cast bronze more delicately, draw living faces from marble, plead cases better, map the heavens; the Roman's arts are these — parcere subiectis et debellare superbos (6.853): to spare the subjected and to war down the proud, to impose the habit of peace. The Latin is quoted; the gloss is this article's. And then the parade ends — not with Augustus but with a funeral. Last in the line stands Marcellus, Octavia's son, Augustus' nephew and heir, dead at nineteen in 23 BCE, the year of the reading at which the Life says his mother fainted (Section II): Anchises breaks off, calls for lilies by the handful, and the vision of Rome's future closes in a father's lament for a boy who will not grow up to inherit it. The charter and the grief are on the same page, in the same speech, by the same poet. Any reading of the poem that keeps only one of them has abridged Virgil.

The Shield of Aeneas (Book VIII)

The shield answers Homer's most famous ecphrasis and reverses its direction. Hephaestus' shield for Achilles, in Iliad XVIII, displays the world in its eternal present: cities at peace and war, harvest, vintage, dance — a cosmos, timeless and anonymous. Vulcan's shield for Aeneas (8.626–731) displays history, in sequence, and Roman: the wolf suckling Romulus and Remus; the rape of the Sabines; the geese that saved the Capitol from the Gauls; Catiline hanging in Tartarus and Cato giving laws among the righteous; and at the shield's centre — the composition is explicit, the middle of the shield is the middle of the poem's claim — the battle of Actium (8.671–713): Augustus standing on his high stern with twin flames streaming from his temples, the Senate and people and the gods of Italy with him, against Antony with the wealth of the Orient and Cleopatra with her sistrum, the monstrous gods of Egypt against Neptune, Venus, and Minerva; and after it, Augustus' triple triumph, the conquered peoples processing in their nations' dress. Aeneas lifts the shield onto his shoulder — and the poem's last word on the matter is the charter's own admission of its mechanism: he rejoices in the image, ignorant of the events, and lifts up the fame and fate of his descendants. Rerum ignarus: he does not know what he is carrying. He carries it anyway. The bearer of Section VIII carries this too — a future he cannot read, wrought by a god, in which the civil war he never lived to see is already, in metal, a victory of gods and order over monsters and the East.

// CHARTER, CULT, AND THE HISTORICAL AUGUSTUS //

The poem places Augustus inside a mythic frame: descended, through Iulus, from Venus; foreseen by Anchises; figured at the shield's centre with flames at his temples like a god's. This is literary apotheosis, and it should be distinguished, as this hub distinguishes throughout, from the institutional facts of the imperial cult — the altars, priests, and provincial temples by which the living and dead emperors were actually worshipped — which belong to Roman Religion and are not to be inferred from a poem. What the poem itself shows is a court culture in which the step from charter-myth to cult language was short and already being taken in verse a generation before the temples; the poem is evidence for the imagination of the principate, not for its ritual calendar. Virgil also handles his living subject with a notable economy: Augustus is named rarely, appears only in prophecy and in image, and never speaks. The charter is the louder for the restraint.

XII. The Ending: Turnus and the Two Voices

The poem ends in a killing, and it ends at the killing, with a completeness so abrupt that readers have doubted it for two thousand years. The situation, in paraphrase: Turnus, the Rutulian prince to whom Lavinia was first promised, lies beaten in the final duel — his sword shattered, his thigh wounded by Aeneas' spear — and supplicates. He concedes Lavinia and the kingdom; he asks, for his aged father Daunus' sake, for his life, or at least his body. And Aeneas hesitates. The poem is explicit that the plea is working: Aeneas stands fierce in arms with rolling eyes, and holds back his hand; the words have begun to sway the wavering man — Anchises' charge in the parade, to spare the subjected, is within a breath of being obeyed. Then Aeneas sees, on Turnus' shoulder, the belt of Pallas: the sword-belt of the boy Evander entrusted to him, whom Turnus killed in Book X and stripped, wearing the spoil ever since as a trophy. The sight ends the hesitation. Aeneas, in the poem's words as standardly rendered, blazing with fury and terrible in anger — furiis accensus et ira terribilis (12.946–947), the gloss this article's — declares that it is Pallas who sacrifices Turnus, Pallas who exacts the penalty from his guilty blood, and buries the sword in his chest. The poem's last line, paraphrased: Turnus' limbs slacken in cold, and with a groan his life flees, resentful — indignata — to the shades below. End of poem. No marriage, no foundation ceremony, no funeral for the enemy, no reconciliation. The Iliad ends with Hector's burial and enemies weeping together; the Aeneid ends with a corpse and a resentful ghost.

Was it meant to end there? The question divides into two. The textual question — did death interrupt the poem before its intended close? — cannot be answered from the text: the architecture of Section VI is complete, the duel is the duel the whole second half has promised, and no ancient source reports a planned continuation; the Renaissance supplied one anyway, Maffeo Vegio's supplementary Book XIII (1428), which married Aeneas to Lavinia and apotheosised him, and which was printed with Virgil for two centuries — evidence of how keenly readers felt the abruptness, not of what Virgil intended. The interpretive question — what does the ending mean, coming where it does, in a poem whose charter commands sparing the subjected? — is the most argued question in Virgilian scholarship, and it is presented here, per this article's discipline, as the modern scholarly debate it is: labelled, with its positions named.

THE EUROPEAN / AUGUSTAN READING

The older dominant tradition of scholarship — associated with Richard Heinze's Virgil's Epic Technique (1903), Viktor Pöschl's The Art of Virgil (1950), and Brooks Otis' Virgil: A Study in Civilized Poetry (1963) — reads the poem as the Augustan settlement's great imaginative statement: Aeneas' killing of Turnus is the necessary, if sorrowful, act of foundation — Turnus, offered terms, had broken the treaty; the belt of Pallas makes the killing an act of pietas toward the dead boy and his father, the counter-obligation that outweighs the obligation to spare. On this reading the ending is justified within the poem's own economy of duty, and its darkness is the darkness of necessity, not of protest.

THE HARVARD SCHOOL / PESSIMIST READING

The counter-tradition is named for the Harvard of Adam Parry, whose 1963 essay “The Two Voices of Virgil's Aeneid” gave the debate its standard form: the poem, Parry argued, speaks in two voices — a public voice of celebration, Rome's triumph and Augustus' achievement, and a private voice of lament for what the triumph cost. Developed by Wendell Clausen, Michael C. J. Putnam (The Poetry of the Aeneid, 1965), and W. R. Johnson (Darkness Visible, 1976), the pessimist reading takes the ending as the private voice's last word: Aeneas, at the moment of victory, is possessed by the furor the poem has everywhere condemned; the man of pietas kills a suppliant in rage, and the poem closes on the failure its charter forbids. On this reading the ending is not necessity but indictment.

THE DEBATE SINCE

Later scholarship has complicated both poles: readers have questioned whether “Augustan” and “pessimist” exhaust the options (the poem may be doing something other than taking sides in a modern referendum on Augustus), whether the two voices are the poet's division or his readers' projection, and whether Roman concepts — ira as a duty of vengeance, the obligations owed a dead ally's father — make the killing legible to its first audience in terms neither modern position captures. Karl Galinsky's work is prominent among the correctives. The debate remains open; survey literature of the last generation treats it as the poem's defining interpretive problem rather than as a settled one.

WHAT THE TEXT SETTLES

What no position disputes: the poem ends where it ends — on the killing, without epilogue; the killer acts in fury, in words the poem itself supplies; the victim's soul flees indignata, resentful, a word Virgil also uses of the unburied and the untimely dead; and the charge to spare the subjected stands sixty lines' journey back in the poem's own charter, unrevoked. The facts are common ground. The poem's meaning, at its most important point, is an argument its readers are still having — which may be the most Virgilian fact of all.

A note on method, because this section is where the article's discipline is most tested. The two-voices debate is a debate among modern scholars about a poem, not a pair of doctrines the poem teaches, and still less a pair of ancient readings: no ancient commentator reads the ending as protest, and the pessimist case is an achievement of twentieth-century criticism reading a first-century text with post-war eyes. That does not make it wrong. It makes it what it is — an interpretation, of great power, held by named scholars, against another interpretation, of great power, held by other named scholars — and it is presented here as exactly that, with neither awarded the poem.

XIII. The Afterlife: School Text, Guide, and Oracle

The Aeneid's afterlife is the longest continuous reading any Latin poem has had, and it begins immediately: Propertius, writing while the poem was still in progress, already announced — paraphrased — that something greater than the Iliad was being born, and Ovid's Metamorphoses, the subject of this hub's Ovid's Metamorphoses, retells the wanderings and the Italian war in Books XIII–XIV as the “little Aeneid” at its own centre — the poem already canonical enough, within a generation, to be the thing its successor measured itself against. What fixed the poem's tenure was the institution that fixed Homer's (see The Homeric Epics, Section XIV): the schoolroom. The Aeneid became, and for some fifteen hundred years remained, the central text of Latin education — the book from which the Latin West learned its grammar, its rhetoric, its mythology, and its idea of Rome. Quintilian's syllabus takes Virgil as the Latin peer of Homer and the starting-point of reading; the commentaries of Servius and the Donatan tradition made the poem a curriculum unto itself; Augustine wept over Dido in it at school (Section IX); and the great capital manuscripts — the Vergilius Vaticanus (Vat. lat. 3225, c. 400 CE) and the Vergilius Romanus (Vat. lat. 3867, fifth century), illustrated codices of a luxury no merely literary text commands — show the poem already, in late antiquity, an object of veneration as much as of study. The medieval spelling Virgilius and the wand it suggested bred the other Virgil of the Middle Ages, Virgil the magician and prophet, of the Neapolitan legends — a folklore the archive records as reception, not as anything the poet was.

The Guide

The afterlife's most famous act is a casting decision. When Dante Alighieri, at the opening of the Inferno (c. 1308–1321), needs a guide through Hell and Purgatory, he chooses Virgil — “my master and my author”, in the poem's famous address, given here in paraphrase — and the choice is the medieval school's verdict made personal: Virgil is reason and tradition themselves, the best the pagan world produced, who can lead the Christian poet through every realm except Paradise, where a soul from that world may not go. Dante's Virgil is a character built out of the schoolroom's Virgil — and out of the poem itself: the guide leads Dante through an underworld mapped on Book VI, and the Commedia's Statius, in the Purgatorio, tells how he was converted to Christianity by reading the fourth Eclogue's prophecy of a child under whom the golden age returns — the “Messianic Eclogue”, read by the Church from Constantine's age onward as an unwitting prophecy of Christ. These are receptions, and are labelled as such: the fourth Eclogue is a poem of 40 BCE about a hoped-for child of the Roman settlement, and its Christian reading is a fact about its readers, not about its composition. But the reception is itself a historical force: for a thousand years the Aeneid's author stood, for Christian Europe, at the threshold of the faith — the pagan who had seen furthest.

The Oracle

Stranger, and briefer: the Sortes Vergilianae, the Virgilian lots. As the Sortes Homericae were drawn from Homer, so Romans of the empire consulted Virgil by lot — opening the poem at random and taking the first line the eye fell on as an oracle for the enquirer's fortune. The practice is attested chiefly in the Historia Augusta, the late-antique collection of imperial biographies whose reliability scholars treat with permanent caution: it reports Virgilian lots drawn for or by emperors — Hadrian, Alexander Severus, Claudius II among them — each, conveniently, prophetic of the reign that followed. The anecdotes are evidence, at the least, that by late antiquity the poem had acquired the status of a sacred book that could be consulted — scripture of the Roman past, opened at random like the Bible in the sortes sanctorum the Church condemned and practised. The practice outlived the empire: Charles I is said to have drawn a Virgilian lot at Oxford during the Civil War and taken Dido's curse for his omen — a story told of him by his contemporaries' tradition, and repeated here as such. A poem men use as an oracle has crossed the line from literature to something else; the Aeneid crossed it early and never crossed back.

The summary judgement belongs to the institution rather than to any reader. For two millennia the Aeneid was the model of epic: the poem against which Camões, Tasso, Spenser, and Milton measured their own, the fixed point of the European curriculum from the Roman schools to the twentieth century, the Latin text more boys learned their language from than any other ever written. Its eclipse in the modern classroom is recent and partial; its presence in the language — in the phrases, situations, and structures European literature assumes — is not. Rome read itself in this poem until Rome was a memory, and then Europe read Rome in it, and the reading has not stopped.

XIV. Editions and Translations: What the Modern Reader Holds

The Aeneid's text is, by classical standards, exceptionally well founded. The tradition descends through the great late-antique capital manuscripts named in Section XIII — Vaticanus, Romanus, and the Medicean (Laurentianus 39.1, fifth century, corrected in 494 CE by the consul Turcius Rufius Apronianus Asterius, whose subscription survives) — supplemented by fragments of other ancient codices and by the ancient commentary tradition, Servius above all, whose lemmata preserve readings older than any surviving manuscript. Between the ancient vulgate and the modern text the differences are matters of lines and words, not of books: the poem's fabric, like Homer's (see The Homeric Epics, Section XVI), crossed the manuscript centuries essentially intact, because an institution was paying for its accurate preservation at every stage. The editor's problems are the poem's special ones: the half-lines, which are authorial and must not be completed; the Helen episode (Section IV), whose absence from the earliest manuscripts and presence in Servius' report of the editors' removal make it the poem's standing crux; and the four ille ego lines (Section III), which the tradition attributes to the poem's opening and the manuscripts do not contain.

Ahl (2007) — Frederick Ahl, Virgil: Aeneid, Oxford World's Classics A verse translation with an exceptionally full introduction and notes, attentive to the Latin's word play and sound-patterning; Ahl's apparatus of notes makes his version a commentary in its own right.
Ferry (2017) — David Ferry, The Aeneid The poet David Ferry's iambic pentameter version, completed in his nineties: limpid, grave, and the most purely pleasurable recent Englishing for a reader who wants the poem as an English poem.

The practical counsel of the Homeric article applies unchanged: read the poem in a translation that keeps the lineation, and read the repeated epithet — pius Aeneas — as the feature it is, the hero's name and office in one word. Then, once, look at the opening of Book I in Mynors or Conte and find a half-line in the apparatus of any book, and notice that the editor has printed the gap and moved on. The poem does not shrink for its unfinishedness. It acquires, like the Iliad in its different way, a depth of field: a deathbed, two faithful editors, an emperor's countermand, five centuries of capital-letter codices, and two thousand years of schoolboys, all holding the same 9,896 lines up to the light.
