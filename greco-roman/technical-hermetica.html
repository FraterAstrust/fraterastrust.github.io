I. The Other Half of the Promise

This article exists because another article in this hub made a promise and then, quite properly, kept only half of it. The hub’s Hermetica article opens — in its Section I, “Two Families Under One Name” — by dividing everything written under the name of Hermes Trismegistus into two families. The philosophical Hermetica are dialogues, discourses, hymns, and prayers about God, the cosmos, and the soul’s way back to its source. The technical Hermetica are practical works: astrological treatises and catalogues, alchemical recipes and allegories, magical procedures, and writings on the hidden properties of plants, stones, and animals. The Hermetica article then follows the first family — the Corpus Hermeticum, the Asclepius, Ficino, Casaubon — because that family has the datable manuscripts, the famous translation, and the famous redating. The second family was named, carded, and left at the door. This article is the second family’s article. It picks up exactly that promise, and the Hermetica article remains what it was designed to be: the owner of the Corpus in this hub, to which the reader should go for the seventeen treatises, the Poimandrēs, and the Renaissance. Nothing in what follows retells that story.

What follows instead is the bench-level literature of the same world. Where the philosophical treatises ask what the soul is and where it is going, the technical writings ask narrower and more urgent questions. Under which decan was this child born, and which part of his body will suffer for it? On which day will this fever turn? Which plant, gathered under which star, answers to this pain? What image, cut into which stone and set above which herb, will hold the illness off? Which hour is fit to begin the work in the furnace, and which is not? The answers were issued under the same name as the dialogues — Hermes, the thrice-greatest — and they were issued earlier. Some of the astrological Hermetica may reach back to the second or third century BCE, centuries before the earliest philosophical treatise that scholarship will accept. The technical family is not the philosophical family’s degeneration, still less its appendix. On the chronology the evidence supports, it is closer to being its elder sibling: the dialogues about escaping fate were written into a world that already possessed, under Hermes’ name, a large literature for calculating fate in detail.

The Corpus Hermeticum, the Latin Asclepius, the Stobaean excerpts, the Vienna fragments, and the Nag Hammadi Hermetica: theology, cosmology, and rebirth through knowledge. Owned, in this hub, by the Hermetica article, whose Section I states the two-families division this article completes.

THE TECHNICAL FAMILY — HERE

Astrological, medical, magical, and alchemical writings under Hermes’ name: the Nechepso–Petosiris tradition, the Liber Hermetis, the decan books, the iatromathematical fragments, the Kyranides, and Hermes as the alchemists cited him. Practical in aim: elections, remedies, operations, and the sympathies of things.

A note on method, in the same terms the sibling article set for itself. No direct quotation from a technical Hermetic text is given in this article unless it is explicitly attributed to a named translation. The technical corpus survives largely in fragments, excerpts, and layered compilations, often in Latin or Arabic dress over lost Greek, and its wording is correspondingly easy to fake and hard to check. The article therefore paraphrases throughout, and says so where it paraphrases. The one extended exception is the prologue of the Kyranides in its seventeenth-century English dress (Section IX), quoted briefly, verbatim, and under its translator’s title, because that wording can be verified against the printed book. Everything else is labelled paraphrase or follows a named modern account — Fowden, Festugière, and the specialist literature listed in Section XVI.

And the standing warning of this hub applies here with double force, because the technical literature invites it: these are pseudepigrapha. No text treated in this article was written by a historical Hermes Trismegistus, for there was no such historical person: there was a god — Thoth in Greek dress — a legendary sage built upon the god, and an authorising name under which priests, astrologers, physicians, and compilers published their work for the better part of a thousand years. “Hermes says” in a technical text means what “Hermes says” means in a philosophical one: a writer, whose name we almost never know, claimed the oldest authority his culture afforded. The hub says this plainly everywhere, and this article says it first rather than last, because the technical texts wear their attributions more variously than the dialogues do — a king of Egypt, a Persian king, an Alexandrian rhetor writing to his daughter, a physician taught face to face by a god — and each costume is part of the evidence, not a distraction from it.

II. What “Technical” Means: Sympathy, Election, and the Working Text

“Technical” is a scholar’s word, and it should be unpacked before it hardens. It does not mean mechanical, and it does not mean modern. It renders the sense of the Greek technē — an art, a craft, a body of procedures that can be taught, looked up, and applied — and it marks the difference of use between the two Hermetic families. A philosophical treatise is read, meditated, and argued with; a technical treatise is consulted. It is built for the moment of need: the consultation, the sickbed, the workshop, the engraving bench. Its characteristic forms follow from that use — the catalogue, the table, the alphabetised entry, the aphorism, the recipe, the list of correspondences — and its characteristic question is never “what is true?” in the abstract but “what answers to what, and what do I do now?”

Festugière’s Occult Sciences

The modern study of the family begins, as so much Hermetic study does, with A.-J. Festugière. The first volume of his La révélation d’Hermès Trismégiste (1944) — L’astrologie et les sciences occultes, “Astrology and the Occult Sciences” — remains the founding survey of the technical corpora, and its taxonomy is still the one the reference literature repeats. Among the works of the occult sciences, Festugière distinguished three great kinds, with three different early edges: astrology, beginning in the third or second century BCE; alchemy, beginning in the second or first century BCE; and magic, recorded in papyri of the fourth to seventh centuries CE that reproduce sources obviously much more ancient. Medicine, in the form of astrological medicine, runs through all three rather than standing as a fourth kind: the decan that governs a body part also governs its diseases, its plants, and the stones cut for its cure.

Festugière’s judgement on the two families’ relation was severe, and it is part of the record. He held, in paraphrase of his position, that the philosophical Hermetica had only slight connections with the technical: the occult texts he was ready to credit to the Egyptian priesthood, while the philosophical treatises he read as the work of Greek writers of modest culture dressing Greek doctrine in Egyptian costume. On that reading the families share a name and little else — two literatures, two milieux, one borrowed brand. The severity matters because the correction of it is the second founding moment of the field, and because something of Festugière’s instinct survives in every library catalogue that shelves the Corpus Hermeticum under philosophy and the Kyranides under folklore.

Fowden’s Single Milieu

Garth Fowden, in The Egyptian Hermes (1986), argued the counter-case that has structured discussion since — the same argument the sibling article adopts for the philosophical family, stated there and applied here to both. The two families, on Fowden’s account, are distinct but interdependent: products of a single, complex Greco-Egyptian milieu — the world of Ptolemaic, Roman, and early Christian Egypt, and above all of its temples and their priestly intellectuals, for whom Greek was a working language and Greek learning a usable idiom, but whose institutions, gods, and practices were their own. The families shared readers, copyists, and assumptions; a man might copy a dialogue on rebirth and a catalogue of decans in the same season, and the alchemist Zosimos, around 300 CE, quotes Hermes in both registers without marking any border (Section XII). More recent work has tightened the connection further still: Christian Bull’s study of the Physica of Hermes (2018) argues, in paraphrase, that a technical treatise known to Zosimos likely stands behind passages of the philosophical Perfect Discourse and the Kore Kosmou — that the traffic between the families ran at the level of composition, not merely of readership. The position this article adopts is Fowden’s, for the reason the sibling article gave: it is the position the evidence currently supports. The division is real as a division of kinds of text and histories of transmission. It is not a wall, and antiquity did not observe it.

SYMPATHY AND ANTIPATHY

The family’s physics in two words. Things that share a star, a decan, a letter, or a nature attract, reinforce, and cure one another; things opposed repel and harm. The Kyranides’ own full title, in paraphrase, announces a book of physical virtues, sympathies, and antipathies — the doctrine is on the title page.

ELECTION — KATARCHĒ

The family’s practical core. Every undertaking has a moment at which it must begin if it is to prosper: the hour to gather the herb, cut the stone, begin the treatment, start the work. Technical astrology is, in large part, the art of finding that moment — and Hermetic astrology is its oldest Greek literature.

CORRESPONDENCE LISTS

The family’s characteristic page. Star to plant, decan to stone, sign to limb, letter to bird, fish, herb, and gem: knowledge stored as alignment, so that the practitioner who knows the row knows the remedy. The list is not a primitive table of contents. It is the theory, in storage form.

THE AUTHORISING NAME

The family’s title page. Hermes, a pharaoh, a priest, a Persian king: attribution as authorisation. A technical claim — this stone cures, this hour avails — needed an authority older than argument, and Egypt, in Greek eyes, was where such authority lived.

One more definition, because the family’s modern reputation suffers from its absence. To call these texts “occult” — Festugière’s word, and the tradition’s — is to say that they traffic in hidden properties: virtues in stones, plants, and animals that no inspection of their manifest qualities reveals, and that operate by sympathy rather than by contact mechanics. It is not to say that their users experienced them as a separate, shadowy domain set against ordinary knowledge. For the physician who consulted a decan table, the astrologer who elected an hour, and the compiler who alphabetised the virtues of birds, this was ordinary knowledge — the working natural philosophy of the world Wing II’s practitioners inhabited. The hub’s foundations cluster exists to make that world legible: the Greek astrology article supplies its sky, the humours and Galen article its learned medicine, and this article its Hermetic working library.

// A DATING WARNING, BEFORE THE INVENTORY //

The technical family has no Casaubon moment and no single corpus to date, and its dates must be handled text by text. Three different things are routinely confused: the date a tradition claims (a pharaoh of the seventh century BCE), the date its material may reach back to (in places, the second or third century BCE), and the date of the compilation or manuscript that actually survives (often Byzantine, sometimes a millennium later). This article keeps the three apart wherever it gives a date, and gives ranges where the scholarship gives ranges. Fowden’s own caution stands at the head of the subject: our earliest firm testimonies to Hermetic astrology belong to the first century BCE, however much older the material they testify to may be.

III. A Working Library, Not a Book: The Shape of the Corpus

The philosophical Hermetica can be tabled in a page: seventeen treatises, a Latin dialogue, a set of excerpts. The technical Hermetica cannot, and the reason is itself informative. There was never a technical corpus in the Byzantine editors’ sense — no Psellos gathered the decan books between two covers. What survives is a working library: hundreds of treatises, excerpts, catalogues, and fragments, in Greek, Latin, Arabic, and the languages beyond them, scattered through astrological miscellanies, medical handbooks, alchemical codices, and magical papyri, much of it anonymous or pseudonymous, much of it still unedited in the manuscripts where the great cataloguing projects of a century ago recorded it. Modern scholarship is candid that the technical Hermetica — Greek, Arabic, and Latin together — remain largely unexplored beside the philosophical treatises. What follows is therefore an inventory of the principal witnesses, the texts a reader of this hub will actually meet cited, with their state of survival stated plainly.

Three features of that inventory deserve emphasis before the detail begins. First, its chronological sprawl: the family’s composition runs from the Hellenistic centuries to the high Middle Ages without a break — Hermetic technical writing was still being produced, translated, and expanded in the twelfth century, long after the philosophical treatises had settled into their Byzantine corpus. Second, its linguistic sprawl: Greek is the family’s first language, but Latin and Arabic are not mere afterlives — the Liber Hermetis survives only in Latin, the Kyranides was reworked in both, and a large Arabic Hermetic literature, some of it translated from Greek or through Persian and Syriac intermediaries, some of it composed in Arabic under the name, awaits the study the Greek material has had. Third, its anonymity in practice: for every text that trumpets Hermes on its title, the astrological codices carry a dozen excerpts headed simply “from Hermes”, “the Egyptian says”, or nothing at all, whose Hermetic paternity is a cataloguer’s inference. The family is best imagined not as a shelf of books but as a sediment — layers of attributed, excerpted, and reattributed working material, in which the name Hermes functions less like an author’s signature than like a stratum marker: this layer claims Egypt, antiquity, and revelation.

IV. Nechepso and Petosiris: The Fountainhead Tradition

Greek astrology, when it tells its own origin story, does not begin with Hermes alone. It begins with a king and a priest. The king is Nechepso — the name under which the historical pharaoh Necho II (reigned 610–595 BCE), of the Twenty-sixth Dynasty, entered the astrological tradition. The priest is Petosiris — “gift of Osiris” — a name borne by a famous high priest of Thoth of about 300 BCE, though whether the astrological author was meant to be that man, or another of the name, cannot be known. Under these two names there circulated, from the second century BCE, a body of astrological writing that later antiquity treated as the fountainhead of the art: the oldest, most authoritative, and most oracular stratum of Greek astrology, from which the named astrologers of the Roman centuries — Dorotheus, Valens, Hephaistio, and their fellows — drew and against which they measured themselves.

The attribution needs its plain statement, in the hub’s manner: the Nechepso–Petosiris texts are pseudepigrapha. Anonymous works of the Hellenistic centuries were fathered on a pharaoh five hundred years dead and on his legendary priest, and the fathering was the point. But the tradition is Hermetic at its root, and says so in its own genealogy. The Latin astrologer Firmicus Maternus, in the fourth century CE, gives the chain of transmission in paraphrase: the material originates with the legendary sage Mercury — Hermes — passes to Asclepius, and is then elaborated by the Egyptian pharaoh Nechepso and the high priest Petosiris. The fragments themselves ascribe the knowledge they convey to the authority of Hermes: Petosiris, in the tradition’s frame, took his revelation from Hermes, and the king was guided by the priest into a vision that granted him horoscopic truth. Hermes stands behind the king and the priest as Thoth stands behind Egyptian writing itself — not the working author of the handbook, but the god of the archive from which the handbook was supposedly copied. Every later Hermetic astrological text lives somewhere downstream of that arrangement.

What Survives, and in Whom

Of the fountainhead itself, nothing survives whole. What survives is a debris of fragments and testimonies — quotations, paraphrases, and respectful citations embedded in the astrologers who used the work — collected by Ernst Riess in 1892–1893 and brought up to date by Stephan Heilen in the present century; a complete modern edition of the fragments is still lacking. The chief quarries are the Anthology of Vettius Valens (second century CE), who cites the king and Petosiris by name at the foundations of his system — the Lot of Fortune’s calculation, in Valens’s account, stands at the opening of the king’s thirteenth book, expounded, as Valens complains in paraphrase, in an enigmatic fashion that later writers have never stopped reinterpreting — and the Apotelesmatika of Hephaistio of Thebes (about 415 CE), whose first book closes with a long excerpt on eclipse delineation that shows the fountainhead’s oldest layer at work. Proclus and John Lydus preserve further fragments in late antiquity. The modern reference classification groups the fragments under four heads, and the grouping is a fair map of the early art’s whole range:

ASTRAL OMENS

General indications from eclipses, comets, halos, winds, and the heliacal rising of Sirius, developed in Ptolemaic Egypt from Mesopotamian prototypes — the Babylonian inheritance in Egyptian dress, and the stratum by which scholars dated the whole to the second century BCE.

THE REVELATION AND THE HOROSCOPE

The vision-text in which the king, guided by Petosiris, receives horoscopic truth, and the doctrines that flow from it — lots, places, and the reading of a nativity. The fountainhead of horoscopic astrology proper, whose machinery the hub’s Greek astrology article owns.

ASTROLOGICAL BOTANY AND DECANIC MEDICINE

A treatise on astrological botany for medical purposes, and another on decanic medicine — the fountainhead already flows into the sickroom. The medical branch of the family (Sections VII–VIII) begins here, in the second century BCE, inside the astrological corpus itself.

NUMEROLOGY AND THE LOTS

Treatises on significant numbers and on the calculated places — the Lots — by which a chart’s abstract geometry was turned into judgements about fortune, spirit, and the times of a life.

Two scholarly cautions belong with the fountainhead, and this article records both rather than smoothing them. The first is the older scholarship’s temptation, now resisted: to assign every anonymous early fragment — every “the ancients say”, every “the Egyptians hold” — to Nechepso and Petosiris, making one lost handbook explain all of early astrology. Joachim Friedrich Quack’s work on the Demotic evidence has shown, in paraphrase of his argument, that a rich Egyptian astrological tradition existed beyond the two names, and that the works attributed to them were not necessarily one and the same book. The fountainhead is a tradition, not a codex. The second caution runs the other way: fragmentary survival is not insignificance. When Valens, the most practical astrologer of antiquity whose work survives whole, builds his Lots on the king’s thirteenth book and argues with Petosiris’s Definitions across six centuries of supposed distance, the deference is itself the datum. The Nechepso–Petosiris texts were to Greek astrology what Hermes was to the whole Hermetic library: the authority that did not need to be read entire to be obeyed — which is fortunate, since they could not be.

// THE EGYPTIAN SIDE OF THE FOUNTAINHEAD //

The names were not Greek inventions pasted onto Egypt from outside. Necho II was a real Saite pharaoh; Petosiris was a real priestly name, borne by the high priest of Thoth whose tomb at Tuna el-Gebel is among the famous monuments of the early Ptolemaic age; and Demotic astrological material under Nechepso’s name has been identified in Egyptian sources. The pseudepigraphy worked because it was plausible: Egypt had priest-astrologers, temple archives, and a star science older than Greece’s, and the Greek handbook claimed, in Greek, to be their translation.

The fountainhead tradition also fixes the family’s geography and social setting at its source. This is the astrology of Ptolemaic Egypt: Babylonian omen lore and mathematical schemes, Egyptian calendar and decan lore, and Greek geometrical and philosophical habits of system, meeting in the temples and the Alexandrian libraries of a kingdom where all three were in daily contact. The Salmeschoiniaka — the “Wandering of the Influences”, perhaps Alexandrian, perhaps of the same second or third century BCE — belongs to the same moment: configurations of stars read as powers moving through the zodiac. The Art of Eudoxus, preserved on a papyrus of the second century BCE and already citing Hermes as an authority, shows the arrangement in physical form within a generation or two of the fountainhead’s composition. When the philosophical Hermetica were written, four centuries later, Hermes the astrologer was already ancient. Hermes the theologian was the newcomer.

V. Hermetic Astrology Proper: The Liber Hermetis and the Catalogue Tradition

Beside the fountainhead tradition — Hermetic by genealogy, royal and priestly by attribution — there stands a second astrological stratum that bears Hermes’ name on its own title: the astrological Hermetica proper. Its principal survivor is the Liber Hermetis, the “Book of Hermes”, and its survival states the family’s condition in one line: the book exists only in Latin, in an early translation conventionally dated to the fourth or fifth century CE, of a Greek compilation whose elements may be traced to the second or third century BCE. The Greek is lost. What the Latin preserves is not a treatise but a compilation of compilations — a gathering of short, freestanding chapters on the working topics of the art, of the kind a practitioner’s handbook accumulates: the thirty-six decans of the twelve signs with their forms; the bright stars and their natures; the lots; the twelve places; the climacteric years, those dangerous years of a life at which the astrologer was expected to look twice; and the consideration of the figure of a nativity as a whole. The standard edition is Wilhelm Gundel’s of 1936 — Neue astrologische Texte des Hermes Trismegistos, “New Astrological Texts of Hermes Trismegistus” — and the book is read today in the Latin Gundel printed, in the English translation Robert Zoller made from it for the Project Hindsight recovery of Hellenistic astrology. Its first chapter, on the decans, is the bridge to Section VI, and the oldest stratum of decan lore in the family’s Greek line.

Around the Liber the catalogue tradition spreads in every direction, and its scale is the point. The great modern map of it is the Catalogus Codicum Astrologorum Graecorum — the CCAG — the twelve-volume catalogue and edition of the Greek astrological manuscripts undertaken under Franz Cumont’s leadership and published at Brussels between 1898 and 1953. The CCAG’s editors scoured the libraries of Europe for astrological codices and printed, volume by volume, the excerpts those codices contained; and through its pages the Hermetic stratum runs like a seam: chapters headed with Hermes’ name on the decans, on the fixed stars, on inceptional astrology — the katarchai, the elections of Section II — on the prognosis of the sick, on thunder and earthquakes, excerpted, re-excerpted, and bound among the works of Valens, Hephaistio, Rhetorius, and the Byzantine compilers. Much of this material has never been translated, and some of it has never been edited since the CCAG printed it. To speak of “Hermetic astrology” as a book is therefore a convenience. It was a stratum of the astrological manuscript tradition, continuously mined by the tradition’s working astrologers, who treated “Hermes” the way their successors treated a standard authority: as the name you cite when the doctrine is older than your other sources.

The Weather Shelf and the Omen Books

The stratum’s humblest members repay a paragraph, because they show the family’s range at its widest. The Brontologion — the thunder book — sets out, in paraphrase of its scheme, what thunder portends in each month of the year: a calendar of the sky’s noises, descended from the Babylonian omen series through the same Egyptian mediation as the fountainhead’s astral omens. The Peri seismōn, “On Earthquakes”, relates earthquakes to the astrological signs under which they occur. Neither text pretends to philosophy; both pretend to Hermes, or travel in his company; and both were copied for centuries because the need they answer — a public event, a frightened city, a ruler who must be told what the sign means — never went away. The technical Hermetica include the literature of the consultation as well as of the study, and the omen books are where the family touches the oldest divinatory practice of the ancient Near East most directly.

What Hermetic Astrology Is Not

A boundary should be drawn here, because the name invites confusion. Not every Greek astrological text is Hermetic, and the hub does not claim them. Ptolemy’s Tetrabiblos, Valens’s Anthology, Dorotheus’s poem, and Firmicus’s Mathesis are the art’s great systematic books, written under their authors’ own names, and they belong to the hub’s Greek astrology article, which owns the horoscope’s machinery — the places, the aspects, the lots, the time-lords — in full. The Hermetic astrology of this article is the attributed stratum those authors themselves cite as older than themselves: the fountainhead behind Valens, the decan books behind the medical writers, the catalogues behind the compilers. The relation is the one Section IV established. The systematists are the family’s readers and witnesses, and it is largely in their pages that the family survives.

VI. The Decans: Egyptian Stars in Greek Dress

If one doctrine certifies the technical family’s Egyptian inheritance beyond argument, it is the decans. The decans — Egyptian baktiu — began as thirty-six groups of stars by which Egyptian astronomers told the hours of the night and divided the year. They appear first in the First Intermediate Period and the early Middle Kingdom, on the painted lids of coffins, in the diagonal star tables that let the dead — and the priests who served them — read the night sky as a clock: each decan rose in turn, ten days gave each its decade of dominance, and thirty-six decades made the 360 days of the Egyptian year, with the five added days standing outside the scheme. The Book of Nut treats them; temple ceilings down to the Dendera zodiac of about 50 BCE display them. They are, in origin, neither Greek nor astrological. They are an Egyptian timekeeping system of great antiquity, staffed by stars that were also gods.

The transformation that made them Hermetic happened in Ptolemaic Egypt, in the same encounter as the fountainhead. When Babylonian zodiacal astrology met the Egyptian calendar, the thirty-six decans were mapped onto the twelve signs: three decans to a sign, ten degrees to a decan — the very name keeps the ten, Greek dekanos from deka. Each decan acquired a face: a planetary ruler assigned in the so-called Chaldean order, through whom its power took character. Each acquired, in the Hermetic texts, a name — Greek transliterations, often garbled in transmission, of Egyptian star names — an image, a described figure, half Egyptian and half Greek in its iconography, and a portfolio: winds and weather, diseases and the body parts they govern, plants and stones that answer to it, and the times at which its image should be engraved for a cure. The decan of the timekeepers has become a minor god of the consultation room, with a dossier. The dossier is the Hermetic contribution, and it is enormous: the Liber Hermetis opens with the decans’ names and forms, the Sacred Book of Hermes to Asclepius is built of nothing else, and decan lists run through the magical papyri, the Testament of Solomon, the Latin and Arabic traditions, and — by the road through India’s Yavanajātaka, the “Greek horoscopy” in Sanskrit — into the image-lists of Varāhamihira. No other piece of pharaonic astronomy travelled so far in working dress.

THE EGYPTIAN STRATUM

Thirty-six star groups; ten-day decades; the coffin-lid star tables; Sirius — Sothis — at the head of the procession, whose heliacal rising marked the Egyptian year. Timekeeping and temple astronomy, a millennium and more older than the texts that Hermeticise them.

THE GREEK DRESS

Three decans to each zodiacal sign; ten degrees each; a planetary face for each; names, images, and portfolios assigned in Greek handbooks. The system the hub’s Greek astrology article treats as part of the horoscope’s furniture.

THE HERMETIC DOSSIER

What the Hermetic books add: the decan as a power with a governed limb, a disease list, a stone, a plant, an engraved image, and a dietary rule. The decan becomes addressable — invocable in sickness, carvable into a ring, avoidable at table.

THE WITNESS OF CELSUS

The pagan philosopher Celsus, about 178 CE, wrote — in paraphrase, as Origen preserves him — that the Egyptians put every part of the human body under the charge of thirty-six daemons or heavenly gods, whose names are invoked in sickness to treat the suffering of their parts. The decans, seen from outside, already at work in the sickroom.

The decans are also the cleanest illustration of this article’s central claim about the two families. The philosophical Hermetica know fate as the administration of the seven planetary governors, and salvation as the soul’s passage up through their spheres, surrendering at each zone what it received on the way down — the Poimandrēs plot, in the sibling article’s paraphrase. The technical Hermetica work the same cosmos from below and in detail: thirty-six sub-governors under the twelve signs, each with jurisdiction over a limb, a season of illness, a plant, and a stone, each approachable by the exact technologies of image, name, and election. It is one universe, described at two resolutions. The dialogue tells the prisoner the prison has a door; the decan book is the locksmith’s manual, and it was written first.

// A NOTE ON THE NAMES //

The decans’ Hermetic names survive in competing lists — the Liber Hermetis, the Sacred Book, Firmicus, the Testament of Solomon, and later compilers all transmit them, and no two lists agree in every spelling. The Egyptian originals behind the transliterations are in many cases unrecoverable, and modern attempts to force the lists into agreement manufacture a precision the manuscripts do not contain. This article therefore names no decan list as canonical, and gives individual decan names only where a named edition’s reading is being reported as such (Section VIII).

VII. Iatromathematics I: The Body Under the Sky

The Greeks had a word for medicine practised by the stars: iatromathematics — the mathematics of the physician, from iatros, a doctor, and mathēmatikos, in its ancient sense, an astrologer. The word names a whole department of ancient practice, not a Hermetic monopoly: Manilius, Ptolemy, Teucer of Babylon, and Firmicus all teach parts of it under their own names, and the hub’s Greek astrology article treats the department as part of the art’s history. What belongs to this article is the department’s Hermetic stratum — the part that circulated under Hermes’ and the fountainhead’s names, which is also, not coincidentally, its oldest part: the Nechepso–Petosiris fragments already include astrological botany and decanic medicine in the second century BCE, before Hippocratic medicine and Babylonian astrology had finished becoming the two learned systems between which iatromathematics would mediate.

Melothesia: The Zodiacal Man

The department’s foundation doctrine is melothesia: the assignment of the parts of the body to the powers of the sky. In its zodiacal form — the form Manilius already reports — the twelve signs are laid over the body from head to feet in their order: Aries the head, Taurus the neck, Gemini the shoulders and arms, Cancer the chest, Leo the heart and sides, Virgo the belly, Libra the loins, Scorpio the genitals, Sagittarius the thighs, Capricorn the knees, Aquarius the legs, Pisces the feet. The scheme is stated here as the tradition’s standard scheme, in paraphrase; its planetary counterpart assigns limbs and faculties to the seven planets, and its decanic refinement — the Hermetic specialty — subdivides the jurisdiction among the thirty-six. The figure the medieval manuscripts would draw from the scheme, the naked man ringed and inscribed with signs, the homo signorum or zodiacal man of a thousand medical codices and folding almanacs, is late in its familiar pictorial form; the doctrine it pictures is Hellenistic, and its decanic version is Hermetic. The body, in this medicine, is a map of the sky worn inside out: to know which sign rises over a pain is to know whose territory is afflicted and which sympathies may be mobilised for its relief.

Critical Days and the Decumbiture

The department’s second instrument is temporal. Hippocratic medicine had long taught that fevers turn on critical days — numbered days of an illness on which its course declares itself toward recovery or death — and astrology systematised the doctrine with enthusiasm: the Moon, coursing rapidly through the zodiac, forms its successive aspects to its place at the illness’s onset, and the crises fall where the geometry says they should. Around the doctrine grew the practice of the decumbiture: a chart cast for the moment the patient took to bed — the illness’s nativity, read as a nativity is read, for the disease’s strength, its course, and its end. Hermetic fragments on exactly this procedure survive among the early astrological remains: examinations of the taking-to-bed, and a numerical method, travelling under Hermes’ name, for pronouncing whether the sick person will live, be in danger, or die. Kindred devices — the “spheres” of life and death, numerical circles attributed variously to Petosiris, to Democritus, and to other ancients, on which the patient’s name and the day’s numbers are reckoned to a verdict — circulated in the same manuscripts for a thousand years. Their attributions wander, as this family’s attributions do; their use did not. A prognosis was the physician’s most public act, the one on which his fee and his reputation turned, and the Hermetic instruments promised to make it calculable.

Elections at the Sickbed

The third instrument completes the set: election. If the sky governs the body’s territories and the illness’s calendar, then the timing of treatment is itself a treatment. The iatromathematical literature elects the hour to administer a drug, to purge, to bleed, to gather the medicinal plant — whose virtue waxes with its star — and to engrave the curative stone, whose image must be cut while its decan is in the ascendant if the ring is to hold the power it pictures. Learned Galenic medicine, whose theory the hub’s humours and Galen article owns, regarded much of this with professional suspicion — Galen himself kept the critical days and discarded most of the apparatus around them — and the boundary between the physician and the iatromathematician was argued over for centuries. But the argument was an argument within ancient medicine, between two of its own departments, not between medicine and an alien superstition: the same codices carry Galen and the decan books, and the same practitioner might dose by the humours in the morning and elect by the Moon after dinner.

// THE CONSULTATION, RECONSTRUCTED FROM THE TEXTS //

A patient falls ill and takes to bed; the hour is noted, and the decumbiture cast. The afflicted part is located on the zodiacal and decanic map; its ruler’s condition — dignified or afflicted, aspected by benefics or malefics — is read for the prognosis, and the Moon’s course plotted against the critical days. A remedy is chosen from the correspondence lists: the plant of the afflicted part’s decan, or its antipathy, the enemy of the disease’s star. An hour is elected for its administration, and, if the case warrants the expense, a ring is commissioned: the decan’s image cut into its stone, the plant of the decan laid beneath the setting, the whole sealed and worn as a phylactery. Every step of that sequence is prescribed, somewhere, in the texts of Sections VII–VIII. The reconstruction is a composite, and is labelled as one; no single surviving text performs the whole consultation end to end.

VIII. Iatromathematics II: The Hermetic Medical Texts

Three bodies of text carry the Hermetic medical tradition in substance, and they show its three characteristic forms: the catalogue, the revelation narrative, and the star-list. Together they are the family’s medical shelf, and the background against which the Kyranides of Sections IX–XI — which is a medical book among its other natures — must be read.

The Sacred Book of Hermes to Asclepius

The Sacred Book of Hermes to Asclepius — also rendered Holy Book; Greek Hierā biblos — is the decan system’s medical summa. It survives in Greek in Byzantine manuscripts, was edited by C.-É. Ruelle in 1908 from the text J. B. Pitra had printed in 1888, and has been studied in depth in Spyros Piperakis’s work on its decanal iconography and natural materials (2017). Piperakis’s description of the work, in paraphrase, is the one this article adopts: a ritual manual for the making of finger-ring amulets, composed in Greek, most probably in early Roman Egypt. Its frame is an address from Hermes to Asclepius — the pupil of the philosophical dialogues, here receiving not theology but a formulary.

The book’s procedure, in paraphrase of Ruelle’s text, runs as follows. It first lays out the parts of the cosmos-body: the zodiacal signs with their governed limbs, in the melothesia of Section VII — Aries the head of the cosmos, and so through the signs to Pisces the feet. It then proceeds decan by decan, thirty-six entries in sign order. Each entry gives, in paraphrase of the pattern the entries follow: the decan’s name; its image, described closely enough to be carved — a child’s face with hands raised and a sceptre, a dog-faced figure with sceptre and disk, a woman with a drum, and so through the thirty-six; the part of the body and the illnesses it governs; the stone on which the image is to be engraved; the plant to be placed with the stone; the metal of the ring in which the whole is set — iron for one decan, gold for another; and a dietary avoidance, a food the wearer must refuse to keep the decan’s favour. The wearer of the completed ring carries, in the text’s own claim as the translations render it, a great phylactery: the afflictions that fall on mankind through the stars are to be healed by the star’s own image, stone, and plant, worn in concert. A fourth-century papyrus fragment closely parallel to a passage of the book shows the material already circulating on papyrus in Roman Egypt itself, outside the Byzantine codex tradition that preserves the whole.

The book repays attention to its logic, because the logic is the family’s in miniature. The decan causes the affliction — the illness belongs to its jurisdiction — and the decan, honoured with its image, its stone, and its plant, withholds or withdraws it. Cause and cure are the same power, differently approached: sympathy weaponised in both directions. Nothing in the procedure requires the wearer to understand the cosmos, to be reborn, or to ascend; it requires the carver to get the image right and the wearer to keep the diet. That is what “technical” means, at the bedside.

Thessalos and the Book That Failed

The medical shelf’s second form is the revelation narrative, and its classic is the prologue of Thessalos of Tralles, On the Virtues of Plants — a work of astrological botany assigning plants to the twelve signs and the seven planets, surviving in Greek and Latin, in the form we have it of the first or second century CE. The prologue is cast as a letter to an emperor — Claudius in one reckoning, Nero in another; the Greek version even misattributes the letter itself to Harpocration, the Alexandrian who will recur in Section X — and it tells, in paraphrase, the most honest story any technical text tells about itself. Thessalos, a young physician trained in Asia Minor and Alexandria, finds in an Alexandrian library an iatromathematical book of King Nechepso promising cures by the zodiac. He tries the recipes. They do not work. Having boasted of them in advance to his family and colleagues, he is ruined and suicidal — until, wandering Egypt, he persuades a priest at Diospolis, Egyptian Thebes, to prepare him for a face-to-face meeting with a god. Purified and shut in the sanctuary, he receives from Asclepius himself — the deified Imhotep, in his Egyptian home — the secret the book had omitted: the plants’ virtues are timed by their stars, and the gathering, not merely the choice, must obey the heavens. The herbal that follows is the corrected Nechepso: the fountainhead’s botany, reissued under a revelation that explains why the first edition failed.

The prologue matters to this article for three reasons. It is documentary evidence, from inside the tradition, that the Nechepso books were physical objects in working libraries, consulted by working physicians who expected them to perform. It shows the tradition debugging itself in its own idiom: failure is explained not by abandoning the system but by a missing key, supplied by a higher revelation — the same pattern by which the Nechepso–Petosiris corpus itself was layered over Hermes. And it fixes the social world of the medical Hermetica with unusual precision: the ambitious provincial student, the Alexandrian library, the Egyptian priest as the tradition’s living gatekeeper, and the god of medicine speaking Greek in a Theban sanctuary. One recension of the prologue even readdresses the revelation to Hermes Trismegistus — the attributions circulate among the same small company of names, as they do throughout the family.

The Star-Lists: Myriogenesis and the Fifteen Stars

The third form is the list at its purest. The Book of Asclepius Called Myriogenesis — the title promises a “myriad genesis”, a generation for every configuration — is a treatise on astrological medicine travelling under Asclepius’s name within the Hermetic circle: the pupil of Hermes and god of medicine lending his authority to the branch of the art that was his own province. The Fifteen Stars, Stones, Plants and Images reduces the decan books’ abundance to a practitioner’s short list: fifteen bright fixed stars, each with the stone that answers to it, the plant that answers to it, and the image to be engraved under its influence. The two texts frame the medical shelf’s economy. At one end, completeness: thirty-six decans, every limb, every affliction, a formulary for the whole body. At the other, portability: fifteen stars, a page or two of correspondences, a kit a travelling practitioner could carry in memory. The family produced both because its readers needed both — the library copy and the travelling copy of the same cosmos.

// WHAT THESE TEXTS ARE NOT //

They are not, and must not be presented as, evidence for the efficacy of astral medicine, and this article makes no such claim for them: it reports what the texts prescribe and what their readers did, as it reports the decans and the elections, because the hub documents the world Wing II’s practitioners worked in. Nor are they Galen: the learned medicine of the humours and Galen article is a different, argumentative, clinical literature, and the two literatures’ coexistence — copied together, quarrelled over, practised by the same hands — is a fact about ancient medicine, not a licence to merge them.

IX. The Kyranides I: The Family’s Best-Surviving Book

The technical family is a literature of fragments, excerpts, and lost Greek behind surviving Latin. One book breaks the pattern. The Kyranides — the spelling varies: Kyranides, Kiranides, Latin Cyranides — survives whole, in Greek, in a form a reader can sit down with: a compilation on the magical and healing properties of stones, plants, birds, fishes, and animals, organised by the letters of the Greek alphabet, which cites Hermes as its fountainhead and stands as the fullest single exhibit of what the technical Hermetica were for. Scholarship has called it, in words the modern accounts repeat, a virtual encyclopaedia of amulets. It is the book this article gives three sections, because it is the one place where the family can be studied entire rather than reconstructed.

The Shape of the Book

The Kyranides in its conventional form comprises four books. The first book, the Kyranis proper, is the compilation’s heart and its only fully self-contained part. It is organised by the twenty-four letters of the Greek alphabet, and under each letter it treats a quartet: a stone, a plant, a bird, and a fish whose Greek names begin with that letter. The four members of a quartet are not a filing accident. They are held to share a nature — a sympathy, running across the kingdoms of nature along the rail of the letter — and the entry for each letter sets out, in paraphrase of the entries’ pattern, the virtues of each member, the greater power of the members in combination, and the amulet to be made from them: the stone engraved with its prescribed figure, the plant, bird, and fish contributing their parts, the whole worn, carried, fumigated, or applied for the cures the entry lists. Books II–IV then expand the scheme into three further alphabetised books — bestiaries in the broad sense, treating the animals of the land, the birds, and the fishes in turn, each creature under its letter with its virtues, its medical uses, and its amuletic applications. A redactor added the fourth book to an original three; the edition of Kaimakis prints, as fifth and sixth books, further material that travels with the work in a few manuscripts but was not transmitted under the Kyranides name — a reminder, built into the standard edition itself, that this is a texte vivant, a living text, abridged, rearranged, and supplemented across its whole transmission, existing in no single authoritative form.

Editions and Translations: How the Book Is Read

The modern reader meets the Kyranides through three doors, and the article names them because the hub’s standard requires a named text behind every claim. The older Greek edition is Ch.-É. Ruelle’s, printed in F. de Mély’s Les lapidaires de l’antiquité et du moyen âge (1898–1899), from which Festugière and his generation worked. The standard modern edition is Dimitris Kaimakis, Die Kyraniden (1976, in the series Beiträge zur klassischen Philologie) — a critical Greek text, with the limitation the bibliographies note: Kaimakis did not take the Latin version into account, though the Latin sometimes preserves the better reading where the Greek tradition has corrupted. The Latin Cyranides is itself a primary witness: translated at Constantinople in 1169 — the prologue’s own dating, in the old English version, gives 1168 — by Paschalis Romanus, a cleric with medical expertise who served as Latin interpreter to the emperor Manuel I Komnenos, and edited in modern times by Louis (Armand) Delatte in Textes latins et vieux français relatifs aux Cyranides (1942). In English, the first book is served by Maryse Waegeman’s Amulet and Alphabet (1987), a translation and commentary on selected passages of Book I; and the whole work has an early modern English dress, The Magick of Kirani King of Persia (1685), translated from the Latin, from which Section IX’s one verbatim quotation is taken. A new critical edition with English translation, by Richard Greenfield and Alexander Alexakis for the Dumbarton Oaks Medieval Library, has been announced for 2028; until it appears, Kaimakis and Delatte remain the doors.

The prologue in that 1685 English version states the book’s claim with a completeness no paraphrase improves, and it is quoted here — briefly, verbatim, and under its translator’s title — as the family’s title page in its own words. Of the book’s contents it says: “twenty four Stones, twenty four Fishes, twenty four Herbs, and twenty four Birds are written of”; and of its authority: “He that received this Book from GOD, was Hermes Trismegistus.” (The Magick of Kirani King of Persia, London, 1685, prologue, in the spelling of the printed text.) The same prologue commands the book be kept from the ignorant and delivered by a father to his children “instead of Gold”, under adjuration to secrecy — the technical family’s transmission ethic, stated as a term of the gift.

// THE NAME //

Kyranides is a plural: the books of Kyranos. The work’s own etymology, offered in the prologue, plays on a Greek word for queens — the books are “queens of all the rest” of their compiler’s books — and modern scholarship records the name’s origin as, frankly, unexplained: attempts to derive it have satisfied no one. The spelling follows the manuscripts’ majority; Cyranides, Kiranides, and the trial record’s Coeranis are the same book wearing its transmission.

X. The Kyranides II: Sources, Layers, and the Stele Fiction

The Kyranides does not present itself as a compilation, and everything about its actual composition must be recovered against its self-presentation. What it presents is a discovery. The work opens with an injunction to secrecy and a narrative — in fact, two layered narratives, whose seams the prologues do not trouble to hide — of how the book came down from antiquity on stone. In one strand, the material belongs to Kyranos, imagined as a king of Persia, who received it from Hermes Trismegistus himself. In the other, it belongs to Harpocration of Alexandria, who addresses his book — the Therapeutic Book — to his own daughter, and tells how, travelling toward Babylon and Seleucia, he found the text inscribed on a stele. Both strands agree on the furniture of the fiction: an original inscription, in Syriac letters on an iron pillar, carried east and copied; a translation from a foreign tongue; an ancient book — the Archaikē, the “Ancient” — of which the surviving Kyranis is the sequel and reworking. The motifs are the technical family’s standard credentials, and the hub has met them before: the vault and the statue under which Balinas finds the Emerald Tablet, the library in which Thessalos finds Nechepso. A technical book authorises itself by being found, preferably inscribed, preferably in a language the finder must translate, always older than any reader who might check.

Against the fiction, the scholarship’s layered account, in paraphrase, runs as follows. The compilation in something like its received form is conventionally placed in the fourth century CE, though the dating of its parts is argued over in both directions — the first book and its prologue may be older, with proposals reaching toward the first or second century, and one line of scholarship has sought its Harpocration in the circle of the emperor Julian. Its materials are older than any of its frames: Greek lapidary and herbal lore, Egyptian and Syrian traditions the text itself advertises, Babylonian motifs behind them, and the whole stock of Mediterranean folk medicine that Pliny had already quarried from the Persian Magi and their kind. Nothing in the language or content indicates a real translation from Syriac or Persian: the work is, on the standard judgement, a Greek original, probably Alexandrian in its formation, wearing translation as a credential. The Byzantine compiler — or compilers, across the centuries of its living transmission — fused the Kyranos material and the Harpocration material, prefixed the prologues, and let the seams show, because seams between authorities were not a defect in a book whose claim was accumulation. A fifth strand of the layering is visible only in summary: the alchemical commentator Olympiodorus preserves, in paraphrase, a passage of the work absent from the abridged text that survives — Hermes speaking riddlingly, in the Ancient Book, of the egg as the substance of gold-solder and of the moon, and of creatures that were once men and were cursed for revealing the secrets of the sun. The passage matters beyond its strangeness: it shows the Kyranides circulating, by the sixth century, in a fuller form than ours, and being read by an alchemical commentator as a source on the art’s first principles. The medical bestiary and the alchemical commentary are, once again, one readership.

KYRANOS OF PERSIA

The royal attribution: a Persian king as the book’s first human holder, receiving from Hermes what Hermes received from God. Persia supplies what Egypt supplies elsewhere in the family — antiquity, magi, and a court where nature’s secrets were state knowledge.

HARPOCRATION OF ALEXANDRIA

The scholarly attribution: an Alexandrian man of letters writing to his daughter — the family’s father-to-child frame in its domestic form — and finding his text on a stele in the east. The same name the Greek Thessalos tradition accidentally gives to Thessalos’s own letter: the attributions wander within a small, reusable cast.

THE IRON PILLAR

The material credential: Syriac letters on iron, a text built to outlast empires, found, measured, and translated. Inscription fictions are the technical family’s charter genre — compare the Emerald Tablet’s vault, in the hub’s Emerald Tablet article.

THE BYZANTINE COMPILER

The actual author, in the only sense the evidence supports: anonymous, layered, and unembarrassed by plurality. The book’s unity is the alphabet’s, not an author’s — which is precisely what made it usable, excerptable, and immortal.

The book’s later languages complete the layering. A ninth-century Arabic translation carried the first book east — in the Arabic version, on the standard account, the references to Hermes fall away, the book naturalised under its other authorities; Isabel Toral-Niehoff’s edition (2004) prints the Arabic Kitāb Gīrānis against its Greek parallels, and Manfred Ullmann has published the Arabic fragments of Books II–IV. Portions of the first book are reflected, further west, in the Old French Livre des secrez de nature. The Latin of Paschalis Romanus (Section IX) made the book a European medical-magical standard, “dangerous and disreputable” in the judgement the later Middle Ages passed on it and copied anyway — Thomas Browne, in the seventeenth century, could still describe it, in paraphrase of his Pseudodoxia Epidemica, as a collection out of Harpocration the Greek and sundry Arabic writers delivering the natural and magical properties of things. Reputation and use travelled separately, as they do throughout this family.

XI. The Kyranides III: At the Bench and the Bedside

It remains to watch the book being used, because use is what the alphabet is for. A philosophical treatise is organised by argument; the Kyranides is organised by lookup. The practitioner who owns the book owns an index of the natural world keyed to the twenty-four letters every literate Greek carried in his head. A patient presents with a complaint; a substance comes to hand — a stone from a dealer, a bird from a market, an herb from a root-cutter; the question is what it is for. The letter gives the chapter; the chapter gives the virtues, the preparations, and the amulet. No theory need be consulted, because the theory — sympathy along the letter’s rail, antipathy across the kingdoms — is built into the shelving.

The Quartet at Work

Take the first book’s procedure in its typical form, in paraphrase of the entries’ common pattern. Under a letter stand the stone, the plant, the bird, and the fish. The entry treats each in turn: the stone’s appearance and provenance, and the figure to be engraved on it — the Kyranides is among our fullest sources for the engraved magical gem, that staple artefact of the Roman world, and its instructions stand behind thousands of surviving stones; the plant’s powers, in potion, in fumigation, worn or laid on; the bird’s parts and their uses; the fish’s likewise. Then the combination: the engraved stone set with the plant beneath it, a part of the bird or fish enclosed with them, the whole bound, sealed, and worn on the body, or hung in the house, or buried at a threshold, as the entry directs, for the purposes the entry lists — against fevers and demons, for the eyes, for the womb, for favour, for love, for victory in court. The four kingdoms of nature, summoned by a letter of the alphabet, are made to act as one instrument. It is the Sacred Book’s ring procedure (Section VIII) generalised from the thirty-six decans to the whole of creation: image plus stone plus plant plus creature, sympathy made portable.

The Bedside Repertory

The bedside entries, sampled here in paraphrase from the modern accounts of the text, show the repertory’s range and its texture. A chapter on the water snake prescribes its bezoar — the stone from the creature’s body — against dropsy. Fish gall is recommended for white spots in the eye, and fish liver against blindness: eye medicine from the creatures of clear water, sympathy by element and by organ at once. Bear’s hair, fumigated or worn, turns away evil spirits and fever — the demon and the disease in one breath, because the book does not distinguish them as separate departments. Against the child-stealing demon Gello, blamed for miscarriage and infant death, the book offers its spells; and the aetites, the eagle-stone, is worn as an amulet against miscarriage — a stone that rattles with a stone inside it, a pregnancy in mineral form, carried to protect the human one. Aphrodisiac entries stand a page from the obstetric ones; a lizard’s tail or a skink’s molar, carried, is prescribed with the same gravity as the dropsy cure. The modern reader who finds the mixture incoherent has imported the departments. In the book’s world, the womb, the eye, the fever, and the demon are all jurisdictions of the same administration of sympathies, and the practitioner’s art is knowing the addresses.

// WHY THE LEARNED LOOKED DOWN — AND COPIED IT ANYWAY //

The Cyranides was held in relatively low esteem, in antiquity and the Middle Ages alike, by the standards of Hippocratic and Galenic medicine: its Greek is vernacular rather than Atticising, and its authority is lore — the root-cutter’s, the gem-cutter’s, the market’s — rather than theory. The judgement is recorded here as the scholarship records it, because it explains the book’s transmission: it survived not as a school text but as a working one, copied by practitioners, excerpted into handbooks, translated by a court interpreter with medical training, and confiscated, in the fourteenth century, from a working magician’s library (Section XIV). Low esteem and long life are, in this family, the usual pair.

Set the Kyranides beside the philosophical treatises one last time in this section, because the contrast is the article in small. The Corpus Hermeticum tells its reader that the cosmos is a living whole, pervaded by divine reason, its parts bound in sympathy — Stoic physics baptised in Platonism, in the sibling article’s account. The Kyranides assumes the same cosmos and asks for its index. Between the doctrine and the index there is no contradiction and no condescension that the texts themselves observe: the index is the doctrine, alphabetised for the bench. If the hub’s reader retains one image of the technical Hermetica, it should be this one — not a magus in a temple, but a practitioner with a book open at a letter, a stone in a vice, and a patient waiting: Hermes not contemplated, but consulted.

XII. The Alchemical Hermetica: Hermes as Workshop Authority

The alchemical branch of the family is treated briefly here, by design: this hub gives alchemy its own articles, and this section’s task is only to place Hermes inside them. The placing is quickly done, because Hermes is not one authority among others in the alchemical corpus. He is its charter. From the first century BCE onward, Greek works on alchemy were attributed to Hermes Trismegistus; those works are now all lost, except for fragments preserved in the alchemical writers of the second and third centuries CE who quoted them. The pattern is the fountainhead’s pattern repeated in the workshop: the oldest stratum survives as citation inside its readers, and its authority grew, as lost authorities’ authority does, in proportion to its unavailability.

The largest surviving fragment of the stratum travels under another name of the family’s inner circle: Isis the Prophetess to Her Son Horus, in which the goddess — the Isis of the Kore Kosmou, in her alchemical register — transmits to her son the secret she received, in the fragment’s frame, from the angels who came down to women: the recipe of the work, the making of gold and silver, delivered as a mother’s legacy under oath of secrecy. Beside it stands the body of Hermetic material known through the family’s greatest ancient reader. Zosimos of Panopolis (active about 300 CE), whose article in this hub — Zosimos of Panopolis — owns his work in full, cites Hermes as the alchemists’ scripture: he quotes a book of Hermes On Natures, sets sayings of Hermes and the philosophers in doxography at the head of the art, and reports Hermes and Zoroaster together declaring the race of philosophers superior to fate — the technical family’s astrology and its alchemy shaking hands in a single citation. In the hub’s summary of the alchemical canon’s own ordering, Hermes stands at its head as its divine author, the authority that does not need to argue.

The Physica of Hermes

Zosimos is also our only source for a series of Hermetic treatises he calls the Physica — the “Books on Nature” — and the passage in which he reports them, preserved in Greek excerpt and in Syriac translation of his letter to Theosebeia, is the alchemical family’s foundation myth in its author’s own tradition. In paraphrase of Zosimos’s report, following Christian Bull’s study of it (2018): the holy scriptures say that a race of angels desired women, descended, and taught them all the works of nature; Hermes mentioned this in his Physica, and nearly every book, secret and public, repeats it. From those angels’ teaching the giants were born; and the first transmission of the arts came through Chemeu, whose book Hermes named — whence, in Zosimos’s etymology, the art itself is called chemeia. The later commentator Olympiodorus identifies Chemeu with Agathodaimon, the Good Daimon, Hermes’s own teacher in the tradition’s genealogy. Bull’s argument, noted in Section II, presses the passage’s implication: a technical treatise of this kind plausibly stands behind material in the philosophical Perfect Discourse and the Kore Kosmou themselves. If he is right — and the article records the argument as an argument, at the strength its author gives it — then the oldest alchemical Hermetica are not merely contemporary with the philosophical family’s sources. They are among them.

HERMES IN THE WORKSHOP

The name over the furnace door: the art is the “Hermetic art” in the tradition’s own later usage, its vessels “Hermetically” sealed — the god of the written recipe become the adjective of the closed vessel. The usage is medieval and later, recorded here as usage, and it measures the attribution’s success.

THE EMERALD TABLET — A POINTER, NOT A RETELLING

The most famous alchemical Hermeticum is an Arabic text of the late eighth or early ninth century, first attested in the Secret of Creation of pseudo-Balinas, with no ancient Greek or Latin manuscript behind it. Its story is owned, in full, by the hub’s Emerald Tablet article, and is deliberately not retold here. It is named in this section for one reason only: to mark it as the technical family’s most successful medieval member, and its most misdated.

What the workshop took from Hermes, beyond the name, was a theory of the book. Alchemical writing in the Greek corpus is citation all the way down: Zosimos expounds Maria and Democritus, the commentators expound Zosimos, and at the head of every chain stands a Hermetic saying, a Hermetic book, a Hermetic revelation to Isis or by Chemeu, licensing the chain. The hub’s articles on the art’s origins and its practitioners — on the origins of Greek alchemy, on Maria the Jewess and her apparatus, on the cover-names catalogued in the Decknamen glossary — all work downstream of that licence. The technical Hermetica are the licence’s archive.

XIII. The Magical Borderland

At the family’s edge lies the territory where attribution dissolves into practice: the Greek Magical Papyri, the great modern collection of spell-books, handbooks, and single-sheet charms from Egypt, dating from the second century BCE to the fifth century CE. The papyri are, by this archive’s standing arrangement, Egyptology’s file: the Egyptology division’s Greek Magical Papyri article treats the corpus whole — its voces magicae, its pantheon, its practitioners, its Demotic parallels — and this section confines itself to the border, and to what crosses it under Hermes’ name.

What crosses is, first, the presiding figure. Thoth-Hermes, in the papyri’s world as the Egyptology article describes him, presides over the written spell itself: several handbooks present their contents as temple books of Hermes, copied from sanctuaries — the technical family’s stele fiction in working form, the found book as a spell’s first ingredient. Individual spells cite Hermes as their source or invoke Hermes among their powers; Hermetic prayers travel inside the handbooks, including the Prayer of Thanksgiving that also closes the Latin Asclepius and stands in Coptic in Nag Hammadi Codex VI — a single text at home in the philosophical corpus, the Coptic codices, and the magician’s handbook, which is Fowden’s single milieu in one exhibit. And the traffic’s other direction is as visible: the decans of Sections VI and VIII march through the papyri as invoked powers, the engraved stones of the Kyranides have their workshops in the same towns, and the boundary between a Hermetic book and a magician’s handbook is, on the ground in Roman Egypt, largely a modern cataloguer’s line. Festugière’s third occult science — magic, in papyri of the fourth to seventh centuries reproducing much older sources — is the family’s least bookish branch precisely because its books were tools, used to pieces.

// WHY THE BORDER IS DRAWN AT ALL //

If the papyri are so Hermetic, why does this hub file them in Egyptology? For the reason the Egyptology article itself gives: the papyri’s centre of gravity is Egyptian — temple, Demotic tradition, and the long history of heka — where the technical Hermetica’s centre of gravity is the Greek attributed book. The border is a shelving decision between two true descriptions, and the cross-references, in both divisions, are the admission printed on the shelf.

One further borderland inhabitant deserves naming before the manuscripts take over: the Arabic magical Hermetica. In the Arabic centuries, Hermes — identified with the prophet Idrīs and multiplied, in some accounts, into three Hermeses, as the sibling article notes — presided over a literature of talismans and planetary spirits whose monuments include the pseudo-Aristotelian Hermetica: books purporting to be Aristotle’s instruction of Alexander in the secrets of Hermes, on the names and powers of the planetary spirits, the making of talismans, and the doctrine of the personal “perfect nature”. Extracts fed the Secret of Creation, the Epistles of the Brethren of Purity, and the Ghāyat al-Ḥakīm — the Picatrix of the Latin West. That literature’s transmission is the hub’s Transmission article’s ground, and it is flagged here, as the Tablet was flagged in Section XII, to complete the family’s map without annexing the neighbours’ articles.

XIV. Circulation and Readers: Codices, Companions, and Copyists

A literature is its copies. The philosophical Hermetica travelled in a handful of Byzantine manuscripts of the treatises, and their history can be told as the history of those books. The technical Hermetica travelled inside other books — excerpted into miscellanies, bound into handbooks, quarried into compilations — and their history must be told as the history of three great containers: the astrological codex, the alchemical codex, and the medical-magical handbook. Who copied these texts, and in what company, is recoverable in unusual detail, because the containers survive and have been catalogued.

The Astrological Codices

The astrological Hermetica travelled in the astrological miscellany: the Byzantine codex that binds a working astrologer’s whole library between two boards — Valens and Hephaistio in excerpt, Rhetorius, the Byzantine compilers, calendars, eclipse tables, and, threaded through them, the Hermetic chapters on decans, stars, elections, and the sick. The CCAG (Section V) is the catalogue of exactly these codices, library by library — Florence, Venice, Milan, Rome, Vienna, Paris, London, Athens, Madrid — and its twelve volumes are the nearest thing the family has to a census. The picture the census gives is of a literature continuously excerpted for use: Hermetic chapters are short because the miscellany needed them short, anonymous because the excerptor needed the doctrine and not the frame, and ubiquitous because every generation’s astrologer rebuilt the handbook from the last generation’s. The readers are written into the codices’ margins and colophons across a millennium: court astrologers, monk-copyists, the physicians and magicians of late Byzantium whose Hermetic books have been studied as a group — the doctors, astrologers, and magicians whose manuscripts carried the Sacred Book and its kin from Constantinople, in the end, to the library shelves of Venice and the Renaissance.

The Alchemical Codices

The alchemical Hermetica travelled in the alchemical corpus, whose three principal codices the hub’s Transmission article treats in full: the Marcianus graecus 299 at Venice, the oldest surviving Greek alchemical codex; the Parisinus graecus 2325, a thirteenth-century workshop handbook in character; and the Parisinus graecus 2327, copied in 1478, the tradition’s great expansion. In those codices the works of each alchemical author are interwoven rather than shelved — Zosimos dispersed through the Marcianus, the early authorities excerpted and re-excerpted — and the Hermetic fragments travel exactly as the astrological ones do: as the corpus’s oldest stratum and highest authority, present everywhere as citation, present entire almost nowhere. The Syriac tradition carried part of the same material east — Zosimos’s letter on the Physica survives in Syriac dress — and the Arabic alchemical centuries multiplied Hermes beyond counting, in the Jabirian corpus and its successors, on the road the Transmission article follows.

The Handbook Tradition and Its Readers

The Kyranides travelled in the third container, the medical-magical handbook, and its readers can be named at a precision the other branches envy. Its Latin translator, Paschalis Romanus (Section IX), was himself a reader of the professional kind the book always found: a cleric with medical expertise at a bilingual court, making the Greek East’s working medicine available to the Latin West. Its Arabic translators did the same work a language eastward. And its most famous Byzantine reader is famous for the wrong reason: Demetrios Chloros, a fourteenth-century cleric and copyist, was put on trial for transcribing magical texts, among them the book the record calls the Coeranis — the Kyranides itself, in a court’s spelling. The trial is the family’s reception history in one entry: a working copyist, a working book, and an authority that valued the book exactly as its users did, as an instrument that worked — which is why it was copied, and why it was prosecuted.

The Latin Middle Ages added the family’s most successful single product. The Centiloquium Hermetis — one hundred astrological aphorisms under Hermes’ name, compiled and translated from Greek by Stephen of Messina for King Manfred of Sicily in the 1260s — is known from more than eighty manuscripts and some sixteen printings between 1484 and 1674. Some of its aphorisms trace to Arabic authorities such as Abū Maʿshar and Māshāʾallāh; others may be its translator’s own work, issued under the name that authorised. The Centiloquium is the technical family in its medieval maturity: no longer Egyptian, no longer even certainly translated, but Hermetic by brand and by function — a hundred numbered sentences a Latin astrologer could cite the way his Greek predecessor cited the king’s thirteenth book. Between the fountainhead and the Centiloquium lie fifteen centuries, and the through-line is unbroken: at every point on it, somebody is copying Hermes because the work in hand needs an authority older than itself.

WHO COPIED THEM

Temple and library excerptors in Roman Egypt; Byzantine astrologers, physicians, and monk-copyists; Syriac and Arabic translators; a Constantinople court interpreter; Latin court astrologers; and working magicians whose libraries survive in trial records. A professional readership, end to end.

IN WHAT COMPANY

With Valens and Hephaistio in the astrological miscellanies; with Zosimos, Maria, and Democritus in the alchemical codices; with Galen and the herbalists in the medical handbooks; with the spells in the magical papyri. The technical Hermetica are never found alone, because they were never used alone.

XV. Closing the Loop: Hermes on the Recipe, the Election, and the Remedy

The sibling article closed its account of the philosophical Hermetica by explaining why they sit beside alchemy in this hub: because alchemy, in its Alexandrian birthplace, is unintelligible without them — one family theological and contemplative, one operative and practical, one name over both doors. This article can now close the loop from the other side. The technical Hermetica are the bench-level literature of the same world Wing II’s named practitioners worked in — not a background hum behind the hub’s alchemists, but the actual paper on their actual benches. When Zosimos cites Hermes On Natures before he describes an apparatus, he is consulting this article’s library. When the Sacred Book prescribes a decan’s ring, it prescribes for the same hands that cut the magical gems in the same towns and set the stones the Kyranides alphabetises. When a Byzantine physician copies a decumbiture fragment into the same codex as his Galen, and a Constantinople interpreter translates the Kyranides for a Latin-reading court, and a Sicilian king commissions a hundred Hermetic aphorisms, the family is not influencing events from a shelf. It is at work.

Three sentences, one for each of the family’s great departments, state what Hermes meant in it. In astrology, Hermes is the name on the election: the authority that the right hour exists, that it can be found, and that finding it is the difference between the work that prospers and the work that fails — the doctrine the fountainhead received from him and every katarchic chapter since has cited him for. In medicine, Hermes is the name on the remedy: the guarantee that the stone, the plant, the bird, and the fish of the letter belong together, that the decan who sent the illness will, properly approached, withdraw it, and that the ring on the patient’s finger is a cosmos in working order. In alchemy, Hermes is the name on the recipe: the first transmitter, through Chemeu and Isis and the fallen angels’ stolen curriculum, of an art whose every later book is a commentary on a Hermetic sentence nobody can produce. Philosopher Hermes tells the soul that the stars’ administration can be transcended. Technical Hermes sells the soul — and the body, and the furnace — the administration’s complete files. The hub’s Wing II needs both Hermeses, because its practitioners, demonstrably, had both.

A last word on evidence, in the hub’s manner. The technical Hermetica are the worse-attested family and the better-used one, and the two facts are the same fact: working books are excerpted, worn out, translated loose, and reattributed, where treasured books are copied whole. What survives of this family survives the way tools survive — in pieces, in other people’s kits, with the handles replaced. The scholarship that has rebuilt it — Festugière’s taxonomy, Gundel’s edition, Kaimakis’s text, the CCAG’s census, Fowden’s milieu, and the specialist work of the last generation on decans, Thessalos, and the Kyranides — is correspondingly a scholarship of fragments honestly labelled. This article has followed it in that honesty: paraphrase where the wording is not verifiable, ranges where the dating is argued, arguments recorded as arguments. The name on the recipe was never the recipe’s author. But for a thousand years it was the reason the recipe was copied, trusted, and tried — and a history of Wing II’s world that left it out would have the practitioners without their library, and the library’s quieter half, the dialogues, without the working world that made their author worth inventing.
