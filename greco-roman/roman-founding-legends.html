THE FOUNDING LEGENDS OF ROME

Lavinium · Alba Longa · the Palatine · tradition to 509 BCE

// AENEAS :: ALBA :: THE TWINS :: SEVEN KINGS //

Every other city in this hub has its myths told about it. Rome is the city that told its myths about itself — in prose, on the record, with dates attached — and then built an empire on the telling. This article treats those charter legends as what they are: legends, with a textual history that can be traced stratum by stratum, from a closing passage of Hesiod to the antiquarian arithmetic that fixed a birthday for a city. Legend is not failed history. Read properly, it is evidence of the first order — for the people who needed it.

I. A City That Wrote Its Own Beginning

Rome is unusual among the cities of this hub, and the unusualness is the subject. The myths of Thebes, of Argos, of Troy itself, come down to us chiefly in poems — in epic, tragedy, and lyric, where a story's business is to be sung, not to be believed on oath. Rome's founding stories come down chiefly in prose histories: in Livy's first book, in the Roman Antiquities of Dionysius of Halicarnassus, in Plutarch's paired Lives of Romulus and Numa. These are works that present themselves as records. They give regnal years. They synchronise the fall of Troy with Olympiads. They argue, in the open, about which of two rival dates for the city's birthday is correct. The Roman foundation legend is therefore not best approached as a tale that happens to have been written down. It is a constructed civic past: assembled by named writers, at datable moments, out of Greek myth, local cult, family pride, and chronological arithmetic, and revised whenever the city's needs changed. This article follows the construction, stratum by stratum, and keeps the scaffolding visible throughout.

That discipline requires three distinctions to be held at every step, and the reader should hold the article to them. First, legend is distinguished from cult practice: that Romans sacrificed to the Penates at Lavinium is a fact of religion, attested and datable; that Aeneas carried those Penates out of burning Troy is the legend the cult carried, and belongs to a different order of claim. Second, the Greek original is distinguished from the Roman reception: the earliest surviving notice connecting Italy with the heroic age is Greek, is about Odysseus rather than Aeneas, and knows nothing of Rome by name; the Aeneas-centred, Rome-centred version is a later synthesis, and the seams show. Third, speculation and scholarly dispute are labelled as such, with positions named: where modern scholarship divides — over the Capitoline Wolf's date, over Andrea Carandini's Palatine wall, over how much of the king list can be salvaged — this article gives the division, not a verdict dressed as consensus.

The shape of the whole, before the detail. A Trojan refugee, Aeneas, escapes the sack of Troy and comes by sea to Italy, where he founds Lavinium and leaves his household gods, the Penates, in its keeping. His son founds Alba Longa, whose line of kings — the Silvii — reigns, the tradition says, for some four centuries, bridging the gap between Troy's fall and the eighth century. From that line, through a dispossessed king, a violated Vestal, and a she-wolf, come the twins Romulus and Remus; Romulus survives the augury contest and the killing of his brother, and founds Rome on the Palatine on 21 April of a year the scholars would eventually fix as 753 BCE. Seven kings follow in sequence — Romulus, Numa, Tullus Hostilius, Ancus Marcius, Tarquinius Priscus, Servius Tullius, Tarquinius Superbus — until the last is expelled in 509 BCE and the Republic begins. Every element of that paragraph is load-bearing in Roman identity, and almost every element can be shown to be a join: two originally separate legends (a Trojan Aeneas in Latium; a local founder Romulus) riveted together by the Alban king list, which exists, as Section V argues, largely in order to be the rivet. The poem that fused the whole into Rome's permanent self-image, Virgil's Aeneid, has its own article in this hub — The Aeneid — and this article's last narrative duty, in Section XIV, is to hand the story over to it.

THE THREE PRINCIPAL WITNESSES

Livy, Ab Urbe Condita Book 1 (late first century BCE): the canonical Roman telling, brisk, sceptical at the edges, apologetic where the story shames Rome. Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Roman Antiquities Books 1–4 (published from 7 BCE): a Greek writing Rome's past for Greeks, fuller, more antiquarian, and our best preserver of the lost early historians. Plutarch, Lives of Romulus and Numa (c. 100 CE): biography, not history, and invaluable precisely because he reports variants his sources disagreed on.

II. The Greek Stratum: Odysseus' Sons, Stesichorus, Hellanicus

The Aeneas legend did not begin as Rome's. It began as a Greek habit of mind: the habit of mapping the peoples of the western Mediterranean onto the genealogies of heroic myth, so that a foreign nation became legible as somebody's descendants. Greeks had been planting colonies in Italy and Sicily since the eighth century BCE; the mythic map was drawn over the colonial one, and Italy filled up with the offspring and the landfalls of Homeric heroes. The earliest surviving text in the whole dossier belongs to this Greek stratum, and its hero is not Aeneas. It is Odysseus.

Hesiod's Closing Lines: Latinus, Son of Odysseus

The Theogony ends — in lines 1011–1016, whose place in the poem is discussed below — with the children Circe bore to Odysseus. In Evelyn-White's Loeb translation (1914), consulted for this article, Circe “loved steadfast Odysseus and bare Agrius and Latinus who was faultless and strong”, and the sons, the passage concludes, ruled over the famous Tyrrhenians, far off in a recess of the holy islands. Three facts in those lines carry the whole Greek stratum. Latinus is the eponym of the Latins — the people of Latium, Rome's own people — here given a Greek father and a divine mother. The Tyrrhenians are the Etruscans, the Latins' powerful northern neighbours, folded into the same genealogy. And the father is Odysseus: in the earliest surviving version, Italy's peoples descend not from Troy's survivor but from Troy's destroyer — from the Greek hero whose wanderings the Odyssey had already scattered across the western sea. Rome is not named. Aeneas is not mentioned. The connection between Latium and the heroic age is older than the connection between Latium and Aeneas, and it runs through the other side of the Trojan War — a point worth holding, because the finished Roman legend, in which Rome is Trojan to the core, can make its Greek and Odyssean beginnings invisible.

// THE STATE OF THE HESIODIC TEXT //

The closing lines of the Theogony are not beyond dispute. The line naming a third son, Telegonus, is bracketed by editors as a probable interpolation, and the whole Circe passage has been regarded by some scholars as an addition rather than Hesiod's own — the genealogical catalogue at the poem's end shades into the separate Catalogue of Women, and its seams were visible in antiquity. The passage is reported here as the tradition transmits it, attributed to Hesiod, with the caveat stated: what is certain is that the lines are early — archaic Greek, not Roman-era fabrication — and that they attest a Greek, pre-Roman linking of Latium to the Odyssean world.

The Odyssean line did not die when the Aeneas line rose. Dionysius (Roman Antiquities 1.72), cataloguing the rival accounts with an antiquarian's patience, reports the historian Xenagoras for a version in which Odysseus and Circe have three sons — Romos, Antias, and Ardeas — who found three cities and give them their names: Rome itself, in this telling, is an Odyssean foundation, named for a son of Circe. The detail is precious precisely because it lost. It shows the foundation of Rome still unsettled property in the Greek imagination, available to be assigned to whichever hero a given author's scheme required, centuries before the Roman state fixed its own version and exported it back to the Greek world as fact.

Stesichorus: Aeneas Sails West

Aeneas enters the western story, so far as surviving evidence goes, with the lyric poet Stesichorus of Himera in Sicily (c. 630–555 BCE), whose Sack of Troy treated the city's fall at a scale the epic Cycle's version — told in this hub's Trojan Cycle — did not exhaust. The poem is lost, but a marble relief of the early Empire, the Tabula Iliaca Capitolina, illustrates the sack “according to Stesichorus”, as its inscription says, and shows Aeneas at the ships, embarking with Anchises, his son, and the household gods, bound — the scene's standard interpretation — for Hesperia, Italy. The attribution rests on that inscription and the relief's iconography, and is reported at that strength. But the geography is eloquent: a Sicilian Greek poet is the first witness we can name who sends Troy's refugee toward Italy. The Etruscans took the story up early — Aeneas carrying Anchises appears on vases from southern Etruria from the late sixth century BCE — material evidence that it circulated in Italy, in non-Roman hands, generations before any Roman wrote it down.

Hellanicus: Aeneas Founds Rome

The decisive Greek step — Aeneas not merely reaching Italy but founding Rome — is credited by Dionysius (1.72) to Hellanicus of Lesbos, the great mythographer of the later fifth century BCE. In the account Dionysius attributes to him, Aeneas came to Italy in company with Odysseus — the two wanderers of the two strata travelling together, in a synthesis that already betrays a systematiser at work — and founded the city, naming it after Romē, one of the Trojan women who had sailed with him: Romē, weary of wandering, persuaded the other women to burn the ships, stranding the expedition in Italy for good. Dionysius attributes a similar account to Damastes of Sigeum, Hellanicus' contemporary. Two features deserve emphasis. First, in Hellanicus there is no chronological gap: Aeneas founds Rome himself, within a generation of Troy's fall — the four-century problem the Alban king list would later be built to solve (Section V) does not yet exist, because no one has yet fixed Troy's fall and Rome's founding far enough apart to create it. Second, the founder's city is named for a woman, and a ship-burner at that: the eponymous Romē is one of several rival eponyms the Greek tradition generated — Romos the son of Odysseus, Romus a son of Aeneas in other accounts — of which the Roman tradition's own candidate, Romulus, is only the one that won. The name of Rome was as contested in antiquity as its date would be. What the Greek stratum bequeathed to Rome was not a story but a field of stories, from which Roman writers would select, suppress, and systematise — the subject of the next section.

III. The Roman Stratum: Naevius, Ennius, Fabius Pictor, Cato

Rome began writing its own past in the third century BCE, in the generation of the Punic Wars — the moment when the city, suddenly a Mediterranean power, needed a past commensurate with its present and legible to the Greek world it was defeating and joining. The Roman stratum of the foundation legend is the work of four writers across roughly a century. All four are lost except in fragments and citations. All four can be placed, and their contributions distinguished, with reasonable confidence — and the distinctions matter, because they show the legend still molten, its central genealogies not yet fixed, well into the second century BCE.

c. 235 BCE

NAEVIUS, BELLUM PUNICUM

Gnaeus Naevius' epic on the First Punic War — the war his own generation fought — reached back, in a long retrospective movement, to the fall of Troy and Aeneas' flight to Italy: the storm at sea, Venus pleading her son's cause before Jupiter, the sojourn at Carthage that later tradition would make the tragedy of Dido. The poem's surviving fragments establish the architecture Virgil would inherit two centuries later: a Roman war explained by a Trojan origin, the present rooted in the mythic past. In Naevius' genealogy, as the fragments and later testimony are usually read, Romulus stood close to Aeneas — his grandson, in the version most often reconstructed — with no Alban centuries between them. The gap had still not been opened.

c. 180–170 BCE

ENNIUS, ANNALES, BOOK 1

Quintus Ennius' eighteen-book epic of Roman history opened with the fall of Troy and devoted its first book to Aeneas, to his daughter Ilia — mother of the twins by Mars, in Ennius' telling, and herself Aeneas' daughter, which makes Romulus Aeneas' grandson by the direct line — to Ilia's famous dream, reported to her sister in a passage later critics quoted with admiration, and to the twins, the augury, and Romulus' eventual taking-up into heaven. Ennius gave Rome its first great poetic past, and his chronology was generous to the point of vagueness: later readers computed from his verses a founding centuries earlier than any annalist would allow. What Ennius fixed was not dates but descent: the founder of Rome was of Aeneas' blood, immediately.

c. 216–200 BCE

FABIUS PICTOR, HISTORY OF ROME (IN GREEK)

Quintus Fabius Pictor, a Roman senator who had fought in the Hannibalic War and been sent on an embassy to Delphi, wrote the first prose history of Rome — in Greek, for a Greek-reading world that needed Rome explained. His history ran from Aeneas to his own day, and the foundation narrative in the form the later tradition inherits — Aeneas in Latium, Lavinium, Alba, the Alban kings, the twins, the founding — is substantially his construction, or his systematisation of materials older than himself. For the twins' story Plutarch names his Greek source: Diocles of Peparethus, whose account, Plutarch says, Fabius followed in most respects — a reminder that even Rome's first historian was working from Greek mythography. Fabius dated the founding to the first year of the eighth Olympiad, 748/7 BCE (Section VIII): the first Roman date, and already a computation, not a memory.

c. 168–149 BCE

CATO, ORIGINES, BOOK 1

Marcus Porcius Cato — the Censor — wrote the first history of Rome in Latin, and its first book treated the foundations of Rome and of the Italian peoples city by city: Aeneas' landing, the Aborigines who already held Latium, King Latinus, the marriage alliance, Lavinium, Alba. Cato's handling, so far as the fragments let us judge, was already rationalising at the edges — peoples and places explained by migration and merger rather than by miracle — and his chronology became the tradition's anchor: Rome founded 432 years after the fall of Troy, which Dionysius, comparing Eratosthenes' date for Troy (1184/3 BCE), converts to the first year of the seventh Olympiad, 752/1 BCE. With Cato, the two ends of the story — Troy and Rome — are both dated, and the distance between them is a stated number. The Alban king list exists to fill exactly that number of years.

Stand back from the four and the pattern is clear. The poets came first, and for the poets the descent from Aeneas was direct and the centuries did not matter. The historians followed, and for the historians centuries were the whole problem, because Greek chronography — the discipline Eratosthenes had just founded — supplied a date for Troy's fall (1184/3 BCE) that no genealogical compression could reconcile with an eighth-century Rome. Between Naevius' grandson-Romulus and Livy's fifteen Alban kings lies the entire labour of the Roman stratum: the invention, or elaboration, of a middle. Two cautions belong here. “Invention” need not mean cynical fabrication: king lists, genealogies, and synchronisms were the ordinary instruments by which the ancient world organised a past it possessed as stories, and Fabius and Cato used them in good faith on materials they believed. And the strata did not stop moving with Cato: the later annalists — Calpurnius Piso, Valerius Antias, Licinius Macer — kept adjusting numbers, names, and motives, and Dionysius and Plutarch preserve their variants. The systematisation this hub's Prose Systematizers article traces in Apollodorus and his kind was, in Rome, a centuries-long civic project, and its product — the smooth narrative of Sections IV–XII below — should be read with the joins kept in view.

IV. Lavinium and the Penates: Aeneas on the Ground

If the Aeneas legend has a home address, it is not Rome. It is Lavinium, on the coast of Latium some thirty kilometres south of the city, at modern Pratica di Mare — and the priority is itself a piece of evidence. A legend invented at Rome, for Rome, would have brought Aeneas straight to the Palatine. Instead the tradition lands him down the coast, marries him to a local king's daughter, seats his gods in a Latin town that is not Rome, and makes Rome a granddaughter-city, founded two removes later. Traditions that preserve inconvenient geography are usually older than the interests they inconvenience: the Lavinium stage of the story looks like a genuinely Latin, pre-Roman or not-only-Roman possession, which Rome adopted — and then, characteristically, put under state supervision.

The story as Livy (1.1–2) and Dionysius (1.55–65) tell it, in paraphrase. Aeneas, escaping Troy with his father, his son Ascanius, and the gods of his house, wanders the Mediterranean and comes at last to the Laurentine shore of Latium. The land is held by Latinus, king of the Aborigines, the people already there. War threatens, then does not: Latinus, warned by an oracle that his daughter Lavinia is destined for a foreign bridegroom, makes alliance and marriage instead — though Lavinia had been promised to Turnus, king of the neighbouring Rutuli, and the war comes anyway, through Turnus' resentment and, in the fuller tellings, the meddling of Juno and the Fury Allecto: that war, its duels, and Turnus' death at Aeneas' hands are the matter of the Aeneid's second half, and are told in this hub's Aeneid article. Aeneas founds a city and names it Lavinium for his wife. He reigns briefly — three years in Livy's reckoning — and dies, killed in battle against the Rutuli and their Etruscan allies at the river Numicus, or simply vanishing there; the tradition promoted him, in death, to a god of the place, Aeneas Indiges, the native-born god, with a hero-shrine on the Numicus' bank.

THE PENATES

The Penates were the gods of the store-cupboard and the household's continuity — in the legend, the gods Aeneas rescued from Troy and carried to their destined home. Their Roman cult was seated at Lavinium: Dionysius (1.67–68) describes the sanctuary and its sacred objects, Trojan in origin as the Lavinates maintained, and records the practice that tied Rome to the place permanently — Roman magistrates, on taking office, travelled to Lavinium to sacrifice to the Penates and to Vesta. The legend is legend; the sacrifice was real, annual, and official. On the Penates' place in lived Roman religion, see Roman Religion.

THE THIRTEEN ALTARS

Excavation at Pratica di Mare uncovered a sanctuary of thirteen altars in a row, built and rebuilt between the sixth and second centuries BCE — a federal cult place of the Latin peoples — and, nearby, a monumentalised tomb of the fourth century BCE which its excavators identified with the heroon, the hero-shrine, of Aeneas that Dionysius describes. The identification is an interpretation, and is reported as one; what is not interpretation is that Lavinium was a major Latin sanctuary, of archaic date, organised around cults the literary tradition connects with Aeneas and the Penates. Here, unusually, legend and spade point at the same town.

WHY LAVINIUM MATTERS TO THE METHOD

Lavinium is the strongest case in this article for the middle position of Section XV. The cult is archaic and real; the federal altars are archaic and real; the magistrates' pilgrimage is historically attested. None of it makes a Trojan refugee of the twelfth century BCE historical. What it makes historical is a Latin community of the archaic period that already understood its origins through a figure the Greek world called Aeneas — the legend at work, early, in cult, before any Roman historian touched it.

Ascanius — called also Iulus, the name from which the Julian family would derive itself (Section XIV) — succeeded, and in the tradition's spacing founded Alba Longa a generation after Lavinium, leaving Lavinium itself to his stepmother Lavinia and her unborn son. With that move the story leaves the coast for the Alban hills, and leaves narrative for something stranger: a list.

V. Alba Longa: The King List as a Bridge

State the problem plainly, because the tradition states it plainly once its own dates are set side by side. Troy fell, on Eratosthenes' accepted computation, in 1184/3 BCE. Rome was founded, on the date that became canonical, in 753 BCE. Between Aeneas, who must reach Italy within years of Troy's fall, and Romulus yawns an interval of 431 years — some fourteen generations — for which the heroic story supplied nothing. Romulus had to be of Aeneas' blood, and the blood had to be carried, reign by reign, across the gap. Alba Longa, the “long white city” under the Alban Mount, is the bridge built to carry it, and its king list is the bridge's roadway. This article states the construction as construction, following the broad consensus of modern scholarship: whatever Alba was in archaic Latium — and the Alban hills held an important settlement zone, with cemeteries to prove it — the list of its kings, in the form Livy and Dionysius transmit, is a literary-chronographical artefact, assembled to fill a measured interval.

Read the table as a chronographer, not as a chronicler, and its workmanship declares itself. The names are eponyms and recyclings: the city (Alba), the river (Tiberinus), the hill (Aventinus), the wood (Silvius), the two great names of the generation before (Aeneas, Latinus) used again, a Trojan name for colour (Capys), and a doublet (Capys, Capetus) where the padding had to thicken. Dionysius' list (1.71) is longer than Livy's and differently stocked — he counts the Alban dynasty at some fifteen kings across 432 years, reigns averaging close to the chronographer's conventional generation-length — and the disagreement between the two lists is itself the evidence: a transmitted record does not wobble like this; a constructed bridge, rebuilt by each engineer to his own span, does. Nor is the device a Roman eccentricity. King lists that stretch a line of rulers across an otherwise empty interval are among the oldest instruments of ancient chronography — the reader of this hub's Antiquity division will know the type from the Antediluvian King Lists of Mesopotamia, where the spans are filled with reigns of fabulous length rather than with extra kings. Rome's list is the same instrument in a sober, republican dress: no king reigns an impossible number of years; there are simply enough kings.

// WHAT THE BRIDGE CARRIES //

Once the list is seen as a bridge, several famous features of the tradition rearrange themselves as its cables. Romulus must descend from Aeneas through Alba — hence his mother is an Alban princess, not a Roman one. Rome must be Alba's daughter — hence the twins are exposed, recognised, and first employed on Alban business, and hence Rome will later destroy Alba utterly and absorb its people and noble families (Section XI): the daughter-city devouring the mother is the bridge being dismantled behind the crossing. And the Julian family, claiming descent from Iulus, stands at the bridge's far end with its pedigree running the whole length of the roadway (Section XIV).

None of this makes Alba Longa nothing. The Alban hills held one of archaic Latium's richest settlement zones; the Latin peoples held their common festival, the Feriae Latinae, on the Alban Mount under Jupiter Latiaris into the Empire; and Rome's claim to head the Latin League was argued, in diplomacy and in war, through its Alban descent. A constructed list can be laid over a real landscape, and usually is: construction works best with genuine materials. What the construction cannot do is testify. The Silvii, as individuals, are the least historical persons in this article — and the tradition, to its credit, barely pretends otherwise: it gives most of them no deeds at all, only names and years, the exact silhouette of a bridge.

VI. Romulus and Remus: The Twins, the Wolf, the Foster-Parents

At the bridge's end the list stops and the story — the most famous foundation tale in the western world — begins. It is told here as Livy (1.3–5), Dionysius (1.76–84), and Plutarch (Romulus 3–8) tell it, in paraphrase, with the variants kept where the sources keep them: in the open.

Proca, king of Alba, left two sons. Numitor, the elder, inherited the throne; Amulius, the younger, seized it — drove his brother out, killed his brother's son, and neutralised his brother's daughter Rhea Silvia (the tradition also calls her Ilia) by making her a Vestal Virgin, sworn to a chastity whose breach was death: a dynastic problem solved by a religious office. The solution failed. Rhea Silvia became pregnant, and declared — Livy (1.4) reports her claim and immediately brackets it, remarking in paraphrase that she named Mars as the father perhaps because she believed it, perhaps because a god made a nobler author of the fault — by Mars. Amulius imprisoned her and ordered the twin boys she bore to be thrown into the Tiber. The river was in flood; the servants, unwilling or unable to reach the deep water, left the basket at the flood's edge below the Palatine, by the wild fig tree called the Ficus Ruminalis. The waters fell, the basket grounded, and a she-wolf, coming down from the hills to drink, heard the crying, suckled the twins, and licked them clean — until Faustulus, the king's herdsman, found the scene, took the boys home, and gave them to his wife Acca Larentia to raise among the shepherds of the Palatine.

LUPA: THE WORDPLAY THE SOURCES THEMSELVES SUPPLY

Latin lupa means a she-wolf. It also means, in the slang of the street, a prostitute — the word survives in lupanar, a brothel. Livy, in the very chapter that tells the wolf story (1.4), records the rationalising alternative without blinking: some, he says in paraphrase, hold that Larentia was called lupa among the shepherds because she made her body common, and that this is the origin of the marvellous tale. The pun is not a modern scholar's debunking. It is in the canonical text, in the first century BCE — evidence that the tradition carried its own demystification alongside its miracle from the moment we can read it whole. Plutarch (Romulus 4) reports the same doubleness.

ACCA LARENTIA, TWICE OVER

The foster-mother's name belongs also to a figure of Roman cult: an Acca Larentia honoured at the Larentalia festival in December, with a tomb in the Velabrum where the state made offerings to the dead. Antiquarians already debated whether the heroine of the cult and Faustulus' wife were one person, two, or a name that had migrated between a cult and a story. The debate is reported here unresolved, as the sources leave it: a tidy specimen of legend and cult practice (distinguished, per Section I, as method) growing through one another until the roots cannot be separated.

THE RECOGNITION

Grown, the twins lived as shepherds and hunters, feuding with the herdsmen of the deposed Numitor. In a clash at the Lupercalia, or in an ambush over cattle in the variant tellings, Remus was captured and hauled before Numitor; the old king, questioning a young man whose bearing did not match his station, heard the story of the basket and the wolf, and Faustulus, arriving with the basket itself, confirmed it. Romulus marched on Alba, Remus rose inside it, Amulius was killed, and Numitor restored. The recognition scene is the hinge of the whole legend — the point where the Alban story and the Roman story are bolted together in narrative, as the king list bolts them in chronology.

Grandfather restored, the twins declined to live at Alba as subjects, and resolved to found a city of their own on the hills where they had been exposed and raised — the Palatine country, already filling, in the tradition's telling, with the shepherds, outlaws, and runaways who followed them. But a city can have only one founder and one name, and the twins were equal in birth, in age, and in following. The tradition is unanimous on how they agreed to settle it, and unanimous on nothing else: they would take the auspices, and the gods would choose between them by birds. What the birds decided, and what it cost, is the next section — the darkest in the legend, and the one where the sources' disagreements are most instructive.

VII. The Augury Contest and the Fratricide

Augury — the reading of the gods' will in the flight of birds — was no decorative detail in Roman life; it was constitutional machinery, and remained so into the late Republic (see Roman Religion). The legend accordingly settles its succession crisis by the state's own instrument. The twins took their stations: Remus on the Aventine — or, in a variant, at a place called Remoria — and Romulus on the Palatine. The agreed sign was vultures. To Remus, first, came six. To Romulus, after, came twelve. And there the sources' agreement ends, because the result was double: Remus had priority, Romulus number. Each party proclaimed its own man, Livy (1.6–7) says in paraphrase, the one side claiming by time, the other by the count of birds; argument became quarrel, quarrel a fight. Rome's first constitutional crisis was an ambiguity in its own sacred law, and it was resolved, as the legend insists on remembering, by blood.

Who Killed Remus? The Variants, Kept Visible

On the manner of Remus' death the tradition does not speak with one voice, and this article follows the hub's discipline of keeping the variants in the open rather than silently electing one.

VARIANT A · THE WALL (LIVY'S “MORE COMMON” ACCOUNT)

The founding proceeded: Romulus ploughed the sacred furrow, the pomerium, round the Palatine — the plough lifted and carried where gates were to be, in Plutarch's detailed account (Romulus 11) — and the first walls rose. Remus, in mockery of the work, leapt over the half-built wall. Romulus killed him on the spot, with the words Livy reports in paraphrase as the tradition's grim charter: so perish everyone who shall hereafter leap over my walls. In this variant the fratricide is an act of foundation law: the wall is sacred from its first course, and its sanctity is consecrated by the founder's own brother's blood.

VARIANT B · THE MELEE (LIVY'S OTHER ACCOUNT; DIONYSIUS)

Livy himself gives, first and more briefly, the version in which the killing belongs to the augury quarrel itself: words came to blows between the two followings, and Remus fell in the fighting — killed by no named hand, a casualty of the disputed omen. Dionysius (1.87) tells a version of this type at length, with the battle between the twins' parties fought out and Faustulus himself among the dead. Here the fratricide is not an execution but a civil brawl — less theatrical, and in its way darker: the city begins not with a sentence pronounced but with a riot over a religious technicality.

VARIANT C · CELER'S SPADE (PLUTARCH; OVID)

Plutarch (Romulus 10) reports, among the accounts he canvasses, the version in which the killer was not Romulus at all but Celer, the overseer of the wall-work, who struck Remus with his spade or mattock for the mocking leap — and Romulus, in this telling, buried his brother with public grief, on the Remoria, and concealed his anger at Celer. Ovid's Fasti (Book 4) tells the Celer version and follows it with Remus' ghost, appearing to the aged Faustulus and Acca Larentia to ask for a festival of the dead: the origin, in Ovid's aetiology, of the Lemuria. In this variant Rome is founded not by a fratricide's hand but by a fratricide it must mourn — and the founder's guilt is displaced, conveniently, onto a subordinate.

WHY THE VARIANTS MATTER

The three variants distribute blame three ways — to the founder as lawgiver, to the crowd as accident, to the lieutenant as scapegoat — and a tradition that preserves all three is telling us something about itself: Rome never settled how guilty its founder was. The fratricide was too structurally necessary to drop (it explains why one twin, one name, one city) and too shameful to fix in one form. Later ages would read it as the original sin of Roman history, the first of the civil wars; that reading, pressed by poets of the late Republic who had seen Romans kill Romans in earnest, is reception, and is labelled as such — but it is reception the legend's own unresolved guilt made possible. For Plutarch's Life as a work of systematising biography, see Prose Systematizers.

What no variant disputes is the sequel: Remus dead, Romulus sole founder, the city named — in the Roman tradition's own etymology — Roma from Romulus, as Romē's and Romos' rival claims (Section II) were quietly set aside. The foundation rite fixed a day: the Parilia, 21 April, the festival of Pales, the shepherds' goddess — an older pastoral feast onto which the city's birthday was grafted, and on which Rome celebrated its dies natalis ever after. The year of that day is another matter, and it occupied some of the best minds of antiquity without ever being observed by anyone. It is the subject of the next section.

VIII. The Founding Date: Varro's 753 and Its Rivals

“753 BCE” is printed in every handbook as the year Rome was founded, and it is, in the strictest sense, a scholarly construction of the first century BCE — the conclusion of an argument, not the survival of a record. No document of the eighth century BCE from Rome exists; none from anywhere mentions Rome. The date was reached by counting: backward from the relatively secure chronology of the later Republic, through the consular lists, through the summed regnal years of the seven kings, to a first year for Romulus. Every link was arguable, ancient scholars argued every link, and the spread of their answers — preserved chiefly by Dionysius (1.74), who lists his predecessors' computations with unusual candour — is the honest picture. The canonical date won; it did not win unanimously, and it did not win early.

AuthorityDate givenBasis, as reported

The computation of Cicero's friend Atticus in his chronological handbook, taken up by Marcus Terentius Varro — whence “the Varronian date”, and the era ab urbe condita, “from the founding of the city”, by which Romans thereafter numbered their years.

The table repays a slow reading. The range is eighty-five years — from Timaeus' 814 to Cincius' 729 — among writers who all believed a founding date existed and could be found. The methods differ in kind, not just in result: Timaeus argues by synchronism (Rome and Carthage as coeval rivals, a historian's thesis about the Punic present projected onto the past); Cato by interval from Troy; the Olympiad dates by conversion into the Greek victor-list era that Timaeus himself had done more than anyone to establish. And the Varronian winner carries a rider that Plutarch preserves and that no handbook should omit: Varro's friend Lucius Tarutius of Firmum, an astrologer, undertook to compute the founding backwards from Romulus' horoscope — Plutarch (Romulus 12) reports, in paraphrase, that Tarutius, working from the founder's supposed birth and conception data as an astrologer works from a nativity, fixed the city's conception and birth to the Parilia, at a stated hour, in the third year of the sixth Olympiad. That the canonical date of Rome's founding rests partly on a piece of first-century astrological back-calculation is not a scandal and not a secret; Plutarch prints it without embarrassment, because in his world astrology was a mathematical science. It is, for this archive, simply the clearest possible proof of the section's thesis: 753 is a result, produced by the best scholarly machinery of its day — machinery whose other products (the king list's regnal years, the consular fasti's early names) are exactly the materials Sections V and XII flag as constructed. Modern scholarship's verdict, stated at its usual strength, is that the argument among the dates is unresolvable and in a sense misconceived: there was no founding day for the date to be the date of (Section XIII). What Varro fixed was not Rome's birthday but Rome's era — and as an era, a counting-frame for all subsequent Roman history, 753 BCE did its work superbly for two thousand years.

// THE DAY IS OLDER THAN THE YEAR //

Note the asymmetry the tradition itself preserves. The day — 21 April, the Parilia — was a real festival of the pastoral calendar, older than any chronography, and the founding was attached to it. The year was computed seven centuries later by scholars who disagreed by decades. Rome's birthday is thus a genuine feast day carrying a conjectural year: half cult, half arithmetic, and the two halves have different kinds of truth. The distinction is this article's whole method in miniature.

IX. The Sabine Women

Romulus' city, in the tradition's own blunt analysis, had men and no future: a following of shepherds and exiles, swollen once Romulus opened his famous asylum on the Capitoline by runaway slaves, debtors, and killers from every neighbouring people, welcomed without inquiry. Rome grew, Livy (1.8) says in paraphrase, to a size that made its power formidable; but the greatness was for one generation only, for want of women, since the new Romans had no right of intermarriage — conubium — with their neighbours, and embassies sent to request it were refused, with contempt the tradition does not hide: the neighbours advised Rome to open an asylum for women too. What followed is the legend's most ethically exposed episode, and the sources know it.

Romulus announced games — the Consualia, in honour of Consus, a god of the stored harvest, whose festival fell in high summer — and proclaimed them through the surrounding country. The neighbours came, the Sabines above all, with wives and children, to see the new city and watch the show. At a signal, the Roman youth rushed the crowd and seized the unmarried girls. The numbers were already disputed in antiquity: Plutarch (Romulus 14) canvasses the tallies his sources offered — thirty, in the lowest account, which the tradition connected with the naming of the thirty curiae, the wards of the Roman people; 527 in Valerius Antias; 683 in Juba — and reports that the one woman taken who was already married, Hersilia, was seized by mistake and became, in the main tradition, the wife of Romulus himself. The parents fled; the girls remained; and Livy puts into Romulus' mouth, and into the Romans' courtship of their captives, the tradition's official mitigation — in paraphrase: that the wrong lay with the parents' pride in refusing lawful marriage; that the women would be wives, not concubines, sharers in property, citizenship, and children; that time and kindness would do the rest. The mitigation is part of the record and is reported as such. It is also, plainly, an apologia composed by the descendants of the seized and the seizers alike, and the article flags it at that value: the legend of the Sabine women is a story about a rape — the Latin tradition's own word for the seizure, raptio, means a carrying-off, and the episode's name in every later language is the Rape of the Sabines — which the tradition simultaneously preserves and labours to excuse.

FIRST WARS

THE NEIGHBOURS ANSWER, ONE BY ONE

The wronged peoples did not combine at once — Rome's luck, and the legend's lesson. The men of Caenina attacked first and alone; Romulus killed their king Acron in single combat and dedicated his armour to Jupiter Feretrius — the first spolia opima, Rome's rarest military honour. Antemnae and Crustumium followed and were beaten; their peoples, in the tradition's recurring pattern, were not exterminated but moved to Rome and made citizens. The city grew by absorbing the peoples it defeated — the asylum's logic, extended by war.

THE SABINE WAR

TARPEIA, TATIUS, AND THE CAPITOL

The Sabines, the most formidable of the wronged, came last and in force under their king Titus Tatius of Cures — and got inside Rome not by assault but by bargain. Tarpeia, daughter of the Capitoline garrison's commander, agreed to open a gate, asking in payment what the Sabines wore on their left arms: gold bracelets, in her meaning; their shields, in the event, under which they crushed her to death. The hill of her betrayal kept her name, the Tarpeian Rock, from which traitors were later thrown. The Sabines held the Capitol; the Romans held the Palatine; and the battle for the city was fought in the marshy valley between — the ground that would become the Forum, fought over in the legend before it was paved in fact.

THE INTERVENTION

THE WOMEN BETWEEN THE ARMIES

The battle's end is the episode's reason for existing. The Sabine women — wives now, and mothers of Roman children — ran onto the field between their fathers and their husbands, hair loosed, in Livy's set-piece (1.13), and made the argument that stopped the war, in paraphrase: whichever side won, they lost — fathers or husbands, it was their own blood on both spears; better to kill them, the cause of the war, than to leave them widowed or orphaned by the same afternoon. The men laid down their arms. Peace followed on terms no Roman victory could have imposed: the two peoples became one, under a joint kingship — Romulus and Tatius ruling together — the Sabines settling the Quirinal, the Romans taking the name Quirites, in the tradition's etymology, from Tatius' city of Cures. Tatius was killed five years later in a feud at Lavinium, and Romulus, who did not avenge him with any energy the sources can find, ruled alone thereafter.

What the episode did in the tradition can be read off its deposits. It explained real features of Roman life: the cry of “Talassio!” at weddings and other marriage customs the antiquarians derived from the seizure; the Sabine strain in Rome's population and cults, which was genuine and deep — Numa himself is a Sabine of Cures, and the joint kingship writes the merger into the legend's constitution; and Rome's defining demographic myth, that the city was from its first generation a mixture, made of whoever came. The founder's own end belongs to the same pattern of unresolved telling as his brother's death: reviewing his army by the Goat's Marsh, Romulus vanished in a sudden storm — torn to pieces by the senators, said the whispered version Livy preserves; taken up to heaven, said the official one, confirmed when Proculus Julius reported the deified Romulus descending to commission Rome's empire and to be worshipped under the Sabine name of Quirinus. The legend's founder becomes a god with a Sabine name: the merger outlasts even the man.

X. Numa: The Religious Founder and His Buried Books

After Romulus, an interregnum — the Senate ruling in rotation while the people's two halves, Roman and Sabine, deadlocked over whose turn it was to supply a king — and then a choice that is itself the legend's thesis about what Rome needed second: not another warrior but a lawgiver of the sacred. Numa Pompilius of Cures, a Sabine, famous through Italy for justice and piety, was summoned to a throne he is shown accepting only after the auspices confirmed it. His traditional reign, 715–673 BCE, is the regal period's long peace, and his biography in Livy (1.18–21) and Plutarch's Numa is less a life than a catalogue of institutions, each one pinned to his name. The catalogue is the point: Roman religion, as the Romans themselves told it, was not immemorial custom but a designed system, and Numa was its designer — the second founder, of the city's soul, as the religion is surveyed in Roman Religion.

THE PRIESTHOODS

To Numa the tradition assigns the ordering of Rome's great priesthoods: the pontifex maximus, to whom Numa handed the whole written corpus of the rites; the great flamines of Jupiter, Mars, and Quirinus, the oldest triad's priests, each hedged in taboo; the Vestal Virgins, with their stipend, privileges, and thirty years of service; the leaping Salii, who carried Mars' sacred shields through the March streets; and the fetiales, the herald-priests of declarations of war (Section XI). Whether any one reign created these colleges, no sober reader claims; that Rome attributed its sacred constitution to a single founding legislator is the fact the legend preserves.

THE CALENDAR AND THE YEAR

Romulus' year, in the tradition, had ten months; Numa's reform made twelve, adding January — for Janus, the two-faced god of beginnings, whose temple Numa built and whose doors stood open in war and shut in peace, shut throughout Numa's reign in the legend's proudest boast — and February, the month of purification. To Numa also go the division of days into fasti and nefasti, lawful and unlawful for public business: time itself given a sacred jurisprudence. The details of the reform are antiquarian reconstruction and are labelled as such by the sources' own disagreements (Plutarch's Numa canvasses rival accounts of the months' order); the attribution, again, is the tradition's thesis that Rome's order in time descends from a single mind.

EGERIA, AND THE SHIELD FROM HEAVEN

Numa's authority, in the legend, was underwritten by a divine counsellor: the nymph Egeria, whom he met by night in her grove by the Porta Capena, and whose instruction Livy (1.19) reports with a raised eyebrow — in paraphrase, Numa pretended to nightly meetings with the goddess, that a fierce people might accept sacred laws it would have rejected from a man. To the same reign belongs the ancile, the figure-of-eight shield that fell from heaven as heaven's pledge of Rome's empire: Numa had the smith Mamurius Veturius forge eleven indistinguishable copies against its theft, and gave the twelve to the Salii to carry. A heaven-sent original protected by state-manufactured duplicates: the Roman sacred, in one object.

THE PYTHAGORAS PROBLEM

Later tradition, eager to give Rome's wisdom a Greek pedigree, made Numa the pupil of Pythagoras. Both Livy (1.18) and Plutarch (Numa 1) reject the claim, and reject it on chronology: Pythagoras belongs to the later sixth century, more than a century after Numa's traditional dates — Livy notes he came to Italy only in the reign of Servius Tullius, four reigns too late. The episode is worth the space it takes here because it shows the tradition's own immune system working: the annalistic and biographical sources could compute, could compare dates, and could strike a beloved synchronism out of the record when the arithmetic failed. Legend, in Rome's prose tradition, was never exempt from audit — which is precisely what makes its surviving claims interesting rather than merely old. The Pythagoras claim would nevertheless return, in material form, in 181 BCE: see the buried books, below.

The Buried Books of Numa, 181 BCE

The strangest document in the whole foundation dossier is not about the eighth century at all. It is a news item of the second, preserved by Livy in Book 40 (40.29) — far from the legendary books, in the fully historical narrative — and it is told here as the tradition tells it, from Livy's account, in paraphrase with one short quotation from the Perseus text of his history. In 181 BCE, labourers digging deep on the land of a scribe, Lucius Petilius, at the foot of the Janiculum, uncovered two stone chests, their lids sealed with lead, each inscribed in Latin and Greek. The first chest, its inscription said, held the body of Numa Pompilius; opened, it was empty — no trace of a corpse, everything dissolved by the years. The second held two bundles tied with waxed cord, each of seven books, intact and looking, Livy says, almost new: “seven in Latin on pontifical law, and seven in Greek dealing with the study of philosophy” — so the passage runs in the translation consulted. The annalist Valerius Antias, Livy adds, identified the Greek books as Pythagorean, and Livy himself comments, in paraphrase, that Antias was fitting the detail to the popular belief that Numa had been Pythagoras' disciple — lending a plausible fiction's support to a story the chronology forbids.

The sequel is the part that makes the episode history and not merely anecdote. The books circulated; readers multiplied; the city praetor, Quintus Petilius — a friend and namesake, not (the sources are careful) the same man as the scribe — obtained them, read the most important passages, and judged that most of their content tended, in Livy's paraphrase of his finding, to the dissolution of the established religion. He swore before the Senate that the books ought not to be read or kept; the Senate accepted his oath, ordered compensation offered to the owner (the scribe refused it), and had the fourteen books burned in the Comitium, in public, by the sacrificial attendants. Plutarch's version (Numa 22) differs in furniture — in his telling Numa had the sacred books buried beside him by his own instruction, as things not to be committed to the living voice's custody; the tomb was exposed by rains some four centuries later; the Senate, examining the books, judged them unfit for disclosure and burned them — while Dionysius hints at a third fate, quiet retention in the pontifices' own keeping. The variants disagree, as this article has taught the reader to expect, about everything except the core: books attributed to Rome's religious founder surfaced, were read by authority, and were suppressed by authority. What the episode evidences, at the strength the evidence allows, is not Numa's library. It is the second-century Republic policing its own foundation legend — deciding, by fire, which parts of the founder's inheritance the public might read. The suppression of 181 BCE belongs to the same decade as the Senate's famous crackdown on the Bacchanalia (186 BCE), and the two together show a state unusually conscious that religion was a branch of public order. Numa, four and a half centuries dead in his own legend's arithmetic, was still governing it.

XI. The War Kings: Tullus Hostilius and Ancus Marcius

The tradition arranges the kings in a rhythm — founder, priest, warrior, builder, and then the foreign dynasty — and the rhythm is so neat that the article flags it before telling it: alternating types, each reign defined against its predecessor, is the shape of moralised history, and Livy, who draws the contrasts most sharply, knew the shape was his as much as his material's. With that caution entered, the third and fourth reigns.

TULLUS HOSTILIUS (TRAD. 673–642 BCE)

Grandson of Hostus Hostilius, the Roman champion who fell in the Sabine war of Section IX, Tullus is Livy's anti-Numa: a king who judged his predecessor's peace a corruption of the national fibre and went looking for war. He found it with Alba Longa — Rome's mother-city, in the legend — over border raids and stolen cattle, each side's fetial heralds demanding restitution in the solemn form Ancus' reign would be credited with recording (Livy 1.32 gives the formula under Ancus, from the Aequicoli). The war was settled by champions: the Horatii of Rome against the Curiatii of Alba, triplet brothers against triplet brothers, bound by marriage ties across the lines. Two Horatii fell; the third, feigning flight, killed the wounded Curiatii one by one as they pursued at unequal speeds. Returning in triumph, he met his sister at the gate — betrothed to one of the dead Curiatii, weeping over his cloak on a slain brother's shoulders — and killed her for mourning an enemy. Tried for murder, he was acquitted on appeal to the people: the legend's charter-case for provocatio, the citizen's appeal, lodged inside a fratricidal triumph that mirrors the foundation's own.

THE END OF ALBA

Alba's dictator Mettius Fufetius, summoned to Rome's aid against Fidenae and Veii, hedged his army on the hillside to join whichever side won. Tullus won without him, and punished the treason with the regal period's most theatrical execution: Mettius, rebuked in a speech Livy composes on the theme of the divided mind, was torn apart between two chariots driven in opposite directions — a punishment Livy himself pauses to call the first and last of its kind among Romans, an act at odds with the laws of humanity. Then Alba was destroyed: its people transferred to Rome and settled on the Caelian, its noble families — the Julii, the Servilii, the Quinctii, the Cloelii among them — enrolled in the Roman Senate, its temples, Livy is careful to say, spared. The mother-city was unbuilt by her daughter in an hour, the tradition says, after four hundred years. Read structurally, the episode is Section V's bridge being dismantled: Alba, its chronological work done, is absorbed into Rome body and aristocracy — and the Julii's Alban pedigree (Section XIV) enters Rome precisely here, in the baggage of a destroyed city.

THE DEATH OF TULLUS

Plague came, and Tullus, campaigning sick and finding no relief, turned at last to the religion he had neglected — and botched it. Consulting Numa's commentaries for a secret rite to Jupiter Elicius, the Jupiter who is drawn down from the sky, he performed it wrongly, in Livy's telling (1.31), and was struck by lightning and burned with his whole house. The moral is the tradition's own and is stated by Livy without ornament: the warrior king dies of a misread ritual text, in the house of the priest-king's books. Tullus' reign also carries, in the annalistic tradition, wars with the Sabines and the building of the Senate's first meeting-house, the Curia Hostilia, and the paved Comitium — the Forum's civic furniture beginning to accumulate around the legends.

ANCUS MARCIUS (TRAD. 640–616 BCE)

Numa's grandson through his daughter, Ancus is the synthesis after the antithesis: Livy (1.32–33) gives him his grandfather's piety and a warrior's competence, and has him publish Numa's sacred ordinances on whitened boards in public, lest a generation raised under Tullus forget them — the legend's own image of tradition rescued by republication. His Latin wars follow the fixed Roman pattern: cities taken, their populations transferred to Rome and settled on the Aventine, the hill of newcomers. To Ancus the tradition assigns Rome's reach to the sea: Ostia founded at the Tiber's mouth with its salt-works; the Janiculum fortified and joined to the city by the Pons Sublicius, the first bridge, built of wood without iron — a detail Rome's ritual memory preserved for a thousand years; and the first state prison, the Carcer. He dies in his bed, the last king everyone in the legend agrees to praise — and the throne passes, by election and intrigue, out of the Italian stock altogether.

XII. The Tarquins, Servius Tullius, and the Expulsion of 509

The last three reigns form a single dynastic story — the rule of a house from Tarquinii in Etruria — and they carry the tradition to the edge of history. The sixth century BCE, to which the tradition assigns them, is the century in which archaeology independently shows Rome becoming a monumental city (Section XIII); the correspondence does not validate the biographies, but it changes their status. These are legends told about a real urban transformation, and some of the names in them may be real names. The article flags the whole sequence as legend with that rider attached.

Tarquinius Priscus (trad. 616–579 BCE)

Demaratus, a Corinthian noble exiled by tyranny, had settled at Tarquinii; his son Lucumo, rich, ambitious, and barred there as a foreigner's son, migrated to Rome at the urging of his wife Tanaquil, skilled, Livy says, as Etruscans were, in reading the sky. Entering the city, Lucumo received his omen: an eagle stooped, lifted his cap, circled, and replaced it — which Tanaquil read as a promise of kingship. In Rome he took the name Lucius Tarquinius, with the surname Priscus, “the Elder”; he courted Ancus, became guardian to his sons, and at the king's death sent the sons hunting and took the throne by election — the legend's first succession managed as a coup in constitutional dress. His reign, in Livy (1.35–38), is conquest and construction: Latin towns stormed, the Sabines beaten, the Senate doubled, the Circus Maximus laid out, the city walls begun in stone, and the great temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus vowed on the Capitol. He was murdered in his thirty-eighth year by two shepherds with axes, hired by Ancus' displaced sons; and the succession was snatched by Tanaquil, who announced from a window that the king lived and needed quiet, while her protégé took hold of the state.

Servius Tullius (trad. 578–535 BCE)

The protégé was Servius Tullius, born, in the main Roman telling, to a slave — Ocrisia, a captive in Tarquin's household — and raised in the palace, where a flame played harmlessly round the sleeping child's head, a portent Tanaquil read as kingship. (An Etruscan counter-tradition survives, preserved here as a variant: the Emperor Claudius, in a speech the Lyon tablet partly preserves, reported that Etruscan sources made Servius the companion of the adventurer Caelius Vivenna, named in their tongue Mastarna — and the François Tomb at Vulci indeed shows a Mastarna freeing Caelius Vivenna. The painting is fact; the identifications are interpretation.) Servius' reign, in Livy (1.42–48), is the constitution-maker's: the census, ranking citizens by property into the classes and centuries that structured army and assembly; four urban tribes; the pomerium extended and the hills enclosed in the circuit later called the Servian Wall; and the temple of Diana on the Aventine founded as a federal sanctuary of the Latins — Rome now hosting the league cult, the hegemony of Latium expressed, as always in this story, as a change of cult address. His end was family murder: his daughter Tullia and Tarquin's son, each having disposed of the spouse between them, married; the younger Tarquin seized the throne by force; Servius was cut down in the street, and Tullia drove home over her father's body. The street kept the name Vicus Sceleratus, the Street of Crime.

Tarquinius Superbus, Lucretia, and 509 BCE

Tarquinius Superbus — “the Proud” — reigned, in the tradition (trad. 535–509 BCE), as the tyrant of the Greek pattern-books: no election, no consultation of Senate or people, capital trials without appeal, the leading men executed or exiled, their property confiscated, foreign policy conducted through his sons. Yet the tyranny built: the Capitoline temple, his father's vow, was completed under him and roofed with terracotta quadrigas from Veii; the great drain, the Cloaca Maxima, was driven through the Forum valley by forced labour the tradition dwells on with disgust; Gabii was taken by his son Sextus' feigned defection and a silent lesson in lopping the tallest poppies. To this reign also belongs the Sibylline Books: a strange woman — the Cumaean Sibyl, in the developed tradition — offered Tarquin nine books of prophecy at a price he refused as absurd; she burned three and offered six at the same price; refused again, burned three more and offered the last three at the original price for nine, and Tarquin bought. The books, Greek verse oracles kept in a stone chest under the Capitoline temple and consulted by decree of the Senate in every crisis of the Republic, are historical as an institution whatever one makes of the haggling scene: Rome's state archive of prophecy enters the tradition as a tyrant's purchase, three-ninths burned by negotiation.

The end came from Ardea, where the king's army sat in siege and his sons, drinking in camp, fell to boasting of their wives. They rode to Rome by night to test the boasts, and found the royal daughters-in-law at a banquet — and Lucretia, wife of Tarquinius Collatinus, late at her wool among her maids. Sextus Tarquin returned some nights later as a guest, and came to her room with a sword: when threats of death failed, he threatened the worse thing, to kill her and lay a slave's corpse beside hers, so that she would be remembered as an adulteress taken in the act — and by that threat, Livy (1.58) says in paraphrase, her resolve was broken. In the morning she summoned her father and her husband, each to bring a trusted friend; Collatinus brought Lucius Junius Brutus, the king's nephew, who had survived the tyranny by playing the fool — Brutus, the dullard, is the name the disguise gave him, and the same Brutus who at Delphi, told that the first to kiss his mother would rule Rome, had pretended to stumble and kissed the earth. Lucretia told what had been done, exacted an oath of vengeance, declared herself innocent of fault though not — by the code she had been raised in — to be acquitted of punishment, and stabbed herself. Brutus drew the knife from the wound and swore the oath that ends the regal period: to expel the Tarquins and to suffer no man thereafter to be king in Rome. The body was carried into the Forum; the city rose; the king, hurrying back from Ardea, found the gates shut. The traditional date of the expulsion, and of the Republic's first consuls — Brutus and Collatinus — is 509 BCE.

// 509 BCE, AT HONEST STRENGTH //

The date 509 is firmer than 753, but it is not a document. It rests on the consular fasti, whose early entries cannot all be verified, and on a striking external anchor: Polybius (3.22) records the first treaty between Rome and Carthage and dates it to the first consuls, twenty-eight years before Xerxes' crossing into Greece — which yields 509/8. The treaty text he quotes, in genuinely archaic Latin, treats Rome as mistress of a stretch of the Latin coast: a real archaic Rome, negotiating as a power. Scholars also note the convenience of 509/8: it synchronises Rome's liberation from tyranny with Athens' (514, and Cleisthenes' reforms of 508/7), and ancient chronographers loved such rhymes. The article's verdict, at the evidence's strength: a change of regime at Rome in the late sixth century is as close to certain as this period allows; the year 509 is the tradition's date for it, supported by Polybius' anchor, not proven by it; and the story of Lucretia — told also by Dionysius (4.64–85) with variations — is a charter myth of the Republic, whatever kernel of outrage it may preserve: the Republic founded, in its own telling, not by a vote but by an oath over a wronged woman's body, exactly as the monarchy was founded over a brother's.

XIII. What the Ground Says: Archaeology's Bearing

Archaeology cannot confirm a legend, and this section will not pretend otherwise. What it can do — what a century and a half of excavation in Rome has done — is establish the setting in which the legends were told: when the hills were settled, when the villages became a city, when Rome was ruled by kings at all. The results cut in two directions at once, and the article reports both at full strength. Against the legend's letter: there was no founding day. For the legend's broadest frame: there was a real archaic Rome, ruled by kings, monumental by the sixth century, whose growth the tradition's outline — settlement on the Palatine, merger of the hilltop villages, a regal period ending around 500 BCE — fits better than its nineteenth-century critics expected.

BEFORE THE CITY: BRONZE AGE TO BURIAL GROUND

Traces of occupation on the Capitoline reach back into the Bronze Age. By about 1000 BCE the valley that would become the Forum was a cemetery — cremation burials in hut-shaped urns, then, from the ninth century, inhumations — serving settlements on the surrounding hills, with further cemeteries on the Esquiline and Quirinal. The hut-urns are scale models, in fired clay, of the oval thatched houses the living built. A community burying its dead in model huts, on ground it would later pave as its civic centre, is a community in place, generations before any plausible “founding”.

THE PALATINE HUTS

On the south-western spur of the Palatine — the Cermalus, above the Lupercal cave of the tradition — excavation uncovered the rock-cut postholes, drainage channels, and floor cuttings of Iron Age huts, oval, of wattle and thatch, dated to the eighth century BCE: the oldest structural evidence on the hill the legend makes the city's cradle, exactly contemporary with the tradition's Romulus. Two cautions, both essential. The Romans themselves preserved a thatched Hut of Romulus (Casa Romuli) on the Palatine, maintained as a relic into late antiquity — but a relic a city preserves testifies to the city's belief, not to the relic's occupant. And huts of this date stand on half the hills of Latium: the Palatine evidence proves a village of the right age in the right place. It cannot name its headman.

CARANDINI'S WALL — FINDING AND CLAIM, SEPARATED

From the late 1980s, Andrea Carandini's excavations on the Palatine's northern slope uncovered a fortification wall and gate which he dated to about 730–720 BCE and published as the wall of Romulus — the circuit, he argued, of the first pomerium, proving a deliberate act of city-foundation in the traditional decade. The reception divides, with positions named: many archaeologists accept the wall and its date as a genuine and important find; many historians — T. J. Cornell's caution is representative of the sceptical mainstream — decline the identification, since a wall attests a community organising its boundary, not a founder, a name, or a rite. Carandini's broader thesis, that the tradition preserves a substantially true account of an eighth-century foundation, remains a minority position held with great learning. The wall is a finding. The attribution is a claim. This archive keeps the two in separate sentences, and so should every reader.

THE FORUM: FROM CEMETERY TO CIVIC CENTRE

The Forum valley tells the city's real biography in stratigraphy. Burials cease in the course of the eighth and seventh centuries; the marshy floor was drained, filled, and given its first pavings by the late seventh and sixth; public and sacred buildings — the Comitium, the Regia, the great sixth-century temples — rise around it in the regal period's traditional span. The process is synoikism: separate hilltop settlements growing together into one polity over generations, the shared valley converted from burial ground to civic space. It is the mainstream archaeological account of Rome's origin, and it flatly contradicts a single-day foundation — while explaining why the legend places a battle between two hills in that valley (Section IX): the Forum really was the seam where two settlements met.

The Lapis Niger and the Word Rex

In 1899 Giacomo Boni, excavating in the Forum before the Curia, uncovered beneath a pavement of black marble — the Lapis Niger, the Black Stone, a spot the late-antique sources already treated as ancient, ominous, and half-understood — an archaic shrine: an altar, a truncated cone, and a squared stone pillar inscribed on four faces in the oldest Latin we possess. The inscription, dated on letter-forms and context to about 570–550 BCE, runs vertically in boustrophedon — the lines turning, as the ox turns in ploughing — and is fragmentary in every line. What can be read includes a sacral curse (sakros esed: “let him be accursed”, in paraphrase of the archaic formula), mention of yoked animals and of a herald (kalator), and one word on which a disproportionate amount of history hangs: RECEI — the dative case, in archaic spelling, of rex: “to (or for) the king”. Here, in stone, in the Forum, within the regal period's traditional dates, is a public religious regulation addressed in part to a king of Rome. The honest ambiguity must be stated: a minority reading takes the rex to be the rex sacrorum, the Republic's priestly “king of the sacred rites”, which would make the stone a Republican document; the majority dating of the stele to the sixth century, on independent archaeological grounds, points to a political king. At that strength the inscription is the single hardest piece of evidence in this article: not for Romulus, not for any named king, but for the institution the legends unanimously presuppose — a Rome of the sixth century BCE that had a king, sacred law, and the Latin language in monumental use. A cup of about 625–600 BCE from the Regia nearby, inscribed with the same word, leans the same way. Kings at Rome are not a legend. Kings named Romulus, Numa, and Tullus, doing what Livy says they did, remain one.

The Capitoline Wolf: A Plain Statement

The most famous image of the foundation legend is the bronze Capitoline Wolf — the she-wolf, tense and snarling, suckling the twins — in the Capitoline Museums since 1471. Its traditional dating, fixed by Winckelmann in the eighteenth century, made the wolf an Etruscan bronze of the fifth century BCE, and the twins — always recognised as stylistically alien to the wolf — Renaissance additions of about 1471, attributed to Antonio del Pollaiuolo. The plain statement this article is charged to make is that the first half of that dating no longer stands. In 2006 the restorer and art historian Anna Maria Carruba, who had conserved the bronze, argued from its casting technique — the wolf is cast in a single piece, by a lost-wax method whose particulars are medieval, not archaic — that the wolf is a work of the eleventh or twelfth century CE. Radiocarbon analysis of organic material from the casting core, carried out at the University of Salento, returned a casting range of 1021–1153 CE; thermoluminescence results concorded; and a lead-isotope study published in 2025 found the bronze's metal consistent with medieval, central-European copper sources. Most specialists now date the wolf to the Middle Ages. A minority of scholars continues to defend the Etruscan dating, and the museum presents the question as open; both facts are recorded here, and neither dilutes the statement of where the weight of the technical evidence lies. The twins, on any account, are fifteenth-century work. The consequences are worth a sentence of their own: the image of the wolf and twins is genuinely ancient — it appears on Rome's earliest coinage of the third century BCE and in authors who describe archaic statues of the group — but this wolf, the icon on a million postcards, is a medieval masterpiece that a Renaissance pope's gift set upon the Capitol, where it absorbed the credit of the ancient originals it resembles. No object in this hub better deserves its own method note. It gets the next section instead.

XIV. What Foundation Legend Did for Rome

A legend that survives a thousand years of telling is doing work. The foundation legends of Rome did at least three kinds of work — constitutional, diplomatic, and dynastic — and the third is the one that finally fixed the story's canonical form.

Charter. The legends are a constitution in narrative form. The asylum explains and justifies Rome's defining practice — the absorption of outsiders into the citizen body — by lodging it in the founder's first act: Rome is mixed by foundation charter, and every later extension of citizenship, down to the enfranchisement of Italy and beyond, could cite Romulus. The Sabine merger charters the dual strands of the Roman people and the institution of conubium between communities. Numa charters the priesthoods and the calendar; Servius charters the census and the tribes; Brutus' oath charters the Republic's founding allergy to the very word rex. To ask of any episode in Sections VI–XII “is it true?” before asking “what does it license?” is to read the dossier in the wrong order. The Romans who transmitted these stories used them as precedents, in speeches, in lawsuits, in senatorial debate: charter first, chronicle second.

Identity and diplomacy. Trojan descent gave Rome a past inside the Greek mythic system but outside the Greek political one — kin to the heroic world, yet of the other side in its great war, and so able to be simultaneously civilised by Greek standards and not Greek. The asset was spent in real diplomacy: communities across the Mediterranean argued kinship (syngeneia) with Rome through the Trojan web — Segesta in Sicily claiming Trojan kinship against its neighbours, Greek cities tracing their founders to Aeneas' company or to Odysseus', Rome itself acknowledging obligations the genealogies created. The Aeneas legend also carried a moral brand the Romans prized: their founding hero is not a conqueror or a trickster but pius Aeneas, the man defined by pietas — duty to gods, father, and son, carried literally on his shoulders out of the fire. A city that believed itself descended from duty had a self-image to live up to, and its historians measured its lapses against the ancestor.

THE JULIAN GENS AND THE SON OF VENUS

No family spent the legend harder than the Julii. Their claim ran the whole length of the structure this article has built: the Julii were an Alban family, transferred to Rome when Tullus destroyed Alba (Section XI), and at Alba their line ran back to Iulus — Ascanius, Aeneas' son — and so to Aeneas, and so to Venus, Aeneas' mother. When Julius Caesar delivered the funeral oration for his aunt Julia, Suetonius (Divus Iulius 6) preserves the claim he made for the family in public, in paraphrase: on the mother's side, descent from kings — the Marcii Reges, heirs of Ancus Marcius; on the father's, from the gods — the Julii from Venus. A pedigree with a king at one end and a goddess at the other is not a family tree; it is a political programme in genealogical form, and Caesar acted on it: he vowed a temple to Venus Genetrix, Venus the Mother, before Pharsalus in 48 BCE and dedicated it in his new forum in 46, with his family's ancestress installed as a goddess of the Roman state; his coinage carried Aeneas carrying Anchises. The foundation legend, in the first century BCE, was quite literally the ruling family's title deed.

AUGUSTUS: THE LEGEND MONUMENTALISED

Caesar's heir completed the appropriation. Augustus — adopted into the Julian gens, son of the deified Julius, and so, by the pedigree's arithmetic, descended from Venus — built the legend into the city's fabric: his Forum, opened in 2 BCE, set Aeneas fleeing Troy and Romulus bearing the spolia opima in facing exedrae, at the head of two galleries of Rome's great men, the whole foundation dossier as a portrait hall; the Ara Pacis carved Aeneas sacrificing at his landing; his house on the Palatine stood, pointedly, beside the preserved Hut of Romulus (Section XIII). And he commissioned the poem. Virgil's Aeneid — in which Jupiter promises Venus an empire without end for Aeneas' descendants, and the Julian line is named in prophecy in the poem's sixth book — is the foundation legend's final and greatest redaction, the moment the charter myth becomes the imperial epic. It is told, as a poem and as an act of state, in this hub's Aeneid article, to which this article now formally hands the story.

One more function, quieter than the rest, completes the account. The foundation legends gave Rome a way to think about its own violence. The city's story, as Rome told it, begins in a fratricide, proceeds by a mass abduction, destroys its own mother-city, and refounds itself over a murdered woman's body — and Rome's literature never stops returning to those scenes, in Horace's seventh Epode reading the civil wars as the working-out of Remus' curse, in Lucan's epic of a republic devouring itself. A charter myth that encoded guilt as faithfully as glory gave the civilisation a diagnostic instrument as well as a title deed. The legends were Rome's memory of what it cost to become Rome — preserved, significantly, not by Rome's enemies but by Rome.

XV. Method Note: Neither Euhemerus nor Contempt

This article closes, as its commission requires, with its method stated in the open — because the foundation legends of Rome are the test case on which two opposite errors have been perfected, and the hub's way lies between them.

The first error is euhemerism, named for Euhemerus of Messene (c. 300 BCE), whose Sacred Record proposed that the gods were great men of the distant past, deified by gratitude and time. Applied to Rome, euhemerism reads the legend as garbled fact: behind Romulus a real chief, behind the wolf a real wet-nurse, behind the augury a real election — the story stripped of its marvels and the residue sworn in as history. The error is seductive because it flatters both parties: the legend keeps a kernel, the historian keeps a subject. But the stripping is arbitrary at exactly the decisive points. The lupa wordplay (Section VI) shows the ancients themselves performing the euhemerist move on the wolf — and producing, not a fact, but a second story, which competes with the first and is no better evidenced. A method that can turn any marvel into a plausible mundane event, without any test that could fail, explains everything and establishes nothing. Where a genuinely independent check exists — the Lapis Niger's rex, Polybius' treaty, the Palatine postholes — this article has used it and said what it proves. Where no check exists, the euhemerist residue is not knowledge; it is the legend wearing modern dress.

The second error is contempt: the legend dismissed as lies — priestly fraud, aristocratic propaganda, fairy tales for children — and therefore as evidence of nothing. This error wastes the dossier. The foundation legends are, demonstrably, evidence of the first order for the centuries that told them: Fabius Pictor's Rome, arming itself with a Greek-written past in the crisis of the Hannibalic War; the second century's state, burning its founder's supposed books to police the boundary of public religion; the Julian family's ascent, argued up a pedigree; Augustus' settlement, legitimised by a commissioned epic. A legend's strata, variants, and suppressions are datable events in the history of the society that kept the legend — as this article has treated them throughout, from Hesiod's Odyssean Latium to Tarutius' astrological arithmetic. Nor is the dismissal even accurate on its own terms: “lies” implies a liar and a deceived, and the record shows instead a tradition openly arguing with itself — Livy bracketing Rhea Silvia's claim in the sentence that reports it, Dionysius listing the rival dates he rejects, Plutarch canvassing three killers for Remus. Rome did not believe its foundation legends the way a child believes; it maintained them the way a state maintains its archives, with custodians, audits, and occasional fires.

POSITION: CORNELL — THE OUTLINE HOLDS

T. J. Cornell, in The Beginnings of Rome (1995), argues the most influential modern middle position: that the received tradition, for all its legendary dressing, preserves a structurally sound outline — a regal period, a sequence of urban growth, institutions of genuine antiquity — because it descends, through the annalists, from materials older than any surviving narrative. On Cornell's reading the historian may use the tradition as a framework while discarding its episodes. His caution over Carandini (Section XIII) shows the position's discipline: framework yes, founder no.

POSITION: WISEMAN — THE MYTH AS CIVIC THEATRE

T. P. Wiseman, in Remus: A Roman Myth (1995), argues from the other side: that much of the foundation story in its surviving form is creation of the fourth century BCE and after — when Rome, newly dominant in Italy, staged its past in dramatic festivals before the whole people, and when the Remus story acquired its fratricidal edge as a meditation on the citizen violence of the age that invented it. On Wiseman's reading the legend dates its tellers, not its events, with unusual precision — which makes it more, not less, valuable as evidence.

THE ARCHIVE'S OWN RULE

Between the positions — and they are less far apart than their reputations suggest, since both treat the tradition as a made thing whose making is the evidence — this archive applies the rule the reader has watched throughout: report the stratum, name the variant, date the telling, and let the ground have the last word where the ground can speak. Legend neither euhemerised into fact nor dismissed as lies is not a compromise. It is a description of what the material actually is: the longest-running act of self-interpretation in European history, still legible, layer by layer, to anyone who reads it as it asks to be read — as Rome's account of Rome, given under no oath but its own.
