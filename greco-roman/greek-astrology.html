I. A Timing Science: What This Article Covers

This article belongs to the Foundations cluster of the Alchemy wing — the small group of articles that sets out the intellectual machinery the Alexandrian art presupposed — and its subject is the machine the whole ancient world used to answer one question: when? When will this life turn? When will this sickness break? When should this voyage, this marriage, this building, this operation begin? Greek astrology, in the form this article treats it, is best understood not as a fortune-telling sideline of ancient religion but as the Hellenistic world's timing science: a complete, teachable, internally consistent system for correlating the configurations of the heavens at a chosen instant with the character and course of whatever began at that instant — a child, an illness, a city, a question, a work.

The system's working life runs, roughly, from the second century BCE, when its characteristic synthesis first becomes visible in Ptolemaic Egypt, to the sixth or seventh century CE, when its last Greek practitioners were still writing in a world already transformed. Between those bounds it was practised from Spain to Mesopotamia, in Greek and Latin and Demotic, by court astrologers and by street-corner consultants, and it generated one of the largest technical literatures of antiquity. Three of its books anchor this article, because between them they show the system from three sides: the didactic poem of Dorotheus of Sidon (first century CE), the electional authority; the nine-book Anthology of Vettius Valens (second century CE), the working practitioner's handbook; and the Tetrabiblos of Claudius Ptolemy (second century CE), the attempt to give the whole art a foundation in natural philosophy. Around those three stand the modern accounts on which this article chiefly rests: Tamsyn Barton's Ancient Astrology (1994) and Roger Beck's A Brief History of Ancient Astrology (2007).

A note on method, because this subject, like the Hermetica treated elsewhere in this wing, attracts invention. No quotation appears in this article unless it was verified in the named translation and is attributed to that translation at the point of quotation. In practice there are only two verbatim quotations, both short, both from F. E. Robbins's Loeb translation of the Tetrabiblos, read in full for this article. Everything else — the contents of Dorotheus's poem, of Valens's handbook, of Ptolemy's first book — is given as labelled paraphrase, following the translations and modern accounts named in Section XV. Where the evidence is disputed — and on Dorotheus's date, on his text's road through Persian into Arabic, and on the standing of Ptolemy's defence, it genuinely is — the dispute is stated as a dispute, in Sections IX and XII, rather than settled by editorial sleight of hand.

GENETHLIALOGY

The science of births (Greek geneseis): casting and interpreting the configuration of the heavens at a person's birth — or, more rarely, conception — to read character, fortunes, and the timed course of a life. The dominant form of Greek astrology, and the trunk from which the other branches grow.

KATARCHAI — BEGINNINGS

From katarchē, a beginning: the astrology of inceptions. Read a beginning that has happened, to judge how the thing begun will fare; or, in the properly electional use, search forward for the beginning whose heavens promise the outcome wanted, and start then. Dorotheus is its founding authority (Section IX).

GENERAL ASTROLOGY

Genethlialogy's methods applied to collectives — peoples, cities, kingdoms — and to weather and crops: the direct heir of the Babylonian omen tradition, and the branch Ptolemy treats in his Book II before turning to individuals.

INTERROGATIONS

Questions put to the astrologer and answered from the heavens at the moment of asking — the ancestor of later horary astrology. In the earliest surviving treatment, Dorotheus's fifth book, interrogations and elections are not yet cleanly separated, and the blur is itself evidence (Section IX).

One framing distinction governs everything that follows, and Beck's account states it with useful bluntness. Ancient astrologers did not, in the main, observe the sky at the moment of a birth. They calculated it — from tables, often years after the event, reconstructing where the Sun, Moon, and five visible planets had stood in relation to each other, to the zodiac, and to the local horizon and meridian of the birthplace at the birth hour. Astrology therefore presupposed astronomy: the better the tables, the more exact the chart, and the system's two greatest surviving authors stand on opposite sides of that dependence — Ptolemy, who wrote the ancient world's standard astronomical tables in the Almagest and then the Tetrabiblos to interpret what they computed, and Valens, who consumed such tables the way a physician consumes a pharmacopoeia. The modern habit of filing astronomy as the science and astrology as its embarrassing parent is, as Beck argues, a poor guide to the ancient material, in which the two formed a single predictive enterprise of greater and lesser certitude. This article follows the ancient filing.

// WORDS: ASTROLOGY AND ASTRONOMY //

Greek astrologia and astronomia were used with little of the modern distinction for much of antiquity; the same author could write both, and Ptolemy calls the predictive art simply the second of the two “means of prediction through astronomy”. “Horoscope” likewise needs its ancient sense recovered: Greek hōroskopos is the hour-marker — the degree, and then the sign, rising at the birth hour — before it is the whole chart, and only later the newspaper paragraph. This article uses “horoscope” for the chart and “Ascendant” or “Hour-Marker” for the rising point itself.

II. The Babylonian Upstream, Kept Brief

This section is deliberately brief. Mesopotamia is another hub's ground: the Before Kemet hub's planned Mesopotamia wing will treat Babylonian celestial divination in the fullness it deserves, and the reader who wants the upstream in depth should begin at that hub's index and watch for the wing. What a reader of the Greek system needs in hand is only the shape of the inheritance — three gifts, and one decisive absence.

The first gift is the omen tradition itself. For well over a millennium, Mesopotamian scribes recorded celestial phenomena — eclipses, halos, planetary appearances and disappearances, unusual colours and conjunctions — and read them as signs addressed, in the first instance, to the king and the land: war, harvest, flood, plague, the fate of armies. The great omen series Enūma Anu Enlil, running to some seventy tablets in its standard form, organised thousands of such protases and apodoses — if the Moon does this, then that will befall — and the scholarly apparatus of the Assyrian and Babylonian courts existed to match the night's sky against the series and warn the king in time. Two features of that world passed intact into the Greek system: the conviction that the heavens are legible, a text written in moving lights; and the professional figure of the scholar who reads them for a client whose fate is at stake. What changed, in transmission, was the client.

The second gift is mathematical: the zodiac of twelve equal signs. Across the fifth century BCE, Babylonian astronomers regularised the constellations along the ecliptic into twelve signs of thirty degrees each — a coordinate grid, independent of the uneven star-pictures, against which any planet's position could be stated exactly and computed in advance. With the zodiac came the sexagesimal arithmetic, the period relations, and eventually the ephemerides that made the Greek astrologer's retrospective calculation possible. Almost everything in Sections V–VII that involves a number — degrees, terms, rising times, planetary periods — is Babylonian in its ancestry, however Greek its dress.

The third gift is the proof of concept: the Babylonian horoscopes. From the late fifth century BCE, Babylonian scribes began applying the sky to individuals, recording the positions of the Moon and planets at a child's birth. The earliest known example is dated to 410 BCE; a small corpus — some thirty tablets, chiefly from Babylon and Uruk, running into the first century BCE — survives. These texts are terse: a birth date, planetary positions in the zodiac, sometimes brief prognostic notes. But the conceptual move is complete in them. The heavens at this person's first moment are worth writing down, because they bear on this person's life. Genethlialogy exists, in embryo, on clay.

// THE DECISIVE ABSENCE //

What the Babylonian horoscopes do not have is the apparatus that makes a chart a chart in the Greek sense: no Ascendant as the pivot of interpretation, no twelve places counted from the rising sign, no developed doctrine of aspects between signs, no lots, no time-lords. The Babylonian gift was the legible sky, the zodiac, and the idea of the birth moment. The interpretive machine built on those gifts is the Hellenistic invention — and the invention, on the evidence, happened in Egypt.

How the transmission ran — through the Persian period, through Babylonian scholars working in a Greek-speaking world, through figures such as Berossus, the Babylonian priest who wrote in Greek and was said to have taught on Cos — is a genuinely murky historical problem, on which Barton is properly cautious: flashes of evidence amid will-o'-the-wisps, in the phrase of her reviewer Alexander Jones, summarising her brief and honest chapter. This article does not pretend to clear the murk. It notes only that by the time Greek astrology becomes fully visible, in the second half of the first century BCE, it already has the shape it will keep for the rest of antiquity — and that its own foundation stories point, unanimously, not to Babylon but to Egypt.

III. The Hellenistic Synthesis in Egypt

The Greek system was made in Egypt — in the temples, cities, and bilingual scholarly culture of the Ptolemaic kingdom and its Roman successor — out of three strands that had never before been one thing. Babylon supplied the zodiac, the planetary periods, and the omen habit of mind. Greece supplied the geometry — the circle divided, the aspects as angular relations, the spherical cosmos of concentric spheres — and, in time, the philosophy: Stoic fate and cosmic sympathy, Platonic cosmology, Aristotelian physics of qualities. Egypt supplied the third strand, and the one modern accounts most easily underrate: the decans, the thirty-six star-groups whose successive risings had marked the hours of the Egyptian night since the early second millennium BCE, together with the temple setting, the priestly scholars, and the immense prestige of Egyptian antiquity in Greek eyes. The decans return, as working apparatus, in Section VI; their role here is as evidence of where the synthesis happened. When the decans were folded into the zodiac — three to a sign, one for each ten degrees — two civilisations' timekeeping became a single instrument.

The system's own memory of its founding is preserved by the fourth-century astrologer Firmicus Maternus, and it is Egyptian to the core. In paraphrase of Firmicus's account: the art descended from Hermes — Mercury, in his Latin — through Asclepius, and was set out in books by Nechepso, a pharaoh, and Petosiris, his priest. Modern scholarship treats Nechepso and Petosiris as the pseudepigraphal authors — real books circulated under those august names — of a foundational astrological literature composed in Greek in, most probably, the second century BCE. Fragments and citations of the Nechepso–Petosiris material survive throughout the later tradition, and its eclipse lore in particular can be set beside its Mesopotamian prototypes. The attribution pattern matters beyond the two names. Like the Hermetica of the companion article, early Greek astrology authorised itself by antiquity and by Egypt: Hermes as inventor, a pharaoh and a priest of Thoth as first expositors. Whether a historical Petosiris — a high priest of Thoth of that name is known around 300 BCE — stands anywhere behind the books cannot be recovered, and this article does not pretend otherwise. What is recoverable is the claim, and the claim is a map of the synthesis: Greek technique, wearing Egyptian authority, built on Babylonian data.

// DENDERA, IN PASSING //

The famous sculpted zodiac from the temple of Hathor at Dendera (c. 50 BCE) — the twelve signs set among Egyptian decans and constellations — is the synthesis carved in stone, and it is the Egyptology division's object, not this article's. It is named here only to fix the point in the reader's eye: by the mid-first century BCE, an Egyptian temple ceiling could display the Babylonian zodiac naturalised among Egypt's own stars. Egyptology owns the monument; this article owns what practitioners did with the system it pictures.

Alexandria is the conventional name for the synthesis's home, and the convention is reasonable — the city was Ptolemaic Egypt's intellectual capital, the natural meeting point of Greek, Egyptian, Babylonian, and Jewish learning — subject to the same warning the Hermetica article issues for its own texts: the surviving astrological papyri come from temple libraries and town rubbish heaps up and down the Nile, and “Alexandria” names a culture more securely than a street address. What can be said without qualification is the chronology. Astrological literature in Greek is being cited, excerpted, and argued with from the first century BCE onward; Greek horoscopes on papyrus survive from the first century CE; and by the second century CE the system exists in the great handbooks — Dorotheus behind them, Valens and Ptolemy at their head — in essentially the form Sections V–VII now set out.

IV. The Thema Mundi: The System's Charter Horoscope

Every mature system carries a picture of itself at its most intelligible, and Greek astrology's picture is a horoscope: the Thema Mundi, the “theme” — the chart — “of the world”. It purports to be the configuration of the heavens at the birth of the cosmos itself, and it is transmitted in late-antique sources, above all Firmicus Maternus (Mathesis III.1) and Macrobius's Commentary on the Dream of Scipio (I.21). It was used in teaching, and its function in the tradition is exactly the function of a charter: from its placements, the system's otherwise arbitrary-looking rules can be read off, as if deduced. Whether it was truly the system's generative starting point — a construction from which the rules were derived — or a later rationalisation built to display rules already in use, is a live question in the modern literature, and this article states it as open. What is not in question is the chart itself, which is perfectly definite.

Read as a teaching diagram, the chart does three pieces of work at once. First, it grounds the domiciles: the assignments of signs to planetary rulers — the Moon to Cancer, the Sun to Leo, and the five planets each to two signs disposed symmetrically outward from the luminaries, Mercury to Virgo and Gemini, Venus to Libra and Taurus, Mars to Scorpio and Aries, Jupiter to Sagittarius and Pisces, Saturn to Capricorn and Aquarius — are simply the Thema Mundi's placements, generalised. Second, it grounds the aspects: standing in the world's first chart, Jupiter's sign beholds the Sun's by trine and Venus's beholds the Moon's by sextile, while Mars's beholds the Sun's by square and Saturn's opposes the Moon's — and the tradition's valuation of the aspects, harmonious trine and sextile against harsh square and opposition, together with the benefic and malefic natures that go with them, can be exhibited as geometry visible in the charter. Third, it fixes the chart's orientation: the world is a night chart, the Sun below the horizon in the second sign from the Ascendant, with the Moon on the Ascendant — a disposition the tradition read as fitting for a cosmos whose sublunar life is generated, embodied, and fortune-governed before it is illuminated. Later summaries add the planetary joys and further schemes to the diagram's yield; the three yields above are the load-bearing ones, and they are enough to show the kind of object the Thema Mundi is.

// AN IMPOSSIBLE CHART, AND WHY THAT IS THE POINT //

The Thema Mundi never happened, and cannot have. Mercury is never more than about twenty-eight degrees from the Sun; the charter puts Mercury a full sign — thirty degrees — away, and at the canonical fifteen degrees of Virgo against the Sun's fifteen of Leo the separation is exactly that impossible thirty. Venus's elongation is likewise strained past its limit in strict degree versions. The ancients who taught from the chart were astronomers enough to know this. The Thema Mundi is not a failed observation but a diagram — a mythical charter in the precise sense that its truth is structural, not historical. It tells the student not what the sky once did, but why the rules are shaped as they are. Treating it as a datable event, as some late-antique and many modern popular accounts do, mistakes the genre — the same mistake, in miniature, as treating the Hermetica's Hermes as a datable author.

One further resonance deserves a sentence, because it ties the charter to the synthesis of Section III. The Ascendant at fifteen degrees of Cancer has repeatedly been connected, in modern discussion, with Sothis — Sirius, whose heliacal rising in the Egyptian summer marked the New Year and the coming inundation, and whose sign in the seasonal calendar is Cancer's season. The connection is suggestive and unprovable: the heliacal rising is not a fixed tropical degree across latitudes and centuries, and no surviving ancient text states the link outright. This article records it as what it is — a modern hypothesis that would, if true, set Egypt's oldest time-signal at the very pivot of the Greek world's first chart — and builds nothing on it.

V. The Working Apparatus I: Signs, Planets, Sect, and Dignity

A Hellenistic chart is a configuration of seven planets — Sun, Moon, Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, Venus, Mercury, in the descending “Chaldean” order of their spheres — disposed in twelve signs, in twelve places, in aspect to one another, and graded by a battery of conditions. This section takes the first layer: the signs and the planets themselves. Section VI takes the places, aspects, and calculated points. The vocabulary is dense, and it is given here in full because the anchor authors of Sections IX–XI are unintelligible without it — Valens's first book is, in large part, simply this vocabulary written out at handbook length.

The Twelve Signs

The signs are thirty-degree divisions of the ecliptic, named for the constellations they once roughly matched, and read in the Greek system as bundles of contrary qualities. They are masculine or feminine in alternation from Aries; they are tropical (Aries, Cancer, Libra, Capricorn — the signs of the equinoxes and solstices, where the Sun “turns”), solid (Taurus, Leo, Scorpio, Aquarius), or bicorporeal / double-bodied (Gemini, Virgo, Sagittarius, Pisces); they group by triplicity into the four elemental families — fiery (Aries, Leo, Sagittarius), earthy (Taurus, Virgo, Capricorn), airy (Gemini, Libra, Aquarius), watery (Cancer, Scorpio, Pisces) — and each carries a freight of further predicates in the handbooks: human or bestial, vocal or mute, fruitful or barren, straight-rising or crooked-rising, and the regions of the world and parts of the body assigned to it. Valens's sign chapters (Anthology I.2, in Riley's translation, in paraphrase) show the genre at full stretch: Aries is Mars's house, masculine, tropical, fiery, governing — and also, by its degrees, stormy in its first portion, mild in its middle, plague-bearing in its last, with the lands from Babylon to Egypt parcelled out among its parts. The modern reader should take the density itself as the datum: a sign, for a Hellenistic practitioner, was not a symbol but a dossier.

The Seven Planets: Natures and Sects

The planets likewise are dossiers, but dossiers with an internal order. Each planet has a nature — a characteristic mode of action, which Ptolemy will ground in the physical qualities (Section XI) and Valens simply catalogues: what the planet signifies in a nativity, from persons and professions to materials, colours, tastes, and the parts of the body it rules. In Riley's translation of Valens I.1, in paraphrase: the Sun indicates kingship, intellect, the father, public reputation, and rules the head, the right eye, and the heart, and among materials gold; the Moon indicates the body, the mother, fortune, voyages, and rules the left eye, the stomach, and the breasts, and among materials silver and glass; Saturn makes its natives, in Valens's unsparing catalogue, care-worn, solitary, downcast, and brings humblings, obstacles, imprisonment, and grief — and also, well placed, great ranks and the management of others' property. The catalogue style is the point: a planet's meaning is a field, and interpretation is the art of narrowing the field by condition and context — the superabundance problem of Section VIII already visible in the raw material.

BENEFICS

Jupiter and Venus — the greater and lesser benefic — with the Moon generally counted kindly: planets whose tempered, fertile natures (in Ptolemy's physics, their share of the hot and the moist) incline their significations toward increase, union, and preservation.

MALEFICS

Saturn and Mars — the greater and lesser malefic: planets of excess, Saturn of cold and Mars of dry heat in Ptolemy's grounding, whose significations incline toward separation, damage, and loss. Both handbooks insist the labels are defaults, not destinies: a malefic in good condition, in its own sect, can bestow the greatest positions, and a benefic ill placed reverses — Valens says so in as many words (Anthology I.1, in paraphrase).

SECT — HAIRĒSIS, THE PARTY OF THE CHART

Every chart is a day chart (Sun above the horizon) or a night chart (Sun below it), and the planets are partisans: Sun, Jupiter, and Saturn belong to the day party; Moon, Venus, and Mars to the night party; Mercury is a turncoat, diurnal when it rises before the Sun, nocturnal when after. A planet in sect — of the chart's ruling party — is a citizen in its own city; a planet out of sect is an exile, and its significations, benefic or malefic, are graded accordingly. Sect is the single most consequential grading in the system, and the first thing a Hellenistic reading establishes.

THE COMMON PLANET

Mercury stands outside the benefic–malefic and, by its turncoat rule, half outside the sect division: common, convertible, taking the character of its company and condition. Its Valens dossier is correspondingly the longest and most various — letters, accounts, markets, theft, interpretation, and every systematic craft from medicine to divination.

Dignity: A Planet at Home, Honoured, or Abroad

A planet's power to deliver its significations is graded, above all, by its relation to the sign it stands in — its dignities, a layered system in which the Thema Mundi (Section IV) is the deep charter. By domicile, the planet rules the sign: the Moon in Cancer, Mars in Scorpio, a planet in its own house, disposing of its own resources. By exaltation, the planet is honoured in a particular sign and degree — the Sun in Aries (exalted at 19°), the Moon in Taurus (3°), Jupiter in Cancer (15°), Saturn in Libra (21°), and their opposites as falls — a guest of honour in another's house. By triplicity, the planet shares rule of an elemental family, with day and night rulers distinguished — a grading that carries unusual weight in Dorotheus, where the triplicity lords of the luminary in a nativity are read in sequence for the thirds of a life. And beneath these, two finer gratings — the decans and the terms — divide every sign internally; they are treated with the places and lots in Section VI, where they belong as apparatus. The contrary conditions complete the scheme: a planet in the sign opposite its domicile is in detriment (exile), opposite its exaltation in fall (humiliation) — the vocabulary of honour and disgrace is the tradition's own, and it is social through and through. A Hellenistic chart is a court: seven great persons, each at home, honoured, visiting, or exiled, in party or out of it, beholding or blind to one another. The metaphor is not decoration. It is how the handbooks teach the eye to read.

VI. The Working Apparatus II: Places, Aspects, Lots, Decans, and Terms

The Twelve Places (Topoi)

The places — later tradition's “houses” — are the twelve-fold division of the chart counted from the Ascendant, and in the Hellenistic mainstream they are whole-sign: the entire sign containing the Ascendant degree is the first place, the next sign the second, and so on, with the degree of the Ascendant and the degree of the Midheaven (which may fall in the ninth, tenth, or eleventh sign) retained as sensitive points inside the scheme. Degree-based quadrant divisions of the kind later astrology favours exist at the tradition's margins, but the whole-sign frame is the one Dorotheus, Valens, and the papyrus horoscopes presuppose. Each place is a topic of life, and the set, in Beck's summary table and the handbooks' standard list, runs as follows.

Two structural rules give the table its dynamics. The four angles or centres — the places anchored to Ascendant, Midheaven, Descendant, and subterranean point (I, X, VII, IV in sign terms, with the pivots falling where they fall) — are the chart's strong places, where planets act with full force; the places cadent from the angles (III, VI, IX, XII) are weak, their planets fallen away from power; and the succedent places (II, V, VIII, XI) stand between. And the places are further graded by their aspect to the Ascendant (below): the places that behold the first — III, IV, V, VII, IX, X, XI by the whole-sign aspects — are operative, while II, VI, VIII, and XII, which stand in aversion to it, are the chart's dark rooms, their topics harder for the native to command. The reader will note that the tradition's bleakest topics — illness, death's place, enemies — cluster precisely in the averted places. The geometry is the doctrine.

Aspects: Beholding and Aversion

Planets and signs relate by aspect — literally, by seeing. The recognised configurations, reckoned by sign and refined by degree, are: conjunction (same sign, bodily presence); sextile (two signs apart, 60°); square (three apart, 90°); trine (four apart, 120°); and opposition (six apart, 180°, diameter). Signs one or five apart — 30° and 150° — stand in aversion: they do not behold each other, and planets so placed are, in the vivid technical vocabulary, blind to one another, unable to witness, assist, or restrain. Around this simple scheme the handbooks build a refined optics: planets apply and separate, cast rays, stand in superior or inferior aspect (the planet earlier in zodiacal order “overcomes” the later), are contained, besieged, or attended — Valens's term is doryphory, spear-bearing, for the planets that attend a luminary as a king's guard attends the king. The valuation of the aspects themselves — trine and sextile harmonious, square and opposition harsh — is, as Section IV showed, read off the Thema Mundi; and their elemental logic reinforces it, trines joining signs of one triplicity, squares and oppositions joining signs at cross-purposes of quality. Ptolemy, characteristically, keeps the geometry and discards much of the optics (Section XI).

The Lots (Klēroi)

The lots are calculated points — not bodies, not stars, but places on the zodiac derived by formula from the chart's own factors, each assigned a topic. The scheme's charter idea is projection: take the arc between two factors, and lay it off from a third. The two great lots govern the system's division of a human being. The Lot of Fortune (Greek Tychē) — by day, the arc from Sun to Moon laid off from the Ascendant; by night, the reverse arc — belongs to the Moon, the body, and everything that befalls a person: health, material circumstance, the given. The Lot of Spirit (Greek Daimōn) — the complementary arc, by day from Moon to Sun — belongs to the Sun, the mind, and everything a person does: intention, action, career. Around these the tradition multiplies lots for the topics of a life — of Eros and of Necessity, of the father, the mother, siblings, marriage, children, debt, accusation, and dozens more; the standard early scheme counts seven lots, Fortune's the most important, as Beck notes in his appendix on the subject, worked from a real papyrus example: Hermon's horoscope (Neugebauer and Van Hoesen no. 81), where the computist Titus Pitenius, having placed the Hour-Marker at 18° Scorpio and found the Moon twenty-nine degrees from the Sun, sets the Lot of Fortune at 17° Sagittarius — Scorpio 18° plus the twenty-nine-degree arc — and remarks, with a practitioner's asperity preserved across nineteen centuries, that those who subtract instead, and put the lot back in Libra, do so out of ignorance. The day/night reversal of the lots' formulas, and the disputes it generates in the sources, are the small print in which sect (Section V) does its quietest and most consequential work.

The Decans: Egypt Inside the Wheel

Every sign divides into three decans of ten degrees — thirty-six in the wheel — and here the Egyptian strand of Section III stands inside the apparatus itself. The decans began as stars: the thirty-six star-groups, Sothis (Sirius) at their head, whose heliacal risings succeeded one another at ten-day intervals through the Egyptian year and whose risings across the night marked its hours, painted as diagonal star tables inside coffin lids from the early second millennium BCE and expounded in the Book of Nut. Folded into the zodiac, the thirty-six became divisions of the circle rather than groups of stars — the number surviving the translation intact, as numbers do — and acquired rulers: in the scheme the later tradition calls Chaldean, the planets in their descending sphere-order (Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, Sun, Venus, Mercury, Moon) repeating across the thirty-six faces; in a rival scheme, by triplicity. Hellenistic practice used the decans heavily and variously: as a dignity (a planet in its own decan, or “face”, has a modest standing of its own), in medical astrology, where decans govern parts of the body and the timing of fevers, and in the Hermetic literature of astral sympathies, where each decan acquires its plants, stones, and spirits — the thread Section XIV picks up. The decan names themselves survive in the papyrus horoscopes (Hermon's chart gives the Ascendant's decan as Thoumouth, the Moon's as Aroth, the Sun's as Sentachor, in Beck's reading): Egyptian gods, still on duty inside a Greek document, in a Roman province, marking the hours of a birth.

The Terms, or Bounds (Horia)

The finest grating is the terms: each sign divides into five unequal segments, each segment the term or bound of one of the five star-planets (the luminaries hold no terms), the segment's length varying by sign — Jupiter's six degrees at the head of Aries, Venus's six, Mercury's eight, Mars's five, Saturn's five, in the table the tradition calls Egyptian and Ptolemy attributes to Egyptian authority. A planet standing in its own terms is, in the house metaphor of Section V, on its own small property within another's estate — a dignity of real weight in Valens, whose Anthology I.3 gives the terms sign by sign with delineations of almost comic specificity: the native of this bound temperate and prominent, of that bound censured in marriage and most unnatural in passion, in Riley's translation, in paraphrase. Ptolemy, characteristically dissatisfied, prints the Egyptian table, prints the rival “Chaldean” scheme, finds fault with both, and supplies a rationalised table of his own (Tetrabiblos I.20–21 in Robbins's numbering) — which posterity largely ignored in favour of the Egyptian bounds he had criticised. The episode is the system in miniature: an inherited table of no recoverable rationale, a philosopher's reform, and a profession that went on using the table.

// DODEKATEMORIA — THE TWELFTH-PARTS //

One refinement more, for completeness: each sign divides also into twelve dodekatemoria of two and a half degrees, each twelfth-part assigned to a sign in sequence from the sign itself, so that a planet's exact degree casts a kind of shadow-position into another sign. Babylonian in ancestry, ubiquitous in the handbooks, and a standing exhibit in the case — Barton's and Beck's alike — that the system's interpretive resources were, in practice, inexhaustible (Section VIII).

VII. Time Inside the Chart: Time-Lords and the Length of Life

Everything so far describes a chart at rest — and a nativity is not at rest. The question every client actually asked was temporal: when will the promised marriage, office, illness, or ruin arrive, and how long will the life itself run? The system's answer is its most technically distinctive layer: the time-lords (Greek chronokratores, rulers of times) — schemes by which the chart's factors take turns governing successive periods of the life, so that the static configuration unfolds as a dated narrative. Valens is the great source, and his Anthology returns to the division of times obsessively, book after book; the techniques below are his in their fullest surviving form, with Dorotheus and the wider tradition concurring in the outlines.

PLANETARY PERIODS

Each planet owns a span of years — its “period”, derived from its astronomical cycles: the Moon 25, Mercury 20, Venus 8, the Sun 19, Mars 15, Jupiter 12, Saturn 30, in the standard “minor years” every student memorised. Periods subdivide into months and days in the same proportions, giving the system a fractal calendar: a life in years, each year in months, each month ruled and sub-ruled.

PROFECTIONS

The simplest clock: the Ascendant advances one whole sign per year of life, and the ruler of the sign it reaches is lord of the year; the same advance runs by month and by day at finer scales. The year's topics are read from the profected sign's place, its ruler's condition, and the planets encountered — a technique any practitioner could run in his head, and the one that made annual consultation a practical business.

ZODIACAL RELEASING

Valens's signature technique (Anthology IV): from the Lot of Fortune, for the body's fortunes, or from the Lot of Spirit, for action and reputation, the life is “released” through the signs in order, each sign governing a span equal to its ruler's minor years, each span subdivided through the signs again. Periods whose signs angle to the lot, or witness it, mark peaks; the moment a releasing sequence breaks its pattern — the “loosing of the bond” — marks the life's sharp turns. In paraphrase of Valens's own framing (Anthology IV, in the Schmidt translation of the relevant chapters): Fortune, being the Moon's lot, governs crises, injuries, and all that appertains to the body; Spirit, being the Sun's, governs action, reputation, and what a person undertakes.

DISTRIBUTIONS AND TRANSITS

Around these stand the supporting clocks: distributions through the terms, in which the Ascendant's directed motion through successive bounds hands the life from term-ruler to term-ruler; and the planets' actual transits — their real-time returns to, and configurations with, the natal places — read as triggers on the periods the slower schemes have armed. The handbooks combine all of them, and expect the practitioner to make them agree.

The Length of Life: Starter and Destroyer

The gravest calculation stood apart. To find how long the life would run, the practitioner first identified the starter — Beck's rendering of the tradition's aphetēs, the releaser or giver of life: a luminary or angle, chosen by strict rules of sect and position, from which the life is launched — and then directed that point, degree by degree and year by year, until it met the configuration of the destroyer (anairetēs), a malefic or a malefic's ray, whose meeting ended the count. Dorotheus's third book is given to the machinery — the haylāj and the kadhkhudāh of the Arabic transmission, the giver of life and the governor of its years — and Valens works the methods at case length. The doctrine's social charge should not be missed: a technique that purported to date deaths, applied to emperors, was the one part of the art that Roman law and Roman fear repeatedly singled out, and the horoscopes of the powerful were state secrets for exactly the reason the starter and destroyer made them dangerous. Beck's eighth chapter — “a matter of life and death” — is the modern account to read for the politics of the calculation, including the paired first-century horoscopes by the astrologer-politician Balbillus around which so much of the evidence turns.

The honest summary of the whole temporal layer is double. As engineering, it is magnificent: a set of nested, rule-governed clocks, computable from tables, turning one configuration into a lifetime of dated, differentiated periods — nothing in the ancient world outside astronomy itself is so elaborately systematic. As knowledge, it multiplies the system's founding difficulty, which Section VIII now states plainly: with this many clocks, some hand is always pointing at something, and the art of the practitioner lay in judging which.

VIII. The Consultation: What Genethlialogy Actually Produced

Strip the theory away and ask the practical question: a client came to an astrologer — in a temple town in Egypt, in Antioch, in Rome — with a birth date and hour, or with a decision to make. What did he or she leave with? The evidence is of three kinds: the handbooks, which show what practitioners were trained to deliver; the surviving original horoscopes on papyrus and ostraca, the profession's actual paperwork, catalogued by Neugebauer and Van Hoesen in Greek Horoscopes (1959); and the literary horoscopes embedded in the handbooks themselves, some hundred and thirty of them in Valens alone — case material, worked after the fact, by which the profession taught and checked itself.

The paperwork comes first, because it corrects the imagination. An original horoscope, Hermon's of Section VI at the elaborate end, Pitenius computing, runs to a full page of dense astronomical prose: each planet placed to the degree and minute, its sign, terms, decan, exaltation or fall, phase, and aspects stated, the Hour-Marker and Midheaven fixed, the Lot of Fortune computed, and the whole closed with the computist's name and a valediction — “Good luck!”, in Beck's rendering of Hermon's document. Most surviving originals are far terser: positions only, the chart without the reading. Which is itself the finding. The written product of a consultation was, typically, the calculation; the interpretation was the astrologer's living voice, delivered in the room, and it has perished with the room. What the client carried away was a dated, placed, graded map of his own heavens — and the memory of what the man who could read it had said.

The handbooks show what that voice was trained to say. A full genethlialogical reading moved through the topics in order — the native's body, life, and character from the first place and its ruler; livelihood; siblings; parents; children; illness; marriage; death; travel and religion; profession and rank; friends; enemies — and then through the times: the life divided by time-lords (Section VII), its rises and crises dated, its length computed by starter and destroyer. Dorotheus's first four books are organised on exactly this plan — upbringing and condition; marriage and children; the years of life; the transfer of years — and Valens follows the same skeleton at ten times the length. Around the nativity stood the consultation's other products, the ones Dorotheus's fifth book (Section IX) serves: elections for the undertakings of the coming season — a voyage to begin, a shop to open, a slave to buy or free, a marriage to contract, a building to found — and answers to questions: the sick man's recovery, the runaway's return, the theft, the quarrelled wife. The profession's full offering, in short, was a life read whole, and a calendar for living it advisedly.

// THE SUPERABUNDANCE PROBLEM — BARTON'S EXPERIMENT //

Could two competent ancient practitioners, given the same chart, produce the same reading? Barton's fifth chapter ran the test modern scholarship still cites: she took one nativity — that of Charles, Prince of Wales — and worked its configurations systematically against the outcome-rules of two authorities, Dorotheus and Firmicus Maternus. The result, as Beck summarises her finding with approval, was not falsehood but contradiction in bulk: the handbooks furnished masses of incompatible outcomes for the same configurations. Barton's conclusion, in Beck's paraphrase and this article's: the handbooks were not do-it-yourself manuals and were never usable as lookup tables; they were show-pieces, exhibitions of the masters' knowledge, presupposing a hands-on art — learned by apprenticeship, exercised by judgement — that selected among the superabundant significations the chart afforded. A system that can say almost anything requires, and produces, a professional trusted to say the right thing. That is not a defect the ancients overlooked. It is, on the evidence of Valens's whole manner (Section X), the thing the profession knew itself to be selling.

Who bought? The case material answers: officials and officers, merchants and ship-owners, parents asking for children, the sick asking about recovery — and, at the summit, emperors, whose horoscopes were worth killing over and whose astrologers, Balbillus under Nero and Vespasian at their head, were courtiers of real power. Roman law's recurring expulsions of astrologers and the treason trials that turned on imperial nativities belong to the hub's wider history; for this article the point is narrower. Genethlialogy was not a marginal superstition tolerated at the empire's edges. It was a standard professional service of the Roman world, with standard paperwork, a standard training literature, and a clientele coextensive with literate society. The system's modern students — Barton and Beck alike — insist on the fact because it disciplines every other judgement: whatever else Greek astrology was, it was normal.

IX. Dorotheus of Sidon: The Carmen and the Art of Beginnings

The earliest of the three anchor authors is a poet. Dorotheus of Sidon, writing in Greek hexameters in the first century CE, composed a five-book didactic poem on the whole of horoscopic astrology — its Greek title is conventionally given as the Pentateuch, the Five Books; the Latin title under which the modern world knows it, Carmen Astrologicum, is an editor's. The poem's example horoscopes are dated between roughly 7 BCE and the 40s CE, and the dating of the composition that Barton proposed and the literature has broadly kept — about 25 to 75 CE — rests substantially on them. The poem was famous at once and for centuries: Firmicus drew on it; Hephaistion of Thebes, in the fifth century, built his second book largely on Ptolemy and Dorotheus and his third book, on beginnings, wholly on Dorotheus's fifth; and through the Persian and Arabic transmissions (below) Dorotheus became, more than any other Greek author, the astrologer of the medieval Islamic and Latin worlds. If one Greek book taught the next thousand years how to practise, it was this poem.

Book V: The Art of Beginnings

Dorotheus is the first known author to treat the katarchai at systematic length, and Book V is the foundation document of Western electional astrology. Its transmitted chapter list — in the Arabic version's titles, which Pingree's edition prints — is the most concrete surviving picture of what ordinary ancient people asked the stars about. After opening chapters on the general judgement of beginnings — the Moon's condition above all, her sign, her ruler, her aspects, the “corruption of the Moon” as the great disqualifier of an undertaking — the book proceeds by undertakings, in paraphrase of its chapter headings: building a building, and demolishing one; hiring and letting; buying and selling; buying land, slaves, animals; freeing a slave; petitioning a ruler; writing to a man, or teaching a man a science or writing; marriage and courtship, in long and socially vivid chapters; partnership; debt; the journey and the return from it; buying a ship, building a ship, and the extraordinary sequence that follows the ship to the water — commencing to row her out; books, messages, and letters; bondage; the sick, twice over; theft and the lost thing; the runaway; and a closing chapter on the commencement of all things. The method throughout is the genethlialogical method reversed, exactly as Beck describes the branch: where the nativity argues from a given configuration to its outcomes, the katarchē argues from a desired outcome back to the configuration that promises it — strengthen the Ascendant and its ruler, strengthen the place of the undertaking and its ruler, keep the Moon waxing, applied to benefics, and uncorrupted, and let the beginning inherit the heavens you have built for it. To elect is to cast a nativity for an act.

Two qualifications keep Book V honest. First, the book as transmitted does not observe the clean modern line between elections (choosing a time) and interrogations (answering a question from the moment of asking): chapters slide between “if you wish to do X, begin when” and “if you are asked whether X, judge by”, and a small number of its chapters — six of the forty-three, on one careful count in the secondary literature — appear to have been retouched in transmission to make electional rules serve horary purposes not yet systematised in Dorotheus's century. Scholars state the mixture plainly, and Pingree's notes mark the suspect passages; the tidy separation of the branches is a later, largely Arabic and Latin, achievement. Second, Ptolemy omits the whole subject. The Tetrabiblos contains no katarchic astrology at all — a silence Section XI weighs, since it is the loudest single evidence that Ptolemy's book and the profession's practice were different things.

The Text's Road: Greek, Persian, Arabic — and the Disputes

Here the article's promise of stated disputes must be kept in full, because Dorotheus's text is the most mediated of any treated in this hub. The Greek poem is lost as a continuous work. What survives is, first, an Arabic prose translation made around 800 CE by ʿUmar ibn al-Farrukhān al-Ṭabarī — Omar Tiberiades to the Latin tradition — preserved in two manuscripts; and second, a body of Greek fragments, quotations, and prose paraphrases in later Greek authors, Hephaistion above all, together with a second, partial Arabic witness in Māshāʾallāh (writing in the 770s) whose citations include Dorothean material that ʿUmar's version lacks. David Pingree's 1976 Teubner edition — Dorothei Sidonii Carmen Astrologicum — prints the Arabic with his English translation and the collected Greek and Latin fragments, and it remains the scholarly foundation; Benjamin Dykes's 2017 retranslation of ʿUmar's version, with an astrologer's annotations, is the modern practitioner's alternative. Every sentence of “Dorotheus” quoted anywhere should be understood as ʿUmar's Arabic of a Greek poem, in a modern scholar's English.

// TWO DISPUTES, STATED //

The Persian intermediary. Pingree held that ʿUmar translated not the Greek directly but a Middle Persian (Pahlavi) version made in the third century and revised, with additions — including Indian material, the navāmśa ninths among it — in the late fourth or early fifth; on that account the Arabic text contains Sasanian-era interpolations, among them two horoscopes dated 281 and 381 CE and matter drawn from Valens, and lacks passages the Greek fragments prove Dorotheus wrote. The Pahlavi stage has since been disputed: it has been suggested that ʿUmar may have worked from a Greek manuscript directly, and the question — how many languages, and how many editors, stand between the poem and us — remains open in the literature. What is not disputed is the fact of mediation and interpolation at some scale: no party to the debate reads ʿUmar's text as a transparent window on the Greek. The date. The composition window (c. 25–75 CE) rests on the poem's internal horoscopes and on its place in the citation chain; it is a scholarly inference, broadly accepted, not an attested fact, and individual datings in the literature range across the later first century. This article uses “first century CE” without a narrower claim wherever the argument does not need one.

The right conclusion from the mediation is not despair but calibration. Where the Arabic Dorotheus agrees with the Greek fragments — and Hephaistion's extensive paraphrase gives the control — the doctrine is Dorotheus's. Where it stands alone, it is Dorothean tradition, which for the history of practice is nearly as good: the medieval world's elections were made from this text, interpolations and all, and Section XIV's workshop timings descend from it by that road.

X. Vettius Valens: The Practitioner's Handbook

If Dorotheus is the profession's founder-poet, Vettius Valens of Antioch (c. 120 – c. 175 CE) is its working master, and his Anthology — nine books, the longest astrological work surviving from Greco-Roman antiquity — is the closest thing the ancient world left to a practitioner's complete bench-book. Valens travelled to Egypt to learn, says so, and complains about the teachers he found there: in paraphrase of Anthology IV, he and his companions traversed many lands and came to Egypt, where they fell in with avaricious teachers — a sentence, preserved in Mark Riley's translation, that tells the reader more about the second-century astrological marketplace than a chapter of generalities. The handbook he wrote out of that apprenticeship is organised as teaching: Book I, the vocabulary (Sections V–VI above are its table of contents, expanded); the middle books, the topical judgements and the great time-lord systems; the later books, length of life, climacteric years, and the transmissions of the times, addressed in part to a student named Marcus. Riley's complete English translation — made freely available online by its translator, and read in full for this article in the 2019 Skopje edition prepared from it — is the modern door into the work, with the translator's own warning, carried in that edition's prefatory note, that the mathematical sections may contain his misunderstandings: a candour Valens himself would have recognised.

The Tone: Initiation, Grievance, and Pride

No ancient technical author has a voice like Valens's, and the voice is evidence. Three registers alternate. There is the initiatory register: at the close of his treatment of the length of life (Anthology V, in Riley's translation, in paraphrase), Valens exacts an oath from his readers — by the Sun, the Moon, the five planets, and the circle of the twelve signs — to guard his teaching as a mystic secret, never to share it with the ignorant or uninitiated, and to remember and honour the one who inducted them into the art; blessings on those who keep the oath, and curses of classical completeness on those who foreswear it. The same oath-gate closes the ninth book's preface, where Valens addresses Marcus. There is the polemical register: Valens derides the bombast and obscurity of predecessors — his set-piece attack on Critodemus's Vision, whose fantastical opening and frightful oaths he mocks before plundering its methods anyway, is the locus classicus — and complains, repeatedly, of rivals who publish nonsense and teachers who hoard. And there is the register of professional pride, the master's insistence that his methods, tested in practice, correct the ancients where the ancients err. The composite is unmistakable: a guild craftsman writing for initiates, in a competitive market, who believes his art is a sacred trust and his version of it the true one. Astrology in Valens is not a science communicating results. It is a mystery with a technique — and the oath is not in tension with the technique's rigour. It is its frame.

The Case Material: A Hundred and Thirty Lives

What lifts the Anthology above every other handbook is its cases. Valens teaches by worked example, and his examples are real nativities — about a hundred and thirty literary horoscopes, roughly four-fifths of all that survive from antiquity, with birth dates running from 37 CE into his own lifetime. The cases are not illustrations chosen to flatter the rules. They are case histories: natives who rose, fell, were exiled, drowned, murdered, enriched, ruined — their charts computed, their periods run, their outcomes matched, in retrospect, to the configurations that (the master argues) had promised them. Beck's account stresses the methodological consequence: the horoscopes of the dead were the profession's empirical check, the dataset against which outcome-rules were supposedly verified — with the standing caveat, Beck's and this article's alike, that a system of such superabundance (Section VIII) can find a configuration for any outcome after the fact, and the check is therefore weaker than it looks.

The most famous case is a collective one, and it deserves its paragraph. In Anthology VII.6, Valens sets out the nativities of six men who were together in a crisis at sea — caught in a storm, nearly wrecked, and then fallen in with pirates, and surviving both. One of the six, on the usual reading, is Valens himself, in his thirty-fifth year. The analysis asks the sharpest question a determinist can ask his own system: six different charts, one shared event — where, in each chart, is the storm? Valens's answer runs through the time-lords: from each nativity he extracts the numbers — periods, rising times, transmissions — that place each man, by his own clock, at a crisis in the same year, and exhibits the convergence as the system's vindication. Modern readers divide. Read generously — and Beck devotes a chapter's centrepiece to the case — it is a quasi-empirical test, an attempt to check one event against six independent datasets. Read sceptically, as Beck also does, the test's scientific merit collapses at the point where Valens, presented with several defensible values for his terms, selects the ones that make the numbers come out. Both readings are given here because both are true to the document: the instinct to test is real, and so is the thumb on the scale. In that doubleness, VII.6 is the whole ancient profession in one chapter.

Work from Riley's translation, and keep two companions open: Neugebauer and Van Hoesen's Greek Horoscopes, which dates and recomputes the case material (most of Valens's horoscopes are translated there), and Pingree's 1986 Greek text, whose page numbers Riley's translation carries alongside Kroll's 1908 edition. Expect accretions: the Anthology was copied more than any other astrological work in antiquity, and used copies attract marginalia that become text. A handbook that smells of the workshop — interpolations, worked examples, an oath — is a handbook that was worked from. The dirt is the provenance.

XI. Ptolemy: The Tetrabiblos and the Natural-Philosophy Defence

Claudius Ptolemy, working in Alexandria in the middle decades of the second century CE, wrote the two books between which all later Western astrology lives: the Almagest, which computes the heavens, and the Tetrabiblos — the Four Books, also titled Apotelesmatika, “On the Effects” — which interprets them. The Tetrabiblos opens (I.1, in Robbins's translation, in paraphrase) by dividing prediction through astronomy into its two means: the first, astronomy proper, apprehends the configurations of Sun, Moon, and stars in themselves, and has been treated demonstratively in the Almagest; the second, the subject of this work, investigates the changes those configurations bring about in what they surround — and is, Ptolemy says at once, the less self-sufficient of the two, to be expounded “in a properly physical way”. The whole defence of Book I, chapters 2 and 3, is the working out of that sentence, and it is the most consequential apologia any ancient art received.

The Mechanism: Power Through the Ambient

The defence of possibility (I.2) argues from the undeniable to the disputable in careful steps. That the Sun governs the seasons, generation, and the daily round of heat, moisture, dryness, and cold, no one denies; that the Moon, nearest of the heavenly bodies, moves the tides and the humours of living things, farmers and sailors already know by observation. The general claim follows in the one sentence of Ptolemy this article quotes at length, verified in Robbins's Loeb translation (Tetrabiblos I.2): a power from the eternal ethereal substance, he writes, “is dispersed through and permeates the whole region about the earth” — fire and air, the upper sublunar elements, being changed by the motions in the ether and in turn changing earth, water, plants, and animals. Astral influence, in Ptolemy, is therefore physical causation through the ambient: not messages, not demons, not sympathies in the occult sense, but the continuous modulation of the elementary qualities of the sublunar world by the motions above it. If the ambient at a birth stamps the temperament of what is born — as seed, soil, and climate visibly do — then a man who knows the heavens' configurations, and the natures of the bodies configured, can read the stamp. That, in paraphrase, is the argument; its conclusion is deliberately modest, and the modesty is load-bearing (below).

The Qualities: Where Planet-Natures Come From

Book I.4 then derives the planets' natures from the mechanism, and the derivation is the Tetrabiblos's most influential page. In Robbins's translation, verified for this article: “The active power of the sun's essential nature is found to be heating and, to a certain degree, drying.” The Moon's power is chiefly humidifying, from her nearness to the earth and its moist exhalations, with a share of heating from the Sun's light she borrows. Saturn, farthest from the Sun's heat and the earth's moisture alike, chiefly cools and moderately dries; Mars dries and burns, by his fiery colour and his sphere's nearness to the Sun; Jupiter, moving between Saturn's cold and Mars's burning, is temperate, heating and humidifying, and produces fertilising winds; Venus shares Jupiter's tempered nature with the emphasis reversed, humidifying chiefly; Mercury, never far from the Sun's heat and just above the Moon's sphere, alternates — drying and humidifying by turns, quick as his motion. Chapter 5 draws the moral the whole tradition had already drawn, now with a physics beneath it: since of the four humours — qualities — the hot and the moist are fertile and active, and the dry and the cold destructive and passive, Jupiter, Venus, and the Moon are beneficent, and Saturn and Mars, abounding in the destructive pair, maleficent. The doctrine is older than Ptolemy. The grounding — planetary character as a theorem of elemental physics — is his contribution, and it is why this cluster files the present article beside The Four Elements, where the quality-system itself is set out, and Stoic Physics, where the rival-and-ally framework of cosmic sympatheia — the whole's parts breathing together — supplies the other ancient language for the same intuition. Ptolemy's ether-power and the Stoics' sympathy are not the same theory; the handbooks' practice drew on both vocabularies without much caring, and the reader of the sources must keep them distinct precisely because the sources often do not.

The Concessions: A Conjectural Art, Defended as Such

What distinguishes Ptolemy's defence from a sales pitch is how much it gives away. In I.2, in paraphrase: every science that deals with the quality of its subject-matter is conjectural and not to be absolutely affirmed, and this one is composed of many unlike elements; the ancient configurations on which its rules rest never exactly recur; in nativities, moreover, the heavens are not the only cause — seed, country, rearing, and custom all contribute, and the astrologer who ignores them errs by his own art's principles. In I.3, the defence of usefulness answers the fatalist objection in kind: foreknowledge does not mock effort, because the heavenly cause works through natural sequence and mutable fate in the sublunar world, not by irrevocable decree; foreseen evils can sometimes be averted or softened, as a physician's prognosis enables treatment — and the comparison with medicine and with navigation, arts honoured though they often err, runs through both chapters. Ptolemy also dismisses, in I.2's paraphrased argument, the profession's own worst advertisements: the charlatans who claim the art's name for impossible feats and bring the genuine subject into disrepute — we do not abolish philosophy, he remarks in effect, because there are rascals among those who pretend to it. The defence, in short, claims for astrology exactly the epistemic status of the other conjectural arts of antiquity, and not a hairsbreadth more. Whether antiquity believed he had claimed more is Section XII's question.

The Purge: What Ptolemy Leaves Out

The Tetrabiblos is as famous for its exclusions as its arguments, and the exclusions follow from the defence: whatever cannot be given a plausible account in terms of primary natural causes is dismissed. In Robbins's translation of I.3's programme (the passage is widely quoted; this article gives it as labelled paraphrase to keep its quotation discipline): the labour wasted on lots and numbers, of which no reasonable explanation can be given, is to be set aside in favour of the science of the aspects of the stars to the places with which they have familiarity. The sentence executes most of the working apparatus of Sections VI–VII at a stroke. The lots go — Ptolemy retains, grudgingly and in passing, a Lot of Fortune for the length-of-life calculation, computed without the sect reversal his colleagues insisted on. The decans go. The terms are reformed (Section VI). Katarchai go entirely — no elections, no interrogations. The time-lord systems shrink to a single austere doctrine of prorogation for the length of life. Mark Riley's classic comparison — “Theoretical and Practical Astrology: Ptolemy and His Colleagues” (1987) — states the resulting shape exactly: Dorotheus, Valens, Firmicus, and Hephaistion each wrote works some three times the Tetrabiblos's length, aimed at the day-to-day needs of clients; Ptolemy wrote a theory of astral influence with an astrological handbook attached, and the two literatures proceeded, respected and largely separate, down the centuries. The profession canonised Ptolemy and practised Dorotheus. Holding that sentence is the key to the next section.

XII. How “Scientific” Was Ptolemy's Defence Taken to Be?

The task set for this section is a dispute, and it is best approached by separating three questions that popular accounts habitually fuse: Was the Tetrabiblos intended as natural science? Was it received as natural science? And did its defence govern the practice of the art it defended? The evidence gives three different answers, and the interest of the episode lies in their divergence.

Intended: Yes — With the Concessions Built In

On intention there is little room for dispute. Ptolemy frames the work as the physical companion to a mathematical treatise, derives his planet-natures from the elementary qualities, purges whatever lacks a plausible natural account (Section XI), and calibrates the art's certainty below astronomy's in his opening pages. A. A. Long's standard treatment — “Astrology: Arguments Pro and Contra” (1982), the account Beck directs his readers to for the philosophical debate — reads Ptolemy as writing for an audience educated as he was, within a live ancient controversy in which astrology's defenders and its philosophical critics (the sceptical tradition descending through Carneades to Sextus Empiricus, and the fatalism debates of the Stoic schools) were arguing about exactly the points I.2–3 addresses: causation, certainty, freedom. On this reading the defence is a serious contribution to ancient philosophy of science, whose concessions are not embarrassment but method. Barton's treatment concurs in substance: the Tetrabiblos is the one ancient astrological work that argues for its art the way a natural philosopher argues, and its influence on the art's respectability — its theological tolerance in the medieval West, its place in university curricula into the Renaissance — is directly credited, in the modern reference literature, to that philosophical defence.

Received: As the Art's Bible — Read Selectively

On reception the record is double, and both halves are documented. The Tetrabiblos was copied, paraphrased (a close paraphrase circulated under Proclus's name), commented on, and translated — into Arabic in the ninth century, into Latin by Plato of Tivoli in the twelfth — until, in Riley's phrase, it became virtually the Bible of astrology. Yet the same centuries that revered the text quietly declined its programme. The lots Ptolemy dismissed returned at the head of every medieval handbook, multiplied into the “Arabic parts”. The elections he omitted became, through Dorotheus's Arabic descendants, the busiest branch of the medieval art. The spurious Centiloquium — a hundred aphorisms fathered on Ptolemy, generally judged not his by modern scholarship, Robbins's introduction included — was for many medieval practitioners the most-used “Ptolemy” of all, aphorism 9 and its doctrine of images included: the authority's name, licensing the practice the authority had purged. Even Ptolemy's own reformed terms lost to the Egyptian bounds in the profession's daily use (Section VI). Reception, in short, took the prestige and the physics and left the purge — a pattern any historian of a canonical text will recognise.

Governed Practice: Largely, No — and Why That Matters Here

On governance, Riley's comparison (Section XI) is the controlling modern judgement, and neither Barton nor Beck dissents from its shape: contemporary and later practitioners respected Ptolemy as a pathfinder and careful researcher while practising an art whose contents — lots, decans, elections, time-lords, case law — came from the Dorothean and Valensian line. The strongest form of the sceptical reading goes further, and should be stated: that Ptolemy's personal commitment to astrology as a practical system of forecasting is itself thinly evidenced — he left no worked nativities, no cases, none of the professional residue Valens left in bulk — and that the Tetrabiblos may be best read as a philosopher's rationalisation of a going concern rather than a practitioner's manual. The counter-reading, that systematising was his practice and the absence of cases a matter of genre, is also held. This article does not settle the question, because the evidence does not settle it; it reports the position the current literature supports, which is the compound one: the defence was taken, by the learned world that read it, as a successful natural-philosophical grounding of the art — successful enough to carry astrology's intellectual respectability for fifteen centuries — and it was taken, by the profession that practised the art, as an authority to cite rather than a method to follow. Both takings are facts of reception, and the gap between them is not a scandal to be explained away. It is the normal fate of a theory more elegant than the practice it theorises.

// A MODERN MISREADING TO AVOID //

It does not follow — and Barton and Beck both warn, in their different registers, against the inference — that because Ptolemy's defence reads to a modern eye like “science”, ancient astrology sorted itself into a scientific party and a superstitious one. Valens, the oath-swearing initiate of Section X, computes with the best tables and tests his cases; Ptolemy, the natural philosopher, retains the benefic–malefic scheme and the length-of-life prorogations of the common art. The ancient fault line runs not between science and superstition but between theory and practice — between explaining the art and working it. Modern accounts that recruit Ptolemy as a proto-scientist manqué, or dismiss Valens as a credulous technician, have imported the fault line from their own century.

XIII. Astrology Among the Arts: Medicine and Iatromathematics

No neighbouring art absorbed more of the system, or shows its social reach better, than medicine. Iatromathematics — medical mathematics, the tradition's own name for astrological medicine — rested on three of the apparatus's pieces already in hand. Melothesia assigned the body to the chart: the signs in order from Aries at the head to Pisces at the feet, the planets over their organs and humours (Valens's dossiers of Section V are melothesia in catalogue form), the decans over finer divisions still. Decumbiture cast the chart for the moment the patient took to bed — a katarchē of illness, judged like any other beginning — to read the sickness's course and kind. And the critical days, the Hippocratic doctrine that an acute fever turns on numbered days — the fourth, the seventh, the eleventh, the fourteenth — received from Galen (second century CE), in his three books On the Critical Days, a lunar mechanism: the critical days fall as the Moon reaches its successive quadratures from its place at the illness's onset, a medical month fitted to the Moon's course. From Galen the doctrine passed, with the rest of his authority, into Arabic and Latin medicine entire.

Galen's own position is the instructive complication, and recent scholarship — Glen M. Cooper's work is the modern reference — states it as a deliberate doubleness. Galen founded medical astrology's central doctrine and must be counted, on the strength of the Critical Days, a founder of iatromathematics; the explicitly astrological Prognostics from Decumbiture that later bore his name is pseudo-Galenic. Yet Galen also mocked the genethliakoi, the horoscope-casters, and Cooper's reading is that his engagement with judicial astrology was rhetorical rather than sincere — a natural astrology of lunar periods and ambient qualities, continuous with Ptolemy's physics, wearing astrological dress for a medical argument. The physician, on Galen's own insistence as Cooper reports it, must not follow the heavenly indicators alone but watch the signs in the patient and weigh all the evidence together. The pattern of Section XII repeats exactly, in a white coat: the theory honoured, the practice hedged, the authority borrowed. For the humoral system that made the whole exchange intelligible — the four humours, the temperaments, and Galen's synthesis of them — see the companion article The Humours and Galen, which treats the medicine from its own side; the present article's point is only that the traffic ran on astrological rails. The astrologer timed the crisis; the physician, increasingly, was expected to know why the timing worked.

One more medical thread leads directly to the final section. Thessalus of Tralles, the first-century Methodist physician whom Galen vituperated, wrote — in Cooper's summary — on astro-pharmacy: the gathering and preparation of medicinal plants under their astral rulers, the plant's power waxing with its planet's condition, the remedy's making timed to the heavens. Thessalus framed the work, characteristically, as a revelation sought in Egypt — a vision of Asclepius, in the Nechepso–Petosiris line of Section III. Herbs, planets, and timing: the triangle is complete, and it is the triangle inside which Alexandrian alchemy also worked. The last section walks through its third side.

XIV. Closing the Loop: Astrology as the Art's Timing Science

This cluster of foundations exists because Alexandrian alchemy did not merely happen in the same cities as Greek astrology. It ran on it. The alchemical work — the preparation, the conjunction of materials, the long digestion, the projection — was an undertaking like any other in Section VIII's world, and undertakings had beginnings, and beginnings had heavens. The art's timing science is the subject of this final section, in three movements: the election, the calendar, and the sympathy.

Elections: A Nativity for a Work

The mechanism is Dorotheus's (Section IX), generalised. To begin a work is to give it a genesis, and the practitioner who could read a genesis could choose one: cast forward through the coming days for the hour when the Ascendant and its ruler stand strong and unafflicted, when the Moon — the universal significatrix of beginnings, whose corruption Dorotheus made the first disqualification — waxes and applies to benefics, when the planet of the work's kind is dignified, in sect, and beholding the Ascendant, and the place of the work's topic is fortified by its ruler's condition. A work of the Sun's kind — a solar medicine, a gold work — wants the Sun's day and hour, the Sun angular and in Leo or its exaltation; a work of Venus, Venus; a work of Saturn, with the care Saturn's elections always demand, Saturn. The later workshop literature states the rules with the bluntness of long habit: begin nothing under a corrupted Moon; let the ruler of the work behold the beginning; let the beginning's chart be, in the old phrase's sense, a good nativity. The doctrine survived its world entire — through the Arabic electional literature that descends from Book V, through the Latin Picatrix tradition of timed image-making, into the Renaissance workshop, where the election of times for operations is a standard chapter of the art — because it answered the practitioner's real question in the only vocabulary the pre-modern workshop possessed for it: not whether the heavens govern beginnings, but which beginning, of those on offer this week, is the one the heavens govern well.

Planetary Days and Hours: The Workshop Calendar

Beneath the full election runs the coarse calendar every practitioner knew by heart, and its mechanism is a small masterpiece of the system's internal arithmetic. Divide the day into twelve hours of daylight and twelve of the night — seasonal hours, lengthening and shortening with the Sun. Assign the hours in turn to the planets in their descending sphere-order — Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, Sun, Venus, Mercury, Moon — the Chaldean order of Section V, cycling without break through day and night alike. The planet ruling the first hour of daylight rules the day; and because twenty-four hours distribute the seven planets three times over with three to spare, the first-hour ruler advances by three places in the order each morning — Sun, Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, Saturn — generating, by pure arithmetic, the week of planetary days whose names the modern European languages still carry: Sunday, Monday, and in the Romance tongues Mars's, Mercury's, Jupiter's, and Venus's days undisguised. The scheme is Hellenistic — Dio Cassius already explains it, as a novelty become universal, in the early third century — and it made every workshop a observatory of the simplest kind: the day itself announced its ruler, the hour refined it, and the operation of the hour took its planet's name and nature. The grimoire tradition's famous tables of the days and hours and virtues of the planets — each experiment to be performed under the planet, and usually in the hour, to which it refers — are this calendar, copied for a thousand years. When a late workshop text directs that a work be begun on its planet's day, in its planet's hour, with the Moon increasing and in a sign of the planet's triplicity, every term of the direction is a term of Sections V–VI of this article. The foundations cluster exists to make such sentences legible.

Sympathies: The Technical Hermetica

The third movement is the deepest, and the hub treats it at length in the companion article on the Technical Hermetica: the astrological, medical, and lapidary literature under Hermes' name, in which the doctrine of astral sympathy becomes a working materia medica. The Cyranides organise the world's birds, fishes, plants, and stones under their planetary and zodiacal rulers, each creature carrying its ruler's virtue and usable, by sympathy and antipathy, against the conditions its ruler governs. The Hermetic Sacred Book tradition assigns plants to decans and planets — Thessalus's astro-pharmacy of Section XIII is its medical face — and the iatromathematical herbals time the gathering to the ruler's strength, on the theory Ptolemy would have recognised in physical dress: the plant gathered under its planet's good condition is richest in the quality that planet pours into the ambient. Alchemy drank from the same spring. The metals' planetary names — gold of the Sun, silver of the Moon, iron of Mars, tin of Jupiter, copper of Venus, lead of Saturn, and quicksilver of Mercury himself — are sympathy-doctrine become nomenclature, and the workshop timings of the preceding movement are sympathy-doctrine become scheduling. Zosimos of Panopolis, writing at the tradition's source around 300 CE, works inside this world without pausing to explain it, as a man does not explain his mother tongue: the visions and the apparatus alike presuppose a cosmos in which times, materials, and spirits answer to the seven rulers and their turning. To read him without the system of this article is to read the words without the grammar.

The loop, then, is closed, and it is worth stating the closure plainly, as the cluster's thesis. Greek astrology was not an ornament on Alexandrian alchemy, nor a superstition the two arts happened to share. It was the timing science of the world that produced them both: the one complete, computable, teachable system antiquity possessed for answering the question every practice asks of time — when, and under what heavens? — and the alchemical work, which of all ancient practices most needed beginnings chosen and durations governed, took the system's answer as its own. The furnace kept celestial time because everything in that world did; but the furnace, alone among workshops, wrote the fact into its literature, and so preserved for us the clearest picture of what the timing science was for. The stars did not make the work succeed. They told the worker when to begin — and in the Hellenistic world, that was not a small thing for a science to do. It was, on the evidence of a thousand years of practice, the main thing.
