THE TROJAN CYCLE: THE WAR AS TRADITION, TEXT, AND RUIN

Mount Ida · Aulis · The Troad · Hisarlik

// NOT TWO POEMS BUT A RING :: CYPRIA TO TELEGONY :: THE MOUND AT HISARLIK //

The Greeks did not remember the Trojan War as two great poems with gaps around them. They remembered it as a circle: a ring of epics running from the wedding at which Strife threw her apple to the death of Odysseus at the hands of his own son. Two of the ring's poems survive. The rest are known from summaries, fragments, and the plays and pictures that fed on them — and at the ring's centre stands a real mound in north-west Anatolia that has been dug, argued over, and misread for a hundred and fifty years. This article keeps the tradition, the texts, and the ruin in three separate registers, and says at every point which register it is speaking in.

I. A War Too Big for Two Poems

Ask a modern reader what the Iliad is about and the answer comes back as the Trojan War. The poem itself refuses the compliment. It covers a few weeks of the war's tenth year — a quarrel in the Greek camp, a withdrawal, a death, a revenge, a funeral — and it ends not with Troy taken but with Troy's greatest defender buried, the city still standing, its fall still in the future and known to every listener in advance. The Odyssey likewise covers the war only in retrospect: songs sung at feasts, veterans' reminiscences, a wooden horse recalled in a Spartan hall and in the underworld. Between them, the two surviving epics narrate perhaps a tenth of the story the Greeks told about Troy. The rest — why the war began, how the fleet was gathered, what it cost to launch it, how Achilles died, how the city actually fell, what happened to the victors on the way home, and how the last of the heroes died — was carried by six further epics, composed in the same dactylic hexameter and the same artificial epic dialect, which together with the Iliad and Odyssey formed the Trojan section of what antiquity called the Epic Cycle (Greek Epikos Kyklos, “the epic circle”).

Those six poems are lost. The Cypria told everything before the Iliad: the divine decision to have a war, the Judgement of Paris, the flight of Helen, the mustering at Aulis, the sacrifice of Iphigenia, and nine years of raiding. The Aethiopis continued from Hector's funeral through the coming of the Amazons and of Memnon to the death of Achilles. The Little Iliad carried the story from the contest for Achilles' arms to the building of the Wooden Horse and the firing of the Greek camp. The Iliou Persis — the Sack of Ilium — told the night itself: the Horse opened, the altars violated, the city burned. The Nostoi, the Returns, followed the victors home into storm, shipwreck, and, in Agamemnon's case, a bath and a murderer. And the Telegony closed the ring after the Odyssey, with Odysseus's further voyages and his death, unknowing, at the hand of the son Circe had borne him. Read in sequence, the eight poems gave a Greek listener the whole war, from a quarrel among goddesses at a wedding to the extinction of the heroic generation. The Iliad and Odyssey were later lifted out of that ring — canonised, commented on, made the centre of Greek education — and the ring itself was allowed to perish. Sections II–III set out what exactly survives of it, and by what fragile route.

This article therefore works in three registers, and it marks them as it goes, because nearly every confusion in the vast popular literature on Troy comes from sliding between them. First, the tradition as told: the story the Greeks themselves organised and retold for a thousand years, in epic, tragedy, painting, and cult. Told whole, in cycle order, it occupies Sections IV–XII. Second, the texts: the lost poems as historical objects — who was said to have composed them, when, how they were summarised and excerpted, and how much of them a modern reader can actually recover. That is the business of Sections II–III and of the poem-by-poem sections that follow, and it is a much thinner record than the story's fame suggests. Third, the archaeology: the mound of Hisarlik in the Troad, its nine superimposed settlements, and the Hittite documents that name a kingdom in the right place at the right time. That record, treated in Sections XVI–XVIII at its real strength — neither inflated into proof of the poems nor dismissed because it cannot prove them — supports a Bronze Age citadel, repeatedly destroyed, one of whose destructions is compatible with a war around the date Greek tradition assigned. It does not support, and no honest reading of it has ever supported, the war as told: ten years, a thousand ships, and named heroes are literature, built — this is the part the spade does warrant — on a real place. The verdict section (XIX) holds those two statements apart, because the distance between them is precisely the subject.

THE RING AT A GLANCE

Eight poems in story order: Cypria (11 books) · Iliad (24) · Aethiopis (5) · Little Iliad (4) · Iliou Persis (2) · Nostoi (5) · Odyssey (24) · Telegony (2). Seventy-seven books of hexameter. Two poems survive entire; six survive as prose summaries, fragments, and echoes.

WHAT “LOST” MEANS HERE

No complete cyclic poem has existed for well over a millennium. What we have is: the prose summaries of Proclus (Section II); a few hundred quoted lines and half-lines preserved by later authors; the parallel prose narrative of Apollodorus's Library and Epitome; and the Cycle's vast reflex in archaic and classical art and tragedy. Reconstruction is possible; recovery is not.

SPELLINGS USED HERE

Greek names are transliterated, not Latinised, as throughout this hub: Ilios (not Ilium, except in the poem-title Iliou Persis as conventionally printed), Aias (not Ajax, except where a quoted translation or an established English title requires it), Alexandros for Paris's second name. Quotations keep their translators' spellings, including Evelyn-White's “Ilium” and “Ajax”.

One boundary should be drawn before proceeding. The Iliad and Odyssey as texts — their composition, their oral-formulaic making, their fixation and canonisation — are the subject of this hub's companion article, The Homeric Epics, and of the Homeric sections of Heroes and Epic. Here the two poems appear in their other capacity: as members of the ring, two segments of a longer story that the tradition told whole. Where this article touches them, it is to mark where they sit in the sequence and what they presuppose — the Iliad opens in the tenth year because the Cypria had already told the first nine — not to re-argue their making.

II. How We Know the Lost Poems: Proclus, Photius, and the Fragments

Everything in Sections IV–XI rests on a chain of transmission that is short enough to state in full, and every reader of the Trojan Cycle should know its links and its weak points. The cyclic poems themselves circulated in antiquity as texts: they were read, quoted, mined by tragedians, illustrated by painters, and eventually excerpted into handbooks. By late antiquity they were already rare, and at some point in the Byzantine centuries the last complete copies perished. What survived the poems is a set of prose summaries of their contents, attributed to a certain Proclus and drawn from his Chrestomathy — a “useful reading” handbook of literary history in four books. Who this Proclus was is genuinely uncertain: candidates canvassed in the scholarship include a grammarian of the second century CE and the Neoplatonist philosopher Proclus (c. 412–485 CE), and the question is not settled. The summaries' value does not depend on settling it. They are businesslike plot abstracts, poem by poem and book-count by book-count, and wherever they can be checked against surviving fragments and against the poems' reflexes in art and tragedy, they check out as faithful — compressed, colourless, but faithful.

The summaries themselves reach us by two narrow channels. The summary of the Cypria is preserved in a number of Iliad manuscripts. The summaries of the remaining five Trojan poems survive essentially in one: the tenth-century Venetus A manuscript of the Iliad, where they were copied into the scholarly apparatus as a guide to the whole saga surrounding the poem. A third witness stands behind both: the ninth-century patriarch and scholar Photius, whose Bibliotheca (codex 239) epitomises Proclus's Chrestomathy and confirms its contents — and also tells us that Proclus's Cycle was larger than the Trojan ring. In Photius's account the Epic Cycle ran from the union of Heaven and Earth, through the Titanomachy and the Theban poems (the Oedipodea, Thebaid, Epigoni, and Alcmaeonis), and only then to Troy: a complete epic history of the mythical world, of which the Trojan War was the final, climactic chapter. This article treats the Trojan section, as the hub's brief requires, but the reader should register the frame: for the tradition that assembled the Cycle, the war at Troy was not the beginning of anything. It was the end of the heroic age — the event after which, as the Cypria's own opening fragment puts it, the earth was emptied of the heroes.

The Fragments, and Apollodorus Beside Proclus

Around the summaries cluster the fragments: lines and passages of the actual poems quoted by later authors — scholiasts explaining Homer, Athenaeus at his dinner-table of learning, Pausanias describing paintings, Plato, Herodotus, the lexicographers. They amount, for all six poems together, to a few hundred lines at most, and they are not a random sample: later authors quoted what served their own purposes, so the fragments skew toward the quotable, the curious, and the disputed. Modern editions — those of Bernabé, Davies, and West are the standard three — gather fragments and testimonia poem by poem, and M. L. West's The Epic Cycle: A Commentary on the Lost Troy Epics (2013) is the first comprehensive commentary: it works through every fragment and testimonium and attempts, poem by poem, to reconstruct each lost epic's plan and course from the summaries, the fragments, and the evidence of early art and literature combined. West's further suggestion, reported here as his, is that both Proclus and Apollodorus ultimately derive their accounts from digests of the cyclic epics made in the Hellenistic period — which, if right, means our two prose witnesses are cousins, not independent checks on each other.

Apollodorus is the second witness, and the fuller storyteller. The Library (Bibliotheca) attributed to Apollodorus — a mythographic handbook of the first or second century CE, by an author modern scholarship calls Pseudo-Apollodorus — retells Greek myth in continuous prose from the beginnings to the end of the heroic age, and its surviving continuation, the Epitome, covers the Trojan War in detail (Epitome 3–7): the Judgement, the oath of Helen's suitors, the muster, Aulis, the war's course, the Horse, the sack, the returns, Agamemnon's murder, and Odysseus's death. The Epitome agrees with Proclus's summaries in sequence and in most particulars, and diverges in some — it names killers where Proclus is silent, and preserves conditions and episodes (the theft of the Palladium as a stated condition of the city's fall, Nauplius's false beacons) that the summaries compress away. Where Proclus and Apollodorus agree, the cyclic poem almost certainly stood behind both. Where they disagree, or where a tragedian disagrees with both, this article says so: the tradition was never a single fixed text, and its variants are evidence, not embarrassment. The sack of Troy offers the sharpest cases (Section IX).

// THE EVIDENCE LADDER, STATED ONCE //

For any given episode of the lost poems, the evidence ranks as follows. Highest: a surviving fragment of the poem itself, quoted by a named ancient author. Next: Proclus's summary, checked against Apollodorus. Next: Apollodorus alone, or a tragedian's or painter's version, which may reflect the cyclic poem or may innovate freely. Lowest: late mythographers and Roman poets — Hyginus, Dictys, Dares, Virgil — whose versions are evidence for the tradition's afterlife, not for the cyclic poems. The famous details that fail this ladder are flagged where they occur: the apple inscribed “for the fairest”, Achilles' vulnerable heel, and Laocoön crushed with both his sons are all later than the Cycle, and none of them is used in this article as cyclic evidence.

Were the Cyclic Poems Made to Fill Homer's Gaps?

Antiquity's own verdict on the cyclic poems was condescending, and it has stuck. Aristotle, in the Poetics (1459a–b, paraphrased), praises Homer for taking not the whole war but one unified part of it, and faults the Cypria and Little Iliad by contrast: they are episodic, strung around a person and a period rather than a single action, so that — his famous count — only one tragedy, or two at most, can be made from the Iliad or the Odyssey, while many have been made from the Cypria and more than eight from the Little Iliad, whose episodes he lists: the Award of the Arms, Philoctetes, Neoptolemus, Eurypylus, the Begging, the Laconian Women, the Sack, the Sailing of the Fleet, Sinon, the Trojan Women. Callimachus, in a much-quoted epigram (fr. 465 Pfeiffer, paraphrased), declares his hatred of the cyclic poem and of everything common and much-travelled. From these judgements grew the modern assumption, dominant into the twentieth century, that the cyclic epics were late, derivative patchwork — composed after Homer, by lesser poets, precisely to fill the gaps before, between, and after the two masterpieces.

Jonathan Burgess's The Tradition of the Trojan War in Homer and the Epic Cycle (2001) is the systematic challenge to that assumption, and its argument is reported here as the current revaluation's core. Burgess's evidence is chiefly the archaic visual record: surveying Trojan War scenes in early Greek art, he shows that episodes from outside the Iliad and Odyssey — the Judgement, the death of Achilles, the sack, the escapes and returns — predominate in archaic representation, while scenes securely tied to the Homeric poems specifically emerge surprisingly late. His conclusion, in the image his reviewers quote: if the tradition of the Trojan War were a tree, the Iliad and Odyssey would initially have been a couple of branches, and the Cycle's material the trunk. On this view the cyclic poems are not Homeric spin-offs but free-standing compositions drawing on a broad, stable, extra-Homeric tradition — “pre-Homeric” in the sense that their material took shape before Homer became the tradition's centre, whatever the dates of the particular texts that were eventually written down and summarised by Proclus. West, for his part, dates the six Trojan cyclic poems as texts to roughly 630–560 BCE — after the Homeric poems' formation, but squarely in the archaic world that also produced the earliest Trojan art. The two positions are compatible where it matters for this article: the story the Cycle tells is older than its surviving arrangement, the poems were independent works and not appendices, and their loss is a loss of archaic literature, not of footnotes to Homer.

III. The Ring on the Shelf: The Cycle Poem by Poem

The table below is the article's map. Attributions are the ancient ones, transmitted with the summaries and the fragments; they are attributions, not title-pages, and several were disputed already in antiquity — the Cypria was ascribed variously to Stasinus of Cyprus, to Hegesias (or Hegesinus) of Salamis, and to Cyprias of Halicarnassus, and one persistent ancient story made it Homer's own work, given to Stasinus as a dowry with his daughter. The floruits antiquity assigned (Arctinus placed at the 27th Olympiad, 776 BCE, in the Eusebian tradition) sit uneasily beside the later dates modern linguistic and historical arguments favour; West's c. 630–560 BCE for the poems as composed texts is given in the final column as the modern working estimate. What is not in dispute is the sequence: Proclus's summaries dovetail — each poem's summary ends where the next begins — and that dovetailing, whether it reflects the poems' original design or a later editor's arrangement of them into a cycle, is what makes the ring a ring.

Two features of the ring deserve emphasis before the story is told. The first is its completeness as design. The seams are deliberate: the Cypria ends with the distribution of the two captive women, Chryseis and Briseis, whose seizure and return drive the Iliad's plot, and with Zeus's plan to relieve the Trojans by detaching Achilles — the very mechanism of the Iliad's opening. The Aethiopis begins, on Proclus's showing, exactly at the Iliad's last line's aftermath: Hector buried, the Amazon arriving. The Telegony begins with the burial of the suitors, the business the Odyssey's final book leaves unfinished. Whoever stitched the ring — the poets themselves accommodating famous neighbours, or Hellenistic editors regularising a corpus — stitched it tightly. The second feature is its asymmetry of survival. The two poems that survived are the two that antiquity's schools adopted; the six that perished include the war's beginning, its end, and its entire moral reckoning in the returns. A reader who knows only Homer knows the wrath and the wanderings. The Cycle's reader knew the consequences — and it is the consequences, as Sections XIII–XIV will show, that classical Greece could not stop staging and ritualising.

BEFORE THE RING: THE WIDER CYCLE

Photius's Proclus began with the Titanomachy and the Theban poems. The Trojan ring is the larger Cycle's final movement — the gods' wars and Thebes' destruction precede it, so that Troy's fall closes the age of heroes altogether. For the divine succession that opens the sequence, see this hub's Theogony.

THE POEMS' OWN DATES

Ancient floruits (Arctinus at 776 BCE) are traditional, not documentary. West's working dates for the six Trojan poems as texts, c. 630–560 BCE, rest on language, on their relations to the Homeric poems, and on their reflexes in datable art. Burgess's argument concerns the tradition's age, which is a different and older quantity.

IV. Cypria I: The Plan of Zeus, Eris, and the Judgement of Paris

The Cypria — eleven books, the longest of the lost poems — does not begin with Helen or Paris. It begins with a decision in heaven, and the poem's surviving first fragment states it with a bleakness that frames the entire Cycle. The fragment is preserved by a scholiast on Iliad 1.5, commenting on Homer's phrase “the plan of Zeus was fulfilled”, and is quoted here in Evelyn-White's translation (1914):

“There was a time when the countless tribes of men, though wide-dispersed, oppressed the surface of the deep-bosomed earth, and Zeus saw it and had pity and in his wise heart resolved to relieve the all-nurturing earth of men by causing the great struggle of the Ilian war, that the load of death might empty the world. And so the heroes were slain in Troy, and the plan of Zeus came to pass.” — CYPRIA FR. 1, PRESERVED BY THE SCHOLIAST ON ILIAD 1.5, TRANS. H. G. EVELYN-WHITE (LOEB, 1914)

In Proclus's summary, Zeus plans the war in council with Themis. The instrument is a wedding. Peleus, a mortal, is to marry the sea-goddess Thetis — herself, in another Cypria fragment, a bride assigned to a mortal because she had refused Zeus and Zeus had sworn it in anger — and the gods feast at the marriage on Mount Pelion. Strife (Eris) arrives at the feast and starts a dispute among Hera, Athena, and Aphrodite as to which of them is fairest. Proclus says no more than that: no apple is mentioned in the summary, and the golden apple inscribed “for the fairest” — the detail every modern retelling leads with — belongs to later versions of the story (it is in Hyginus and in Lucian, not in Proclus, and the inscription is later still). The archive records the apple as the tradition's most successful later addition, and the summary's plainer quarrel as the cyclic evidence. Zeus will not judge his wife, his daughter, and Aphrodite himself. At his command Hermes leads the three to Alexandros — Paris — a king's son herding on Mount Ida above Troy, and Alexandros, in Proclus's blunt phrase, “lured by his promised marriage with Helen, decides in favour of Aphrodite”. Apollodorus (Epitome 3.2, paraphrased) spells out the three offers as the tradition standardised them: Hera offered rule over all men (in some tellings, kingship of Europe and Asia), Athena victory in war, Aphrodite the marriage with Helen, the most beautiful woman alive. Paris chose the woman. The bribes are the point: the Judgement is not a beauty contest misjudged but a choice among three lives — empire, glory, desire — and the herdsman chose desire, and the world paid.

Helen, and How She Came to Be Helen

The Cypria also carried Helen's own strange origin, in a fragment preserved by Athenaeus (viii. 334 B, in Evelyn-White's translation, paraphrased here except for the sense): Helen was the child of Zeus and Nemesis — Retribution herself — who fled the god across land and sea, changing shape from fish to beast to beast, until Zeus caught her; the egg that resulted passed, in the fuller tradition, to Leda of Sparta, and Helen hatched from it, “a marvel to men”. Other accounts made Helen simply Leda's daughter by Zeus in swan form; the Cypria's version, with Nemesis as mother, is the darker and arguably the more programmatic one: the woman over whom Europe and Asia would fight was Retribution's child. Grown, Helen was already a prize contested by heroes before Paris: Theseus had carried her off as a girl, in the cyclic tradition the scholia preserve, and her brothers Castor and Polydeuces (the Dioscuri) had rescued her, sacking Aphidna and Athens in the process. She was then married to Menelaus of Sparta — chosen from a crowd of suitors under the oath that Section V describes — and it was as Menelaus's wife, queen in his palace, and mother of his daughter Hermione, that Paris found her.

The Flight

Proclus's summary of the abduction is a masterpiece of compression, and this article follows it step by step (paraphrased from Evelyn-White's rendering). Aphrodite, her promise to redeem, directs Alexandros to build ships. His brother Helenus, who has the gift of prophecy, foretells what will come of the voyage; his sister Cassandra prophesies likewise, and is — here as always in her story — not believed. Aeneas sails with him at Aphrodite's order. Alexandros lands in Lacedaemon, is entertained first by the sons of Tyndareus and then by Menelaus in Sparta, and at a feast gives gifts to Helen. Menelaus then sails for Crete, leaving Helen — the detail is Proclus's own, and it is devastating in its domesticity — with instructions to furnish the guests with all they require until they depart. Aphrodite brings Helen and Alexandros together; after their union they load very great treasures aboard and sail by night. Hera raises a storm; they are driven to Sidon, which Alexandros sacks; from Sidon he sails to Troy and celebrates his marriage with Helen. (Herodotus, at 2.117, cites the Cypria itself against the storm voyage: in the poem as he read it, Alexandrus reached Ilium from Sparta in three days with a fair wind — a reminder that even the cyclic text had variants, and that Herodotus used the poem as a documentary source for the story's geography.) Meanwhile, in a parallel strand the poem wove in, Castor and Polydeuces are caught stealing the cattle of Idas and Lynceus; Castor is killed, the killers are killed, and Zeus grants the brothers their strange shared immortality, each alive on alternate days — Helen's brothers thus removed from the war their sister is about to cause, and translated out of the mortal story at the moment it begins. Iris brings the news to Menelaus. The wronged husband comes home to an empty palace, stolen treasure, and a guest-friendship violated in his own hall. The war now needs only its machinery.

// GUEST-FRIENDSHIP: THE CRIME INSIDE THE CRIME //

Paris was Menelaus's xenos — his guest, bound by the sacred reciprocity Zeus himself protected as Zeus Xenios. The abduction was therefore not only adultery and theft but sacrilege against the institution that made travel, diplomacy, and civilisation possible. Greek tellings never let the point drop: it is why the war is, in the tradition's own terms, just — and why its conduct, Section IX, is so damning when the avengers commit sacrileges of their own.

V. Cypria II: The Oath, Aulis and Iphigenia, and the Road to Troy

The Oath of Tyndareus

Why did all Greece sail? The tradition's answer is a contract. When Helen came of age, her suitors — in Apollodorus's catalogue (Library 3.10, paraphrased), a roll of the heroic age's kings and champions — competed for her at Sparta, and her father Tyndareus, fearing that the losers would make war on the winner, bound them all by oath, on the advice (in Apollodorus's telling) of Odysseus, who extracted Penelope's hand as his fee for the idea. The suitors swore, standing on the cut pieces of a sacrificed horse, that they would defend the chosen husband against any who wronged him in respect of the marriage, and would join in exacting redress. Menelaus was chosen. Years later, when Paris carried Helen off, the oath fell due — and the Cypria's muster is the oath being collected. The mechanism matters to the tradition's moral architecture: the expedition is not a riot of volunteers but a coalition bound by a sworn instrument, led by Menelaus's elder brother Agamemnon, king of Mycenae, as commander-in-chief. It also explains the coalition's reluctant members — above all Odysseus, who had devised the oath and now feigned madness to escape it, yoking mismatched animals and sowing salt (the fuller tradition, in Apollodorus and Hyginus), until Palamedes exposed him by laying the infant Telemachus in the plough's path. Proclus's summary records the exposure laconically: the leaders, gathering the host, detect Odysseus “when he pretends to be mad, not wishing to join the expedition, by seizing his son Telemachus for punishment at the suggestion of Palamedes” (Evelyn-White's rendering, paraphrased in compression). Odysseus never forgave Palamedes; the Cypria itself contained Palamedes' death — drowned, Pausanias reports from his reading of the poem (10.31.2, paraphrased), by Odysseus and Diomedes while he was out fishing. The coalition's cleverest man murdered its most inconvenient witness before the war had properly begun, and the cyclic poem said so.

Aulis: The Omen, the False Start, and the Stag

The host gathered at Aulis, the Boeotian harbour opposite Euboea, and there the tradition sets its first great omen. As the army sacrificed, a serpent darted from the altar, climbed a plane tree, and devoured a sparrow and her eight nestlings in the nest — nine birds — before Zeus turned it to stone where it clung. Calchas, the expedition's seer, read the sign: nine years of war, and Troy taken in the tenth. The Iliad itself preserves the omen, in Odysseus's retelling (2.308–330), which is one of the clearest places where the surviving poem leans on the cyclic story it presupposes. The fleet sailed — and landed in the wrong country. In Mysia, far south of the Troad, the Greeks stormed Teuthrania taking it for Troy, and were met by Telephus, the local king, a son of Heracles, who killed Thersander and was himself wounded in the thigh by Achilles before Dionysus tripped him with a vine. A storm scattered the fleet on its withdrawal, and the expedition dissolved homeward — Achilles to Scyros, where he married Deidameia, daughter of Lycomedes, a marriage whose son, Neoptolemus, will end the war his father could not. Eight years passed, in the tradition's arithmetic. Telephus's wound would not heal; an oracle told him that his wounder would heal him, and he came to Argos, where Achilles scraped rust from the very spear that had wounded him into the wound, and Telephus in return guided the fleet to Troy. The episode — the wounder as healer — became one of the tradition's most durable images, and Telephus's son Eurypylus will repay the debt in blood in Section VIII.

Iphigenia at Aulis

The fleet mustered a second time at Aulis, and now the story produces the episode by which the whole war's cost is first measured. Agamemnon, hunting, shot a stag and boasted that he surpassed even Artemis. The goddess, in anger, sent storms that pinned the fleet in harbour. Calchas declared her anger and its price: the army could not sail until Agamemnon sacrificed his daughter Iphigenia to Artemis. The girl was fetched from Mycenae on a lie — she was told she was being brought to marry Achilles. And then, in Proclus's summary of the Cypria, the poem performs the first of the tradition's great rescues: at the altar, Artemis snatched Iphigenia away, transported her to the land of the Taurians on the Black Sea, and made her immortal, setting a stag on the altar in her place. The army saw a deer die and sailed.

// TWO IPHIGENIAS, AND THE ARCHIVE KEEPS BOTH //

The substitution is the Cypria's version, and it is not the only one the Greeks told. Aeschylus's Agamemnon (458 BCE) remembers Iphigenia as really killed — gagged, lifted over the altar “like a goat”, her saffron robe slipping, her eyes pleading (paraphrased) — and makes the killing the moral engine of the whole Oresteia: it is why Clytemnestra waits ten years with an axe. Euripides staged both versions in two plays: in Iphigenia among the Taurians the rescued girl is found alive, a priestess compelled to sacrifice strangers; in Iphigenia at Aulis she goes willingly to a death the transmitted ending then undoes with a reported substitution — an ending scholars have long suspected of being a later addition to Euripides' text. The cyclic poem, the tragedians, and the mythographers do not agree, and no ancient authority reconciled them. Section XIII returns to what tragedy did with the disagreement.

Tenedos, Lemnos, and the First Man Ashore

Released, the fleet sailed to Tenedos, the island off the Troad, and there the expedition shed another indispensable man. At a feast, Philoctetes — the archer who held the bow and arrows of Heracles, without which, though the Greeks did not yet know it, Troy could not fall — was bitten by a snake. The wound festered and stank until the army could not bear it, and Philoctetes was put ashore on Lemnos and left there, alone with his bow, for ten years. Proclus records the abandonment in one clause; Sophocles built one of his greatest plays out of the moral filth of it (Section XIII). At the same feast, Achilles quarrelled with Agamemnon over a late invitation — the Cypria's small rehearsal for the great quarrel of the Iliad. Then the fleet crossed to the Troad. The Trojans contested the landing; Protesilaus, first of the Greeks to leap ashore, was killed by Hector — the first Greek death of the war, fulfilling the oracle that the first to land would be the first to die. Achilles killed Cycnus, a son of Poseidon invulnerable to iron, and drove the Trojans from the beach. The Greeks sent Menelaus and Odysseus as envoys into Troy to demand Helen and the treasure back — the tradition's formal last chance of peace, preserved also in the Iliad's recollections — and the Trojans refused. The army assaulted the walls, failed, and settled into the war the Cypria spends its remaining books narrating: nine years of raiding the Troad and its allied cities, Lyrnessus and Pedasus and the cities of Aeneas's country among them, Achilles killing Troilus, a son of Priam, in Apollo's sanctuary (a killing the archaic painters loved and the tradition counted among the war's darkening acts), Patroclus selling Priam's son Lycaon into slavery on Lemnos. Out of the spoils of those raids, Achilles received Briseis as his prize of honour, and Agamemnon received Chryseis. The summary then names, in its final lines, the death of Palamedes, the plan of Zeus to relieve the Trojans by detaching Achilles from the confederacy, and a catalogue of the Trojan allies — and stops. The wrath is loaded. The Iliad begins.

VI. The Tenth Year: The Iliad Inside the Ring

What the Iliad narrates — the plague, the quarrel over Chryseis and Briseis, Achilles' withdrawal, the embassy, Patroclus's aristeia and death at Hector's hands, Achilles' return, the killing of Hector, Priam's night journey to ransom his son's body — is treated in this hub at The Homeric Epics, and is not retold here. What belongs to this article is the poem's position, and two observations about it. The first is how much of the Cycle the Iliad assumes its audience already knows. It alludes to the Judgement of Paris exactly once, in its final book (24.28–30), as a thing of long ago that explains Hera's and Athena's hatred. It recalls the Aulis omen, the embassy to Troy, Philoctetes marooned on Lemnos with his wound (2.716–725, in the Catalogue, with the prophecy that the Greeks would soon remember him), Protesilaus dead and his wife tearing her cheeks (2.695–710), Telephus, the raids, the distribution of the captives — the Cypria's whole contents, in glancing references that make sense only as pointers into a story already familiar. And it prophesies, with complete specificity, everything the later poems will narrate: Achilles' death, foretold by his mother, by Hector dying, and by his own horse; the fall of the city, foretold over Hector's body and in Andromache's laments, down to Astyanax hurled from the towers. The Iliad is a poem that declines to narrate the war's end while keeping that end in view on every page. The second observation is Burgess's (Section II): that allusive density is what a poem looks like when it is composed inside a living tradition, not at its head. The ring did not grow around the Iliad like ivy around a statue. The Iliad grew, like the other poems, out of the common stock — and happened, by genius and then by canonisation, to be the branch that survived.

The Iliad's last line gives Hector's burial. The war is not over: Achilles lives, Troy stands, and every listener knows both are doomed. The ring continues.

VII. Aethiopis: Penthesilea, Memnon, and the Death of Achilles

The Aethiopis, in five books, attributed to Arctinus of Miletus, takes up the story — Proclus is explicit about the dovetailing — where the Iliad sets it down. Its matter is the war's last great arrivals and the death toward which the whole tradition has been moving. Troy, Hector dead, receives two champions from the edges of the world. First comes Penthesilea, daughter of Ares, a Thracian, leading her Amazons to fight for Troy — in the fuller tradition, seeking purification at Priam's hands for the accidental killing of her sister. She has her day of glory in battle, and Achilles kills her. Then, in the episode the archaic world found unbearable and unforgettable, Achilles is mocked by Thersites — the one common soldier the Iliad gives a voice, there beaten for his mockery — who taunts him with love for the dead Amazon; and Achilles kills Thersites with a blow. A killing inside the army, of a Greek by the army's champion, over a corpse: the Achaeans are thrown into dispute, and Achilles must sail to Lesbos, sacrifice to Apollo, Artemis, and Leto, and be purified of the bloodshed by Odysseus. The war's greatest killer is ritually unclean, cleansed, and returned to the field — a sequence the epic treats as normal procedure, and which tells a modern reader more about archaic pollution law than any handbook.

Second comes Memnon, son of Eos, the Dawn, and of Tithonus, Priam's brother — the king of the Aethiopians, wearing armour made by Hephaestus, as Achilles does. Thetis warns her son about Memnon, in a mirroring of the whole poem's structure: two goddess-born or goddess-descended champions, two sets of god-made armour, two mothers who will mourn. In the battle Memnon kills Antilochus, the son of Nestor — Achilles' dearest friend after Patroclus, who dies, in the fuller tradition, interposing himself to save his father. Achilles kills Memnon in revenge. Eos obtains from Zeus the gift she begs, and Memnon is made immortal — the consolation Achilles will not get. For Achilles now does what the Iliad always said he would do: he routs the Trojans, pursues them into the very gates — and there, in Proclus's summary as Evelyn-White renders it, in the sentence this article quotes because the whole Cycle turns on it:

“Achilles routs the Trojans, and, rushing into the city with them, is killed by Paris and Apollo.” — PROCLUS, SUMMARY OF THE AETHIOPIS (CHRESTOMATHY II), TRANS. H. G. EVELYN-WHITE (LOEB, 1914)

Paris's arrow and Apollo's guidance — the tradition is consistent that it took a god and the war's originating villain together. (The heel, and the arrow to the heel, are not in Proclus, nor in any early version: Achilles' single vulnerable spot is a later development, fully formed only in Roman-era sources such as Statius and Hyginus. The archaic Achilles is not invulnerable; he is simply the best fighter alive, and he dies as fighters do.) What follows is the Iliad's funeral grammar repeated at one remove higher: a great fight over the body, Aias carrying it on his back to the ships while Odysseus holds off the pursuit — the image archaic art reproduced obsessively — the burial of Antilochus, the lying-out of Achilles, Thetis rising from the sea with the Muses and her Nereid sisters to bewail him. And then the poem's strangest gift: Thetis snatches her son from the pyre and transports him to the White Island — Leuke, in the Black Sea at the Danube's mouth — where, the Aethiopis said, Achilles lives after death. The Achaeans pile his mound on the Hellespont shore all the same, and hold funeral games, and the army's grief turns instantly to the question that opens the next poem: who inherits the dead man's arms, the god-made armour of Achilles? The dispute between Odysseus and Aias over those arms is the hinge of the Little Iliad — and of Sophocles' Ajax, and of one of the ugliest judgements in the whole tradition.

PENTHESILEA IN ART

The Amazon's arrival and death are among the most-depicted Trojan episodes in archaic art — Exekias's black-figure amphora (c. 540 BCE, British Museum), showing Achilles' spear entering her throat as their eyes meet, is the famous case. Burgess's argument (Section II) rests substantially on such images: the archaic imagination's Troy was the Cycle's Troy, not only Homer's.

MEMNON ON THE SCALES

The weighing of the fates of Achilles and Memnon — Zeus holding the balance while Thetis and Eos plead, the psychostasia — was dramatised by Aeschylus in a lost play and painted repeatedly. The Aethiopis's pairing of the two champions, each with a goddess for a mother or grandmother and a divine armourer, is the Cycle's most deliberate piece of symmetry.

VIII. Little Iliad: The Arms, the Conditions, and the Building of the Horse

The Little Iliad — four books, attributed to Lesches of Pyrrha on Lesbos — is “little” only in bulk. Aristotle's list (Section II) already shows its density: more tragedies were quarried from it than from any poem except the Cypria. It opens with the judgement of the arms. The god-made armour of Achilles is awarded — by a vote of the army, in some versions by the testimony of Trojan prisoners or of Trojan girls overheard at the walls, in all versions by the contriving of Athena — to Odysseus. Aias, the army's second champion, the man who carried Achilles' body out of the fight, is passed over. In the night he goes mad — Athena sends the madness — and slaughters the army's herds, torturing a ram he takes for Odysseus; at dawn, sane, surrounded by the evidence, he kills himself. The Little Iliad, a fragment preserved by a scholiast adds, gave him no cremation and no mound: he was buried in a coffin, in dishonour, because of the king's anger. The army that could not take Troy by force is now consuming its own champions — Achilles dead, Aias dead by his own hand, the arms that were the prize of excellence become the instrument of the army's self-destruction. Sophocles' Ajax (Section XIII) is this poem's episode slowed to the speed of a single day and a single mind.

The Conditions of the Fall

Troy, the tradition now insists, could not be taken until certain conditions were met — and the Little Iliad is the poem of their fulfilment. Odysseus ambushes Helenus, Priam's prophet-son, and Helenus, captured, prophesies the terms of the city's taking. Diomedes fetches Philoctetes from Lemnos; Machaon heals his wound; and Philoctetes, the abandoned man with Heracles' bow, meets Paris in single combat and kills him — the war's author shot down by the war's most wronged Greek. Menelaus outrages the corpse; the Trojans recover and bury it. Helen is then married to Deiphobus, Paris's brother — the third husband of the war, a detail tragedy will not leave alone. Odysseus brings Neoptolemus, Achilles' son by Deidameia, from Scyros, and gives him his father's arms — the arms over which Aias died, now on the shoulders of a boy who will outdo his father's savagery in a single night; Achilles' ghost appears to him. Eurypylus, son of Telephus, comes from Mysia to aid Troy — his grandfather's debt inverted: Telephus guided the Greeks to Troy; his son dies defending it, killed by Neoptolemus. The Trojans are shut inside their walls at last. Force has failed for ten years. The tradition turns, with a kind of relief, to fraud.

The Palladium and the Horse

Two operations precede the end. Odysseus disfigures himself — scourged, in rags, as a beggar — and enters Troy as a spy; Helen recognises him, does not betray him, and plots with him for the city's taking (the Odyssey itself tells this, in Helen's own voice, at 4.240–264). And Odysseus, with Diomedes, steals the Palladium — the ancient image of Athena on which the city's safety depended — out of Troy by night. (Arctinus, a fragment of the Iliou Persis tradition reports, held that the Greeks stole only a copy, the true Palladium remaining hidden in Troy until the end; the variants begin before the sack itself does.) Then Epeius, a craftsman-boxer of no account in battle, builds, by Athena's instruction, the Wooden Horse. The Greeks put their best men inside it, burn their camp and huts, and sail away — to Tenedos, in sight of the city, to wait for a signal. The Trojans come out of their gates at dawn to an empty shore, a burned camp, and a vast wooden animal standing where the enemy had been. Proclus's summary of the Little Iliad ends there, with the Trojans feasting in their city around the Horse. The Iliou Persis opens with the debate over what it is.

// SINON, AND WHO INVENTED HIM //

The spy who stays behind to lie the Horse into the city — Sinon, with his tale of desertion and his signal-fire to the fleet — belongs to the Little Iliad's ending and the Iliou Persis's opening in Proclus: the summary of the Persis has Sinon raise the fire-signal to the Achaeans, “having previously got into the city by pretence”. His full, piteous, perjured speech is Virgil's creation (Aeneid 2), and it is Virgil's Sinon the modern world remembers. The cyclic Sinon is a signal and a pretence; the eloquence is Rome's.

IX. Iliou Persis: The Night of the Sack

The Iliou Persis — the Sack of Ilium, two books, Arctinus's second poem in the ring — is the shortest of the lost epics and the one whose loss is felt most, because it contained the event the entire Cycle exists to reach. Proclus's summary, followed here closely in paraphrase, opens with the Trojans standing around the Horse in debate. Three counsels divide them: hurl it down from the rocks; burn it; or dedicate it to Athena as an offering. The third prevails — the pious counsel, and the fatal one. The city turns to mirth and feasting, believing the war at an end. At that moment two serpents appear from the sea and destroy Laocoön, the priest who had argued against the Horse, and one of his two sons. Aeneas and his followers, reading the portent, withdraw to Mount Ida — the tradition's mechanism for saving the one Trojan prince whose line must continue. (Virgil's version, in which Laocoön and both his sons are crushed together after he hurls a spear at the Horse, is the Aeneid's; Proclus's cyclic version kills the priest and one son, and gives the serpents the role of a sign misread — the Trojans take the death of the Horse's opponent as heaven's endorsement of their feast.) Sinon raises the fire-signal. The fleet comes back from Tenedos in the dark. The men in the Horse come out, open the gates, and fall on a city drunk, unarmed, and asleep.

The Killings at the Altars

What the summary then lists, in flat sequence, is the moral centre of the whole Cycle, and this article will not soften the list. Neoptolemus kills Priam, the old king of Troy, who had fled for sanctuary to the altar of Zeus Herkeios — Zeus of the Courtyard, protector of the household — in his own palace court. Menelaus finds Helen and takes her to the ships, after killing Deiphobus, her third husband, in his own bedroom. Aias the son of Oileus — the Locrian Aias, the lesser Aias — drags Cassandra, Priam's prophetess daughter, from sanctuary by force, and in tearing her from the image of Athena to which she clings, tears the goddess's image away with her: sacrilege in the temple, in the moment of victory, against the Greeks' own divine patron. The army is so enraged that it determines to stone Aias on the spot; he escapes only by taking refuge at Athena's altar himself — the violator saved by the very institution he violated. The Greeks burn the city. At the tomb of Achilles on the shore they sacrifice Polyxena, Priam's youngest daughter, to the dead hero's ghost — a human sacrifice on a grave, demanded, in the fuller tradition, by Achilles' spirit appearing above his mound. Astyanax, Hector's infant son, the child the Iliad showed us frightened by his father's helmet plume, is killed — thrown from the towers. Andromache, Hector's wife, is assigned to Neoptolemus — the son of her husband's killer, and the same boy who killed her father-in-law at the altar. The remaining spoil and the remaining women are divided. Demophon and Acamas, the sons of Theseus, find their grandmother Aethra, held in Troy since the Dioscuri's war, and take her home. The Greeks sail away. And Athena — whose image was dragged from her temple, whose priestess was violated in her sanctuary — plans to destroy them on the high seas. The Iliou Persis ends with the victors already under sentence. The Nostoi is the sentence being carried out.

// WHO KILLED ASTYANAX? THE VARIANTS, KEPT VISIBLE //

Proclus's summary of the Iliou Persis assigns the killing to Odysseus. A fragment of the Little Iliad, preserved by the scholiast on Lycophron, assigns it to Neoptolemus, in verse: the son of Achilles snatches the child from his nurse's breast, seizes him by the foot, and casts him from a tower. Pausanias, describing Polygnotus's great painting of the sack at Delphi, reports Lesches' version of Priam's death as differing too: in the Little Iliad Priam was not killed at the altar of Zeus Herkeios but dragged away from it and killed at his own doors. Euripides' Trojan Women makes Astyanax's death a formal Greek decision, announced by Talthybius and carried out offstage. The tradition does not give the archive a single killer to name, and this article does not invent one: it reports the cyclic summary's attribution, notes the Little Iliad's rival verse, and observes that on the fact itself — the child was killed, deliberately, by the victors, as policy — every version agrees.

It is worth pausing on the structure the Cycle has built. The war began, in the tradition's own telling, as the punishment of a sacrilege: Paris's violation of guest-friendship. It ends with the punishers committing, in a single night, a worse catalogue of sacrileges than the crime they avenged: a king butchered at an altar, a priestess violated in a temple, a maiden sacrificed on a tomb, an infant thrown from a wall, suppliants dragged from sanctuary. The gods of the Cycle keep accounts, and the returns are the audit. No later moralist invented this reading; it is the Iliou Persis's own ending — Athena planning the wreck of the fleet — and it is why the Greek imagination treated the sack not as a triumph with regrettable incidents but as a victory indistinguishable from a crime. Tragedy, Section XIII, simply moves into the house the Cycle built.

X. Nostoi: The Returns and the Murder of Agamemnon

The Nostoi — five books, attributed to Agias of Troezen — opens with the fleet still at Troy and Athena already at work: she causes a quarrel between Agamemnon and Menelaus about the voyage home. Half the army, in the fuller tradition, wants to sail at once; half wants to stay and appease the goddess. Agamemnon stays, sacrificing. Diomedes and Nestor put to sea early and reach home safely — Nestor's safe return is the frame of his long reminiscences in the Odyssey, and Diomedes', as the later tradition tells it, brought him home to a faithless wife and exile in Italy. Menelaus sails next and is battered down to Egypt with five ships surviving of his fleet; his seven years of wandering with Helen, and his eventual homecoming, are the Odyssey's Book 4 material and the Nostoi's close. Calchas, Leontes, and Polypoetes decline the sea altogether and walk home overland to Colophon, where they bury the seer Teiresias. When Agamemnon's squadron at last sails, the ghost of Achilles rises and tries to hold it back, foretelling what is coming — the dead champion still, in the Cycle's economy, on duty over the army he no longer belongs to. What is coming is the storm at the Caphereus rocks, the southern cape of Euboea, where the returning fleet is wrecked wholesale and where the Locrian Aias, surviving the sea, boasts from a rock that he is saved in spite of the gods — and Poseidon splits the rock with his trident and drowns him for the blasphemy on top of the sacrilege. Apollodorus's Epitome (6, paraphrased) fills the storm out with Nauplius, Palamedes' father, lighting false beacons on the cape to lure the ships onto the rocks in revenge for his son — the Cypria's drowned man avenged, two poems later, by his father's wrecking-lights. The Cycle's memory is long, and so is its bookkeeping.

Neoptolemus, warned by Thetis, travels home overland, buries Phoenix on the way, meets Odysseus at Maronea in Thrace — the last meeting of the ring's two survivors of the Horse — and reaches the Molossians, where his line, in the tradition, becomes kings. And then the Nostoi arrives at its climax, the event toward which Aeschylus will build the Oresteia: the murder of Agamemnon. The commander-in-chief, who sacrificed his daughter to launch the fleet and led it for ten years, comes home to Mycenae with Cassandra as his prize, and is killed in his own house by Aegisthus — his cousin, who has taken his throne and his wife during his absence — and by Clytemnestra, the wife herself. Apollodorus (Epitome 6.23, in Frazer's translation, paraphrased here) gives the killing its famous mechanism: Clytemnestra gives him a garment without sleeves and without a neck opening, and as he struggles in it, tangled and helpless, he is cut down; Cassandra is killed with him. Then, in the Nostoi's final movement, comes the vengeance of Orestes and Pylades — the son returns and kills the murderers, his mother among them — and, last of all, Menelaus returns home: the king whose wife began the war is the poem's final homecomer, closing the account the Cypria opened. The Odyssey sits, in the ring's order, between the Nostoi and the Telegony — Odysseus's return is one of the returns, the longest and the only fully happy one, and the ring treats it as such: not the war's story, but one strand of its unravelling. Odysseus's own tale is at The Homeric Epics. The ring has one more death to record, and it is his.

XI. Telegony: The Death of Odysseus and the Closing of the Ring

The Telegony — two books, attributed to Eugammon of Cyrene — is the Cycle's epilogue and its strangest poem. It opens with the burial of Penelope's suitors by their kinsmen, the unfinished business of the Odyssey's last book. Odysseus, after sacrifices to the Nymphs, sails to Elis to inspect his herds there, then returns to Ithaca and performs the sacrifices Teiresias had prescribed in the underworld. Then the poem sends him travelling again — to Thesprotia, in the north-west, where he marries Callidice, the queen, leads the Thesprotians in their war against the Brygi (a war in which Ares and Athena fight on opposite sides until Apollo separates them), and on Callidice's death leaves their son Polypoetes on the Thesprotian throne and returns to Ithaca, to Penelope, to grow old. Meanwhile Telegonus — Odysseus's son by Circe, born on Aeaea after the father's departure — sets out to find his father, is storm-driven to Ithaca, and, not knowing where he has landed, begins ravaging the island for food. Odysseus comes out to defend his country and is killed by his son, who does not know him. The weapon, in the tradition Apollodorus preserves (Epitome 7, paraphrased), is a spear tipped with the spine of a stingray — so that Teiresias's prophecy, that death would come to Odysseus “from the sea”, is fulfilled by a sea-creature's barb on a son's spear, in the gentlest and cruellest of the prophecy's possible senses. Telegonus, learning what he has done, carries his father's body, with Penelope and Telemachus, to Circe's island. Circe makes them immortal. And the ring closes with two marriages: Telegonus marries Penelope, his father's widow; Telemachus marries Circe, his father's lover. Modern readers have always found the ending queasy; the archaic tradition seems to have found it conclusive. The heroic generation is extinguished, its last households folded into immortality and silence, and the world of the poems — the world the wider Cycle had been narrating since the union of Heaven and Earth — is over. What remains is the iron age of the audience, and the songs.

// HOW THIS ARCHIVE READS THE TELEGONY //

The Telegony is the weakest-attested poem in the ring and the one modern scholarship has been readiest to dismiss as a late, clumsy sequel. The archive's position is narrower and, it submits, fairer: the poem's materials — the Thesprotian episode, the stingray death, Telegonus himself — are genuinely archaic tradition, presupposed by the Odyssey's own prophecies and by Hesiod's Theogony, which already knows Circe's sons by Odysseus (1011–1016). Whether Eugammon's two books did them justice is unknowable; the poem is lost. What the ring needed at its end — the death of the last hero, foreseen since the underworld scene — the tradition supplied, and Proclus's summary preserves its shape.

XII. The Story Told Whole: The War in Cycle Order

The preceding sections have told the story poem by poem, with the evidence attached. The timeline below strips the apparatus away and tells it once, whole, in the order the Cycle told it — the tradition as told, register (a) of Section I, with the poem responsible for each movement named at its head. Dates within the story are the tradition's own (a war of ten years; a muster eight years after the first false start); the one calendar date in the table's final column of the mind — the fall of Troy in 1184/3 BCE — is Eratosthenes' scholarly calculation, which became the canonical ancient date. Herodotus's reckoning put the war nearer 1250 BCE; the Parian Marble, 1209/8 BCE. The Greeks themselves never agreed, and Section XVII will show the archaeology landing, for one destruction layer, suggestively close to Eratosthenes — a coincidence of ranges that must not be upgraded into a confirmation.

CYPRIA

THE PLAN AND THE JUDGEMENT

Zeus, to relieve the earth of the multitude of men, resolves on the war. At the wedding of Peleus and Thetis, Strife sets Hera, Athena, and Aphrodite quarrelling over beauty. Paris of Troy, judging on Mount Ida, awards the prize to Aphrodite, who has promised him Helen.

CYPRIA

THE FLIGHT OF HELEN

Paris, entertained as a guest in Menelaus's Sparta, sails by night with Helen and the treasure, sacks Sidon on the storm-driven way, and marries Helen in Troy. Menelaus and Agamemnon collect the oath of Helen's suitors.

CYPRIA

AULIS, THE FALSE START, AND IPHIGENIA

The serpent devours nine sparrows: nine years of war, Troy taken in the tenth. The fleet storms Mysia by mistake; Telephus is wounded and, years later, healed by Achilles and guides the fleet. At the second muster, Agamemnon's boast against Artemis is paid for at the altar: Iphigenia is summoned by a lie — and, in the poem, snatched away by the goddess, a stag dying in her place.

CYPRIA

THE LANDING AND THE NINE YEARS

Philoctetes is abandoned on Lemnos. Protesilaus, first ashore, is the first to die. The embassy demanding Helen is refused; the assault on the walls fails; the army raids the Troad and its neighbours for nine years. Palamedes is drowned. Briseis falls to Achilles, Chryseis to Agamemnon.

ILIAD

THE WRATH

Tenth year. The quarrel, the withdrawal, Patroclus's death, Achilles' return, Hector killed, Priam's ransom, Hector buried. Troy still stands. See The Homeric Epics.

AETHIOPIS

THE LAST CHAMPIONS, AND ACHILLES DEAD

Penthesilea comes, triumphs, and is killed by Achilles, who kills Thersites and is purified on Lesbos. Memnon comes, kills Antilochus, and is killed. Achilles, pursuing the Trojans into the gates, is killed by Paris and Apollo. Thetis carries him to the White Island. The army disputes his arms.

LITTLE ILIAD

THE CONDITIONS FULFILLED

The arms go to Odysseus; Aias, mad, slaughters the herds and kills himself. Helenus is captured and prophesies. Philoctetes is fetched and healed, and kills Paris. Neoptolemus is fetched from Scyros and kills Eurypylus. The Palladium is stolen. Epeius builds the Horse; the camp is burned; the fleet waits at Tenedos.

ILIOU PERSIS

THE SACK

The Horse is dedicated to Athena and taken in. Laocoön and one son are killed by serpents; Aeneas withdraws to Ida. By night the fleet returns and the city is stormed. Priam is killed at the altar of Zeus Herkeios; Cassandra is dragged from Athena's image; Polyxena is sacrificed at Achilles' tomb; Astyanax is killed; the women are divided; the city burns. Athena plans the wreck of the victors.

NOSTOI

THE RETURNS

Nestor and Diomedes reach home. Menelaus is wrecked down to Egypt. The fleet is shattered at Caphereus; the Locrian Aias is drowned for his sacrilege and his boast. Agamemnon is murdered at home by Aegisthus and Clytemnestra; Orestes avenges him. Menelaus, last, comes home.

ODYSSEY · TELEGONY

THE LAST HERO

Odysseus, after ten years, reaches Ithaca and destroys the suitors. Years later Telegonus, his son by Circe, lands raiding on Ithaca and kills him, unknowing, with a stingray-spined spear. The heroic age ends.

XIII. The Cycle on Stage: Tragedy as the Cycle's Classical Commentary

Fifth-century Athens did not, in the main, dramatise Homer. It dramatised the Cycle. Aristotle's arithmetic (Section II) makes the point from the critic's side — the episodic cyclic poems were a quarry of self-contained plots, the unified Homeric poems were not — and the surviving record confirms it from the stage's side: of the Trojan tragedies whose subjects can be identified, the great majority take episodes that lie outside the Iliad and Odyssey — before the wrath, after Hector, inside the sack, and in the returns. Tragedy is therefore the Cycle's classical commentary in a precise sense: a century of the Greek world's best minds taking the ring's episodes one by one and asking, in public, what they meant and whether they could be borne. What follows treats the surviving peaks; the lost Trojan plays of all three tragedians — Aeschylus's Achilles trilogy, his Palamedes, Sophocles' dozens of Trojan titles, Euripides' Alexandros and Palamedes among them — would fill a catalogue of their own.

Aeschylus: The Oresteia (458 BCE)

The Nostoi's last movement — Agamemnon's homecoming and murder, Orestes' vengeance — becomes, in Aeschylus's hands, the only complete tragic trilogy that survives: Agamemnon, Libation Bearers, Eumenides, produced in 458 BCE. Aeschylus takes the Cycle's bookkeeping and gives it a jurisprudence. The Agamemnon opens with the beacon-chain announcing Troy's fall and spends its length asking what the victory cost and what the victor did to win it: the chorus remembers Iphigenia at the altar — really dead, in this telling (Section V) — and Clytemnestra, who has waited ten years, receives her husband on a path of crimson tapestries, talks him into the sacrilege of treading them, and kills him in his bath, entangled in a robe, striking — as she boasts over the bodies — a third blow in completion. Cassandra, dragged from Troy to die as the sack's delayed aftershock, walks into the house knowing exactly what is inside it, in the trilogy's most terrible scene: the Iliou Persis's altar-violation come home to roost in Argos. The Libation Bearers stages Orestes' return and the matricide the Nostoi recorded in a clause; the Eumenides then does what epic never attempted — it puts the cycle of vengeance itself on trial, invents (in the myth's terms) the homicide court of the Areopagus, and acquits Orestes on Athena's casting vote, converting the Erinyes who pursued him into honoured residents of Athens. The Cycle's story ended with a son killing his mother for killing his father for killing his daughter. Aeschylus's ending — law replacing feud, by a single vote, with the Furies pensioned rather than abolished — is classical Athens commenting on its inheritance and amending it in the same gesture.

Euripides: The Women, and the Two Iphigenias

Euripides returned to the Cycle more obsessively than any other tragedian, and almost always to its victims. The Trojan Women, produced in 415 BCE — in the shadow of Athens' destruction of Melos the winter before and on the eve of the Sicilian expedition, a context ancient and modern readers alike have heard in it, though the play names neither — is the Iliou Persis staged from inside the burning city, at dawn, among the women waiting to be allotted. Poseidon and Athena open it conspiring to wreck the Greek fleet: the Persis's final sentence as a prologue. Hecuba, Andromache, Cassandra, and Helen pass in review; Astyanax is condemned by vote of the Greek army and his small body is brought back on Hector's shield for burial; Helen argues for her life against Hecuba's prosecution before Menelaus, in a formal debate the play refuses to settle; and the city burns as the women are marched to the ships. Almost nothing happens, by epic standards. That is the commentary: the Cycle's climax, held still and looked at. Hecuba (c. 424 BCE) joins the Persis's Polyxena — here demanding, in Euripides' unsettling revision, to die free rather than live a slave — to the discovery of Polydorus's murdered body and Hecuba's revenge on his killer, Polymestor, whom she blinds after luring him into her tent and killing his sons: the victim of the sack becoming, by the play's end, something the Greeks at the altar would have recognised. And the two Iphigenia plays (Section V) work the Cypria's Aulis episode from both ends: Iphigenia among the Taurians (c. 414–412 BCE) finds the rescued girl on the Black Sea, priestess of a cult that sacrifices shipwrecked Greeks, on the point of sacrificing her own unrecognised brother; Iphigenia at Aulis (produced posthumously, 405 BCE) stages the muster's moral collapse — an army baying for a girl's death, a father lying to fetch her, Achilles discovering his name has been used as the bait, and Iphigenia herself converting, in the play's most argued-over movement, from terror to a willingness that has struck many readers as the tragedy's darkest stroke of all.

Sophocles: Ajax and Philoctetes

Sophocles' two surviving Trojan plays take the Little Iliad's two great wrongs. The Ajax (undated; usually placed early in his career, c. 450s–440s BCE) begins where the cyclic poem's summary begins to hurry: the arms awarded away, the madness, the slaughtered herds. Sophocles slows the episode into an examination of a heroic code that has no procedure for dishonour — Aias's great speech on time and change, his deception of Tecmessa and the chorus, his suicide on the sword Hector gave him (the enemy's gift, as he notes, doing the work) — and then, extraordinarily, spends its second half on the burial: whether the army's commanders may forbid the traitor-madman's corpse its rites, and Odysseus, the very man who won the arms, arguing them into permitting it. The Cycle recorded that Aias was buried without honour; Sophocles stages the argument that dishonour would have been. The Philoctetes, produced in 409 BCE and awarded first prize, takes the Cypria's one-clause abandonment and the Little Iliad's retrieval and makes of them a play about the education of a young man: Neoptolemus, sent with Odysseus to trick the wounded outcast out of his bow, finds he cannot sustain the lie, returns the bow, and is prepared to sail home in failure rather than succeed by fraud — until Heracles descends, the bow's first owner, and commands the ending the tradition requires: Philoctetes to Troy, to be healed and to kill Paris. Both plays are, formally, commentaries: they accept the Cycle's facts entire and dispute only their meaning, which is to say they dispute everything that matters. That is how classical Greece read its ring of poems — not as scripture to be believed, but as a shared inheritance to be argued with, in the theatre, before the whole city, at a festival of Dionysus. XIV. Cult: The War's Dead at Their Tombs

The Cycle's dead did not stay in the poems. Greek hero cult — the institution this hub's Heroes and Epic article treats in full — gave the war's heroes addresses: tombs at which they could be honoured, appeased, and consulted, and the Troad in particular was dense with them. Strabo (13.1.32, paraphrased) records the sanctuary and monument of Achilles near Sigeum, and monuments of Patroclus and Antilochus beside it, at which the people of Ilion performed sacred ceremonies in honour of all three — and of Aias, whose mound stood at Rhoeteum across the strait of the story. The great tumuli of the Troad's coast, some of them Bronze Age or earlier in origin, were identified in antiquity with the heroes' graves and functioned as the landscape's title-deeds: to sail the Hellespont was to pass Achilles' mound, exactly as the Odyssey (24) had said his bones were laid in a great tomb on a jutting headland, a landmark for men at sea. The cult was Panhellenic as well as local. Thessaly, Achilles' homeland, sent sacred embassies to his tomb; Sparta and Elis and the cities of Magna Graecia maintained Achilles cults of their own; and the Aethiopis's White Island became a real cult place — Leuke, modern Snake Island in the Black Sea off the Danube mouth, where a temple of Achilles stood, sailors left offerings, and the hero was said to appear to those who anchored there. Inscriptions from the Pontic cities attest the cult of Achilles Pontarches, “lord of the Pontus”, from the sixth century BCE onward. The poem said Thetis carried her son to the White Island; the archaic world built him a temple on it. Between those two sentences lies the whole mechanism by which epic and cult, kept analytically apart in this archive, fed each other in life.

The Locrian Maidens: Atonement as an Attested Custom

The strangest cultic consequence of the war — and the one this article reports, as the brief requires, as the attested custom it was — is the tribute of the Locrian maidens. Its aetiology is the Iliou Persis's central sacrilege: the Locrian Aias's violation of Cassandra in Athena's temple. In Apollodorus's account (Epitome 6.20–22), the Locrians, home from the war, are visited three years later by a plague and receive an oracle bidding them — in Frazer's translation, quoted here:

“to propitiate Athena at Ilium and to send two maidens as suppliants for a thousand years.” — APOLLODORUS, EPITOME 6.20, TRANS. J. G. FRAZER (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY), VIA PERSEUS

Apollodorus continues (paraphrased): the lot fell first on Periboea and Cleopatra; the maidens, reaching Troy, were chased by the inhabitants and took refuge in the sanctuary, where they served the goddess — sweeping and sprinkling the temple, never approaching the image, cropped-haired, in a single garment, barefoot — and when they died, others were sent, entering the city by night because a maiden caught outside the precinct could lawfully be killed; and after the thousand years were passed, following the Phocian War (third century BCE), the sending ceased. The custom is not attested by Apollodorus alone. Callimachus knew it in the third century BCE; Lycophron's Alexandra builds its dark prophecy around it; Strabo (13.1.40) discusses it — and remarks, characteristically, that the sending, whatever the legend's chronology, began in historical fact only after the Persian period; Polybius knows the Locrians of Italy in connection with it; and an inscription from Locris itself has been read as regulating the maidens' dispatch. Modern scholarship treats the tribute as a real, long-lived ritual institution of the archaic and classical city of Opountian Locris — its origins debated (a genuine archaic institution, its aetiology in Ajax's crime attached as explanation; possibly, as has been argued, a rite by which Locris annually advertised its link to the Homeric tradition and its hero, at real cost and real risk to real girls) — and its details, the night entry, the temple servitude, the liability to be killed en route, are reported by sources who treat them as current practice, not fable. This archive's wording follows that evidence: the maidens were sent; the thousand years is the oracle's term as the sources give it, not a verified duration; and the crime they atoned for belongs to the tradition, while the atonement belongs to history.

XV. Troy as Pilgrimage: Xerxes and Alexander, Reported as Reported

Because the war's dead had addresses, Troy became a place to visit — and the two most famous visits are reported here exactly at the strength of their sources, as the brief requires: reported as reported. In 480 BCE, the Persian king Xerxes, marching against Greece, came to the Scamander and — in Herodotus's account (7.43, paraphrased) — went up to the citadel of Priam, desiring to see it; having seen it and heard the story told, he sacrificed a thousand cattle to Athena of Ilion, and his Magi poured libations to the heroes. Herodotus reports it in two sentences, without comment. Modern readers have supplied the comment for him: a Persian king honouring Troy's goddess and Troy's heroes, on his way to fight the descendants of Troy's destroyers, is staging himself — to Greek eyes in his army and ahead of it — as Priam's avenger; and the Persian court's own claim, which Herodotus records elsewhere (7.150; 1.5), that the war of East and West began with the reciprocal abductions of women and was opened in earnest at Troy, made the stop a piece of campaign rhetoric in ritual form. What Xerxes actually saw on the citadel was the small Greek city of Ilion (Troy VIII, Section XVI) and its temple of Athena — the Bronze Age ruins were under his feet, not before his eyes. The visit's historicity rests on Herodotus; its interpretation is modern inference, and is labelled as such.

A century and a half later came the visit that set the pattern for every conqueror's pilgrimage since. In 334 BCE Alexander of Macedon crossed into Asia and went up to Ilion, in the accounts of Arrian (Anabasis 1.11–12), Plutarch (Alexander 15), Diodorus (17.17–18), and Strabo (13.1.26, 32) — paraphrased here from those accounts. He sacrificed to Athena of Ilion and poured libations to the heroes; he exchanged his own armour for ancient arms kept in the temple as relics of the war, which his bodyguard thereafter carried before him into battle; and at the tomb of Achilles he anointed himself with oil and ran naked around the mound with his companions, in the Greek athletic custom, laying a wreath and calling Achilles blessed — Arrian's version adds the reason, because Achilles had Homer to proclaim his deeds. Hephaestion, in the same accounts, laid a wreath on Patroclus's tomb. Alexander, who slept with the Iliad under his pillow and modelled himself on Achilles down to the grief for a Hephaestion, was performing the tradition's most consequential piece of self-casting: the new war against Persia framed as the old war resumed, its captain as Achilles returned. The sources are later than the event by centuries — Arrian writes in the second century CE from lost contemporary histories — and the details have the polish of a story much retold. But the visit itself is multiply attested, its political function is legible, and Ilion's subsequent privileges under Alexander and his successors are documentary fact. Rome completed the pattern: Ilium, as the ancestral city of Aeneas's line, received tax immunities and Roman rebuilding (Troy IX), Julius Caesar and Augustus patronised it, and the emperor Caracalla, in 214 CE, staged funeral games at Achilles' tomb for his own dead favourite in imitation of Achilles and Patroclus. Pilgrimage to Troy is not a modern invention laid over the site. It is one of the site's own longest-running institutions — older, by far, than its excavation.

XVI. Hisarlik: The Mound and Its Nine Cities

The mound the tradition pointed to is Hisarlik — “place of the fortress” in Turkish — a low hill at the southern end of the Dardanelles, in the plain of the Scamander (Karamenderes) and Simoeis rivers, in what is now Çanakkale province, Türkiye. In antiquity the Greek and Roman city of Ilion/Ilium stood there, and its identification with Homer's Troy was the ancient world's own settled opinion; the site's modern history begins when that identification was re-argued from the texts — by Charles Maclaren in 1822 — and then tested on the ground by Frank Calvert, a Levantine consular official whose family owned part of the mound, who dug trial trenches in 1863–65, identified Hisarlik as Troy, and persuaded the wealthy German businessman Heinrich Schliemann to dig there in force. Schliemann's campaigns (1871–73, 1878–79, 1882, 1889–90) cut a great trench through the mound's centre in search of Homer's city at the bottom of it, destroying or disturbing much of the upper, later strata — which included, though he did not know it, the very levels he was looking for. He found, instead, a succession of superimposed settlements, gold, and fame. His architect colleague Wilhelm Dörpfeld, continuing in 1893–94, imposed stratigraphic order on the mound and made the case for the great-walled sixth settlement as Homer's city. Carl Blegen of the University of Cincinnati (1932–38) refined the sequence into the nine major settlements, with their forty-odd sub-phases, that scholarship still uses, and moved the Homeric candidacy one level up. Manfred Korfmann of Tübingen (1988–2005), with C. Brian Rose directing the post-Bronze Age work, extended the excavation beyond the citadel by geophysical survey and reopened every question of the city's size and standing; the work continues under his successors. The mound is a UNESCO World Heritage site (1998).

“Priam's Treasure”: The Wrong Stratum, Stated Plainly

In May 1873 Schliemann announced the discovery, in a burned level near the mound's ramp, of a great hoard of gold and bronze — diadems, earrings, necklaces, vessels — which he named the Treasure of Priam, smuggled out of Ottoman territory, and publicised with the famous (staged, and later much dissected) photograph of his wife Sophia wearing the diadems as “the jewels of Helen”. The hoard is real, and it is magnificent — and it comes from Troy II, an Early Bronze Age level of roughly 2550–2300 BCE, destroyed by fire a full millennium before the earliest date anyone has ever proposed for a historical Trojan War. Schliemann had dug through Homer's city, if it was there, to find a treasure of the wrong age and name it for the wrong king. Modern reassessment has complicated even the hoard's unity — the “treasure” as published appears to be an assemblage gathered from more than one findspot in the 1873 season, unified in the telling — and its modern itinerary is a lesson in the archaeology of archaeology: taken to Berlin, removed to Moscow in 1945, and held today by the Pushkin Museum, its ownership still disputed among Türkiye, Germany, and Russia. The archive states the case without varnish because the case is the point: the most famous object ever attached to the Trojan War is genuine, is Trojan, and has nothing to do with Priam. It is evidence for Troy II's wealth in the third millennium — which is not nothing; a place rich a thousand years before the war is a place with a long memory available to it — and for nothing else.

XVII. Troy VI and Troy VIIa: Earthquake, Fire, and the Limits of Proof

Two levels, and only two, have ever been serious candidates for the city of the tradition, and the choice between them is the mound's central argument. Troy VI is the city a visitor would have chosen: a high citadel of fine limestone masonry, its great walls — in places five metres thick and still standing eight metres high — sloping outward as they rise, with towers, paved streets, and large free-standing houses, occupied for over four centuries and importing Mycenaean pottery in its later phases. Dörpfeld made VI his Homeric Troy, and its scale is the scale the poems imagine. But VI ended, on the reading Blegen established and later work has sustained, not by assault but by earthquake: its walls were found displaced and tilted off their courses in a pattern characteristic of seismic damage, houses cracked and collapsed, and — the telling negative — no general burning, no weapons in the ruins, no unburied dead. A city shaken down around 1300 BCE and rebuilt by its own survivors is a catastrophe, but it is not a sack. Troy VIIa, the rebuilding, is a poorer and more crowded place: VI's walls patched and reused, the citadel's open terraces filled in with small houses packed wall to wall, and in house after house great storage jars — pithoi — sunk into the floors, which Blegen read as a population stocking itself against siege. VIIa ended, around 1180 BCE, in fire: burned destruction debris across the excavated area, and in its ruins the finds on which the war reading rests — bronze arrowheads and stores of slingstones in the destruction context, and a small number of human skeletons in streets and houses, unburied where they fell. Blegen's conclusion, in his own careful terms (paraphrased), was that VIIa was captured, looted, and destroyed by fire in a military assault, and that it, rather than the grander VI, was the Troy of the Trojan War tradition.

// WHAT EACH DESTRUCTION DOES AND DOES NOT SHOW //

Troy VI's end shows a major destruction at the right place at roughly 1300 BCE, with damage signatures the excavators read as seismic. It does not show an enemy, a siege, or a fire, and an earthquake cannot be the Iliad's sack however grand the city it ruined. Troy VIIa's end shows a violent, fiery destruction at roughly 1180 BCE — within the band of the traditional dates (Eratosthenes: 1184/3 BCE) — with modest but real traces consistent with fighting. It does not show who attacked, why, for how long, or with how many ships; fire and a handful of dead cannot distinguish a Mycenaean coalition's ten-year siege from a single season's raid, a Sea Peoples' incursion, or a local war, and the destruction's date has margins of decades, not years. Both statements are the excavators' evidence at full strength. Neither is a proof of the poems, and the archive does not ask either to be one.

The Lower City and the Korfmann Controversy

For most of the twentieth century, one objection shadowed both candidates: the citadel is small — some two hectares of fortified hilltop, a princely stronghold rather than the great city of the poems and the Hittite letters. Korfmann's campaigns changed the question. Magnetometry survey south of the citadel revealed a lower city: street grids and building anomalies extending hundreds of metres, and, excavated from 1993, a wide defensive ditch cut into the bedrock, with a second ditch further out in the final phase, which Korfmann read as an anti-chariot defence fronted by a palisade. On his reconstruction the Late Bronze Age settlement covered on the order of twenty hectares or more — ten times the citadel — with a population in the several thousands, a harbour-facing trading place at the Aegean's north-east gate, Anatolian in culture and connected to both the Hittite world and the Aegean. The 1995 find of a bronze biconvex seal inscribed in Luwian hieroglyphs — the first written object from Bronze Age Troy, naming a scribe and his wife — anchored the city's Anatolian, literate-world affiliation in an object. In 2001 the Tübingen ancient historian Frank Kolb attacked the reconstruction publicly and sharply: the lower city's building density, the ditch's date and function, and Troy's elevation into a commercial metropolis were, he argued, inflations of thin evidence in the Homeric story's service. A university symposium at Tübingen in February 2002 heard the case out; a four-author reassessment by Easton, Hawkins, Sherratt, and Sherratt (Anatolian Studies 52, 2002) concluded that the criticisms were themselves considerably exaggerated, while Hertel and Kolb's rejoinder (Anatolian Studies 53, 2003) maintained them. The controversy's residue, a generation on, is a defensible middle: a lower settlement existed and was defended — the ditch is real, whatever its precise purpose — and Hisarlik in its Late Bronze Age phases was a substantial fortified centre by the standards of the north-west Anatolian coast; how substantial, how urban, and how rich remain argued, and Korfmann's maximal figures should be cited as his, not as the field's.

XVIII. The Hittite Witness: Wilusa, Alaksandu, and Ahhiyawa

The mound does not testify alone. From the archives of the Hittite capital at Hattusa come cuneiform documents of the fourteenth and thirteenth centuries BCE that name, in exactly the right quarter of Anatolia, a kingdom called Wilusa — earlier also Wilusiya, paired once with a neighbour, Taruisa. The identification of Wilusa with Troy rests first on geography: the Hittite texts place Wilusa in the far north-west, beyond the Seha River land, at the edge of Hittite reach — where Hisarlik is. It rests second, and most famously, on language. The Greek name of Homer's city, Ilios, is shown by the metre of the Homeric poems themselves to have once begun with a w-sound — Wilios — the lost letter digamma whose ghost still disrupts the verse wherever the older form underlies it. Wilusa and (W)ilios are, on the standard linguistic account, the same name in Hittite and Greek dress; and Taruisa has been matched, with somewhat less certainty, to Greek Troia — so that both of Homer's names for the city would descend from Bronze Age Anatolian originals. Joachim Latacz's Troy and Homer (German 2001; English 2004) built the fullest statement of the case: Wilusa is Ilios, Hisarlik is Wilusa's citadel, and the epic tradition preserves, through the digamma among other fossils, a genuine Bronze Age inheritance. This article presents that identification as what it is — the majority working position among Hittitologists and a great many Homerists, argued with real evidence, and not a demonstrated certainty: the equations are linguistic inferences, the geography, though tight, is reconstructed from the same corpus it is used to interpret, and dissent — Hertel's most prominently — has been argued in print and never refuted to everyone's satisfaction.

The documents give Wilusa a history, and it is a history of exactly the right kind of trouble. Wilusa appears first in the annals of Tudhaliya I/II (early fourteenth century BCE) as a member of the Assuwa coalition that fought Hatti in the north-west. Thereafter it is a Hittite vassal — and a contested one. Around 1280 BCE the Hittite king Muwatalli II concluded a treaty with Wilusa's king, who bears the name Alaksandu: a name indistinguishable from Greek Alexandros — the second name of Paris of Troy. The treaty's historical preamble reaches back generations, recording Wilusa's loyalty and its troubles; its list of divine witnesses includes a god Appaliunas, widely identified with Apollo — who is, in the Iliad, Troy's great divine protector, and whose temple stood at Wilusa's gate in the poem's geography of the city's sanctuaries. A generation later the Manapa-Tarhunta letter reports the renegade Piyama-Radu attacking Wilusa itself, and the Tawagalawa letter — a Hittite king writing to the king of Ahhiyawa — refers to an earlier quarrel between Hatti and Ahhiyawa over Wilusa, since composed. Ahhiyawa, in the majority reading of the last half-century's scholarship, is the Mycenaean Greek world: Homer's Achaeans, in Hittite spelling. Set the pieces side by side and the Hittite archive attests, independently of any Greek poem: a real kingdom at the right place, bearing the right name; a king of it, around 1280 BCE, bearing the name of the poem's Trojan prince; its chief god plausibly the poem's Trojan god; and documented friction over it, within living memory of Troy VIIa's floruit, between the Hittite empire and a sea-power across the Aegean whose name matches the Achaeans. What the archive does not attest is a Trojan War: no ten-year siege, no coalition of the poem's scale, no Helen. Latacz's own formulation, in his Archaeology Magazine essay (paraphrased), is the honest one: we cannot prove the Trojan War took place; the circumstantial evidence points to armed conflicts around 1200 BCE between the Greek world and Wilusa, and such conflicts could have left their mark in the poetry of the time and been carried down the centuries in song. The archive adopts that sentence as the Hittite evidence's ceiling.

THE NAME CHAIN

Hittite Wilusa → early Greek Wilios (the digamma still felt in Homer's metre) → classical Ilios → Latin Ilium. The survival of the lost w inside the verse is the single most concrete thread between the Bronze Age city and the poems: a sound the singers no longer pronounced, preserved because the lines would not scan without it.

ALAKSANDU ≠ PARIS

The treaty king of c. 1280 BCE cannot be the poem's Paris: he belongs a generation or more before VIIa's destruction, and Alexandros is a Greek name a Wilusan dynast could bear for many reasons — including, as has often been noted, a Greek mother, a Greek marriage-alliance, or simple fashion at a bilingual court. The name is a resonance, not an identification, and this article treats it as such.

XIX. Verdict: A Real Place, a Literary War

The hub's manner, in its verdict sections, is to say plainly what the evidence assembled will and will not carry. For Troy it carries this. There stood, at Hisarlik, at the gate of the Dardanelles, a fortified Bronze Age citadel with a defended lower settlement — Anatolian in culture, named in the Hittite archives under a name that is the Greek name of Homer's city in its older form, ruled around 1280 BCE by a king whose name is a Trojan prince's, and fought over, in the documents, by the Hittite empire and a power across the sea. That citadel was destroyed more than once: its grandest phase ended, apparently by earthquake, around 1300 BCE, and its successor settlement burned, with traces of violence, around 1180 BCE — inside the range of dates Greek scholarship itself computed for the fall of Troy, and at the very end of the Bronze Age world whose collapse scattered the Mycenaean palaces whose kings the poems remember. A war — or wars — between Aegean Greeks and this city in the thirteenth or early twelfth century is not merely possible; it is the kind of event the Hittite letters show happening around Wilusa, and the kind of event a singing tradition, of the kind this hub's Heroes and Epic article describes, demonstrably carried across centuries — carrying, in this case, even the fossil of the city's Bronze Age name inside its verse.

And it will not carry this. No excavation has found, or can be expected to find, the war as told: a single ten-year siege by a coalition of the Catalogue's 1,186 ships (the Iliad's own count, Book 2 — the source of Marlowe's “thousand ships”) and the seventy to a hundred and thirty thousand men Thucydides already calculated from it and already doubted; a war caused by one abducted queen; a war whose named heroes — Achilles, Hector, Priam, Paris — did the deeds and died the deaths the poems assign them. The heroes have no more documentary existence than Helen has; the Catalogue describes, in its geography, a Greece that is part Bronze Age memory and part the world of the poems' own making; and the ten years are a storyteller's round number with an omen built to explain it. Between the two statements — a real place, repeatedly destroyed, one destruction compatible with a war around the traditional date; and the war as told, which is literature — this article has kept the gap visible throughout, because the gap is not a failure of the evidence. It is the finding. What the Greeks made of whatever happened at Wilusa was not a chronicle but a cycle: a ring of poems that begins with a god deciding the earth is too full of men and ends with the last hero killed by his own son, and that between those poles works out, with a completeness no surviving literature matches, what victory costs the victors. The mound at Hisarlik tells us the story had an address. The ring tells us why, for three thousand years, nobody has been able to leave the address alone.

Probable, and labelled as inference: Wilusa = (W)ilios = Hisarlik; Ahhiyawa = the Mycenaean world; one or more Aegean attacks on Wilusa among the memories the epic tradition carried. Literature, and honoured as such: the ten years, the thousand ships, the Judgement, the Horse, and every named hero's deeds. The tradition is not diminished by the sorting. It is the only one of the three that was ever trying to tell the truth about war.
