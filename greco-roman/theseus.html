I. A Hero Made to Order: Theseus and Athens

Every Greek city had heroes, but Athens, in the course of the sixth and fifth centuries BCE, did something more systematic with hers than most: it built one. Theseus — son of Aegeus, king of Athens, or of the god Poseidon, depending on which parentage a given passage needs — is, in the form in which the tradition hands him down, a comparatively late and deliberately political hero. Heracles, the hero against whom Theseus is constantly and explicitly measured in the sources themselves, is Panhellenic and archaic: he belongs to Argos and Thebes and Tiryns, he is already fully formed in Homer and Hesiod, and no single city owns him. Theseus, by contrast, barely appears in Homer at all — a handful of lines, one of them suspected already in antiquity of having been inserted to please Athens (Section II) — and then, from about 570 BCE onward, his deeds multiply in Athenian vase-painting with a speed and in a pattern that scholars have long read as promotion rather than survival. First the Cretan adventure, then, around 520 BCE, a whole new cycle of “Attic deeds” along the road from Troezen, laid out as a deliberate counterpart to the labours of Heracles. In the fifth century the tragedians take him up as Athens' spokesman — the king who argues for democracy in Euripides' Suppliants and buries other men's dead at the cost of a war. In 476/5 BCE the Athenian general Cimon conquers an island, finds an outsized coffin, and brings home what the city receives, with processions and sacrifices, as the hero's bones. A sanctuary is built for them in the heart of Athens. A great festival, the Theseia, is attached to his name. The sequence is not a legend slowly accreting. It is, at several identifiable points, hero-making as statecraft — and the remarkable thing is that the ancient sources, read carefully, let us watch it happen.

This article therefore keeps three things apart on every page, and asks the reader to keep them apart too. (a) The myth as told: the connected story of the tokens, the road, the Labyrinth, the black sail, the unification of Attica, the Amazons, Phaedra, the descent, and the death on Scyros, as Plutarch, Apollodorus, and the poets give it. (b) The cultic, civic, and historical footing: what can be dated and placed — vase-painting from c. 570 BCE, the festival calendar, the recovery of the bones in 476/5 BCE, the Theseion, the paintings Pausanias saw, the synoecism as a claim made by Thucydides and the Atthidographers. (c) The later readings: what subsequent ages made of the material — the philosophers' argument over Theseus's ship, the Amazonomachy read as an allegory of the Persian Wars, Sir Arthur Evans reading the palace at Knossos as the Labyrinth, and the modern scholarly argument, associated above all with H. J. Walker's Theseus and Athens (1995), about when and why Athens promoted its hero. Confusing these three layers is the one error that makes Theseus unintelligible. Keeping them apart makes him one of the most instructive figures in Greek myth, because in his case the machinery that usually hides behind a hero is unusually visible.

A word on the hero's character as the tradition itself presents it, because the civic portrait is only half of him. The Theseus of the full story is brave, public-spirited, and repeatedly careless or worse with the people closest to him: he abandons Ariadne, whose help saved his life, on Naxos — or loses her, or is forced to leave her; the sources cannot agree, and Section IX gives the disagreement in full. His forgetfulness kills his father. His credulity, armed with a curse borrowed from Poseidon, kills his son. His last great enterprise, undertaken at fifty, is the abduction of a child, Helen, and an attempt to abduct a goddess, Persephone, which ends with him stuck fast in the underworld while Athens is invaded and his throne is taken. Athens knew all of this — its own tragedians staged the worst of it — and promoted him anyway. That too is part of what this article is about: a city's hero did not have to be blameless. He had to be theirs, and he had to have founded the things they valued. Theseus unified Attica, humbled Crete, repelled the Amazons, sheltered suppliants, and — in the telling Athens settled on — handed power to the people. The rest could be carried.

THE HERO AT A GLANCE

Son of Aegeus (or Poseidon) and Aethra of Troezen. Raised at Troezen; proves himself by lifting the rock and taking his father's sword and sandals; clears the Isthmus road of six killers; sails to Crete as one of the tribute youths — or as a volunteer — kills the Minotaur, and escapes by Ariadne's thread; becomes king, unifies Attica, fights the Amazons, loses his son Hippolytus through Phaedra's accusation and his own curse; descends with Pirithous to seize Persephone and is rescued by Heracles; is driven from Athens and killed on Scyros.

THE CIVIC HERO AT A GLANCE

Founder, in Athenian telling, of the unified Attic state (the synoecism), of the Panathenaea in its enlarged form, of the Oschophoria, the Pyanepsia, and the Synoikia festival; patron of suppliants, slaves, and the poor; apparition at Marathon in 490 BCE; bones recovered by Cimon in 476/5 BCE and enshrined in the Theseion; chief festival, the Theseia, on 8 Pyanepsion.

II. The Sources, and Why They Disagree

No single ancient author tells the whole Theseus story, and the authors who tell parts of it disagree with one another constantly — about whether the tribute went to Crete every year or every nine years, about whether Theseus was chosen by lot or volunteered or was picked out by Minos in person, about what happened to Ariadne, about whether the Labyrinth held a monster at all. This is not a defect in the evidence to be apologised for. It is the evidence. Theseus was a living civic tradition for a thousand years, retold by epic poets, lyric poets, tragedians, local historians of Attica (the Atthidographers), vase-painters, and eventually biographers, and every retelling served the teller's city, genre, and moment. The honest method — the one this hub uses throughout — is to name the source at the claim and let the disagreements stand visible, rather than to blend the versions into a smooth composite that no ancient reader ever possessed.

The Early Traces: Homer, Hesiod, and a Suspect Line

Theseus is a shadow in the earliest poetry. The Iliad mentions him once in passing, in a list of heroes of a former generation whom Nestor had known (Iliad 1.265, paraphrased). The Odyssey names him twice: Ariadne, Odysseus says in the underworld, was carried from Crete toward Athens by Theseus but slain by Artemis on Dia before he had joy of her (Odyssey 11.321–325, paraphrased) — the oldest surviving sentence about the Cretan adventure, and already a version in which Ariadne simply dies. The second Odyssey passage, the line “Theseus, Peirithous, illustrious children of Heaven”, seen among the heroes in Hades (Odyssey 11.631, in the wording Plutarch quotes), was already suspected in antiquity: Plutarch reports the claim of Hereas of Megara that Peisistratus inserted that line into Homer, and removed from Hesiod a line about Theseus's passion for Aegle, in each case to gratify the Athenians (Theseus 20.2, paraphrased). The report is itself a piece of Megarian polemic against Athens and cannot be verified; but its existence shows that by the Hellenistic period, scholars understood perfectly well that Theseus's presence in the old poems might be an Athenian editorial product. An epic Theseid also existed — Plutarch dismisses one episode from it, the Amazons' counter-attack at Theseus's wedding to Phaedra, as having “every appearance of fable and invention” (Theseus 28.1, paraphrased) — and a fragmentary epic on the descent of Pirithous, ascribed to Hesiod and known to Pausanias, told the underworld adventure (Section XV). Of the early Theseus poetry, what survives entire is not epic but lyric: Bacchylides' two dithyrambs (Section VII).

The Classical Witnesses: Bacchylides, Tragedy, Thucydides

Bacchylides of Ceos (born c. 510 BCE) wrote at least two dithyrambs on Theseus for Athenian performance. Ode 17 puts him on the ship to Crete, defying Minos and diving for the ring that proves his divine birth (Section VII). Ode 18 is a small drama in itself: a dialogue between Aegeus and a chorus of Athenians, as news arrives, deed by deed, of the unknown young man clearing the road from Troezen — Theseus approaching his own city as a rumour before he arrives as a person. The two poems, recovered from an Egyptian papyrus published in 1897, are the earliest substantial Theseus texts we have, and they are already Athenian civic pieces, written for the festivals of the city whose hero they celebrate.

Tragedy made Theseus Athens' moral spokesman. In Euripides' Suppliants (c. 423 BCE) he debates a Theban herald on democracy versus tyranny and goes to war to recover the unburied Argive dead (Sections XII and XVII). In the same poet's Hippolytus (428 BCE, first prize) he is the tragedy's instrument of destruction, cursing his innocent son on a dead woman's written word (Section XIV). Aeschylus, in his lost Eleusinians, had Theseus recover the Argive dead by persuasion rather than battle — Plutarch prefers that version and says so (Theseus 29.4–5, paraphrased). Sophocles gives the aged, exiled Theseus a last gracious appearance in the Oedipus at Colonus, sheltering the blind Oedipus on Attic soil. The tragedians' Theseus is not consistent — Euripides' two plays alone give a democrat-philosopher and a catastrophic father — because each play needs a different Athens, and Theseus is the shape Athens takes on stage.

Thucydides, the historian, gives the synoecism its first explicit surviving statement (2.15, paraphrased): before Theseus, Attica was a scatter of independent townships with their own magistrates and council-chambers; Theseus abolished the local governments, established one council-chamber and prytaneum in Athens, and instituted the Synoikia festival in commemoration — though the inhabitants, Thucydides adds drily, went on living on their own land as before. It matters that the first explicit account of Theseus's greatest political deed appears not in poetry or art but in a work of critical history of the late fifth century BCE; modern scholarship (Section XII) has drawn the obvious inference about when the synoecism story itself took shape.

The Systematisers: Apollodorus, Plutarch, Pausanias

The connected narrative this article follows comes chiefly from two late, learned compilations. The Library (Greek Bibliotheca) transmitted under the name of Apollodorus — a mythographic handbook of the first or second century CE, not by the second-century BCE scholar whose name it bears — tells the Theseus story in Library 3.15–16 and in the Epitome (1.1–24), the surviving summary of its lost final book. Apollodorus is terse, sequential, and invaluable: where Plutarch debates his sources, Apollodorus simply gives the story, occasionally noting a variant (“or, as some affirm, he offered himself voluntarily”). His Frazer translation (Loeb, 1921) is the version quoted here.

Plutarch (c. 46–after 119 CE) made Theseus the subject of the first of his Parallel Lives, paired with Romulus — founder of Athens against founder of Rome. His opening is the most honest statement of method any ancient biographer of a hero ever wrote: having reached periods where, in his image, geographers write “what lies beyond is full of marvels and unreality, a land of poets and fabulists”, he asks his readers' indulgence where fable obstinately refuses to submit to reason (paraphrased from Theseus 1). The Life that follows is a critical anthology as much as a narrative: Plutarch names his authorities — Hellanicus, Philochorus, Pherecydes, Cleidemus, Demon, Ion of Chios, Aristotle — reports their disagreements, occasionally rationalises (the Minotaur becomes, in Philochorus's Cretan version, a general named Taurus; the underworld becomes a Molossian king named Aidoneus with a wife called Phersephone and a dog called Cerberus), and flags invention where he smells it. His Life of Cimon gives the recovery of the bones a second telling. Bernadotte Perrin's Loeb translation (1914) is the version quoted here, and this article follows Plutarch's chapter numbers throughout.

Pausanias, the second-century CE traveller, supplies the cultic and monumental record: the Theseion and its paintings (1.17.2–3), the Amazonomachy and the Marathon painting in the Stoa Poikile, where Theseus rises from the underworld among the fighters (1.15.3), Ariadne asleep in a painting on the Acropolis as Theseus sails away and Dionysus arrives (1.20.3), and, far away at Delphi, Polygnotus's great underworld painting, in which Theseus and Pirithous sit in their chairs, Theseus holding both swords while Pirithous gazes at the now useless blades (10.29.9) — all paraphrased here from Pausanias's Description of Greece. Pausanias is this article's standing reminder that Theseus was not only read. He was looked at, walked past, sacrificed to, and taken refuge with, in specific buildings on specific streets.

Three modern works frame this article's second and third layers. Timothy Gantz, Early Greek Myth (1993), is the standard source-by-source survey, and its ninth chapter assembles the Theseus testimonia in chronological order — the basis for the claim, made repeatedly below, that a given episode is early, late, or Athenian. H. J. Walker, Theseus and Athens (Oxford University Press, 1995), argues the promotion thesis in its fullest form: that the Theseus cycle was substantially built up in the sixth century, in the Athens of the Peisistratids and then of the young democracy, as a civic counterweight to Heracles. Walter Burkert, Greek Religion (English translation 1985), supplies the framework for hero-cult used in Section XVII: the hero as a dead man of power, worshipped at a tomb, in a city that has reasons of its own for keeping him. Where these scholars disagree with each other — and on Theseus they do — the text says so rather than silently choosing.

III. Birth at Troezen: Two Fathers and the Tokens Under the Rock

The story begins, as Athenian stories about legitimacy tend to, with a childless king and an ambiguous oracle. Aegeus, king of Athens, had no children. Consulting Delphi, he received the celebrated riddling response — in Perrin's translation of Plutarch, the verse runs: “Loose not the wine-skin's jutting neck, great chief of the people, / Until thou shalt have come once more to the city of Athens” (Theseus 3.3). Not understanding it, Aegeus stopped at Troezen, on the Saronic coast of the Argolid, and repeated it to his host Pittheus, son of Pelops, a man reputed the wisest of his time. Pittheus understood — the oracle forbade Aegeus to lie with a woman before reaching Athens — and, by persuasion or guile (Plutarch's alternatives, Theseus 3.4, paraphrased), brought it about that Aegeus lay instead with Pittheus's own daughter, Aethra. Apollodorus is blunter: Pittheus, understanding the oracle, made Aegeus drunk and caused him to lie with Aethra (Library 3.15.6, paraphrased). The dynastic point is plain in both versions. Pittheus, by one night's hospitality, attached the childless Athenian royal house to his own; and the child, if child there was, would be at once Aegeus's heir and Pelops's great-grandson.

Before leaving, Aegeus hid a sword and a pair of sandals under a great rock that had a hollow just large enough to receive them, and told Aethra alone the secret: if she bore a son, and the son, grown to manhood, could lift the rock and take out what lay beneath, she was to send him to Athens with the tokens, in secrecy — for Aegeus feared the fifty sons of Pallas, his nephews, who plotted against the childless king and despised him (Plutarch, Theseus 3.5, paraphrased). The tokens — Greek gnōrismata, “recognition-things” — are the story's founding device, and they are worth pausing on. Greek myth is full of exposed infants recognised by trinkets; Theseus's tokens are the adult, civic version of the same machinery. The sword and sandals do not merely identify a lost child. They are a test (the rock must be lifted), a credential (they must be produced in Athens), and a claim (they assert, against fifty rival nephews, that this stranger is the king's son). When the recognition finally happens, in Section V, it happens by the sword alone, at a banquet, at the last possible moment — the story spends its founding device with a dramatist's economy.

The Second Father

Aethra bore a son. But whose? The tradition, characteristically, refuses to choose, and the refusal is functional. Plutarch reports that Aethra kept the truth concealed and that Pittheus spread the report that the boy was begotten by Poseidon — a claim Troezen had every reason to welcome, since Poseidon was the city's patron, honoured with first-fruits and stamped, as a trident, on its coinage (Theseus 6.1, paraphrased). Apollodorus preserves the mechanism in its most startling form: on the same night that Aegeus lay with Aethra, Poseidon also lay with her (Library 3.15.7, paraphrased). The double paternity is a known Greek pattern — Heracles is son of Zeus and of Amphitryon in the same way — but in Theseus's case it does specific work for the rest of the story. The mortal father gives him the Athenian throne and the plot. The divine father gives him his sea-affinity (the dive in Bacchylides, Section VII), his standing with Poseidon — who grants him, in the tradition Euripides uses, three curses, one of which he will spend on his own son (Section XIV) — and his calendar: Athens honoured Theseus on the eighth day of the month, Poseidon's day (Section XVII). Even the boy's upbringing is doubled. He is reared at Troezen by Pittheus, with a tutor, Connidas, to whom the Athenians still sacrificed a ram on the day before the Theseia in Plutarch's own time (Theseus 4.1, paraphrased) — a minor cult detail that shows how completely the hero's biography had been furnished with commemorations, down to his schoolmaster.

The name itself attracted ancient etymology: Plutarch reports that the boy was called Theseus either because the tokens had been “placed” (tithēmi, to place) in hiding for him, or because Aegeus “acknowledged” him at Athens — two derivations offered side by side, neither endorsed (Theseus 4.1, paraphrased). Modern philology treats the name as pre-Greek, like so much else in the story — the hero, his city, and his monster all carry names older than the Greek language that tells of them. What is certain is the sequence the story now sets running. The boy grows up at Troezen displaying, with his strength, “a firm spirit united with intelligence and sagacity” (Perrin's wording, Theseus 6.2, paraphrased in the surrounding text). Aethra brings him to the rock, tells him the truth, and bids him take the tokens and go — by sea, the safe way, as both she and Pittheus beg him to do. He lifts the rock easily. And he refuses the sea.

Plutarch pauses, before the journey, on a tonsure. It was the custom, he says, for youths coming of age to go to Delphi and offer their hair to the god; Theseus went, and sheared only the front of his head, as Homer says the Abantes did, so that enemies could not seize him by it in close fighting — and this cut was called the Theseis after him (Theseus 5, paraphrased). A place at Delphi was still called the Theseia from his visit. The detail is small and characteristic: even the hero's haircut had become an Athenian institution with a name.

IV. The Road to Athens: The Six Labours of the Isthmus

The land road from Troezen to Athens — across the Isthmus of Corinth and through the Megarid — was, in the story's geography, infested. Plutarch frames the age itself as the problem: it produced men of extraordinary strength and endurance who used their powers for nothing fitting, exulting in cruelty and holding reverence and justice to be the virtues of cowards (Theseus 6.4, paraphrased). Some of these Heracles had destroyed on his travels; others had crouched out of his sight, and when Heracles fell into misfortune and servitude to Omphale in Lydia, the old villainies burst out again in Greece, there being none to restrain them (Theseus 6.5, paraphrased). Pittheus, describing each miscreant in detail, tried to persuade his grandson to sail. The persuasion failed for the reason the whole cycle exists: Theseus had long been secretly fired by the glory of Heracles. He listened most eagerly to those who had seen the hero; he dreamed of his achievements by night; and he judged it dreadful that his famous cousin — the two were kin, Aethra and Alcmene being first cousins through Pelops's children — should purge land and sea while he himself arrived at Athens by the safe route carrying, as proofs of his birth, “only sandals and a sword unstained with blood” (Perrin's wording, Theseus 7.1, closely paraphrased). He set out by land, determined, Plutarch says, to do no man any wrong, but to punish those who offered him violence.

The six encounters that follow are the labours of the road, and their pattern is exact. In almost every case Theseus kills the killer by his own method: the clubber is clubbed, the pine-bender is torn apart by bent pines, the foot-washer is kicked into the sea, the bed-stretcher is fitted to his own bed. Plutarch states the principle openly and attributes it to the model: Theseus did this in imitation of Heracles, who punished those who offered him violence in the manner in which they had plotted to serve him (Theseus 11.1, paraphrased). The justice of the road is talionic — measure for measure — and it is also, read civically, a programme: the future founder of the unified state begins by making its one land approach safe for travellers. The road from the Peloponnese to Athens runs through the story in the order the encounters are given below: Epidaurus, the Isthmus, Crommyon, the Megarian cliffs, Eleusis, Erineus.

1. PERIPHETES, THE CLUB-BEARER — EPIDAURUS

First, in Epidaurian territory, Periphetes, who fought with an iron-bound club and was called the Club-bearer (Korynetēs), laid hold of Theseus and tried to stop his progress; Theseus grappled with him and slew him (Plutarch, Theseus 8.1; Apollodorus, Library 3.16.1, paraphrased). Pleased with the club, Theseus took it and carried it ever after — as Heracles carried the lion's skin: the weapon of the vanquished, in the victor's hands, made invincible. Apollodorus adds that Periphetes was a son of Hephaestus, lame like his father.

2. SINIS, THE PINE-BENDER — THE ISTHMUS

On the Isthmus, Sinis bent pine trees down, fastened his victims to them, and let the trees tear them apart. Theseus slew him in the very manner in which he had destroyed many men — without practice or acquaintance with the device, showing, Plutarch says, that valour is superior to all device and practice (Theseus 8.2; Apollodorus, Library 3.16.2, paraphrased). The sequel is darker than the labour: Sinis's daughter Perigune fled and hid among rushes and wild asparagus, vowing never to trample them if they saved her; Theseus pledged her safety, and she bore him a son, Melanippus (Theseus 8.2–3, paraphrased). The road-clearer's first conquest is also the story's first of many women taken along the way.

3. THE CROMMYONIAN SOW — CROMMYON

The sow of Crommyon, called Phaea — whether after the old woman who reared it, as Apollodorus has it, the creature was no insignificant beast but fierce and hard to master. Theseus went out of his way to encounter and slay it, Plutarch says, so that he might not seem to perform all his exploits under compulsion, and because the brave man ought to attack villainous men in self-defence but seek occasion against the nobler beasts (Theseus 9.1; Apollodorus, Library 3.16.2, paraphrased). Some rationalisers, Plutarch notes, made Phaea a female robber nicknamed Sow for her life and manners. In Apollodorus the sow is a child of Echidna and Typhon — of the monster-brood catalogued in this hub's Theogony article.

4. SCIRON — THE MEGARIAN CLIFFS

On the borders of Megara, Sciron robbed passers-by from the cliffs that bear his name; in the prevalent tradition he insolently thrust out his feet and bade strangers wash them, then kicked them, mid-washing, into the sea below, where a great turtle waited in some tellings. Theseus hurled him down the cliffs himself (Plutarch, Theseus 10.1; Apollodorus, Epitome 1.2, paraphrased). Plutarch immediately reports the counter-tradition: Megarian writers, “waging war with antiquity”, insisted Sciron was no robber but a chastiser of robbers, respectably married into the families of Aeacus and Cychreus, and that Theseus killed him later, in war, not on this journey (Theseus 10.2–3, paraphrased). The labour, that is, was already contested local history in antiquity: what Athens remembered as road-clearing, Megara remembered as murder.

5. CERCYON — ELEUSIS

At Eleusis, Cercyon the Arcadian challenged passers-by to wrestle and killed them in the bout. Theseus out-wrestled him and killed him (Plutarch, Theseus 11.1; Apollodorus, Epitome 1.3, paraphrased — in Frazer's wording, he lifted him up on high and dashed him to the ground, the wrestler's throw that later tradition made the invention of scientific wrestling itself). The place matters: Eleusis, later home of the Mysteries treated in this hub's Mysteries article, enters the Theseus story first as a strong man's killing-ground on the Athens road.

6. PROCRUSTES — ERINEUS

Last, at Erineus, Damastes, surnamed Procrustes — “the Stretcher” — who kept a bed (two beds, in Apollodorus: a short one and a long one) and fitted his guests to it, stretching the short and lopping the tall. Theseus killed him by compelling him to make his own body fit his bed, as he had been wont to do with strangers (Plutarch, Theseus 11.1; Apollodorus, Epitome 1.4, paraphrased). The name outlived the story: a “Procrustean bed” is still English for a standard imposed by violence on things that do not fit it — the one labour of the six whose villain, not whose hero, the language kept.

A Cycle Built Backwards

Now the second layer, because with the road labours it is unusually well documented. In Athenian vase-painting, the Cretan adventure — Theseus and the Minotaur — appears from about c. 570 BCE; the road labours as a cycle, the so-called Attic deeds, appear from about c. 520 BCE, suddenly and as a set. The dating is the art historians' (the sequence is conveniently summarised in the scholarship cited in Section XVIII: Minotaur and Centaur fights from c. 570, the Attic deeds from c. 520, monumental public compositions from c. 480–420). The implication drawn by Walker and others is that the cycle was assembled at that moment — older, separate strong-man tales of the Isthmus and the Megarid gathered, ordered along a road, and issued as a set, precisely so that Athens' hero should have what Heracles had: a canonical series of labours, performed in his own territory, on his way to his own city. The sculptural programmes then canonised the pairing: on the Athenian Treasury at Delphi (c. 490 BCE) and on the Hephaisteion above the Athenian Agora, the deeds of Theseus are carved in direct counterpart to the labours of Heracles, metope answering metope. None of this makes the individual tales new — Sciron, Sinis, and Procrustes may each be old local figures, and Megara's protest (above) proves the Sciron story was old enough to fight over. What the late sixth century added was the frame: one hero, one road, six labours, one destination. The frame is the politics.

The journey ends in a rite, not a fight. At the river Cephisus, men of the Phytalidae met the bloodstained traveller, purified him from bloodshed at his request with the customary rites, made propitiatory sacrifices, and feasted him in their house — the first kindness, Plutarch says, that he met with on his journey (Theseus 12.1, paraphrased). Theseus remembered it: the Phytalidae were later given charge of his own sacrifices at Athens (Section X). And so, purified, on the eighth day of the month then called Cronius and later Hecatombaeon, he entered Athens — a city, Plutarch says, full of confusion and dissension, and a royal household in a distressing condition.

V. Arrival: Medea's Cup, the Recognition, and the Marathonian Bull

The household's distress had a name. Medea, fled from Corinth, was living with Aegeus, having promised by her sorceries to cure his childlessness. She learned who the arriving stranger was before Aegeus did; and Aegeus, old, ignorant of his son, and afraid of everything because of the faction in the city, was persuaded by her to entertain Theseus as a guest and take him off by poison (Plutarch, Theseus 12.2, paraphrased). Apollodorus adds the errand that precedes the banquet: Medea persuaded Aegeus to beware of the stranger as a traitor, and Aegeus, not knowing his own son, sent him first against the Marathonian bull — the bull that was ravaging the Marathonian Tetrapolis, in some accounts the very bull of Crete, Heracles' seventh labour, released and wandered north (Apollodorus, Epitome 1.5, paraphrased; Plutarch places the bull after the recognition, at Theseus 14 — the sources differ on the order, and the difference is noted here rather than harmonised). Theseus killed the bull. Then came the banquet, and the poison, which Aegeus had received that same day from Medea.

The recognition is the story's most perfectly constructed scene, and both systematisers tell it the same way. Theseus, at the table, chose not to announce himself, but to give his father the means of discovery: when the meats were served, he drew his sword — the sword from under the rock — as if to carve with it, and brought it to his father's notice. Aegeus perceived it, dashed down the proffered cup of poison, questioned his son, embraced him, and formally recognised him before an assembly of the citizens, who received him gladly for his valour (Plutarch, Theseus 12.2–3, paraphrased; in Apollodorus, Epitome 1.6, Frazer's wording has Aegeus dash the cup from his hands on recognising the sword). Plutarch adds the aetiological garnish his readers expected: the spilled poison, they said, marked the spot where the enclosure in the Delphinium later stood, by Aegeus's house. Medea, her plot exposed, was expelled — in Apollodorus's word — and vanishes from the Athenian story as suddenly as she entered it.

Recognition made Theseus heir, and heirship made him a target. The sons of Pallas, who had expected the kingdom on Aegeus's childless death, went to war: exasperated, Plutarch says, that Aegeus should be king at all, as an adopted son of Pandion unrelated to the house of Erechtheus, and that an immigrant stranger should now succeed him (Theseus 13.1, paraphrased). They divided into two bands — one marching openly from Sphettus, one in ambush at Gargettus — but their herald, Leos of Agnus, betrayed the plan; Theseus fell on the ambush and slew them all, and the other band dispersed. Plutarch preserves the feud's fossil in local custom: the township of Pallene, he says, still would not intermarry with Agnus, nor allow heralds to cry their customary “Hear, ye people!” (Akouete leō) there, for hatred of the word that sounds like the traitor's name (Theseus 13.3, paraphrased). The episode is worth its space because it states the charge against Theseus that the rest of his career must answer, in Athens' own telling: he was a foreigner. The hero of Athens was born at Troezen, of a Troezenian mother, and arrived as an immigrant with a sword. The civic myth does not hide this. It builds on it — the outsider who becomes the founder is a stronger claim than a founder who was never anything else — and Section XVI shows the charge returning, fatally, at the end.

The Marathonian bull, in Plutarch's ordering, belongs here: Theseus, desiring to be at work and to court the people's favour, went out against the bull that was doing no small mischief to the Tetrapolis, mastered it, drove it alive through the city in display, and sacrificed it to Delphinian Apollo (Theseus 14.1, paraphrased). Attached to the expedition is the story of Hecale, the poor old woman of a roadside deme who entertained the young hero on his way to the bull, vowed a sacrifice to Zeus if he returned safe, and died before he did; Theseus, returning, commanded the honours that became the Hecalesia festival, paid to Zeus Hecalus and to Hecale herself (Theseus 14.2–3, paraphrased, on the authority of Philochorus). The episode — the subject of a whole Hellenistic poem by Callimachus, surviving in fragments — is the gentle counterpart to the road labours: the founder's Athens is prefigured not only in the strong men he kills but in the poor woman's hut where he is a guest, and whose kindness the state then commemorates for centuries. Hero-making, in the Athenian style, sacralises hospitality as carefully as violence.

VI. Why Athens Owed Crete a Tribute: Minos, Androgeos, and the Terms

Not long afterwards, Plutarch says, there came from Crete for the third time the collectors of the tribute (Theseus 15.1). The Cretan expedition is the centre of the Theseus story — its oldest attested episode, its most painted, its most consequential — and its premise is a debt of blood. Androgeos, son of Minos, king of Crete, had been killed in Attica — treacherously, as the Cretans held; in Apollodorus's account he had won all the events at the Panathenaea, and Aegeus, fearing his friendship with the sons of Pallas, sent him against the Marathonian bull, by which he was killed (Library 3.15.7–8, paraphrased). Other versions made him the victim of Athenian or Megarian ambush after his victories. Minos made war on Athens and could not take the city; he prayed to Zeus, his father, for vengeance; and famine and pestilence fell on Attica. The Athenians, consulting the oracle, were told to give Minos whatever satisfaction he might choose. Minos chose this: that Athens send seven youths and seven maidens, unarmed, as fodder for the Minotaur (Apollodorus, Library 3.15.8, paraphrased). Plutarch's version agrees on the numbers and the cause — war, famine, pestilence, dried-up rivers, and an oracle commanding reconciliation with Minos (Theseus 15.1, paraphrased).

The Minotaur himself — the creature at the centre of the debt — is, in Apollodorus's genealogy, the end of a chain of divine insult and monstrous desire. Minos, claiming the Cretan throne, had prayed to Poseidon for a bull from the sea as a sign, vowing to sacrifice it; the bull was too beautiful to kill, and Minos kept it. Poseidon's answer was to make Minos's wife Pasiphaë — daughter of the Sun — desire the bull; the craftsman Daedalus, then an exile at the Cretan court, built her a hollow wooden cow in which to receive it; and from that union came the Minotaur, Asterius by proper name: a man's body and a bull's head, fed, in the Athenian telling, on Athenian flesh (Apollodorus, Library 3.1.3–4 and 3.15.8, paraphrased). Minos shut the creature in the Labyrinth, the winding structure Daedalus built for the purpose, in which, Apollodorus says, he who entered could not find his way out, for many a winding turn shut off the secret outward way. Plutarch quotes Euripides on the creature's shape — “A mingled form and hybrid birth of monstrous shape”, two natures, man and bull, joined in him (Theseus 15.2, the Euripidean fragment in Perrin's rendering) — and then, characteristically, spends a whole chapter on the version in which there was no creature at all (the table above). Both belong in the record. The monster is the myth as Athens told it on its stages and painted it on its pots. The prison, the games, and General Taurus are what Plutarch says the Cretans answered — and his dry conclusion, that it is a grievous thing to be at enmity with a city that has a language and a literature, since Minos was reviled in the Attic theatre as cruel and violent although Hesiod called him “most royal” and Homer made him a confidant of Zeus (Theseus 16.3, paraphrased), is the single most perceptive sentence any ancient author wrote about this story. The Cretan expedition, as we have it, is Athenian war-propaganda that hardened into myth — which is not to say it is only that, as Section XI will show, but the saying belongs at the story's centre, not in a footnote.

The politics of the embarkation complete the premise. Plutarch gives the Athenian fathers' grievance in full: Aegeus, the cause of all their trouble, alone had no share in the punishment, but devolved the kingdom on a bastard and foreign son while legitimate children were taken from their own houses (Theseus 17.1, paraphrased). It was this reproach, Plutarch says, that moved Theseus to offer himself independently of the lot — the immigrant heir proving his belonging by taking the city's worst risk on his own body. Aegeus, unable to turn him, cast the lots for the rest. And because on the two former voyages no hope of safety had been entertained, the ship had sailed with a black sail; this time Theseus boasted he would master the Minotaur, and Aegeus gave the pilot a second sail, a white one, with the order that has governed the story ever since: if Theseus returned alive, hoist the white; otherwise sail with the black, and so indicate the affliction (Theseus 17.4, paraphrased). Simonides, Plutarch notes, made the sail not white but scarlet, dyed with the flower of the holm-oak — a variant worth keeping, because it shows the signal-sail was already a poetic property, worked and reworked, within a generation of the story's fixing. The ship, its sails, and its pilot's orders are now all in place. What happens on the voyage out belongs to the lyric poets.

VII. The Voyage: Bacchylides, Minos, and the Ring in the Sea

Before the ship reached Crete, the story's oldest surviving poet gives Theseus a trial that the prose systematisers never mention — and it is not a trial of strength but of birth. Bacchylides' Ode 17, a dithyramb composed for Athenian performance in the early fifth century BCE, opens on the dark-prowed ship cleaving the Cretan sea with Theseus and the twice-seven Athenian youths and maidens aboard (in Jebb's translation, paraphrased here). Minos, stung by desire, lays a hand on one of the maidens, Eriboea. Theseus stops him, and the two exchange claims of divine parentage: Minos is son of Zeus; Theseus, he declares, is son of Poseidon, and will defend the Athenian girls as his father's son should. Minos answers with a test. He prays to Zeus for a lightning-flash as a sign of his own birth — and the lightning comes. Then he takes the gold ring from his hand, throws it into the sea, and challenges the supposed son of the sea-god to fetch it from his father's house.

Theseus dives. Dolphins carry him down to the hall of Poseidon, where he sees the Nereids dancing, their limbs gleaming as of fire, and is received by his father's wife, the ox-eyed Amphitrite, who robes him in a purple cloak and sets on his hair a wreath of roses, her wedding-gift from Aphrodite. He returns to the ship with the ring — and, in Jebb's telling of the poem's close, comes up from the sea unwetted, to the wonder of all, while the Athenian youths raise the paean and the maidens crown him with garlands (Ode 17, paraphrased from Jebb's translation throughout this section). The episode does three kinds of work at once. It proves the Poseidon paternity that Troezen claimed and Athens used. It establishes, before Crete is even reached, that the contest between Theseus and Minos is a contest of two divine sons, Zeus's and Poseidon's, in which the Athenian is not outranked. And it gives the tribute-voyage a liturgical frame — the paean, the garlands — that the Athenian festivals of the return (Section X) will institutionalise. That Plutarch and Apollodorus omit the dive entirely is itself instructive: the prose tradition, rationalising as it goes, quietly drops the miracle and keeps the wrestling-match with Taurus that Philochorus offered instead (Section VI). The lyric Theseus is a god's son proving it in the deep. The biographical Theseus is an athlete. Both were Athens'.

Bacchylides' other Theseus poem, Ode 18, covers the road labours from the receiving end. It is a dialogue — almost a miniature drama, unique among surviving dithyrambs — between the aged Aegeus and a chorus of Athenians. A war-trumpet has sounded; a herald has come from the Isthmus; Aegeus, anxious, questions the chorus, who report, deed by deed, the unknown young man who has killed Sinis, the Crommyonian sow, Sciron, Cercyon, and Procrustes, and is now approaching Athens with two men only in attendance (paraphrased from Jebb's translation). The poem ends before the arrival: Theseus exists, for his own father and his own city, as a sequence of reports — a reputation travelling faster than the man. As a piece of civic theatre it is exact. Athens first hears of its hero, labour by labour, in the order of the road; the audience, who knew the ending, watched the city in the poem learn what they already possessed. Performed at an Athenian festival, Ode 18 is the promotion of Theseus caught in the act of promoting itself.

VIII. The Labyrinth, the Clew, and the Killing of the Minotaur

At Crete, the story narrows to a door, a thread, and a room at the centre. The fullest plain telling is Apollodorus's. When Theseus came to Crete, Ariadne, daughter of Minos, fell in love with him, and offered to help him if he would agree to carry her away to Athens and have her to wife. Theseus swore the oath. Ariadne then begged Daedalus — the Labyrinth's own builder, the one man who knew its secret — to disclose the way out, and at his suggestion gave Theseus the means of escape (Epitome 1.8, paraphrased). In Frazer's translation, the passage runs:

“And at his suggestion she gave Theseus a clue when he went in; Theseus fastened it to the door, and, drawing it after him, entered in. And having found the Minotaur in the last part of the labyrinth, he killed him by smiting him with his fists.” — APOLLODORUS, EPITOME 1.9, TRANS. J. G. FRAZER (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY, 1921)

Frazer's word is “clue”, and Frazer, in his note on the passage, explains what the thing was: a ball of thread, which Ariadne had begged of Daedalus; Theseus was to fasten one end to the lintel of the door on entering, hold the ball in his hand and unwind the skein as he penetrated deeper, find the Minotaur — asleep, in the scholiasts' elaboration Frazer reports — in the inmost recess, kill him, and retrace his steps, gathering up the thread behind him (Frazer's note on Epitome 1.9, paraphrased). Plutarch's version is the same in substance and terser in form: most historians and poets, he says, tell that Theseus got from Ariadne, who had fallen in love with him, the famous thread, was instructed by her how to make his way through the intricacies of the Labyrinth, slew the Minotaur, and sailed off with Ariadne and the youths (Theseus 19.1, paraphrased). Pherecydes added that he staved in the bottoms of the Cretan ships to prevent pursuit — the hero as saboteur, covering his escape with the thoroughness of a raider rather than a bridegroom.

The Word: Clew, Then Clue

The thread deserves its philology, because the English language kept it. The old English word for a ball of thread or yarn is clew — from Old English cliewen, a ball or skein, a word in use before the twelfth century for the literal object. Because Ariadne's ball of thread was the thing that guided a man through an intricate place, clew came to mean anything that so guides; and in the fifteenth to seventeenth centuries the spelling was remodelled, by analogy with French-derived words in -ue, into clue — which then detached from thread altogether and became the modern word for a piece of evidence that leads toward the solution of a problem. Every detective's clue is therefore, etymologically, Ariadne's ball of thread, with the Labyrinth forgotten and the thread retained. Frazer already translates with the modern spelling; this article uses clew for the object itself, to keep the ball of thread visible inside the familiar word. It is the story's most complete survival: the monster, the maze, and the hero are all arguable, but the clew is in the dictionary.

The killing itself is strikingly under-described in the early prose tradition — Apollodorus gives it a clause, with the fists; no sword is mentioned, and the tribute youths had come unarmed, as the terms required. Art is more forthcoming than text: Athenian vase-painting from the sixth century onward shows the fight repeatedly, most often with the sword — the father's sword, by narrative logic — and shows Theseus dragging the dead or dying Minotaur out of a structure that the painters render, when they render it at all, as a columned facade or a meander-patterned block: the Labyrinth as an emblem, not a floor-plan. One famous Attic cup by the painter Aison (c. 425–410 BCE) shows Theseus hauling the Minotaur from a temple-like building while Athena, Athens' own goddess, attends him — the Cretan exploit re-staged, in the city's art, under Athenian patronage. What the images agree on, across two centuries, is the composition: the hero upright and calm, the bull-man collapsing. The fight is always already won. That is what a civic emblem requires, and by the time of the Aison cup the Minotaur fight had long been one.

// WHAT THE LABYRINTH WAS, IN THE TELLING //

Three Labyrinths circulate in the sources, and this article keeps them distinct. (1) The mythic maze: Daedalus's building at Cnossus, of many winding turns, built to hold the Minotaur (Apollodorus). (2) The Cretan counter-claim reported by Plutarch from Philochorus: a prison from which escape was impossible, its “monster” the general Taurus. (3) The dancing-floor: in the Iliad (18.590–594), the one Homeric mention of Daedalus's Cretan work is not a maze at all but a dancing-ground he made for Ariadne at Cnossus — the image later tradition, and Plutarch's account of the Delian crane-dance (Section IX), connects with the winding paths. Section XI takes up what archaeology can and cannot add to all three.

IX. Naxos: Ariadne Abandoned — and the Versions That Would Not Agree

By night, Apollodorus says, Theseus arrived with Ariadne and the rescued youths at Naxos. There the story — which has run on rails since the embarkation — breaks into its most famous disagreement. Plutarch opens his chapter on the subject with a sentence every student of myth should have by heart, in paraphrase: there are many other stories about these matters, and also about Ariadne, but they do not agree at all (Theseus 20.1). He then gives the variants, and this section follows his arrangement, adding the older witnesses he presupposes.

The Variants, in Order of Their Tendency

The oldest: she dies. In the Odyssey (11.321–325), Theseus never had joy of Ariadne: Artemis slew her on sea-girt Dia — the island later identified with Naxos — “because of the witness of Dionysus”, a phrase antiquity itself found obscure and explained as Dionysus informing against her, or testifying to her union with Theseus (paraphrased). No abandonment, no rescue: the Cretan princess is simply killed by a goddess on the way home.

The canonical: Dionysus takes her. In Apollodorus, Dionysus fell in love with Ariadne at Naxos, carried her off, brought her to Lemnos, and begat on her Thoas, Staphylus, Oenopion, and Peparethus (Epitome 1.9, paraphrased) — Frazer notes that Apollodorus seems to be the only extant author who brings her to Lemnos. In Hesiod's Theogony (947–949), Dionysus made Ariadne his wife and Zeus made her immortal and ageless (paraphrased). Pausanias saw the scene painted on the Athenian Acropolis: Ariadne asleep, Theseus putting out to sea, Dionysus arriving to carry her off (1.20.3, paraphrased). In this version the abandonment is real but providential — the hero leaves a sleeping girl, and a god collects her; later tellers, Diodorus among them, soften it further by having Dionysus appear to Theseus in a dream and demand her, so that the desertion becomes obedience.

The hostile: he left her for another woman. Some said, Plutarch reports, that Ariadne hanged herself, abandoned by Theseus; others that sailors conveyed her to Naxos, where she lived with Oenarus, a priest of Dionysus, and that Theseus abandoned her because he loved another woman — Aegle, daughter of Panopeus, in the verse Plutarch quotes and then reports was expunged from Hesiod by Peisistratus to protect the hero's reputation (Theseus 20.1–2, paraphrased). The same chapter notes that Ion of Chios gave Ariadne sons by Theseus himself, Oenopion and Staphylus. The variants are not drifting randomly. Each is a verdict on Theseus: the god took her (acquittal), she died by heaven's act (no case), he deserted her for Aegle (conviction).

The Cypriot: she dies in childbed, and he grieves. The most peculiar account, Plutarch says, is that of Paeon the Amathusian, and it is a local cult-story of Amathus on Cyprus, where Ariadne was worshipped with Aphrodite. Theseus, driven off course by a storm to Cyprus, had with him Ariadne, pregnant and in sore distress from the tossing of the sea; he set her ashore alone, and was himself borne out to sea again while trying to succour the ship. The women of the island cared for her, tried to comfort her loneliness — even bringing her forged letters purporting to be from Theseus — ministered to her in the pangs of travail, and buried her when she died before her child was born. Theseus returned, was greatly afflicted, left money for sacrifices to her, and set up two little statuettes in her honour, one of silver and one of bronze; and at her festival there, on the second day of the month Gorpiaeus, a young man lies down and imitates the cries and gestures of a woman in labour (Theseus 20.3–4, paraphrased). The grove of her tomb was called the grove of Ariadne Aphrodite. As narrative, it acquits Theseus by weather. As evidence, it is priceless in a different way: it shows a local cult of a dying Ariadne, complete with tomb, images, endowment, and a rite of sympathetic labour-pains, generating its own version of the pan-Hellenic story to fit its own shrine.

The Naxian solution: there were two of her. Some of the Naxians, Plutarch concludes, had a story of their own: there were two Minoses and two Ariadnes. The elder Ariadne was married to Dionysus on Naxos and bore him Staphylus and his brother; the younger, of a later time, was carried off by Theseus, abandoned, came to Naxos with her nurse Corcyne — whose tomb they show — and died there. The festivals keep the two apart: the first Ariadne's is celebrated with mirth and revels, the second's with sorrow and mourning (Theseus 20.5, paraphrased). Faced with irreconcilable traditions, Naxos did not choose. It doubled its heroine, assigned each version its own cult, and charged both to the island's account. The reader who has followed this hub's method will recognise the manoeuvre: it is what the evidence itself does, performed this time by the worshippers.

// THE STOP AT DELOS //

On the voyage home, Plutarch says, Theseus put in at Delos, sacrificed to Apollo, dedicated the image of Aphrodite he had received from Ariadne, and danced with his youths a dance still performed by the Delians in Plutarch's day: an imitation of the circling passages of the Labyrinth, of rhythmic involutions and evolutions, called the Crane, danced round the altar of horns (Theseus 21, paraphrased). The detail links the Cretan story to the Iliad's dancing-ground for Ariadne (Section VIII) and to living ritual at once: the maze, escaped, becomes a dance; the dance, performed, becomes the maze remembered. Theseus was also said to have founded the Delian games on this visit, and the custom of the victor's palm.

X. The Black Sail, the Death of Aegeus, and the Ship of Theseus

As the ship drew near the coast of Attica, Theseus himself forgot — and his pilot forgot, such was their joy and exultation — to hoist the sail that was to have been the token of their safety. Aegeus, watching, saw the black sail, concluded his son was dead, and in despair threw himself down from the rock and was dashed in pieces (Plutarch, Theseus 22.1, paraphrased). Apollodorus tells it with the same economy: Theseus, in his grief for Ariadne, forgot to spread the white sails, and Aegeus, seeing the black sail from the Acropolis, threw himself down and died (Epitome 1.10, paraphrased). The two systematisers assign the forgetting different causes — Plutarch's is joy, Apollodorus's is grief for the lost Ariadne — and the difference is a small masterclass in how moralising works on a story: the same fatal omission, read as carelessness in triumph or as distraction in sorrow, makes two different heroes. Later tradition attached the king's fall to the naming of the Aegean Sea; the ancient sources used here do not make that etymology explicit, and this article does not make it for them.

The arrival scene that follows is, in Plutarch, a study in a city receiving two pieces of news at once. Theseus landed at Phalerum, performed in person the sacrifices he had vowed at his setting-out, and sent a herald ahead to the city to announce his safe return. The herald found many of the people bewailing the death of their king, and others full of joy at his tidings and eager to crown him with garlands. He accepted the garlands but twined them about his herald's staff, and returning to the shore, waited outside the sacred precinct while Theseus finished his libations before announcing Aegeus's death — whence, they say, at the festival of the Oschophoria to this day it is not the herald who is crowned, but his staff (Theseus 22.2–3, paraphrased). After burying his father, Theseus paid his vows to Apollo on the seventh day of Pyanepsion — the day of the return to the city — when the rescued youths pooled their remaining provisions, boiled them in one common pot, and feasted together: the origin, in the aetiology, of the Pyanepsia, the festival of the boiling of pulse, with its eiresiōnē, the olive bough wreathed with wool and laden with fruits, carried in procession with its song (Theseus 22.4–5, paraphrased). The return from Crete is thus written into the Athenian autumn calendar at three points — Oschophoria, Pyanepsia, and the Theseia itself (Section XVII) — so that the city re-enacted the voyage's ending, in grief and joy together, every year. This is layer (b) at its most legible: whatever did or did not happen on the voyage, the festivals happened, annually, and they taught the story to everyone who ate the boiled pulse.

The Ship

And the ship itself was kept. Plutarch's sentence on it became, quite separately from anything else in the Life, one of the most consequential sentences in Western philosophy:

“The ship on which Theseus sailed with the youths and returned in safety, the thirty-oared galley, was preserved by the Athenians down to the time of Demetrius Phalereus. They took away the old timbers from time to time, and put new and sound ones in their places” — PLUTARCH, THESEUS 23.1, TRANS. BERNADOTTE PERRIN (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY, 1914)

Plutarch completes the thought: so that the vessel became, in Perrin's continuation, a standing illustration for the philosophers in the mooted question of growth — some declaring that it remained the same, others that it was not the same vessel (Theseus 23.1). Demetrius Phalereus ruled Athens in 317–307 BCE, so the claim is that the Athenians maintained the relic, plank by replaced plank, for some eight centuries on the story's own chronology — or, on any sober chronology, for as long as the classical city endured. The same vessel had a working liturgical life as well: Plato, in the Phaedo (58a–c, paraphrased), describes the sacred ship sent annually to Delos — the ship, Athenian tradition said, in which Theseus had sailed to Crete — during whose absence on the holy mission no public execution could be carried out at Athens; it is that ship's delayed return that postpones the death of Socrates in 399 BCE. The relic was not a museum piece. It sailed, on the city's holiest errand, and the city's justice waited for it.

The later reading — layer (c), and it must be labelled as such — is the fame. Plutarch's report is about growth: the philosophers' question, as he frames it, is the question of how a living thing, or a thing maintained like one, persists through the replacement of its material. Later philosophy generalised the case into the Ship of Theseus paradox, a standing problem of identity over time: if every plank is replaced, is it the same ship? Thomas Hobbes, in the seventeenth century, sharpened it with the addition Plutarch never made — suppose the discarded original planks were collected and built into a second ship; which of the two is Theseus's? The reassembled-planks ship is Hobbes's, not Plutarch's, and accounts that attribute it to antiquity are misreading their source. What Plutarch actually gives is both simpler and, for this article's purposes, better: a city keeping a hero's ship in being by continuous repair, and philosophers arguing over the result. The Athenians who replaced the timbers were not solving a paradox. They were doing with the ship exactly what they did with the hero — maintaining an identity across centuries by replacing, piece by piece, everything it was made of, and insisting, against all comers, that it remained the same.

XI. The Cretan Background, Honestly: Knossos, Evans, and the Limits of the Match

Behind the Cretan expedition stands a real place and a real civilisation, and this section states what is known of them at full strength — and then states, with equal plainness, why the myth is not a memory of them in any simple sense. The hub's index timeline sets the frame: the Greco-Roman timeline opens with Minoan Crete, the first Aegean palace civilisation, whose non-Greek-speaking builders raised unfortified palace complexes at Knossos, Phaistos, Malia, and Zakros from about 2000 BCE, and whose world the Mycenaean Greeks took over, at Knossos around 1450 BCE. The names themselves confess the relationship: “Minoan” is a modern coinage, taken from the legendary King Minos, applied by archaeology to a civilisation whose own name for itself is unknown. Greek myth did not preserve the Bronze Age Cretans. It preserved a king's name, a palace's reputation, and a bull — and archaeology, arriving three thousand years later, named the whole civilisation after the myth.

Evans at Knossos

The excavation that forced the question was Sir Arthur Evans's. After preliminary digging by Minos Kalokairinos in 1878, Evans began systematic excavation at Knossos in 1900 and uncovered the great palace: a sprawling, multi-storeyed complex of hundreds of rooms, corridors, staircases, and courts, covering some five acres with its dependencies, rebuilt and elaborated over centuries until its final destruction in the later Bronze Age. Evans — who also gave the civilisation its name, “Minoan”, and reconstructed parts of the palace in reinforced concrete in forms that remain controversial — read the ruins through the myth he had brought with him, and said so. The palace's bewildering plan, he proposed, was the origin of the Labyrinth: not a purpose-built maze, but a real building complex so intricate that later Greek visitors and storytellers remembered it as one. The double-axe signs carved on its stone blocks supplied his etymology: labyrinthos, he suggested, might be analysed as the “house of the labrys” — labrys being the word, of Lydian origin reported by Plutarch himself in another work, for a double-headed axe — with the pre-Greek suffix -inthos denoting place, as in Corinth. He named a great hall of the palace the Hall of the Double Axes accordingly.

The bull material was as striking as the plan. The palace produced bull imagery in abundance, most famously the Bull-Leaping Fresco: a great bull at the gallop, with figures — in the restored composition — grasping its horns, somersaulting over its back, and landing behind it. Evans interpreted the scene as a ritual sport, bull-leaping or bull-grappling, performed by young men and women; related images, and bull's-head rhytons and horns of consecration from across Minoan Crete, established the bull as a central animal of Minoan cult and display. To a generation raised on the myth, the inference seemed irresistible: here were the youths and maidens, here was the bull, here was the maze-like palace — the tribute story was the folk-memory of Minoan Crete exacting human tribute from the mainland, and the Minotaur the folk-memory of its bull-cult.

// THE MATCH, WEIGHED //

What is solid: a real, powerful, palace-building Crete of the Bronze Age; a palace at Knossos of genuinely labyrinthine complexity; the double axe as a major Minoan cult sign, carved at Knossos; bull-leaping as a depicted Minoan performance; and a Greek myth, fixed a millennium later, that puts a bull-monster in a maze under a Cretan king's palace and sends mainland youths to it. What is hypothesis, and is labelled hypothesis wherever this hub uses it: the derivation of labyrinthos from labrys (the word is pre-Greek and its etymology is unresolved; the labrys connection, first suggested by Maximilian Mayer in 1892 and taken up by Evans, remains one proposal among others, and the double axe in Crete accompanies goddesses and women, not a bull-god or a king); the identification of any excavated room as “the” Labyrinth; and any direct line from bull-leaping to the Minotaur, whose man-bull form has Near Eastern and Greek parallels that need no fresco to explain them. What is not supported at all: that the tribute story records a historical Minoan tax of Athenian youths. No Minoan or Egyptian record attests it; Athens itself has no Bronze Age palace of the kind the story presupposes as Crete's counterpart; and the tale's structure — the youngest son, the helpful princess, the thread, the forgotten signal — is the structure of folktale, found worldwide. The honest sentence is the layered one: the myth's setting plausibly preserves a genuine, if garbled, Greek awareness that Crete had once held great palaces and sea-power — the same awareness behind the hub timeline's Minoan entry — while the myth's events are story, and its meaning, as Athens used it, is Athenian. Evans, to put it in this article's terms, excavated layer (b) and repeatedly announced he had found layer (a). The spade cannot do that, and the modern literature on Knossos, which has also had to reckon with how much of “the palace” visitors see is Evans's own concrete imagination, no longer asks it to.

One further Cretan survival deserves note, because it is a text and not a conjecture. In the Linear B tablets from Knossos — Mycenaean Greek administrative records of the palace's last phase — scholars have read a word da-pu-ri-to, interpreted as daburinthos, a form of “labyrinth”, in an offering record mentioning a “Mistress of the Labyrinth” (da-pu-ri-to-jo po-ti-ni-ja). The reading and interpretation are debated among specialists, and this article reports them as debated. If the interpretation holds, the word and a goddess attached to it were already at home in the palace in the Bronze Age — the strongest single thread connecting the Greek word to the Cretan building, and still, even at its strongest, evidence for a word and a cult-title, not for a monster, a maze-prison, or a tribute of Athenian children.

XII. Theseus as King: The Synoecism of Attica

After the death of Aegeus, Plutarch says, Theseus conceived a wonderful design (Theseus 24.1). Attica — the whole territory of modern Attica, some 2,500 square kilometres of plain, hill, and coast — was inhabited, in the story's premise, by a scatter of separate communities, each with its own town-hall, council-chamber, and magistrates, not easily called together for common interests and sometimes at war with one another. Theseus went through them township by township and clan by clan, persuading: the common folk and the poor answered quickly; to the powerful he promised a government without a king — a democracy, in Plutarch's word — in which he himself should be only commander in war and guardian of the laws, while in all else everyone should be on an equal footing. Some he persuaded readily; others, fearing his power, chose to be persuaded rather than forced. He then abolished the local town-halls, council-chambers, and magistracies, built one common town-hall and council-chamber where the upper town of Athens now stands, named the city Athens, and instituted the Panathenaic festival as the common festival of the united people; and he instituted the Metoecia — the Festival of Settlement, the Synoikia — on the sixteenth of Hecatombaeon, which, Plutarch says, is still celebrated (Theseus 24.2–4, paraphrased). Laying aside the royal power as he had agreed, he organised the citizen body — the first, Aristotle said, to show a leaning towards the multitude and give up absolute rule — into the three orders of noblemen, husbandmen, and handicraftsmen, assigning to the noblemen the care of religious rites, the magistracies, and the interpretation of law and heaven's will (Theseus 25, paraphrased). He invited all comers to the enlarged city on equal terms — “Come hither all ye people” was remembered as his proclamation — coined money stamped with an ox, fixed the boundary with Megara at the Isthmus with an inscribed pillar (“Here is not Peloponnesus, but Ionia” / “Here is Peloponnesus, not Ionia”), and refounded the Isthmian games in honour of Poseidon in emulation of Heracles' Olympic foundation (Theseus 25.3–5, paraphrased).

This is the synoecism — Greek synoikismos, a “dwelling-together” — and it is the deed on which Theseus's whole civic importance rests. It must now be read in the second layer, where it becomes more interesting, not less. As history, a single act of political unification of Attica by one Bronze Age king cannot be verified: Attica was unified remarkably early by Greek standards — unlike the Peloponnese, it never fragmented into warring city-states — and archaeology finds a major Mycenaean centre on the Athenian Acropolis; but no evidence dates the unification to one reign or one man, and sober accounts spread it across the early Iron Age. As a story, the synoecism's own history can be traced, and it is late. The first explicit surviving statement is Thucydides (2.15), in the late fifth century BCE, who treats it as sober constitutional history and gives Theseus the credit — while noting that the country people, in his own day still, were reluctant to leave their ancestral homes and shrines, a reluctance he explains by the depth of the old local attachments the synoecism had overridden. Modern scholarship — the argument is associated with the Atthidographic tradition's modern readers and is conveniently set out in the literature cited in Section XVIII — notes that the synoecism does not appear in art or poetry before Thucydides, unlike every other Thesean deed, and proposes that the story in its developed form is a product of the fifth century: the century in which Athens, now an imperial democracy governing an Attica whose unity was its strength, projected its own founding act backwards onto its founder-hero, and celebrated it annually at the Synoikia, a real festival of Zeus Phratrios and Athena Phratria whose Thesean aetiology is the projection's ritual form. Whether Thucydides preserves an old tradition or ratifies a recent one is, on present evidence, undecidable — and this article leaves it undecided, with the observation that the question itself could only be asked about a hero whose deeds were still being assigned in the historian's own lifetime.

The Democrat on Stage

The synoecism's Theseus — the king who limits himself — becomes, in Euripides' Suppliants (c. 423 BCE), democracy's first spokesman. The play's business is the recovery of the Argive dead: Adrastus and the mothers of the Seven, refused burial of their sons by Thebes, supplicate at Eleusis; Aethra persuades her son; and Theseus, before he will act, insists on the consent of the Athenian people — and then faces a Theban herald who asks, in Coleridge's translation, who is the despot of this land, to whom he must announce Creon's message. Theseus's answer is the most quoted political speech ever put in the hero's mouth:

“Sir stranger, thou hast made a false beginning to thy speech, in seeking here a despot. For this city is not ruled by one man, but is free. The people rule in succession year by year, allowing no preference to wealth, but the poor man shares equally with the rich.” — EURIPIDES, SUPPLIANTS 404–408, TRANS. E. P. COLERIDGE

The debate that follows — the herald attacking popular government as the rule of the glib and the ignorant, Theseus answering that written laws give rich and poor equal justice and that nothing is more hostile to a city than a despot (paraphrased) — is fifth-century Athenian political theory in a Bronze Age king's mouth, and no ancient spectator can have missed the anachronism or its point. The same play then sends Theseus to war for the unwritten law of Hellas, the burial of the dead; he defeats Thebes, washes the corpses with his own hands, and — the restraint is the lesson — refuses to sack the city, having come, as he says, to bury the dead, not to destroy the living (paraphrased). Plutarch, it should be recorded, corrects the play: Theseus, he says, recovered the bodies not by battle, as Euripides has it, but by persuading the Thebans to a truce — the first truce ever made for recovering the slain — and he cites Aeschylus's lost Eleusinians, in which Theseus related the matter so, against Euripides (Theseus 29.4–5, paraphrased). Even the hero's most democratic hour was contested between the tragedians and the historians. What no version contests is the function: by the 420s, Theseus is the figure through whom Athens argues with itself about what Athens is — free city, law's champion, refuge of suppliants — and the argument is conducted, on stage, before the assembled people whose ancestor he is made to be. For the democracy's later, visual form of the same claim — Theseus painted on the wall of a public building beside personified Demokratia and Demos, with the caption-tradition Pausanias reports, that Theseus was the one who instituted politics on a basis of equality for the Athenians (Pausanias 1.3.3, paraphrased) — see Section XVII.

XIII. The Amazonomachy: Antiope, Hippolyte, and a War in the City's Streets

The second great military deed of the reign is the war with the Amazons — and it begins, as so much in this hero's life begins, with a woman taken from a ship. Theseus sailed to the land of the Amazons on the southern shore of the Black Sea, to Themicyra on the river Thermodon. Whether he went with Heracles, on the expedition for the girdle of the Amazon queen, and received the Amazon Antiope as his prize of valour, or — as the majority of writers, including Pherecydes, Hellanicus, and Herodorus, held, and as Plutarch judges the more probable story — sailed on his own account after Heracles' time and took her himself, the tradition is divided at the source (Theseus 26.1, paraphrased). Bion's version made the taking a stratagem: the Amazons, naturally friendly to men, sent presents; Theseus invited the bearer aboard his ship; she came; and he put out to sea (Theseus 26.2, paraphrased). The queen herself is named differently across the tradition — Antiope in most accounts, Hippolyte in others, the two names sometimes distributed between two women, the captive and the queen whose girdle Heracles took. The confusion is ancient and irreducible on the evidence we have; this article uses Antiope for Theseus's Amazon, following Plutarch's majority, and flags the alternative where it matters — her son is Hippolytus, or, as Pindar said, Demophoon (Theseus 28.2, paraphrased).

The Amazons answered the abduction with an invasion of Attica — and not a raid, in Plutarch's emphatic telling, but a war: they would not, he argues, have pitched their camp within the city and fought hand-to-hand battles in the neighbourhood of the Pnyx and the Museum hill had they not mastered the surrounding country and approached with impunity (Theseus 27.1, paraphrased). Whether they came round by the Cimmerian Bosporus, crossing on the ice, as Hellanicus wrote, Plutarch doubts; the encampment in the city's heart he treats as attested — by the names of the localities and by the graves of the fallen. After long hesitation on both sides, Theseus, having sacrificed to Fear in obedience to an oracle, joined battle on a day of the month Boedromion still marked in Plutarch's time by the Athenian festival of the Boedromia. Cleidemus, the most minute of the Atthidographers, supplied a full battle-piece: the Amazon left wing reaching to the place later called the Amazoneum, their right touching the Pnyx; the Athenians routed from the Museum as far as the shrine of the Eumenides; the counter-attack from the Palladium, Ardettus, and the Lyceum driving the Amazon right back to its camp; and after three months a treaty, negotiated — in Cleidemus's naming — through Hippolyta (Theseus 27.3–4, paraphrased). Some said the Amazon who fought at Theseus's side was slain by a javelin from her own kinswoman Molpadia, and that the pillar by the sanctuary of Olympian Earth was her memorial. That the war ended in solemn treaty Plutarch treats as proved by the name of the place adjoining the Theseum called Horcomosium, the Place of Oaths, and by the sacrifice offered to the Amazons, in ancient times, before the festival of Theseus (Theseus 27.5, paraphrased). Amazon graves were shown at Megara, at Chaeroneia by the stream once called Thermodon after the Amazons' own river, and in Thessaly near Scotussa and Cynoscephalae — a whole invasion-route of tombs, mapped across central Greece.

Read in layer (b), the Amazonomachy is the most public of all Theseus's deeds. It was carved on the west metopes of the Parthenon; painted by Micon in the Stoa Poikile, where Pausanias saw it hanging in sequence with the battle of Marathon (1.15.2, paraphrased); painted again in the Theseion itself (Section XVII). No other episode of the hero's life received comparable monumental investment. Read in layer (c), the reason is not far to seek, and modern scholarship has generally found it in the Persian Wars: an invasion of Attica from the east, by an enemy figured as at once foreign, formidable, and transgressive of the Greek order of the sexes, repelled in the streets of Athens itself by the city's founder — the parallel with 480 BCE, when the Persians took and burned an Athens whose people had withdrawn, needed no stating to an Athenian audience, and the Stoa's hanging of Amazonomachy and Marathon in one room stated it in paint. The reading is a reading: no ancient author says in so many words that the Amazons “are” the Persians, and the Amazonomachy is older in art than the Persian Wars themselves. But its monumental promotion belongs to the decades after those wars, in the building programme of the same Cimon who brought home the bones — and the pairing, in one portico, of Theseus's victory with Miltiades', is the city's own juxtaposition, not a modern scholar's. The Amazons, like the Minotaur, were something Athens needed to have defeated in its streets, at its beginning, by its founder. What the war cost the founder is the next section's subject: Antiope died of it, one way or another; and Theseus, after her death, married Phaedra of Crete — Minos's daughter, Ariadne's sister. The Cretan story, which the hero thought he had escaped by a thread, re-entered his house by marriage.

XIV. Phaedra and Hippolytus: The House at Troezen

Of the calamities that befell Phaedra and the son of Theseus by Antiope, Plutarch writes, since there is no conflict between historians and tragic poets, we must suppose that they happened as the poets uniformly represent them (Theseus 28.2, paraphrased). The uniformity is real, and the surviving representation is Euripides' Hippolytus, produced in 428 BCE and awarded first prize — the second treatment of the story by Euripides, an earlier, lost Hippolytus Veiled having offended its audience with a Phaedra who propositioned her stepson in person. The surviving play is set at Troezen, the hero's own birthplace, where Theseus is serving a year's exile for a killing and where his son Hippolytus — the Amazon's son, reared by the aged Pittheus — has devoted himself, in scandalous completeness, to Artemis and to chastity, refusing Aphrodite her honours. Aphrodite speaks the prologue, and the play's machinery is her revenge: she has filled Phaedra, Theseus's wife, with a maddening desire for her stepson (paraphrased from the play throughout this section).

The Euripidean Phaedra is not a villainess but a woman fighting, with increasing desperation, for her good name: she has starved herself for three days, meaning to die with her honour intact, when her Nurse drags the confession from her — and then, meaning to help, betrays it, carrying the secret to Hippolytus under an oath of silence. Hippolytus's response is the play's most notorious passage: a furious tirade against women, overheard by Phaedra, who understands that her reputation is lost whether the oath holds or not. She hangs herself — and leaves, clasped in her hand, a written tablet accusing Hippolytus of having assaulted her. Theseus returns from his exile to find his wife dead and the letter speaking for her. He does not investigate. He does not, in the play's terms, even pause: he spends, on the spot, one of the three curses that Poseidon — his father, in the play's premise — had promised him, calling down his son's death, and banishes Hippolytus in the same breath. Hippolytus, bound by his oath to the Nurse, cannot speak the one fact that would clear him, and goes into exile protesting only his innocence in general terms. On the shore road, as he drives away, a monstrous bull rises from the sea in a great wave — Poseidon's answer to the curse — the horses panic, the chariot is smashed against the rocks, and Hippolytus is dragged, tangled in his own reins, nearly dead. He is carried back to Troezen in time for Artemis to appear, tell Theseus the truth entire — Phaedra's desire, the Nurse's betrayal, the false letter, and Theseus's own culpable haste — and for father and son to be reconciled over the son's broken body, Hippolytus forgiving Theseus as he dies (paraphrased).

Apollodorus's prose version keeps the skeleton and changes the moral lighting: in the Epitome (1.18–19, paraphrased), Phaedra, having been rejected when she propositioned Hippolytus by letter herself, accuses him falsely to Theseus, who believes her, prays to Poseidon, and sends the bull; Phaedra, discovered, hangs herself. The differences between the versions — who carries the desire, who writes the letter, whether Phaedra acts from Aphrodite's compulsion or her own passion — are the tradition worrying at the same intolerable knot: a father's curse, once spoken, cannot be recalled, and the gods honour it anyway. For this article's argument, the play's deepest point is civic. Athens' founder, on its own stage, is shown destroying his house by the precise failure his political myth exists to exclude: he judges on a written accusation, without hearing the accused, in anger, using a god's prerogative as a private weapon. The Suppliants Theseus of five years later proclaims that written laws give rich and poor equal justice. The Hippolytus Theseus gives his son no justice at all. Tragedy held both portraits because the city needed both: the founder as its ideal, and the founder as its warning. Hippolytus, for his part, received what the story's victims sometimes receive — a cult: at Troezen, Pausanias reports, he had a precinct and annual sacrifices, and Troezenian girls before marriage cut a lock of their hair for him (2.32.1, paraphrased). The Amazon's son, killed by his father's word, became the patron of the threshold his stepmother's desire had barred to him.

// THE THREE CURSES //

The three curses — or three prayers — that Poseidon grants Theseus are a fixture of the Hippolytus tradition, stated by Euripides and repeated by Apollodorus. They have no place in the earlier Theseus material and no function anywhere else in his story; they exist, as a piece of machinery, to make a father's angry word irrevocable. This article notes them as the tradition's device, not as a datum of the hero's Poseidon-descent generally: the divine paternity that in Bacchylides proved itself by a dive and a wreath of roses proves itself here by a bull from the sea and a dead son. The same inheritance, in the two genres, is glory and catastrophe.

XV. Pirithous: The Wedding, Helen, and the Descent

The last movement of the life belongs to the friendship. Pirithous (Peirithous in the translators' spelling), king of the Lapiths of Thessaly, son of Ixion, tested the famous stranger in the way of heroes: he drove off Theseus's cattle from Marathon, and when Theseus pursued in arms, turned and faced him — whereupon, Plutarch says, each beholding the other with astonishment at his beauty and admiration of his daring, they refrained from battle, and Pirithous stretched out his hand and bade Theseus himself be judge of the robbery, and Theseus remitted the penalty and invited him to be a friend and brother in arms, and they ratified the friendship with oaths (Theseus 30.1–2, paraphrased). At Pirithous's wedding to Deidameia — Hippodameia in other accounts — the invited Centaurs, flown with insolence and wine, laid hands on the women, and the Lapiths, with Theseus fighting beside them at the banquet and in the war that followed, slew some on the spot and expelled the rest from the country (Theseus 30.3, paraphrased). The Centauromachy became, with the Amazonomachy, one of the two great battle-pieces of Athenian public art — carved on the Parthenon's south metopes and painted in the Theseion — and its civic sense is the Amazonomachy's twin: civilised order, at table and under law, beating back the beast-nature that drinks and grabs. Theseus at the wedding is the synoecist in arms, defending marriage itself, the institution his own career honoured so unevenly.

The friendship's price was a compact, and the compact is the story's moral collapse. The two heroes agreed, in Apollodorus's formulation, that they would marry daughters of Zeus (Epitome 1.23, paraphrased). For Theseus, the daughter chosen was Helen — daughter of Zeus and Leda, and a child. Plutarch does not flinch from the chronology: Theseus was already fifty years old, according to Hellanicus, when he took part in the rape of Helen, who was not of marriageable age; and he reports, without endorsing, the apologists who corrected the heaviest accusation against the hero by making him merely Helen's guardian, entrusted with her by others (Theseus 31.1, paraphrased). The most probable account, Plutarch judges — the one with most witnesses — is the worst: Theseus and Pirithous went to Sparta together, seized the girl as she danced in the temple of Artemis Orthia, fled beyond pursuit, cast lots for her, and Theseus, winning, conveyed her to Aphidnae in Attica, leaving her there in the keeping of his friend Aphidnus, with his own mother Aethra as her companion, in complete secrecy (Theseus 31.2–3, paraphrased). Then, to return Pirithous's service, Theseus went with him to claim the second daughter of Zeus: Persephone, queen of the dead.

The Descent

The katabasis — the going-down — is treated in full, as a pattern across Greek myth, in this hub's Descents to the Underworld article; what belongs here is Theseus's part in it. In Apollodorus's telling, the two arrived in Hades and were beguiled: Hades, on pretence of hospitality, bade them first be seated on the Chair of Forgetfulness; they grew to it and were held fast by coils of serpents (Epitome 1.24, paraphrased). Pirithous, the suitor of the goddess, remained bound for ever. Theseus was raised up by Heracles, who had come down for Cerberus — in the Library's account (2.5.12, paraphrased), the two stretched out their hands to him as to one who should raise them from the dead; Theseus he took by the hand and raised, but when he would have raised Pirithous too, the earth quaked, and he let go. Later embroidery, reported by Frazer from the scholia, had Theseus stick so fast that he left part of his flesh adhering to the rock — the aetiology, offered in earnest by somebody, for the Athenians' leanness in that part. Plutarch, true to his rationalising habit, relocates the whole episode to Epirus: the “underworld” was the court of Aidoneus, king of the Molossians, whose wife was called Phersephone, whose daughter was Cora, and whose great dog Cerberus fought her suitors; Pirithous was killed by the dog, Theseus imprisoned, and Heracles, arriving as a guest, begged his release from the king and obtained it (Theseus 31.4; 35.1, paraphrased). The episode was old — an epic Descent of Pirithous, ascribed to Hesiod, already told it, and the pair appear in Hades on an Argive shield-band of about 575–550 BCE — and it was canonical enough for Polygnotus to give it the place of honour in his underworld painting in the Lesche of the Cnidians at Delphi, where Pausanias saw the two seated, Theseus holding his friend's sword and his own, Pirithous gazing wistfully at the blades that had done such good service in the world of light (10.29.9, paraphrased). Theseus, returned to the upper world, dedicated to Heracles the precincts Athens had set apart for himself — all but four, Philochorus said, were renamed Heracleia from Theseia (Theseus 35.2, paraphrased). The detail is the friendship-cycle's perfect emblem: the rescued hero signs over his own cult to his rescuer, the elder hero in whose image he had modelled his life, and to whom, in the underworld as on the road, he remains the second.

The cost fell due at once, and at home. While Theseus sat in Hades, Helen's brothers, the Dioscuri, invaded Attica to recover her. Athens, worked on by Menestheus — the demagogue of Section XVI — and betrayed by Academus, who revealed Helen's hiding-place, opened its gates; Aphidnae was stormed; Helen was recovered; and Aethra, Theseus's mother, was carried off captive with her, to Sparta and in the sequel, the tradition said, to Troy itself, where Homer shows her among Helen's attendants (Iliad 3.144, paraphrased; Plutarch, Theseus 32–34). The Dioscuri, masters of everything, demanded only initiation into the Eleusinian Mysteries and departed as benefactors. Theseus returned from the dead to a city that had been invaded in his absence, for his crime, and had learned to govern itself under another man. The founder's last chapter is an exile's.

XVI. Exile and Death on Scyros

Plutarch's account of the fall is political analysis as much as biography, and it is the analysis of a man who understood democracies. Menestheus, son of Peteos, of the house of Erechtheus — the first of men, as they say, to affect popularity and ingratiate himself with the multitude — stirred up the chief men against Theseus: they had long hated him, thinking he had robbed each of the country nobles of his royal office and shut them all up in a single city, where he treated them as subjects and slaves. And the common people Menestheus threw into commotion with a single, devastating reproach: they thought they had a vision of liberty, but in reality they had been robbed of their native homes and religions in order that, in place of many good kings of their own blood, they might look obediently to one master who was an immigrant and an alien (Theseus 32.1, paraphrased). The synoecism, the hero's founding deed, is here re-read by its victims as dispossession — and the charge of foreignness, planted in Section V by the sons of Pallas, returns as the demagogue's weapon. The Dioscuri's invasion furthered the sedition; Menestheus received the invaders as benefactors; and when Theseus came back from the underworld and tried to rule again as before, he found those who had hated him adding contempt to their hatred, and a people corrupted, wishing to be cajoled rather than commanded. Attempting force, he was overpowered by demagogues and factions (Theseus 35.2–3, paraphrased).

Despairing of his cause, Theseus sent his children away privately into Euboea, to Elephenor; and he himself, after invoking curses upon the Athenians at Gargettus — where, Plutarch says, there is to this day the place called Araterion, the Place of Cursing — sailed away to Scyros, the rocky island north-east of Euboea, where the people were friendly to him, as he thought, and where he had ancestral estates (Theseus 35.3, paraphrased). There Lycomedes was king. Theseus applied to him for the restoration of his lands, intending to dwell there — though some said he asked Lycomedes' aid against the Athenians. Lycomedes, either fearing a man of such fame or doing Menestheus a favour, led him up to the high places of the land on pretence of showing him his estates from there, threw him down the cliffs, and killed him. Some, however, say that he slipped and fell of himself, walking there after supper, as was his custom (Theseus 35.4, paraphrased). Apollodorus's version is a clause: thence he was driven by Menestheus, went to Lycomedes on Scyros, and was cast down a cliff by him and died (Epitome 1.24, paraphrased).

At the time, Plutarch says, no one made any account of his death. Menestheus reigned at Athens; Theseus's sons, Demophon and Acamas, went as private men to Troy with Elephenor, and recovered their kingdom only after Menestheus died there (Theseus 35.5, paraphrased). The founder of Athens died unburied in a foreign island's soil, unmourned, his city in another man's hands, his mother a captive, his friend still sitting in the underworld. That is where the myth as told leaves him — and it is precisely the completeness of the abandonment that sets up the myth's last and most historical act. For in after times, Plutarch continues, the Athenians were moved to honour Theseus as a demigod — especially because many of those who fought at Marathon against the Medes thought they saw an apparition of Theseus in arms, rushing on in front of them against the barbarians (Theseus 35.5, paraphrased). The hero who could not hold his city in life returned to fight for it in 490 BCE, four and a half centuries, on the story's chronology, after his death. Pausanias preserves the sequel in paint: in the Marathon picture in the Stoa Poikile, among Callimachus and Miltiades and the heroes, stood Theseus, represented as coming up from beneath the earth (1.15.3, paraphrased). The exile was being rehabilitated. What remained was to bring him home.

// THE OTHER EXILE'S SHELTER //

The tradition could also remember the exiled Theseus graciously. In Sophocles' Oedipus at Colonus — produced posthumously in 401 BCE, and set in the playwright's own deme — the aged Theseus, king again in the play's timeless Athens, receives the blind, polluted Oedipus as a suppliant, protects him against Thebes, and accepts the gift of his buried body as a protection for Attic soil for ever (paraphrased). The play's Athens is the Suppliants' Athens: the city whose king shelters the helpless stranger. That Sophocles could stage that Theseus in the same decades in which the exile-and-murder story was also current is the tradition's usual doubleness — Athens kept both the founder it had driven out and the king who would never drive anyone out, and saw no need to choose between them.

XVII. Cult and Civic Footing: The Bones, the Theseion, the Theseia

After the Persian Wars, in the archonship of Phaedo — 476/5 BCE — the Athenians, consulting the oracle at Delphi, were told by the Pythian priestess to take up the bones of Theseus, give them honourable burial at Athens, and guard them there. It was difficult to find the grave, because of the inhospitable and savage nature of the Dolopians who then held Scyros. However, Cimon, son of Miltiades — the victor of Eurymedon, then at the height of his ascendancy — took the island, expelled the Dolopians, and planted Athenian settlers on it; and being ambitious to discover the grave, he saw an eagle, in a place where there was the semblance of a mound, pecking and tearing up the ground with its talons. By some divine ordering he understood the meaning and dug there — and there was found a coffin of a man of extraordinary size, a bronze spear-head lying by its side, and a sword. When these relics were brought home on Cimon's trireme, the Athenians were delighted, and received them with splendid processions and sacrifices, as though Theseus himself were returning to his city (Plutarch, Theseus 36.1–2, paraphrased; the same story is told in the Life of Cimon 8.3–6, where Perrin's Plutarch adds that the bones were brought back “after an absence of about four hundred years” and that this was the chief reason why the people took kindly to Cimon).

Every element of the episode deserves its layer-label. As event, it is as historical as anything in Cimon's career: Thucydides independently records the capture of Scyros and the expulsion of the Dolopians (1.98, paraphrased), and the bones' translation fits the documented pattern of fifth-century hero-relations — Sparta had fetched the bones of Orestes from Tegea a century earlier, on Delphi's instructions, to secure victory over the Tegeans (Herodotus 1.67–68, paraphrased), and Greek cities treated a hero's remains as a palladium, a physical guarantee of the hero's presence and protection. As identification, it is an act of faith performed with a spade: a large coffin, a spear, and a sword are what a Bronze Age or early Iron Age notable's burial on Scyros would in any case contain, and the eagle is the oracle's signature, not evidence. As politics, it is transparent and was transparent at the time: Cimon, whose father's glory at Marathon the apparition-story had already annexed to Theseus, annexed Theseus in return — the general who brought the founder home embodied the continuity of aristocratic leadership and democratic hero in one procession, and Plutarch says plainly that the coup made the people love him. Hero-making as statecraft is not a modern suspicion about this episode. It is the episode's own contemporary description.

The Theseion and the Festival

The bones were laid, Plutarch says, in the heart of the city, near the gymnasium of Ptolemy — Pausanias places the Theseion in the same quarter, east or south-east of the Agora (1.17.2, paraphrased) — and the tomb became a sanctuary and a place of refuge for runaway slaves and for all men of low estate who were afraid of men in power, since Theseus was a champion and helper of such during his life, and graciously received the supplications of the poor and needy (Theseus 36.2, paraphrased). The sanctuary was decorated with paintings Pausanias describes: the battle of the Athenians against the Amazons; the battle of the Lapiths and Centaurs, with Theseus already having slain one Centaur while the fight around the others hung in the balance; and Theseus's visit to the sea-god's house, with the ring — the Bacchylides episode, monumentalised (1.17.2–3, paraphrased). The building Pausanias walked through has not been certainly identified: the handsome Doric temple above the Agora that tourists long called the Theseion is the Hephaisteion, the temple of Hephaestus and Athena Ergane — its Theseus metopes (Section IV) earned it the hero's name in modern error — and the real Theseion, sanctuary rather than temple in the sources, awaits certain identification in the area Pausanias indicates. The confusion is itself a small parable of the hero's afterlife: his name adhered, for centuries, to the most conspicuous available building.

The chief sacrifice in his honour, Plutarch says, fell on the eighth day of the month Pyanepsion — the day, by the aetiology, of his return from Crete with the youths — and the Athenians honoured him on the eighth day of the other months too, either because he first came to Athens from Troezen on the eighth of Hecatombaeon, or because the number befitted a son of Poseidon, who is honoured on the eighth of every month (Theseus 36.3–4, paraphrased). Around that day clustered the Theseia, the hero's great festival: processions and sacrifices, a torch-race, gymnastic contests, competitions in horsemanship and in the military bearing the Greeks called euandria, and the funeral honours for Athens' war dead, the Epitaphia, which the calendar had attached to the founder's feast — the whole set within the same Pyanepsion season as the Oschophoria and Pyanepsia of Section X, so that the return-voyage, the founder, and the city's own fallen were commemorated in one autumnal complex. Inscriptional accounts of the fourth century BCE show the Theseia funded on a scale exceeding the Lesser Panathenaia of the year before — this was no antiquarian survival but one of the city's major festivals, still being elaborated, with ephebic contests and processions, in the Hellenistic age. The festival of Connidas's ram (Section III), the sacrifice to the Amazons before the Theseia (Section XIII), the herald's crowned staff at the Oschophoria: the calendar is the hero's biography rewritten as a city's year.

The Democratic Patron — and the Verdict on the Making

What Athens finally made of Theseus can be seen in one painting and one custom. The painting, on the wall of a building behind the Stoa of the King Archon, showed — Pausanias reports — Theseus, and with him Demokratia and Demos, Democracy and the People personified; and the painting's meaning, Pausanias says, was that Theseus was the man who instituted political equality for the Athenians, for a story had become current among the many that Theseus handed over the government to the people and that Athens was a democracy from his time until the tyranny of Peisistratus (1.3.3, paraphrased). The custom is the Theseion's asylum (above): the founder's tomb as the one place in the city where a runaway slave could not be seized. Between the painting and the asylum stands the complete civic construction — Theseus the democrat by doctrine, Theseus the poor man's protector by cult — and it was built, on the evidence assembled in this article, between about 520 and about 460 BCE, by vase-painters, tragedians, festival-founders, one ambitious general, and a people who needed their unprecedented present to have an ancient warrant. Burkert's framework for hero-cult gives the construction its religious grammar: the hero is a dead man whose power persists at his tomb, and a city that possesses the tomb possesses the power. Walker's argument gives it its political chronology. Gantz's testimonia give it its documentary spine. The three do not cancel one another; they are the same process described at the levels of cult, of politics, and of text.

It remains to say what this article's three layers finally amount to. The myth as told (a) is a magnificent, incoherent, multiply-authored story — tokens and monsters, a thread and a black sail, a founder who unifies a nation and a father who kills his son — no part of which can be shown to record an event. The civic footing (b) is solid and datable: a hero promoted in art from c. 570 BCE, given a labour-cycle c. 520, staged as democracy's founder in the 420s, seen at Marathon in 490, and physically installed in his city, bones and all, in 476/5. The later readings (c) — Evans's palace, the philosophers' ship, the Amazonomachy as Persian allegory — are the story continuing to do its work on new materials. Athens set out to make a hero who would make Athens intelligible to itself, and succeeded so completely that the making is still visible, which is the rarest thing in myth. The ship's timbers were replaced one by one, and the Athenians, who knew exactly what they were doing, called it the same ship. They were right in the only sense they ever claimed: it was the ship they kept. Theseus is the hero they kept.
