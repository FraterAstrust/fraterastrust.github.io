Halicarnassus · Lydia · Chaeronea · c. 440 BCE – c. 180 CE

Almost every cult fact in this hub — every rite described, every image identified, every local legend attached to a place — reaches us through prose, and most of it through three prose writers: Herodotus, who asked where the gods came from; Pausanias, who walked Greece and wrote down what stood there and what was said about it; and Plutarch, who tried to make the myths philosophically respectable. This article is their source-critical home: what each man was doing, how each man's evidence works, and why the hub trusts each of them for different things.

I. What “Systematizer” Means Here

The poets came first, and this hub has treated them as texts in their own right: Homer in The Homeric Epics, Hesiod in Theogony and the Succession Myth. But a poem is not the only machine antiquity built for organising myth. From the fifth century BCE onward, prose took up the same material and did different work with it. Prose could ask questions a hexameter could not: Who told you this? Did you see it yourself? What stands on the spot now? What does the story mean? The three writers of this article are the three great prose answers to those questions, and “systematizer” is this hub's word for what they share: each of them took the inherited mass of Greek myth — local, contradictory, unowned — and forced it into a system, an ordered whole with a principle of arrangement that a reader can inspect.

The systems differ, and the difference is the article's subject. Herodotus organised myth by inquiry (historiē): he travelled, asked, compared peoples, and reported what was said, arranging the gods themselves into a history of transmission — who had them first, who borrowed them, who named them. Pausanias organised myth by place (periēgēsis, a “leading around”): he walked mainland Greece sanctuary by sanctuary and city by city, and pinned each story to the altar, image, tomb, or spring where it was told. Plutarch organised myth by person and meaning: in the Lives he fitted the legendary founders into biography beside documented statesmen, and in the essays he asked what the stories signify — the question that makes him the ancestor of every allegorist this hub treats in Wing II.

HERODOTUS · INQUIRY

The organising question is whence? Myth becomes evidence in an investigation: reports are collected, compared, and sometimes judged. The system is a history of peoples and their customs, in which the gods have a transmission history like a trade good.

PAUSANIAS · PERIEGESIS

The organising question is where, and what is there? Myth becomes topography: every story is anchored to a visible object or a performed rite at a named place. The system is a map of Greece made of sanctuaries.

PLUTARCH · BIOGRAPHY AND PHILOSOPHY

The organising questions are what was he like? and what does it mean? Myth becomes character in the Lives and doctrine in the essays. The system is a moral and metaphysical order, into which the old stories must be made to fit — or be explained until they do.

WHY THE HUB NEEDS ALL THREE

Between them the three cover the whole life of a Greek cult fact: its earliest surviving report (often Herodotus), its state on the ground in the high Roman period (Pausanias), and its philosophical afterlife (Plutarch). No one of them can substitute for the others, and Section XVI states the hub's rules for keeping their evidence apart.

A fourth prose systematizer stands behind all three in this hub's architecture and has an article of his own in this same phase of building: Apollodorus, whose Library organised myth by genealogy into the handbook every later writer plundered — see Apollodorus and the Library. The difference is instructive. Apollodorus systematised the stories; Herodotus, Pausanias, and Plutarch systematised the evidence — testimony, place, and interpretation. That is why they, and not the handbook tradition, carry the weight of this hub's cult claims, and why they get the source-critical treatment here. A name-mention count across the hub's files makes the dependence plain: Pausanias is named some 135 times, Herodotus some 130, and Plutarch structures the whole of Theseus. An archive that leans that hard on three witnesses owes its readers an account of the witnesses.

// QUOTATIONS IN THIS ARTICLE //

Godley's Loeb Herodotus, George Rawlinson's Herodotus, and Bernadotte Perrin's Loeb Plutarch — and are identified at the point of quotation. Everything else that renders an ancient author's sense is paraphrase, and is written as paraphrase. References of the form “2.53” are to book and chapter of the Histories; Pausanias is cited by book, chapter, and section; Plutarch's essays by their Latin titles and, for On Isis and Osiris, by the standard Moralia pagination (351C–384C).

One caution governs everything that follows, and it is the hub's standing discipline applied to its own favourite sources. None of the three is a neutral recording instrument. Herodotus has a thesis about Egypt. Pausanias has a canon of what counts as worth recording, and it excludes most of the living Roman present. Plutarch has a philosophy, and he bends stories toward it openly. To use them well is not to strip those agendas away — they cannot be stripped — but to know, for each claim, which agenda produced it and what the claim is therefore evidence of. A Herodotean sentence is excellent evidence for what a fifth-century inquirer could be told. A Pausanian sentence is excellent evidence for what stood at a place in the second century CE and what its custodians said. A Plutarchan sentence is excellent evidence for what an educated Platonist priest could make of a myth around 100 CE. Confusing those three evidentiary values is the single commonest error in popular writing on Greek religion, and this article exists to make it harder to commit here.

II. Three Writers, Three Centuries, Three Projects

The three were not contemporaries, collaborators, or even readers of one another in any simple line. Six centuries separate the first from the last, and the Greek world each wrote in was a different world.

c. 484 – c. 425 BCE

HERODOTUS OF HALICARNASSUS

A Greek of a mixed Greek-Carian city on the Anatolian coast, writing in the generation after the Persian Wars, in a world of independent city-states. His Histories — the inquiry into the wars between Greeks and barbarians — ranges over the whole known world and treats Egypt, Babylon, Scythia, and Libya as fully as Greece. He is the earliest of the three by half a millennium, and for many cults his is the earliest prose testimony that survives at all.

fl. c. 150 – 180 CE

PAUSANIAS OF LYDIA

A Greek of Roman Asia Minor — probably from the region of Magnesia under Sipylus, an inference from his intimate knowledge of Lydia rather than a stated fact — writing under the Antonine emperors, when Greece was a museum- province of the Roman Empire. His Periegesis (Description of Greece) in ten books walks the mainland in the age of Hadrian's and Herodes Atticus' building programmes, and systematically prefers the old Greece to the new.

c. 46 – after 119 CE

PLUTARCH OF CHAERONEA

A Greek of a small Boeotian town, educated at Athens, widely travelled, a priest of Apollo at Delphi, writing under Trajan and Hadrian — a generation or two before Pausanias, whom he does not appear to have known. His Parallel Lives pair Greek with Roman statesmen from Theseus to the early Empire; his essays (the Moralia) range over philosophy, religion, and antiquarian lore. He is the only one of the three who is himself a cult official, and it shows.

The order of this article is chronological, but the order of dependence in the hub runs the other way. When a cult is described in this archive — the rites at Eleusis in The Mysteries, the images of the Olympians, the hero-shrines of Theseus — the description on the ground is usually Pausanias'; the earliest check on it is Herodotus'; and the interpretation layered over it in later antiquity is usually Plutarch's. Reading the three in chronological order, as this article does, is reading the evidence in the order it was produced; using them in the hub means running the film backward, from the latest fullest description to the earliest testimony, and noticing what each stage added.

Each of the next three parts of the article takes one writer in three movements: the man and the shape of his book; his method, stated in his own practice; and the characteristic failure mode a reader must discount. The comparison, the hub's rules of use, and the editions follow.

III. Herodotus: The Inquirer and His Book

Herodotus opens his work by naming himself, his city, and his purpose: the book is a historiēs apodexis, a “display of inquiry”, meant — in paraphrase of his famous first sentence — to keep human deeds from being erased by time and to explain the cause of the war between Greeks and barbarians. The word historiē is the programme. It does not yet mean “history” in the modern disciplinary sense; it means asking and finding out, and the inquirer (histōr) was originally a man who knows because he has asked and seen. Everything in the nine books — the division into nine, named for the Muses, is later editorial work, not his — is the record of an inquiry: wars, yes, but also rivers, customs, animal lore, king-lists, temple inventories, and the origins of the gods.

The structure matters for the myth-reader. The Histories is built of logoi — accounts, each attached to a people or a place: the Lydian logos, the Persian logos, and, longest and most consequential for this hub, the Egyptian logos of Book 2, a self-contained treatise on Egypt's land, customs, kings, and religion that runs to nearly a fifth of the whole work. Myth enters Herodotus not as narrative ornament but as data inside logoi: when he reports what the Egyptians say about Heracles, or what the priestesses of Dodona say about the doves, he is filing testimony. His myth-material is therefore always attributed — someone, somewhere, says this — and the attribution is part of the evidence. This is a different thing from Hesiod's inspired catalogue or Homer's Muse-given song, and it is the birth of the source-critical habit this hub tries to practise.

His travels were real and wide — Egypt up the Nile to Elephantine by his own account (2.29), Babylon, the Black Sea coast, Tyre, Cyrene — though the modern debate about how much he saw, and how much he took from books and talk, is genuine and is flagged in Section V. His informants were typically the people a Greek traveller could actually reach: priests through interpreters, temple staff, local notables, other travellers. He names classes of sources more often than individuals: “the priests of Hephaestus at Memphis”, “the priestesses at Dodona”, “the Egyptians say”. A reader who keeps asking who could have told him this, and what did they want him to believe? is reading Herodotus the way Herodotus asks to be read — because he asks exactly that question about his own informants, repeatedly, in the text.

THE SHAPE OF THE HISTORIES

Book 1: Lydia, Persia's rise, Croesus. Book 2: Egypt. Book 3: Persia and the conquest of Egypt. Book 4: Scythia and Libya. Book 5–6: the Ionian revolt and Marathon. Books 7–9: Xerxes' invasion, Thermopylae, Salamis, Plataea. The war narrative is the spine; the ethnographic logoi are the ribs, and the ribs carry most of the myth.

THE MAN

Born at Halicarnassus in Caria, a Dorian Greek city under Persian rule; exiled in the city's politics; travelled the eastern Mediterranean; associated in later life with the Athenian colony of Thurii in Italy, where he probably died. The biographical tradition about him is late and unreliable in detail; the book is the man we have.

THE REPUTATION

Cicero called him the father of history — and in the same breath noted the fables. Later antiquity split: admirers of his inquiry against critics, Plutarch among them, who wrote a whole essay On the Malice of Herodotus accusing him of bias. Both verdicts are in the record from early on; the modern scholarly verdict (Section V) is a argued middle: a genuine inquirer working with the informants and the critical tools his century had.

IV. Book 2: The Egyptian Logos and the Gods from Egypt

Book 2 is the reason Herodotus is a founding source for this hub, and its central claim is the boldest in all Greek prose about religion. Having described Egypt's land and customs, Herodotus turns to its gods and states, as the conclusion of his inquiries, that the names of nearly all the Greek gods came to Greece from Egypt (2.50). In paraphrase of Godley's Loeb rendering: he holds it proven that the names came from the barbarians, and from Egypt above all; the Egyptians, he says, were the first of mankind to use divine names, to build altars, images, and temples, and to carve figures in stone (2.4, 2.50). Greek religion, on this account, is a reception — an imported system, naturalised so long ago that the Greeks had forgotten the import. It is the first surviving statement of the diffusionist thesis about Greek myth, and every later version of that thesis — including the Egyptology hub's careful, evidence- tiered version of it — is written in its shadow.

The claim is built up in stages through the book, and the stages are worth separating, because they do not all stand at the same strength.

The Stages of the Argument

The nameless gods of the Pelasgians (2.52). Before the names came, Herodotus says, the Pelasgians — his name for the pre-Greek or earliest Greek population — sacrificed to gods they called simply theoi, “gods”, without surnames or personal names, because they had not yet heard any. The word itself he derives, in a characteristic folk etymology, from the gods having “set all things in order”. The named, personalised Olympian system is thus, for Herodotus, a second stage in Greek religion — and the names that define it are the import.

Heracles, the test case (2.43–44). Herodotus argues at length that the Greek hero Heracles, son of Amphitryon, must be distinguished from an ancient god Heracles whom the Egyptians reckoned among their twelve gods — and that the Greeks took the name from Egypt and applied it to the hero. He clinches the argument, in his own presentation, by travel: he went to Tyre in Phoenicia, where there is a famous temple of Heracles, and was told the temple was founded when Tyre was, some 2,300 years before his visit; and he saw a second Tyrian temple of “Thasian Heracles”, and went to Thasos too, where the Phoenicians had founded a Heracles cult five generations before the Greek hero's birth. Whether or not the argument persuades a modern reader — the Egyptian god behind it is presumably Khonsu or Shu, identified with Heracles by interpretatio graeca — its form is remarkable: a theological claim tested by autopsy at three sites. This is inquiry doing the work that myth used to do.

Dionysus and the rites (2.49). The worship of Dionysus, Herodotus says, came to Greece later than the other gods' names, introduced — as he reconstructs it — by Melampus, who learned the rites from Cadmus of Tyre and the Phoenicians, who had themselves received them from Egypt; the phallic processions of Dionysus are his exhibit. And at 2.51 the ithyphallic images of Hermes are traced not to Egypt but to the Pelasgians, with the explanation lodged in the mysteries of Samothrace — a reminder that Herodotus' diffusionism is not a single-source dogma: different elements have different routes, and he tracks them separately.

The mysteries and the silences (2.171). Book 2 also gives this hub one of its most-quoted Herodotean habits: the reverent refusal. Describing the rites at Saïs for the god he will not name in that connection — Osiris — Herodotus says, in paraphrase, that although he knows the details he will keep a pious silence about them; and on the rites the Greeks call Thesmophoria he will say only that they were brought from Egypt by the daughters of Danaus, and no more. The silence is itself evidence of two things: that mystery cult imposed real reticence even on a foreign inquirer, and that Herodotus observed it — a datum the Mysteries article leans on when it weighs what can and cannot be known about Eleusis.

// READING THE EGYPTIAN CLAIM TODAY //

Herodotus' thesis that Greece's gods came from Egypt is presented in this hub as Herodotus' thesis — a fifth-century Greek construction built from priestly informants, interpretatio (the Greek habit of matching foreign gods to Greek names), and genuine observation of Egypt's vastly older temple culture. Modern scholarship accepts real Egyptian and Near Eastern influence on Greek religion in specific, demonstrable channels, and rejects the wholesale derivation of the Olympian names. The Egyptology hub's treatment of the same encounter — including its adjudication of the Osiris material in Osiris, Section XIX — is written from the Egyptian evidence outward, and the two hubs' accounts should be read as complements, not duplicates: Herodotus tells us what a Greek could conclude; the Egyptian record tells us what was there.

One more feature of Book 2 deserves emphasis, because it is the seed of this whole article. At 2.3 Herodotus remarks — again in paraphrase — that all men know equally about the gods: which is to say, nobody's theology is knowledge. The Egyptian priests have older records, longer king-lists (2.142 counts 341 generations of kings and priests, more than eleven thousand years), and older temples; but on the nature of the gods themselves, Egyptian and Greek stand in the same ignorance, and what each people has is what is said. That levelling remark is the philosophical ground of the discipline examined next.

V. “I Report What Is Said”: Herodotus' Discipline and Its Limits

Herodotus states his rule of evidence twice in programmatic places, and the two statements are not identical — the gap between them is where his method lives. At 2.123, closing an Egyptian account of the soul's transmigration, he remarks, in paraphrase of the standard renderings, that whoever finds such stories credible may believe them; for his own part, throughout the whole work, he keeps to the rule of recording what is said by each party. At 7.152, in the middle of the war narrative, he states the rule at full strength, and this article quotes it in Rawlinson's translation:

“For myself, my duty is to report all that is said; but I am not obliged to believe it all alike — a remark which may be understood to apply to my whole History.” — HERODOTUS, HISTORIES 7.152, TRANS. GEORGE RAWLINSON (1862)

The rule sounds like abdication — report everything, judge nothing — but Herodotus' practice is better than his formula. In the text he judges constantly: he marks stories he disbelieves (“the Greeks tell many things without due investigation, among them this silly tale”, of Heracles in Egypt, 2.45 — paraphrase); he gives rival versions side by side and sometimes declares for one; he tests claims against geography, chronology, and probability, most famously doubting the Phoenician circumnavigation of Africa on every point except the one detail — the sun on their right hand — that a modern reader recognises as the strongest proof they really did it (4.42). The discipline is therefore better described as a two-tier system: the record is comprehensive and attributed; the judgement is intermittent, personal, and flagged when it occurs. For a myth-source this is nearly ideal. The hub can take the report as testimony — this was said, by these people, at this date — without inheriting the inquirer's verdicts, and can take his verdicts as a fifth-century critical intelligence at work, without mistaking them for facts.

The Limits

The limits are equally definite, and the article names them so that the hub's citations can be discounted correctly.

Informants. Herodotus worked through interpreters and through the class of Egyptians who talked to Greeks. His Egyptian priests were real, but they were also performing Egypt for a Greek audience — a performance with its own antiquarian pride and its own inventions, as the Egyptology hub's source criticism shows in detail. When Herodotus reports that the Egyptians told him a thing, the claim that survives scrutiny is the telling.

Interpretatio. His comparative method requires foreign gods to wear Greek names — the Egyptian god at Sais is “Athena”, the Theban god is “Zeus” — and the equations are his or his informants' Greek grid laid over foreign material. The grid is evidence for Greek religious thought; it is weak evidence for the foreign cult underneath it, which is why this hub cross-checks every Herodotean Egyptian claim against the Egyptian record before using it.

Chronology. Herodotus believes in deep Egyptian time and reports its figures faithfully — and the figures are priestly constructions, not records. His Greek chronology, built from genealogies counted at three generations to the century, is a rational instrument applied to legendary material: it produces dates that look like history for persons who belong to myth. The hub therefore never cites a Herodotean date for a mythic event as a date; it cites it as Herodotus' computation.

The source-citation debate. Modern scholarship has fought over how far the citations themselves can be trusted. Detlev Fehling's Herodotus and His ‘Sources’ (German 1971; English 1989) argued that the source attributions follow literary patterns so regular that they should be read as a fiction of the genre rather than as records of real inquiries. W. Kendrick Pritchett's The Liar School of Herodotos (1993) answered with a point-by-point defence, marshalling the archaeological and epigraphic confirmations of Herodotean reports. The dispute is not settled, and this hub's position is the working middle the evidence supports: Herodotus travelled and inquired genuinely, and also shaped, compressed, and on occasion ventriloquised his authorities, as every ancient historian did. Citations in this archive therefore read “Herodotus reports that the Egyptians say”, never simply “the Egyptians held”.

VI. Histories 2.53: Canon-Formation in One Sentence

The most consequential single sentence Herodotus wrote about religion stands at 2.53, at the end of the Egyptian argument. The Greeks, he has been saying, learned the gods' names from Egypt only recently, as human affairs go — and then he says who it was that turned the imported names into the Greek pantheon the world knows. This article quotes the sentence in A. D. Godley's Loeb translation:

“For Hesiod and Homer I suppose were four hundred years before my time and not more, and these are they who made a theogony for the Hellenes and gave the titles to the gods and distributed to them honours and arts, and set forth their forms.” — HERODOTUS, HISTORIES 2.53, TRANS. A. D. GODLEY (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY, 1920)

Read it slowly. Herodotus does not say that Homer and Hesiod recorded the theogony, or received it. He says they made it — poiēsantes theogoniēn, the verb of the poiētēs, the poet as maker. In his account, the Greek divine order — who the gods are, what they are called, which honours and crafts belong to each, what they look like — is a literary artefact, assembled by two poets at a datable moment, four hundred years and not more before his own time. The gods are older than the poems; Herodotus has spent Book 2 arguing their names are Egyptian and immemorial. But the system — the pantheon as a cast of named, titled, specialised, visualised persons — is the poets' work. This is, so far as surviving texts go, the first statement in European literature that a religion's canonical form was authored.

Herodotus immediately draws the corollary, and it is as sharp as the claim. The poets said to be earlier than Homer and Hesiod — Orpheus and Musaeus and their kind — were, in his opinion, actually later (2.53.3). Antiquity of attribution is not antiquity of fact; a poem travelling under an immemorial name may be a recent composition wearing it. The sentence at 2.53 thus does two pieces of canon-critical work at once: it dates the canonisation (the two poets, four centuries back), and it supplies the test by which pretenders to greater antiquity are detected and redated. This hub's own treatment of the Orphic material, and of the Homeric Hymns' travelling attribution, runs on exactly this Herodotean track.

For the hub's method, 2.53 is the charter text, and it cuts in two directions. In one direction it licenses the archive's whole procedure: if the pantheon's canonical form is a made thing with makers and a date, then it can be studied as a made thing — its seams found, its sources traced, its local variants distinguished from the Panhellenic overlay the poets spread over them, as the Olympians article does throughout. In the other direction it warns: what Homer and Hesiod made was a theogony for the Hellenes — a Panhellenic literary system — and cult on the ground, in the sanctuaries Pausanias walked, was always older, stranger, and more local than the system. The gap between the poets' pantheon and the practised religion is precisely the gap this hub exists to map, and Herodotus, in one sentence, is the first writer to mark both its sides.

// HOW THE HUB CITES HERODOTUS //

Herodotus is cited for early testimony and reception: what was being said about a god or rite by the fifth century BCE, and what a critical Greek intelligence of that century made of it. He is not cited as an authority on Egyptian religion in himself — for that, the Egyptology hub's record governs — nor for the state of any Greek cult in periods after his own. A Herodotean myth-report is evidence with a date, a place, and an informant-class attached; the hub keeps all three attached.

VII. Pausanias: The Periegete and His Ten Books

Pausanias wrote one work, and it is unlike anything else that survives from antiquity. The Periegesis Hellados — Description of Greece, or more exactly a “leading-around of Greece” — is a ten-book account of mainland Greece organised as a journey: it begins at Cape Sunium, comes up to Athens, and proceeds region by region through the Peloponnese and central Greece, ending at Delphi. At every stop it does the same three things: it describes what stands there (temples, images, altars, tombs, paintings, inscriptions); it tells what is said there (the local myths, hero-legends, and historical memories attached to the objects); and it records what is done there (the rites, festivals, and customs still performed). No other ancient text attempts the whole of a country's sacred topography in this way, and none of the partial parallels survives. Pausanias is, quite literally, irreplaceable: for hundreds of cults, images, and local myths he is the only witness there will ever be.

The scope is as deliberate as the method. Pausanias covers the mainland — the Greece of the classical city-states — and not the islands, not Crete, not the Greek cities of Asia Minor from which he himself came. The omission is not an accident of survival; the work as transmitted is complete in its design, and the design is a statement: the Greece that matters is the old Greece, the landscape in which the myths happened. Within that scope he is guided by a selection principle he states in his own way throughout — he records, as he repeatedly puts it in paraphrase, the things most worth telling (logou axia), and passes over the rest without apology. What counts as worth telling is the subject of Section IX; the method by which the telling was produced is the subject of the next section.

Of the man himself almost nothing is known outside the book — no ancient biography, no portrait, no certain date. The work's internal references place its composition across roughly the middle decades of the second century CE, with material gathered over years of travel; he mentions seeing places far beyond Greece — Babylon's ruins, Jerusalem, Rome, Egypt — in passing, always in service of a comparison. The anonymity is fitting. Pausanias effaces himself more completely than almost any ancient author: no proem survives in which he states his programme at length, no first-person manifesto like Herodotus' opening or Plutarch's proems. The system must be inferred from the practice, and the practice is consistent enough — across ten books and some thousands of entries — to infer it with confidence.

VIII. Pausanias' Method: Autopsy, Guides, and the Exegesis of Images

Pausanias' method has three working parts, and this hub's confidence in him rests on all three being visible in the text, operating entry by entry.

1 · Autopsy: He Went and Looked

The first-person verbs of seeing run through the work like a watermark: I saw, it still stands, in my time. Pausanias measures with his eyes — he gives the relative sizes of images, the materials, the state of preservation, the order of monuments along a road — and he corrects predecessors against the ground. Where a thing he expected is absent, ruined, or different, he says so. This is the quality that made the nineteenth-century excavators treat him as a colleague (Section X): his descriptions are checkable, and they were checked, and the checking is the foundation of his modern reputation. The hub's rule follows from it: a Pausanian description of an object's existence, material, and location is the strongest class of literary evidence this archive handles.

2 · Local Informants: Guides, Priests, Exegetes

What Pausanias could not see, he asked about on the spot. His named classes of informant are the local ones: the exegetai — the official or semi-official explainers attached to sanctuaries, whose job was precisely to expound the images and rites to visitors — priests and temple staff, guides, and “the people of the place” whose account he introduces with formulas like “the Corinthians say”. The localness is the value. A story Pausanias files from Lycosura is the story Lycosura told about itself in the second century CE, attached to the image Lycosura showed; it is not a Panhellenic handbook's smoothing. This hub's articles repeatedly prefer Pausanias' local version to Apollodorus' systematised one for exactly this reason (see Apollodorus and the Library on the contrast), and repeatedly note where the local version contradicts the literary canon — the contradiction is the signature of genuine local tradition.

He is not a passive recorder of his informants. He reports rival local accounts side by side; he occasionally marks a story as one he does not believe, or as one he accepts only because of its antiquity; and on the most sensitive material — the content of mystery rites — he maintains the same reverent silence as Herodotus, most famously at Eleusis, where he relates, in paraphrase, that a dream forbade him to describe what lies within the sanctuary wall, and that the uninitiated are in any case barred from learning what they are barred from seeing (1.38.7). The silence is methodological gold: it shows a witness who knows the difference between what he may report and what he may not, which is the precondition of trusting what he does report.

3 · Exegesis: The Object Expounded

The distinctively Pausanian act is the exegesis of the object: he stands before an image, a relief, a painting, and expounds it figure by figure, identifying each personage and telling the myth that explains the grouping. The great set-pieces are famous: the Chest of Cypselus at Olympia (5.17–19), a cedar chest covered in carved and inlaid mythological scenes, which he describes band by band; the throne of Apollo at Amyclae (3.18–19), a sculptural programme wrapped around an archaic cult image; and above all the paintings of Polygnotus in the Lesche of the Cnidians at Delphi (10.25–31) — the sack of Troy and the underworld, expounded figure by figure for chapters, our fullest surviving encounter with classical painting. In these passages Pausanias is doing what the sanctuary exegetes did, at literary length and with a scholar's cross-references: reading images as texts. For a hub whose subject is the meeting of myth and cult object, this is the single most useful ancient skill, and Pausanias is its only surviving master-practitioner.

// WHY THE HUB NAMES HIM THE MODEL CULT WITNESS //

The hub's standard for a cult claim is: a named place, a visible object or performed rite, a local account attached to it, and a witness who distinguishes what he saw from what he was told. That standard is not an invention of this archive. It is Pausanias' own practice, generalised. Where a claim in these pages rests on testimony of the Pausanian type, it is labelled as resting on it; where it rests on weaker testimony — a poet's allusion, a scholiast, a late allegorist — it is labelled with that weaker class instead. The labelling system is Section XVI's subject.

IX. Pausanias' Silences and the Archaising Agenda

A witness is defined as much by what he will not record as by what he will. Pausanias' silences are systematic, and the modern scholarship — Christian Habicht's Pausanias' Guide to Ancient Greece (1985) is the landmark statement, with John Elsner's reading of the Periegesis as a work of Greek pilgrimage under Rome (1992) and Maria Pretzler's studies developing the picture — has taught us to read them as an agenda, not as accidents.

The preference for the old. Habicht's summary, paraphrased here, is the standard one: Pausanias prefers the old to the new and the sacred to the profane; there is far more about classical than about contemporary art, far more about temples and images than about the buildings of civic life. The omissions can be startling: the Stoa of Attalus in the Athenian Agora — a vast royal building, rebuilt in modern times from its ancient remains — is not mentioned; the great exedra of Herodes Atticus at Olympia, his own contemporary's monument, is passed over. Roman Greece, the Greece of his own lifetime, enters the work mainly where it touches the old sanctuaries: an emperor's dedication, a Roman's restoration. A reader who used Pausanias as a guide to second-century Greece would misunderstand the century; he is a guide to what second-century Greece preserved of its past, curated by a man who believed the past was the point.

The religious silence. The Greece Pausanias walks was a religiously plural country — Jews, Christians, the cults of Isis and Mithras, the imperial cult in full operation. Of most of this his work says almost nothing: Christianity is effectively invisible in it, and foreign cults appear, when they appear, as antiquarian data at the margins of Greek places. The silence is of a piece with the archaism: the Periegesis constructs a Greece whose religion is the ancestral Greek one, whole and continuous, at the very moment when that wholeness was a cultural project rather than a demographic fact. Scholars of the Second Sophistic — the great archaizing movement of Greek letters under the Empire — place Pausanias squarely in it: his Atticising restraint, his classical canon, his whole enterprise are the literary form of Greek identity maintained under Roman rule.

The consequence for the hub. Pausanias' silences do not weaken his testimony about what he records; they discipline our use of his absences. That Pausanias does not mention a cult at a place is evidence that the cult was not, in his judgement, among the things most worth telling about that place — it is not evidence that the cult was not there. This archive therefore never argues from Pausanias' silence alone, and it treats his Greece as what it is: the finest surviving map of the Greek sacred past as a second-century Greek patriot chose to preserve it — which is to say, a map drawn by a man with a thesis, whose thesis we know.

// THE OLDER VERDICT, AND ITS REVERSAL //

It was not always so. Nineteenth-century scholarship was divided about Pausanias, and an influential line of criticism — associated above all with Wilamowitz — held him a derivative compiler who copied earlier periegetic literature and had seen less than he claimed. The excavations (Section X) and Habicht's systematic re-examination reversed the verdict: the consensus of current scholarship is that Pausanias travelled, saw, and recorded with a care that can be checked and usually confirmed. This article follows the current consensus, and flags it as a consensus — reached, argued, and in principle revisable — rather than as a fact of nature.

X. The Test of the Spade: Olympia and Mycenae

Pausanias is the only one of the three systematizers whose work could be tested against the physical world at scale — and it was. The great age of excavation in Greece, from the 1870s on, was conducted with the Periegesis open on the trench edge, and the results made his reputation. Two cases stand for the whole.

Olympia

The German excavations at Olympia (1875–1881, directed under Ernst Curtius' programme) were undertaken in explicit reliance on Pausanias' Books 5 and 6. The Altis — the sacred precinct whose every temple, altar, treasury, and statue Pausanias had enumerated in walking order — emerged from the Alpheus silt in an arrangement that matched his sequence closely enough for the excavators to identify building after building from his text: the temple of Zeus with its pediments and the chryselephantine image whose base he described; the Heraion; the Metroon; the Philippeion, the round building of Philip of Macedon, where he said it stood. Not every identification held, and Pausanias was not right about everything — but a copyist inventing a sanctuary from books, as the old criticism imagined, could not have produced a text that functioned as a site plan. The excavations at Delphi (the French “Great Excavation” from 1892) repeated the experience on the Sacred Way of Book 10.

Mycenae

At Mycenae (2.16.5–7) Pausanias describes the ruins as the city of Perseus and the Atreids: the circuit wall with the Lion Gate, the underground treasury of Atreus and his sons, and — the crucial detail — the graves of Atreus' house inside the circuit, where Agamemnon and his companions were said to lie, with Clytemnestra and Aegisthus buried outside the wall, as unworthy to lie within. When Heinrich Schliemann excavated Mycenae in 1876, guided by this passage, he found the shaft graves of Grave Circle A exactly where Pausanias' account put burials: inside the wall, by the Lion Gate. It remains the most dramatic vindication of a classical text by a spade.

// WHAT THE VINDICATION DOES AND DOES NOT PROVE //

The shaft graves of Grave Circle A are dated by their contents to roughly the sixteenth century BCE — centuries before any date that can be proposed for a historical Trojan war, and so centuries before any conceivable Agamemnon. Pausanias is therefore vindicated on topography and local memory: graves of great antiquity stood within the walls in his day, were shown to visitors, and carried the Atreid names in local tradition. He is not vindicated on the identification: whose graves they were, no inscription says, and the names are the tradition's, not the graves'. The hub cites the case in exactly that divided form — and notes that the division is itself the Pausanian pattern: what stood there, certain; what it was called, local report; whose it truly was, beyond the witness's reach.

Sir James Frazer's six-volume translation and commentary (1898), the fruit of travelling Greece with the text in hand, consolidated the verdict the excavations had reached, and Habicht's 1985 re-examination closed the argument for the modern period. The hub's assessment, stated once here and applied throughout: Pausanias is a reliable witness to what existed and what was said at Greek sanctuaries in the second century CE; he is a fallible guide to the remoter past those sanctuaries claimed, and he never pretended otherwise. His local myths are recorded as local beliefs of his own time about that past — which is, for the study of Greek religion as actually practised and remembered, the most valuable thing they could be.

// HOW THE HUB CITES PAUSANIAS //

Pausanias is cited for cult fact: the existence, appearance, and location of images, altars, and sanctuaries; the performance of rites; and the content of local tradition at a named place. Claims resting on him are stated as of his period — “at Lycosura in Pausanias' day” — unless earlier evidence carries them further back. His identifications of objects with mythic persons are cited as local tradition, never as archaeology.

XI. Plutarch: The Biographer-Philosopher of Chaeronea

Plutarch is the most various of the three, and the hardest to use well, because he wrote in two genres that handle myth in opposite ways and because his own commitments are written on his sleeve. He was a Platonist philosopher, a civic notable of Chaeronea in Boeotia, a Roman citizen (his Roman name, Lucius Mestrius Plutarchus, records his patron), a traveller who had lectured in Rome and walked the sites of Greece and Egypt's approaches, and — Section XIV's subject — a serving priest of Apollo at Delphi. Two great bodies of work survive: the Parallel Lives and the essays conventionally called the Moralia.

The Lives are arranged in pairs — a Greek set beside a Roman, with a formal comparison (synkrisis) closing most pairs. Twenty-three pairs survive, together with four unpaired lives; the series runs from the frankly legendary — Theseus paired with Romulus, Lycurgus with Numa — through the fully historical statesmen of Greece and Rome. Plutarch states his biographical principle in the Alexander, in paraphrase of Perrin's rendering: he is writing lives, not histories; the most brilliant achievements do not always reveal a man's virtue or vice, whereas a small thing — a phrase, a jest — often discloses character more than battles and sieges. The principle explains the Lives' texture: anecdote, saying, and omen are evidence in them, because the quarry is character, not event. A myth-reader must hold that firmly: when Plutarch tells a story about Theseus, the story is in the text because of what it shows about Theseus — its historical credentials are a separate question, and Plutarch, to his credit, often says so.

The Moralia — some seventy-eight essays and dialogues survive under the heading — range across ethics, education, politics, natural questions, antiquarian lore, and religion. For this hub the load-bearing essays are the religious ones: On Isis and Osiris (Section XIII); the three Delphic dialogues, On the E at Delphi, On the Pythia's Prophecies, and On the Obsolescence of Oracles, which are our fullest inside account of a great oracle in decline; On Superstition, an attack on fear-driven religion from a priest who considered himself orthodox; On the Face in the Moon and the myth that closes it; and the antiquarian Greek Questions and Roman Questions, which preserve dozens of local cult aetiologies in question-and-answer form. Plutarch is also, incidentally, one of Herodotus' sharpest ancient critics: On the Malice of Herodotus prosecutes the earlier systematizer for partiality — a useful reminder that the three witnesses of this article were not a school, and did not spare one another.

PLUTARCH THE SOURCE-COLLECTOR

The Lives of the legendary period are built from named predecessors — historians, chroniclers, and local writers, most of them now lost — whom Plutarch cites, compares, and sometimes contradicts in the open. The Theseus names Hellanicus, Philochorus, and a shelf of Attic and Megarian writers. Plutarch is thus a conduit: much of what he transmits is older than him by centuries, and the hub cites the conduit and the named source together wherever he gives one.

PLUTARCH THE RATIONALISER

Where a tale offends probability, Plutarch offers the rationalised version beside the marvellous one. In the Theseus, the Minotaur is glossed — on a tradition he attributes to Philochorus — as a man: Taurus, a general of Minos, defeated by Theseus in wrestling at the Cretan games. The gloss is euhemerising in method: the monster dissolves into a misreported man. Plutarch retails such glosses without always endorsing them; the hub reports them as Plutarchan reception, never as the myth's meaning.

PLUTARCH THE ALLEGORIST

Where a tale offends philosophy, Plutarch interprets: the story is a veil, and under it lies physics or metaphysics. This is the dominant method of On Isis and Osiris and the seed of the whole later allegorical tradition treated in this hub's Alchemical Allegory. The hub's rule for it is absolute and is stated in Section XVI: an allegorical reading is presented as its author's reading, dated to its author, and never back-projected into the myth's origin.

XII. The Theseus Proem: Fable Shading into History

The Life of Theseus opens with the most famous methodological proem in ancient biography, and this hub's Theseus article is built on the Life it introduces. Plutarch knows he is crossing a border — backward in time, from the documented statesmen of the earlier Lives to a hero of the age of fable — and he marks the crossing with a cartographer's image. This article quotes the proem in Bernadotte Perrin's Loeb translation (1914):

“Just as geographers, O Socius Senecio, crowd on to the outer edges of their maps the parts of the earth which elude their knowledge, with explanatory notes that ‘What lies beyond is sandy desert without water and full of wild beasts,’ or ‘blind marsh,’ or ‘Scythian cold,’ or ‘frozen sea,’ so in the writing of my Parallel Lives, now that I have traversed those periods of time which are accessible to probable reasoning and which afford basis for a history dealing with facts, I might well say of the earlier periods ‘What lies beyond is full of marvels and unreality, a land of poets and fabulists, of doubt and obscurity.’” — PLUTARCH, THESEUS 1.1, TRANS. BERNADOTTE PERRIN (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY, 1914)

And then — the turn that makes the proem a programme rather than a disclaimer — he crosses anyway. Having published Lycurgus and Numa, he writes, he thought he might “not unreasonably go back still farther to Romulus”, and pair him with Theseus, the founder of Athens against the father of Rome. The hope for the enterprise follows, in Perrin's rendering: “May I therefore succeed in purifying Fable, making her submit to reason and take on the semblance of History. But where she obstinately disdains to make herself credible, and refuses to admit any element of probability, I shall pray for kindly readers, and such as receive with indulgence the tales of antiquity.” (Theseus 1.3.)

Every element of Plutarch's handling of myth is in those two passages. There is a map: a known country of probable reasoning and fact, and a beyond of marvel and obscurity, and the border between them is drawn by the historian's judgement, not by the material's nature. There is a method for the beyond: purification — Fable is to be made to submit to reason, which in practice means the operations of Section XI: pruning the impossible, preferring the version that coheres, rationalising the residue. And there is an appeal: where purification fails, the reader is asked for indulgence toward antiquity — the tales are to be kept, even where they cannot be believed, because they are the inheritance. The hub's Theseus article follows this proem's own discipline: Plutarch's narrative is its spine, his named sources are checked where they can be, his rationalisations are labelled as his, and the marvels he could not purify are reported as marvels, with his indulgence-appeal noted and not silently granted.

It should also be said plainly what the proem concedes. On Plutarch's own showing, the Theseus is not history and does not become history by being written soberly: it is fable treated historically, by a writer who tells the reader exactly what he is doing. That candour is why the hub can build on him. A source that announces its own evidentiary status — here be marvels; here I have rationalised; here my authorities disagree — can be used with precision. The danger in Plutarch is never deception. It is seduction: the purified version reads so much more sensibly than the fable that a reader forgets it was manufactured in the purification. The hub's labelling exists to prevent exactly that forgetting.

XIII. On Isis and Osiris: Egypt Through Platonic Eyes

If the Theseus is Plutarch's charter for myth-history, On Isis and Osiris (De Iside et Osiride, Moralia 351C–384C) is his masterpiece of myth-interpretation — and the single most influential ancient text on Egyptian religion ever written in Greek. It is addressed to Clea, a woman of Plutarch's circle who held priestly office at Delphi and was herself devoted to Isis; and it gives, in its early chapters, the fullest connected Greek narrative of the Osiris myth that survives: the conspiracy of Typhon (the Greek name Plutarch uses for Set), the coffin floated down the Nile, Isis' search and the episode at Byblos, the dismemberment of the body into fourteen parts, Isis' recovery and reassembly of the parts, and the posthumous conception of Horus. For many details of that narrative Plutarch is the only ancient source, and the Egyptology hub's Osiris article, Section XIX, gives the Egyptian-side adjudication of his account in full; the Isis article likewise tests his famous report of the veiled inscription at Saïs against the record. What belongs here is the method of the essay, because the method is the systematizing.

Plutarch proceeds by layers of interpretation, and he is explicit that they are layers. He rehearses the euhemeristic reading — the gods were ancient kings and benefactors, Osiris a civilising monarch murdered by a rival — and finds it insufficient. He rehearses the daemonological reading — Osiris and Typhon as great daimones, powers intermediate between gods and men — and gives it more room. He rehearses the physical allegory: Osiris as the Nile and the principle of moisture and generation, Typhon as the sea, the drought, the scorching wind that destroys it; Isis as the receptive earth of Egypt. And he crowns the series with his own Platonic reading, in which Osiris is the intelligible logos, the divine reason and form; Isis is the receptacle of the Timaeus, the female, receptive principle that yearns for the good and receives its imprint; and Typhon is the disorderly, destructive element in the soul of the world, necessary and never finally abolished. The myth, on this reading, is a veiled metaphysics of form, receptacle, and disorder — Plato's cosmology wearing Egyptian dress.

// WHOSE MEANING IS IT? //

The Platonic reading of the Osiris myth is Plutarch's. It is presented in this hub — here, in the Egyptology hub, and wherever the essay is cited — as the interpretation of a Greek Platonist of around 100 CE, working on Egyptian material through Greek categories. It is not presented as the doctrine of Egyptian priests, whose own theology, as the Egyptian texts state it, operates in its own terms; and it is not the myth's “original meaning”, a category this archive does not use for material of this age. Plutarch himself, to his credit, frames his readings as likenesses and probabilities, and warns against both the literalism that believes the story as told and the reductionism that dissolves it into mere physics. The hub follows his framing and declines his conclusions — the standard treatment for a philosophical reception of the first importance.

Two further services of the essay should be recorded. First, it is a quarry of Egyptian data amid the interpretation: festival dates, the names and forms of cult objects, priestly customs such as the abstentions of the Isiac priesthood, the geography of the dismemberment tradition town by town. J. Gwyn Griffiths' great commentary (1970) spent a volume testing those data against the Egyptian record, and the results are the usual Plutarchan mixture: much is garbled or Hellenised, and much — more than sceptics expected — checks out against sources Plutarch cannot have invented. Second, the essay is the headwaters of the Western esoteric Egypt: through it, and through the Hermetica treated in Wing II, the Plutarchan Isis and Osiris flow into Renaissance Hermetism and the whole later tradition this hub's second wing documents. When a later alchemist reads Osiris as a principle of the art, the reading descends from this essay's method — allegory laid over myth by a philosopher-priest — whether or not it descends from this essay's page.

XIV. The Priest of Delphi: Plutarch Inside the Cult He Describes

The last of Plutarch's qualifications is the one no modern scholar can replicate: he wrote about Greek religion from inside its priesthood. In his later years — probably from the 90s CE, and for a long tenure — Plutarch served as one of the two priests of Apollo at Delphi, the most prestigious oracular sanctuary in the Greek world, and he also held office in the Delphic Amphictyony, the council that administered the sanctuary. The Delphic dialogues are written from the sanctuary's own circle: their speakers include his brother, his sons, his teacher, and the sanctuary's prophetess' attendants, and their subjects are the sanctuary's working problems — why the oracle no longer speaks in verse, why oracles fail, what the letter E on the temple front means.

The priesthood changes the value of his evidence in both directions, and the hub weighs both. For the better: Plutarch knows how a sanctuary actually runs — its finances, its personnel, its rituals, the feel of a consultation day — and his antiquarian lore is constantly checked against living practice in a way no outsider's could be. When he describes the Delphic rite or reports what the priestesses' attendants said, he is Pausanias' peer: an insider-witness of the second-century cult, and for Delphi a better one. For the worse: he is a stakeholder. His essays defend the oracle's honour in an age of its decline; his theology — providential, daemon-haunted, Platonic — is the official philosophy of a man whose office required the gods to be respectable. His allegorising is not disinterested: it is the rescue operation of a priest who has read Plato and cannot un-read him, performed on stories his congregation still tells.

That doubleness is, in the end, what makes Plutarch the most instructive of the three systematizers for this hub's readers. Herodotus shows inquiry applied to myth from outside; Pausanias shows testimony gathered at the shrine door; Plutarch shows what the tradition's own educated custodians did with the inheritance when philosophy made parts of it incredible — they interpreted it, rationalised it, moralised it, and kept it. Every later age of Greek religion's reception, including the alchemical age of Wing II and including the modern one, repeats Plutarch's operation with different philosophies. To have watched him do it, openly and with the method stated, is the best training this archive can give its readers in watching it done.

// HOW THE HUB CITES PLUTARCH //

Plutarch is cited for late philosophical reception — what the educated Greek world of around 100 CE believed, told, and made of the myths — and, through his named sources, as a conduit for earlier material, with the conduit named. His narratives of the legendary period are cited as his construction of them. His interpretations are cited as his interpretations. He is not cited as an authority on what a myth originally meant, on Egyptian doctrine, or on the archaic and classical cults except where his priestly and antiquarian testimony is itself the evidence.

XV. The Three Compared: Three Kinds of Prose, Three Kinds of Evidence

Set side by side, the three systematizers divide the evidentiary world between them with almost no overlap — which is why the hub can use all three without redundancy, and why substituting one for another is always an error.

The table also exposes the chronological trap the three set for the unwary. They span six centuries, but they are usually read — and in popular books, quoted — as if they were a single contemporary chorus called “the ancient sources”. They are not. Pausanias describes a Greece six hundred years older than Herodotus' and already ancient to itself; Plutarch's Theseus is separated from any historical kernel of a Bronze Age Athens by a longer span than separates Plutarch from us. The hub's citations therefore always carry their author's date implicitly in the form of the claim: not “the Greeks believed”, but “Herodotus reports”, “Pausanias saw”, “Plutarch interprets”. Three kinds of prose; three kinds of evidence; three different verbs.

XVI. How This Hub Uses Each

The rules below are stated here, once, so that the rest of the hub can apply them without re-arguing them. They are the practical upshot of everything the article has established.

RULE 1 · CULT CLAIMS REST ON PAUSANIAS-TYPE TESTIMONY

A claim that a rite was performed, an image stood, or a local myth was told at a place rests, wherever possible, on testimony of the Pausanian type: named place, described object or rite, local account, witness distinguishing sight from hearsay. Where the testimony is weaker — a poet, a scholiast, a late compiler — the claim is phrased to the weaker strength.

RULE 2 · HERODOTUS FOR EARLY TESTIMONY AND RECEPTION

Herodotus anchors the early end: the first prose report of a cult or myth, the fifth-century state of the telling, and the first comparative judgements. His foreign material is used as Greek reception of it, cross-checked against the foreign record — for Egypt, against the Egyptology hub — before any use as evidence about the foreign cult itself.

RULE 3 · PLUTARCH FOR LATE RECEPTION, LABELLED

Plutarch anchors the philosophical end: his narratives are cited as his constructions, his rationalisations as euhemerising reception, his allegories as Platonic reception — always with his name and approximate date on the claim. His value as a conduit for named lost sources is used with the conduit named.

RULE 4 · NO BACK-PROJECTION

Nothing is read backward. A second-century description does not describe the fifth century BCE without argument; a Platonic allegory does not state a Bronze Age meaning; a local legend recorded by Pausanias is evidence for the legend's currency in his day before it is evidence for anything earlier. Each layer of the tradition is dated to its layer.

Applied across the hub, the rules produce the texture the reader will already have met: the Mysteries article resting its Eleusinian topography on Pausanias and its earliest testimony on Herodotus' reticence; the Olympians article distinguishing the poets' Panhellenic system — Herodotus 2.53's authored theogony — from the local images and epithets Pausanias records; the Theseus article walking Plutarch's Life with its proem's warning posted at the gate. This article is the hub's methods page for its three chief witnesses; the rest of the archive is the application.

XVII. Survival and Editions

Three prose corpora, three different survivals. The differences matter, because a text's transmission is part of its evidence: what was copied was what institutions valued, and gaps in a text are facts about its readers as well as its author.

Herodotus

The Histories descends through two medieval manuscript families — conventionally the Florentine family, headed by a tenth-century Laurentian codex, and the Roman family — whose agreement gives good confidence in the text and whose divergences fill the apparatus of the standard edition: N. G. Wilson's Oxford Classical Text (2 vols., 2015), which supersedes Hude's. The first printed edition was the Aldine of 1502. The translation this hub recommends first is Robin Waterfield's (Oxford World's Classics, 1998, with notes by Carolyn Dewald) — the livest modern English Herodotus; the Loeb of A. D. Godley (1920–25) remains the scholar's crib and supplied this article's quotation of 2.53, while Rawlinson's Victorian version (1862) supplied 7.152.

Pausanias

The Periegesis survived by a much thinner thread: read little in later antiquity, it comes down through a small number of fifteenth-century manuscripts, themselves derived from a damaged archetype, and its text is correspondingly more conjectural than Herodotus' — place-names and numerals, the load-bearing words of a topography, are exactly where copyists err. The editio princeps was the Aldine of 1516. The standard English versions are the Loeb of W. H. S. Jones (4 vols., 1918–35; a fifth volume of maps and index followed, compiled by R. E. Wycherley, 1955), which prints the Greek and keeps the work's own order; and Peter Levi's Penguin translation (2 vols., 1971), the most readable English Pausanias — with the caveat, noted by Elsner among others, that Levi rearranges the text into the order of a modern guidebook, dissolving the periegetic structure that is itself part of the evidence. Frazer's six-volume translation with commentary (1898) remains a monument of the age that vindicated him.

Plutarch

Plutarch was never lost: the Lives were among the most copied and translated of all Greek prose in the Byzantine and Renaissance worlds — through Amyot's French and North's English they became Shakespeare's quarry — and the Moralia survived in bulk, catalogued already in antiquity by the so-called Lamprias catalogue, which also records works now lost. The standard English Lives is Bernadotte Perrin's Loeb (11 vols., 1914–26), from which this article quotes the Theseus proem; the Moralia fills sixteen Loeb volumes by many hands, with On Isis and Osiris in volume V in Frank Cole Babbitt's translation (1936), and J. Gwyn Griffiths' edition with translation and commentary (1970) as the work's scholarly monument.

References elsewhere in the hub of the form “Herodotus 2.44”, “Pausanias 8.25.5”, or “Plutarch, Theseus 15” are to the standard book-chapter-section numeration, stable across all the editions named above. A reader with any of them in hand can verify any claim in this hub in minutes — which is, in the end, the systematizers' own standard turned back on the archive that uses them.
