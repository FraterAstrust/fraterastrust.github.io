I. A Story Already Old When Homer Heard It

Most Greek myths reach us the way a river reaches the sea: we can name the large late channels — a tragedy, an epic, a mythographer's handbook — but the headwaters are lost in a landscape of oral telling that left no texts. The Argonaut saga is unusual because its antiquity is not an inference. It is attested, inside our earliest surviving Greek poetry, as already famous. In Book 12 of the Odyssey, Circe warns Odysseus about the Wandering Rocks, the Planktai, and explains that only one seagoing ship has ever passed them: the Argo, on her way back from Aeëtes, saved because Hera loved Jason (Odyssey 12.69–72, paraphrased). The ship is described, in the Greek, as Argo pasi melousa — the Argo “known to all”, a concern to everyone. A poet does not gloss a reference his audience will not catch. Whoever composed that passage, in the tradition that produced the Odyssey in the late eighth century BCE, expected every hearer to know who Jason was, where he had been, and why Hera protected him. The voyage was, by then, common property.

The Iliad knows Jason without knowing, or without needing, the quest. It mentions Euneus, the son Jason fathered on Hypsipyle of Lemnos (Iliad 7.467–469, 21.40–41, 23.745–747, paraphrased) — which means the Lemnos episode, a single stop on the outward voyage, was already a fixed part of the story in the epic tradition. Hesiod, around 700 BCE, knows the ending: the closing lines of the Theogony (992–1002) tell, in paraphrase, how Jason, having completed the grievous labours imposed by Pelias, carried off Aeëtes' daughter from Colchis, made her his wife, and fathered on her a son, Medeios, whom Cheiron reared in the mountains. For the Theogony itself — its poet, its date, and its place in this hub — see Theogony. Three of our oldest witnesses, then, each presuppose the saga and use a different piece of it: Homer the return past the Rocks, the Iliad the Lemnian son, Hesiod the marriage and the child. None of them tells the story whole, because none of them needed to.

What follows from that distribution is the first discipline of this article. The Argonaut story is older than any surviving telling of it. There was never an original text of which Apollonius or Apollodorus is a copy. There was a saga — a Thessalian hero, a marvellous ship, a voyage to the eastern end of the sea, a golden object guarded in a king's grove, a foreign princess who betrays her father for love — circulating in song for generations before anyone whose work survives wrote a line of it. Every author in this article is therefore a version, selecting, reordering, and inventing within a tradition his audience already knew. When two versions disagree — and they disagree constantly, about who was on the ship, how Medea's brother died, and how the children at Corinth died — the disagreement is not corruption of a lost original. It is the tradition working as it always worked. Timothy Gantz's Early Greek Myth (1993), the standard guide to the literary and artistic sources, organises its Argonaut chapter on exactly this principle, source by source and century by century, and this article follows his method throughout.

THE STORY AT A GLANCE

Pelias, king of Iolcus in Thessaly, sends his nephew Jason to fetch the Golden Fleece from Aeëtes, king of Colchis at the eastern end of the Black Sea. Jason builds the Argo, crews her with the heroes of his generation, sails out past the Clashing Rocks, and reaches Colchis. Aeëtes sets impossible trials. His daughter Medea — in love, and in the later epic divinely made to be in love — supplies the drugs and the knowledge that carry Jason through them, drugs the guardian dragon to sleep, and flees with the Fleece, her brother, and the Argonauts. The return, the revenge on Pelias, and the catastrophe at Corinth follow.

SPELLINGS USED HERE

Greek names are transliterated, not Latinised: Aeëtes (not Aeetes in the article's own prose, though quotations keep their translators' spellings), Polydeuces where the Greek matters and Pollux where a Latin source is being followed, Apsyrtus (the same figure older translations call Absyrtus). Medea's name is Greek Mēdeia, traditionally connected with mēdomai, to devise or plan — the Schemer. The connection is ancient and is reported as a traditional etymology, not proved as a linguistic one.

A word about the geography, because the myth's geography is part of its meaning. Iolcus — modern Volos, on the Pagasitic Gulf in Thessaly — was a real and ancient place, a Mycenaean centre whose palace site at Dimini and whose great tholos tombs made it, in the Bronze Age, one of the powers of the Greek mainland. Colchis was equally real: the lowland at the eastern end of the Black Sea, in modern Georgia, watered by the river Phasis (the modern Rioni), and known to the Greek world from the archaic colonising centuries onward as a source of gold, linen, timber, and slaves. Between the two lay the whole length of the Greek sea-world: the Aegean, the Hellespont, the Propontis, the Bosphorus, and the long southern shore of the Black Sea. The saga's outward route follows that real seaway station by station, which is one reason later ages kept trying to read the voyage as garbled history. Section XVII states what can and cannot be claimed on that score. The myth itself makes a simpler point with the same map: the Fleece hangs at the edge of the known world, in the keeping of a son of the Sun, and getting it is a voyage out of the human map and back.

II. The Texts: Transmission and the Principal Versions

Because no archaic Argonaut epic survives, the tradition reaches us through a sequence of witnesses of very different kinds, and the reader of this article is entitled to the sequence plainly laid out before the story is told from any one of them. What follows is the dossier in chronological order. Lost works are included where their existence is attested and their loss matters — which, for this saga, is often.

The Archaic and Classical Witnesses

Homer and Hesiod (late 8th–c. 700 BCE) give the allusions catalogued in Section I: presupposition, not narration. The lost epic Naupactia, an archaic poem named in the ancient sources and quoted by the scholia on Apollonius, treated the Argonauts at some length; only fragments and testimonia survive, but its existence shows the saga had its own epic in the archaic period. The elegist Mimnermus of Colophon (late 7th century BCE) is, on Strabo's testimony (Geography 1.2.40), our first witness to Jason's voyage to Aia, Aeëtes' city at the edge of Ocean, in quest of the Fleece — the passage is paraphrased here from Strabo's report, the elegy itself being lost. Eumelus of Corinth, to whom the fragmentary epic Korinthiaka was attributed, gave the Corinthian version of Medea — in which she is not a refugee but a queen by inheritance — and his testimony, preserved in Pausanias and the scholia, matters greatly in Sections XV and XVII. Simonides (6th–5th century BCE) is credited in the scholia with a poem on the voyage. None of these survives whole. The archaic Argonautica is a library of titles.

Pindar's Pythian Ode 4, composed for Arcesilaus of Cyrene's chariot victory and conventionally dated to 462 BCE, is the earliest detailed account of the expedition that survives entire — and it survives entire because it is not an epic but a victory ode, into which the Argonaut story is folded for a precise local reason: Cyrene's founding line ran back through Battus to Euphemus, an Argonaut who received from the god Triton, on the Libyan leg of the return, a clod of earth that was the seed of the future colony. Pindar's telling (299 lines, the longest of his odes) is therefore radically selective. It opens with Medea herself, prophesying on Thera; it gives Jason's arrival at Iolcus and his confrontation with Pelias at length; it compresses the outward voyage almost to nothing; it expands the Colchian trials; and it closes the voyage with the dragon slain, the Fleece taken, and the Libyan episode that its Cyrene audience existed to hear. Section XI quotes its version of the trials. Pindar's Medea is already, in 462 BCE, the story's prophetic centre: the ode's first speaking voice is hers.

Tragedy worked the saga's edges rather than its voyage. Euripides' Medea (431 BCE) treats only the Corinthian end, and is given its own movement in Section XV. Sophocles wrote a Colchian Women (Kolchides) and a Root-Cutters (Rhizotomoi) — the latter apparently showing Medea gathering magical herbs — both lost except for fragments and the scholia's reports; Frazer's notes to Apollodorus, consulted for this article, record both plays' bearing on the Colchian episodes. Aeschylus is credited with Argonaut titles in the ancient catalogues, likewise lost. The classical stage, in short, treated the voyage as prologue and mined the marriage for drama — a distribution of interest the whole later tradition inherited.

The Hellenistic and Imperial Witnesses

Apollonius Rhodius, Argonautica (3rd century BCE) is the saga's one surviving epic and the backbone of Sections III–XIII: four books, some 5,835 hexameter lines, telling the voyage out, the winning of the Fleece, and the return. It is treated in full in the two sections that follow. Callimachus, Apollonius's older contemporary at Alexandria, handled Argonaut material in his Aetia in the aetiological manner — explaining rites and place-names by their Argonautic origins — and the fragments show how thoroughly the saga had become, by the third century, a quarry of local explanations. The prose mythographers of the Hellenistic age summarised the saga repeatedly; their summaries are lost, but their tradition feeds the one prose summary that survives.

That summary is the Library (Bibliotheca) attributed to Apollodorus (1.9.16–28 for the Argonauts). The attribution is conventional and false in the simple sense: the work is not by Apollodorus of Athens, the second-century BCE scholar whose name it bears, and modern scholarship treats its author as pseudo-Apollodorus, compiling in the first or second century CE from older epic and tragic sources. What the Library gives — and why this article uses it constantly alongside Apollonius — is the saga in its handbook form: names, parentages, episodes in sequence, variants noted in a clause and passed over. Where Apollonius is a poem, with a poem's selectivity and psychology, the Library is a map of the whole tradition, including roads Apollonius did not take. Its Argonaut narrative is quoted in this article from J. G. Frazer's Loeb translation (1921), whose dense footnotes — comparing Apollonius, the Orphic poem, Valerius Flaccus, Hyginus, Pindar, and the scholia at every episode — are themselves a conspectus of the tradition, and were read for this article alongside the text.

The Roman witnesses are treated in Section XVIII: Valerius Flaccus' unfinished Latin Argonautica (Flavian period, 1st century CE), Ovid's Metamorphoses Book 7, which gives the fullest surviving telling of Medea's rejuvenations and the death of Pelias, and the late antique Greek Orphic Argonautica, narrated by Orpheus himself. Hyginus's Fabulae (2nd century CE) preserve a Latin prose summary and one of the fullest crew lists. Diodorus Siculus (1st century BCE, Library 4.40–56) gives a rationalising prose version, valuable precisely for its embarrassments — it is antiquity trying to make the saga respectable history, and Section XVI returns to it.

One further class of witness deserves naming before the narrative begins, because this hub's method requires it: the scholia — the marginal commentaries on Apollonius preserved in his manuscripts, compiled in late antiquity and Byzantium from Hellenistic scholarship. The scholia are where most of our knowledge of the lost versions lives: it is a scholiast who tells us what Sophocles' Colchian Women did with Apsyrtus, which return route Hecataeus proposed, and which episodes Apollonius took from which predecessor. They are cited in this article, where they are used, as scholia — ancient scholarship about the poem, not the poem — and Frazer's Apollodorus notes, which digest them at every step, are the form in which they were chiefly consulted.

III. Apollonius Rhodius and Alexandria: The Scholar-Poet

Apollonius was a product of the most deliberate literary institution of the ancient world. Alexandria, founded by Alexander in 331 BCE and ruled by the Ptolemies, housed the Museum — a royally salaried community of scholars — and its Library, where Greek literature was for the first time collected entire, edited, catalogued, and argued over. The poets of that world were scholars and the scholars were poets: they edited Homer in the morning and wrote learned verse, dense with Homeric allusion and recondite geography, in the afternoon. Apollonius belonged to the second generation of that world. The ancient biographical tradition — the two Lives transmitted with his poem and the Byzantine Suda entry, all of it late and all of it to be handled as tradition rather than record — makes him a native of Alexandria (some sources say Naucratis), a pupil of Callimachus, tutor to a Ptolemaic prince, and, in the best-known anecdote, a young poet whose first recitation of the Argonautica was so coldly received that he withdrew to Rhodes, revised the poem there, and won the fame that earned him the name Rhodius, “the Rhodian”. Modern scholarship repeats the outline with caution flags attached: the dates proposed for his birth run from the 290s to the 260s BCE, his floruit falls under Ptolemy II Philadelphus and Ptolemy III Euergetes (reigned 246–221 BCE), and the famous quarrel with Callimachus — the great feud of Alexandrian letters, long poem against short — is now widely doubted as biography, however real the difference of poetic programme behind it. What is not doubted is the work's character. The Argonautica is a scholar's epic: a deliberate, knowing re-composition of Homer by a man who had Homer by heart and expected his readers to have him by heart too.

The poem announces that programme in its first line. In R. C. Seaton's Loeb translation (1912), the opening runs:

“Beginning with thee, O Phoebus, I will recount the famous deeds of men of old, who, at the behest of King Pelias, down through the mouth of Pontus and between the Cyanean rocks, sped well-benched Argo in quest of the golden fleece.” — APOLLONIUS RHODIUS, ARGONAUTICA 1.1–4, TRANS. R. C. SEATON (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY, 1912)

Every choice in those four lines is a position taken. The invocation is to Apollo, not to the Muse of the Iliad's opening — though the Muses are invoked a few lines later, in a doubled, self-conscious gesture, as the poet's “inspirers” or, in other renderings, his prompters and assistants: the Alexandrian poet does not pretend to be a passive mouthpiece. The heroes are “men of old”, figures of a past the poet knows he is reconstructing. And the whole quest — the mouth of Pontus, the Cyanean Rocks, the Fleece — is compressed into the proem, because the audience, like Homer's audience five centuries earlier, already knows the story and is here for the telling. Seaton's Edwardian prose, from which this article quotes Apollonius, is the Loeb version of 1912; the modern Loeb is William H. Race's edition and translation (2008), and the most widely read modern English version is E. V. Rieu's Penguin prose translation (1959, revised by Doreen Innes, 2006). The article's narrative paraphrases are its own, checked against Seaton's text; only Seaton's wording is ever placed in quotation marks.

What Apollonius did to the saga can be stated in three strokes, each of which the narrative sections below document in place. First, he made it a geography. The poem is saturated with the Hellenistic science of its city: real coastlines, real peoples, rivers whose courses are argued about, place-names explained by aitia — origin-stories — at a density no earlier epic attempts; scholars have counted some eighty aitia in the poem, many of them explaining rites and customs “still” observed in the poet's own day. The myth is pinned, deliberately, to the map of the third-century world. Second, he made Jason unheroic in a new way. Apollonius's Jason is repeatedly amēchanos — at a loss, resourceless, sunk in the helpless despondency the poem names again and again — a leader chosen by vote who leads by conciliation, diplomacy, and, at the crisis, by accepting a woman's help. Against Homer's Achilles and Odysseus the contrast is programmatic, and ancient and modern readers have argued ever since whether it is realism, irony, or decline. Third — and this is the poem's permanent achievement — he made Medea a mind. Book 3, in which Medea falls in love, argues with herself, dreams, wavers, and decides, contains the most sustained interior portrait of a woman in surviving Greek epic, and one of the earliest great studies of erotic psychology in European literature. Section XI treats it at the length it deserves. The scholar-poet of Alexandria, writing five centuries after the saga was already old, found in its foreign princess the thing archaic epic had not thought to look for: an inside.

The tale of the failed first recitation, the withdrawal to Rhodes, and the triumphant revision comes from the ancient Lives of Apollonius, written centuries after his death. It is biography of the anecdotal kind ancient scholarship manufactured for its authors — the pattern (rejection, exile, vindication) is a stock one. This article reports it as the tradition's story about the poem's making, not as an established event. The poem's two-stage composition has been argued on internal grounds as well, and remains unproved either way.

The poem's survival is itself a fact of transmission worth recording. Unlike the lost archaic Argonaut epics, the Argonautica was a school and library text: it was copied with its scholia through the Byzantine centuries, and the medieval manuscripts — the best of them the tenth- or eleventh-century Laurentian codex — preserve a text in good order. Virgil read it closely: the fourth book of the Aeneid, Dido's tragedy, is unthinkable without Apollonius's Medea, and the debt was already a commonplace of ancient criticism. When the Latin West reworked the saga in its turn (Section XVIII), it was Apollonius's version — Jason's hesitations, Medea's interior war, the long impossible return — that set the terms.

IV. The Argonautica in Four Books

The poem's architecture is the voyage's own shape, and Apollonius's disposition of his material across the four books is the clearest guide to where his interests lay. The outward voyage takes two books; Colchis takes one; the return — which a lesser poet would have compressed into a coda — takes the whole of the fourth, and is longer in incident than the outward journey it answers. The proportions are the argument: for Apollonius, winning the Fleece is the middle of the story, not the end of it.

BOOK 1 — THE MUSTER AND THE FIRST LOSSES

Proem and catalogue of the Argonauts (1–233); the farewell at Iolcus and the launching; the election of Jason as leader over Heracles' own nomination; Lemnos and Hypsipyle; Samothrace; the Doliones and the killing of Cyzicus; Mysia, the rape of Hylas, and the leaving behind of Heracles and Polyphemus. The book ends with the ship already diminished: its strongest man is gone before the Black Sea is reached.

BOOK 2 — THE COAST OF PERILS

Amycus and the Bebryces; Phineus and the Harpies; the passage of the Clashing Rocks; the long coasting voyage east — Mariandyni and the deaths of Idmon and Tiphys, the Amazons' shore, the island of Ares and its bronze-feathered birds, the wrecked sons of Phrixus rescued and taken aboard — and the arrival, at the book's close, in the river Phasis, under the Caucasus.

BOOK 3 — MEDEA

Hera and Athena enlist Aphrodite; Eros shoots Medea; the embassy to Aeëtes and the setting of the trials; Medea's night of deliberation, her meeting with Jason at Hecate's temple, the gift of the drug and the instructions; the yoking of the bulls, the sowing, the earthborn warriors. The book is the poem's centre of gravity, and Jason performs its climax entirely inside Medea's knowledge.

BOOK 4 — THE FLEECE, THE FLIGHT, THE WAY BACK

The dragon drugged and the Fleece taken; the pursuit; Apsyrtus lured and killed; Zeus's anger and the speaking ship's demand for purification; the impossible return up the Ister (Danube), across to the Adriatic, up the Eridanus and down the Rhodanus to Circe; the Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, the Phaeacians and the wedding; Libya, the carrying of the ship, Triton; Crete and Talos; Anaphe; home.

Two structural features deserve emphasis because they govern how the narrative sections below cite the poem. The first is the aetiological weave. Apollonius interrupts his heroic past, constantly and on principle, to connect it with the reader's present: this altar is still sacrificed at, this rite is still performed, this people still does this because the Argonauts did that. The Doliones still mourn Cyzicus; the Mysians still call for Hylas in an annual rite the poem describes; the Apsyrtides islands still bear Apsyrtus's name. The device is Alexandrian scholarship turned into narrative form — the poem footnotes itself — and it is also, for this archive's purposes, the poem's own claim about cult: Apollonius presents these rites as real practices of his own world explained by the saga. Whether any given aition records a genuine cult fact or invents a plausible one is a question that has to be asked case by case, and Section XVII asks it.

The second is the handling of the divine. Apollonius's gods are Homeric in machinery and un-Homeric in tone. Hera and Athena, Jason's patrons, conduct their campaign for him in Book 3 as a drawing-room intrigue, descending on Aphrodite and bribing the child Eros with a golden ball — a toy described in a set-piece of Alexandrian jewel-work — to shoot Medea. The scene is charming and faintly comic, and it is also the poem's theology in miniature: Medea's love, the engine of the whole quest, is at once a goddess's stratagem, a child's arrow, and — once it lands — a fully human catastrophe of loyalty divided against itself. Apollonius never lets the reader settle for one of those registers alone. Fritz Graf's essay “Medea, the Enchantress from Afar” (in Medea: Essays on Medea in Myth, Literature, Philosophy, and Art, 1997), consulted for this article, takes its start from exactly this layering: the Medea each age knows is one link in a chain of versions, and Apollonius's link is the one that forged her interior life.

A note on length and scale, for proportion. At some 5,835 lines the Argonautica is a third the length of the Odyssey and little more than a third of the Iliad. In a literary culture whose most influential critic-poet, Callimachus, had made the short, dense, polished poem the mark of modern taste — and whose famous dictum held a big book to be a big evil — an epic of even this moderate size, in Homer's metre and on Homer's scale of subject, was a polemical act: a demonstration that the old form could still be written, by a scholar, out of the Library's resources. The poem's Hellenistic readers understood it so, and the scholia argue its Homeric debts line by line.

V. Before the Voyage: Pelias, the One Sandal, Phrixus and the Ram

Every version of the saga opens with the same two antecedent stories, and the narrative in this article follows them in the order the sources give: the crime that put the Fleece in Colchis, and the usurpation that sent Jason to fetch it.

Phrixus and the Ram: How the Fleece Came to Colchis

The Fleece was, first, a ram. In the account the Library gives at the head of its Argonaut narrative (1.9.1, paraphrased from Frazer's translation), Athamas, king in Boeotia, had by his first wife Nephele — Cloud — two children, Phrixus and Helle. His second wife Ino, the children's stepmother, plotted against them, and by a contrivance with the sowing of parched seed and a bribed oracle brought it about that Phrixus was to be sacrificed. At the altar, the children were rescued by a marvel: a golden-fleeced, winged ram, sent by their mother Nephele, which took them up and carried them eastward over the sea. Helle fell from its back into the strait that bears her name ever after — the Hellespont. Phrixus came safe to Colchis, where he sacrificed the ram to Zeus Phyxius, Zeus of Escape, and gave its fleece to the country's king, Aeëtes, who hung it on an oak in a grove of Ares and set a sleepless dragon to guard it. Aeëtes also gave Phrixus his daughter Chalciope in marriage — a detail the voyage will need, since Phrixus's sons are still in Colchis when the Argo arrives. The ram itself, in the catasterism attached to the story, became the constellation Aries. The fleece thus begins the saga already double: it is a rescued child's thank-offering and a king's treasure, a thing of Zeus's altar and of Ares' grove, Greek in origin and Colchian in keeping — which is to say, it is exactly the kind of object two worlds can go to war over.

The tale-type deserves a sentence of its own. The wicked stepmother, the child at the altar, the animal substitute or rescuer: Greek myth tells the pattern again with Iphigenia and the deer at Aulis, and the Hebrew tradition tells it with Isaac and the ram in the thicket. The comparison is noted here as a comparison of narrative patterns, nothing more; no source known to this article derives any one of these stories from another. What the Greek version uniquely adds is the sequel: the rescued animal's skin becomes a kingdom's palladium and the goal of the next generation's quest.

Pelias and the One Sandal: How Jason Was Sent

Iolcus, in the saga's present, is ruled by Pelias. The Library's genealogy (1.9.8–16, paraphrased) makes the wrong precise: Cretheus founded Iolcus; his son Aeson, Jason's father, was the line of succession; Pelias — Aeson's half-brother, a son of Poseidon — held the kingdom instead. Jason grew up in the country, in some versions under the tuition of the centaur Cheiron on Mount Pelion, the mountain that overhangs Iolcus and supplied the Argo's timbers. Pelias, consulting the oracle about his throne, was warned to beware the man with one sandal. The scene that follows is told by Pindar, Apollonius, and Apollodorus with unusual agreement. Pelias was sacrificing to Poseidon by the sea; Jason, hurrying to attend, crossed the river Anaurus and lost a sandal in the mud; he arrived with one foot bare. In Frazer's Apollodorus (1.9.16), the king, remembering the oracle, tests the young man with a question — what would he do, if an oracle had told him one of his citizens would kill him? Jason's answer, in Frazer's translation, quoted here in its short form, is the hinge of the whole saga:

“I would command him,” said he, “to bring the Golden Fleece.” — PSEUDO-APOLLODORUS, LIBRARY 1.9.16, TRANS. J. G. FRAZER (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY, 1921)

Apollodorus, with a mythographer's honesty, gives two motives for the answer in a single breath: Jason spoke whether at haphazard or instigated by the angry Hera — Hera, whom Pelias had neglected to honour, arranging that Medea should one day be Pelias's curse. Pelias takes the answer as his instrument: very well, go and fetch it. The errand is a death sentence dressed as a quest, and every hearer of the saga knew it: Colchis is at the end of the world, its king is the Sun's son, and the Fleece has a dragon. Pindar's version (Pythian 4.156–168, paraphrased) dresses the same transaction in diplomacy — Jason first offers Pelias a settlement, the flocks and lands against the sceptre that was Aeson's, and Pelias, too smooth to refuse outright, produces the Fleece as the price, adding that the spirit of Phrixus himself demands its return. The two motives the tradition always keeps in play are both present from the start: the Fleece is Jason's title-deed — fetch it, and the usurper's own terms make him king — and it is his death warrant. The saga never resolves which errand he is really on. It will matter, at Corinth, that he accomplished the errand and it did not make him king.

VI. The Building of the Argo and the Speaking Timber

The Argo is the saga's second protagonist, and the sources treat her building as an event of the same order as the muster of the heroes. In the Library's account (1.9.16), quoted from Frazer's translation in the short passage that follows, Jason called in Argus, son of Phrixus — the Fleece's first owner's son, a living link between the two halves of the story — and Argus built the ship:

“Argus, by Athena's advice, built a ship of fifty oars named Argo after its builder; and at the prow Athena fitted in a speaking timber from the oak of Dodona.” — PSEUDO-APOLLODORUS, LIBRARY 1.9.16, TRANS. J. G. FRAZER (LOEB, 1921)

Three facts in that one sentence carry the whole weight of the ship in the saga. Fifty oars: the Argo is a penteconter, the long, narrow galley of the early Greek world, rowed by some fifty men — which is why the crew lists hover around fifty names and why “Argonaut”, Argo-sailor, names a company and not a crowd. She is, in the tradition's own estimation, a new kind of thing: the Orphic poem (Section XVIII) claims outright that she was the first ship ever built, and even the soberer sources treat her as the first long-voyage vessel, built for a journey no ship had made. Athena's advice: the goddess of craft supervises the construction, as she supervises Odysseus's raft and a hundred other makings; the ship is a work of technē, skill, and the saga insists that skill, not strength, is what the voyage runs on — a thesis the Colchian books will prove at Jason's expense. And the speaking timber: at the prow Athena set a piece of the oak of Dodona.

Dodona, in Epirus in north-western Greece, was the oldest oracle of Zeus in the Greek world — older, in the Greeks' own estimation, than Delphi. Its sanctuary centred on a sacred oak, from whose rustling leaves and whose doves the god's responses were read; Herodotus (2.54–57) retails the foundation legends, and the shrine's lead question-tablets, excavated in quantity, show ordinary people asking Zeus of Dodona about marriages, journeys, and thefts. A timber from that oak is therefore not a decorative marvel. It is a piece of an oracle, built into the ship: the Argo carries a voice of Zeus with her, and at the voyage's moments of moral crisis the voice speaks. Apollonius uses it sparingly and to great effect — its great utterance comes in Book 4, when the ship itself announces Zeus's anger at the murder of Apsyrtus and names the remedy, purification by Circe (Section XIII). Apollodorus has the ship speak at the same juncture (1.9.24). A vessel that can pronounce on blood-guilt is not transport. It is a member of the expedition with standing to judge it — and the saga's only member, it is worth noticing, whose judgement no one argues with.

// THE NAME OF THE SHIP //

Apollodorus derives Argo from her builder, Argus. The tradition also heard in the name the Greek argos, swift — the ship as the Swift One. Both derivations circulate in the ancient sources and the scholia; neither can be proved, and the article records both as the tradition's own etymologies. What the crew's name settles is simpler: whatever the ship's name meant, the men are named from the ship, not from their leader — Argonauts, not Jasonites. The saga is, by its own naming, about a company and a vessel before it is about a captain.

Apollonius's building scene (1.18–22, 1.524–579, paraphrased from Seaton) is briefer than its fame suggests — the ship, he says, “as former bards relate”, Argus wrought by Athena's guidance, and the poet hurries on to the catalogue — but the launching is given in full: the heroes haul her down rollers to the water at Pagasae, the harbour of Iolcus, with Orpheus's lyre setting the stroke, and the ship, already conscious in the poem's handling, takes the sea. The beach at Pagasae kept the memory: the place was called Aphetae, the Launching, and the name is one of the saga's aitia that corresponds to a real Thessalian toponym. Section XVII weighs what such correspondences are worth.

VII. The Crew: Catalogues and Who Counts as an Argonaut

No two sources crew the Argo with the same men, and the disagreement is structural, not accidental. A catalogue of Argonauts is a roll of the heroic generation — the generation before Troy, the fathers of the Iliad's fighters — and every Greek city and family that could claim a hero of that generation had an interest in his being on the ship. The lists grew accordingly. Apollonius opens his poem with a formal catalogue of fifty-four names (1.23–233), modelled on the Iliad's Catalogue of Ships and headed, pointedly, not by Jason or Heracles but by Orpheus — “First then let us name Orpheus”, in Seaton's rendering — because song, in this poem, goes first. Apollodorus's list (1.9.16) runs to some fifty-five names and differs from Apollonius's in both directions: it includes Theseus and Atalanta, whom Apollonius omits (Atalanta's absence from his poem is pointed — Jason, in Apollonius's telling of the muster tradition, is made to fear the strife a woman aboard would cause, and she is not enrolled), and it lacks several of Apollonius's Thessalian and northern names. Hyginus's list is longer again. Pindar, a century and a half before Apollonius, names only the handful his ode needs. Gantz's source-by-source treatment, followed here, draws the only conclusion the evidence supports: there was no fixed crew in the tradition, only a stable core and a penumbra that each telling adjusted.

The stable core is worth setting out, because the narrative needs it. Around Jason stand, in every full account: Heracles, the strongest man alive, with his young companion Hylas; Orpheus, whose lyre is equipment, not ornament; the twins Castor and Polydeuces (Pollux), the Dioscuri, sons of Zeus and Leda, horseman and boxer; Zetes and Calais, the winged sons of Boreas the North Wind; Telamon and Peleus, the sons of Aeacus — Peleus is Achilles' father, and the wedding of Peleus and Thetis belongs to this same heroic generation; Tiphys, the helmsman, and after his death Ancaeus; Argus the builder; the seers Idmon and Mopsus, both of whom sail knowing, in Idmon's case, that he will not return; Meleager of Calydon; Admetus of Pherae; Euphemus, whose Libyan clod carries Cyrene's future; and the sons of Phrixus, taken aboard on the outward coast, who guide the ship into Colchis — the Fleece's own grandsons ferrying its thieves. The catalogue is a map of heroic Greece: Thessaly, Boeotia, Aetolia, Arcadia, Sparta, Athens in some lists, the islands — the whole world of the generation that Greek myth placed between the founding heroes and Troy.

Two features of the Apollonian muster carry narrative weight beyond the roll itself. The first is the election. When the company assembles, Apollonius has Jason propose that the heroes choose their leader — and the company acclaims Heracles, who refuses and nominates Jason, on the ground that the man who gathered them should command them (1.336–347, paraphrased). The scene does three things at once: it establishes Jason's authority as granted, not seized — the anti-Pelias; it establishes its limit, since the company's real centre of force has publicly stood aside; and it sets up the poem's great structural irony, that the expedition will lose Heracles within the first book and succeed anyway, by means no one at the election could have foreseen. The second feature is Idmon's embarkation: the seer joins knowing from his own art that he will die on the voyage, and goes anyway, Apollonius says, so that his fame might not be lost (1.139–145, paraphrased). The catalogue is thus seeded, from the start, with the poem's two governing facts — the strongest man will be left behind, and foreknowledge saves no one. // A NOTE ON THE NUMBERS //

Fifty oars, some fifty heroes: the correspondence is the tradition's ideal, not a manifest. Apollonius's catalogue names fifty-four; Apollodorus's, depending on how double names and alternatives are counted, fifty-five or fifty-six; rowers, officers, and supernumeraries (Argus, Tiphys, the seers) overlap in ways no source systematises. This article gives the counts as the sources present them and does not reconcile them, because the sources do not.

VIII. Outward I: Lemnos and the Doliones

The outward voyage, as Apollonius and Apollodorus tell it in substantially the same sequence, is a chain of episodes each of which costs the expedition something or teaches it something. This article follows the sequence station by station, naming the version where the versions divide. The first two stations are the voyage's two great studies in hospitality gone wrong — once by delay, once by disaster.

Lemnos: The Island Without Men

Lemnos, in the northern Aegean, is the Argo's first landfall, and the Library (1.9.17, paraphrased from Frazer) gives the island's condition in its handbook starkness. The Lemnian women had neglected the worship of Aphrodite; the goddess afflicted them with a foul smell; their husbands took Thracian captive women to their beds instead; and the wives, dishonoured, murdered their husbands and fathers in a single night — all but one. Queen Hypsipyle, daughter of King Thoas, hid her father and set him adrift in a chest, saving the island's one male life. When the Argo arrived, Lemnos was a polity of women, armed and wary, who debated whether the strangers were Thracian raiders and resolved, on Hypsipyle's counsel, to admit them — for reasons the myth states without blinking: an island of women without men is a generation from extinction, and the Argonauts are to be, in the plainest sense, the means of repopulation. The Argonauts stayed. Jason was received into Hypsipyle's palace and her bed — the Iliad's Euneus is the issue — and the company settled into the island's life until Heracles, who had kept apart from the women, shamed them back to the ship with the poem's first great speech of recall (Apollonius 1.854–878, paraphrased): was this what they had sailed for; would the Fleece be won in Lemnian beds? Jason's farewell to Hypsipyle (1.900–909, paraphrased) is Apollonius at his most delicate — if she bears a son, she is to send him, grown, to Iolcus, to comfort Jason's parents in their old age — a kindness that is also a measure of how lightly the poem's hero holds what Lemnos offered. Apollonius gives the episode more than three hundred lines, the longest stop of the outward voyage: it is the expedition's first temptation, and the temptation is not danger but stopping — a home, a queen, a kingdom for the taking, everything Jason claims to be sailing to win, available at the first island for the price of abandoning the errand. The saga will offer him the same bargain again at every scale, and the Lemnos pattern — delay, pleasure, a woman left behind with a son — is the template his story with Medea will invert: Medea is the woman who cannot be left.

A cult footnote belongs to Lemnos, of the kind this hub is charged to keep separate from the narrative. Lemnos was, in historical Greece, the island of Hephaestus — his fall from Olympus landed there, in the Iliad's telling — and of the pre-Greek Cabeiri cults akin to those of Samothrace, the Argo's next sacred stop (Apollonius 1.915–921 has the Argonauts initiated in the Samothracian rites for safety at sea, a detail Frazer's notes cross-reference). The Samothracian initiation of the Argonauts is treated in this hub at The Mysteries, where the island's Great Gods and their protection of sailors are given at the evidence's real strength. Apollonius's casual half-dozen lines on the initiation are among our earliest literary witnesses to that cult's reputation — a reminder that the poem's geography is religious as well as nautical.

The Doliones: The Host Killed in the Dark

Through the Hellespont into the Propontis, the Argo came to the land of the Doliones, whose young king Cyzicus — newly married to Cleite — received the Argonauts with full hospitality: gifts, a feast, and counsel about the coast ahead (Apollonius 1.961–988; Apollodorus 1.9.18; paraphrased). While Jason climbed Mount Dindymum to survey the sea-roads, the ship's guard under Heracles beat off an attack by the six-armed Earthborn, the wild neighbours of the Doliones — the episode's one straightforward fight. The disaster came at the departure. A storm drove the ship back by night to the same coast; neither side recognised the other in the dark; the Doliones took the returned strangers for Pelasgian raiders, the Argonauts took their hosts for enemies, and in the night battle Jason killed Cyzicus — in Apollonius (1.1030–1035, paraphrased), striking him in the chest as the king rushed him. Dawn showed both sides what they had done. The Argonauts buried Cyzicus with full honours and held funeral games; Cleite hanged herself over his body; and the expedition, pinned by contrary winds for twelve days, expiated the killing by founding, on the seer Mopsus's instruction, a cult of Rhea on Dindymum — Jason carving her image from a vine-stock, the Argonauts dancing in armour on the mountain to drown the sound of mourning below (1.1078–1152, paraphrased).

The episode is the voyage's moral foundation-stone, and Apollonius places it accordingly early. The Argonauts have killed, in ignorance and in the dark, the one king who had received them perfectly — and the poem makes them stop, for nearly a fortnight of narrative time, to bury him, mourn him, and found the rite that answers the deed. The aition Apollonius attaches — that the Doliones mourn Cyzicus still, and that the mill at Cyzicus still grinds the meal for his funeral offering — claims a living Cyzicene rite for the story's sequel; the claim is reported here as Apollonius's aition, the kind of cult-anchoring Section XVII weighs. Structurally, the Doliones episode establishes the rule the return voyage will test at full scale: blood shed on this journey, even blamelessly, must be answered — with burial, with cult, with purification — before the ship can proceed. The killing of Apsyrtus will be the same problem without the innocence.

IX. Outward II: Hylas, Amycus, Phineus and the Harpies

Mysia: The Loss of Hylas — and of Heracles

The next landfall, at Cius in Mysia, is the outward voyage's turning point, and it is a turning point made of absences. The company went ashore; Heracles, whose oar had snapped in the rowing, went inland to cut a fir for a new one; Hylas, his young companion, went to draw water at a spring called Pegae (Apollonius 1.1153–1260, paraphrased throughout). At the spring, the nymphs were dancing in the moonlight; the spring's own nymph, seeing the boy dip his pitcher, was struck by his beauty, and as he leaned over the water she drew him down into it. His cry was heard by one man only — Polyphemus the Argonaut, who ran to the spring with drawn sword and met Heracles in the dark, each taking the other for a raider. By dawn the ship, with a fair wind rising, had sailed — and in the morning light the company discovered that Heracles, Polyphemus, and Hylas were gone. The quarrel that follows (1.1280–1362, paraphrased) is the poem's sharpest scene of command: Telamon turns on Jason in fury, accusing him of engineering the loss out of jealousy of Heracles' glory; Jason, in his characteristic register, answers mildly and offers to turn back; and the quarrel is closed from outside the human argument altogether when the sea-god Glaucus rises from the water beside the ship and pronounces the divine disposition — it is not fated for Heracles to reach Colchis; his labours await him in Greece; Polyphemus is to found Cius; Hylas is the nymph's husband now. The heroes are reconciled, and the Argo sails on, permanently, without the strongest man in the world.

The loss is the saga's most consequential piece of narrative engineering, and the sources know it. A voyage with Heracles aboard cannot be in real danger from anything the Black Sea holds, and a quest Heracles could accomplish by force has no need of Medea. The tradition therefore removes him — early, blamelessly, and by fate's own pronouncement — and the removal is so necessary that every full version performs it. Apollodorus (1.9.19, paraphrased) tells it more briefly and adds the aition Apollonius also gives: the Mysians' annual rite, in which a priest still calls Hylas's name three times at the spring — a ritual cry in search of a lost boy, performed in historical times and attested beyond the poem. The Hylas rite is among the strongest of the saga's cult footprints, and Section XVII returns to it. Heracles' own Argonaut career ends here, at the spring: the labours, Eurystheus, and the whole Heracles cycle resume in Greece without reference to Colchis, and the two greatest enterprises of the heroic generation proceed on separate tracks.

The Bebryces: Polydeuces and Amycus

Book 2 opens with the voyage's set-piece of single combat (Apollonius 2.1–97; Apollodorus 1.9.20; paraphrased). The Bebryces, on the eastern shore of the Propontis, were ruled by Amycus, a son of Poseidon of enormous size, whose law for strangers was the saga's purest statement of anti-hospitality: no traveller left his country until he had boxed with the king, and the king had killed a long roll of travellers. The Argonauts' answer was Polydeuces — Pollux — the boxer of the Dioscuri, who accepted the challenge with a calm the poem dwells on, bound on the king's rawhide thongs, and fought him: youth, science, and footwork against bulk and fury, until, as Amycus lunged, Polydeuces struck him on the temple at the ear and killed him. The Bebryces rushed to avenge their king and were beaten off in a general fight, and the Argonauts sailed with Bebrycian sheep aboard. The episode is the outward voyage's one clean heroic victory, and Apollonius places it, pointedly, at the head of the book that follows Heracles' loss: the company can still fight. What it cannot do by fighting is everything that remains — prophecy, passage, and Colchis all lie on the far side of kinds of knowledge the fist cannot supply.

Phineus and the Harpies: Prophecy at a Price

At Salmydessus, on the Thracian shore of the Black Sea, the Argonauts found the expedition's indispensable informant in its most pitiable figure (Apollonius 2.178–300; Apollodorus 1.9.21; paraphrased). Phineus, a son of Agenor, had been a king and a seer; his prophetic gift had cost him his sight and very nearly his life. The sources differ on the offence — he had revealed too much of the gods' counsels to men, in Apollonius's version; other accounts, which Frazer's notes rehearse, make the blinding a punishment connected with his sons by a first marriage and a slandering stepmother — and they agree on the torment: the Harpies, winged snatchers, swooped on every meal set before him, carrying off the food and leaving a stench on what they dropped, so that the seer was starving by inches in his own hall. Deliverance was itself prophesied: the sons of Boreas would free him. Zetes and Calais, the winged Argonauts, waited at his table with drawn swords, sprang up with the Harpies as they descended, and chased them through the sky — in Apollonius, as far as the Strophades islands in the Ionian Sea, where the messenger Iris turned the brothers back with a divine assurance: the Harpies, Zeus's hounds, would trouble Phineus no more, and it was not lawful for the Boreads to kill them. The detail is a fine specimen of the saga's internal cross-referencing: the Strophades, the “Turning Islands”, take their name from the turning of this chase, and the Aeneid will find the Harpies still there, waiting for Aeneas, three centuries of tradition later.

Phineus paid for his deliverance with the voyage's chart (Apollonius 2.301–447; Apollodorus 1.9.22; paraphrased). His prophecy is the poem's second great speech of instruction and it is carefully incomplete — a seer who has been blinded for telling too much now tells exactly what is permitted: how to pass the Clashing Rocks (Section X), the sequence of peoples and dangers along the southern Black Sea coast, the warning against attempting Colchis by force, and, at the limit of his licence, the hint that the return and the winning alike depend on Aphrodite — on love, though he does not name Medea. The bargain structure is the saga's own economy in miniature: knowledge is never free, it is always partial, and it always arrives through suffering — Phineus's hunger buys the chart as surely as Medea's betrayal will buy the Fleece.

X. The Clashing Rocks and the Black Sea Coast

The Symplegades — the Clashing Rocks, Apollonius's Cyanean Rocks, the dark-blue rocks of Euripides' opening line — stood where the Bosphorus meets the Black Sea: two rocks that were not fixed but moved, crashing together on whatever tried to pass between them, so that no ship had ever come through. Phineus's instruction (Apollodorus 1.9.22 gives it in handbook form, and Apollonius 2.549–647 stages it in full) was a test by proxy: let a dove fly through first; if she is lost, turn back; if she passes, row as they have never rowed. In Frazer's Apollodorus the dove passes with the tip of her tail nipped off by the clash; the Argonauts, watching the rocks recoil, drive the Argo through on the opening with hard rowing and Hera's help, and the ship's carved stern-ornament is shorn away as the rocks close behind her. And then, in the myth's own physics, the Rocks stop: it was fated, Apollodorus says, that once a ship had passed, they would come to rest completely — and the Bosphorus's entrance, fixed and navigable, is the saga's explanation of a piece of the world's present shape. Apollonius adds Athena's hand on the ship, thrusting her through at the crisis, and gives the passage the full epic treatment: it is the threshold of the outward voyage, the gate between the Greek sea-world and the Pontus beyond, and the poem spends its finest descriptive power on foam, rock, and rowing.

“Henceforth the Clashing Rocks stood still; for it was fated that, so soon as a ship had made the passage, they should come to rest completely.” — PSEUDO-APOLLODORUS, LIBRARY 1.9.22, TRANS. J. G. FRAZER (LOEB, 1921)

A geographical caution belongs here, of the kind Section XVII generalises. Homer's Wandering Rocks, the Planktai of Odyssey 12, and the Symplegades of the Argonaut tradition are not certainly the same rocks: the Odyssey places its Planktai on Odysseus's fabulous western route, near Scylla and Charybdis, while the Argonaut tradition fixes its Clashing Rocks firmly at the Bosphorus mouth. Later antiquity conflated them freely — Apollonius's return voyage passes the Planktai as a separate danger (Section XIII) — and modern retellings usually merge them without comment. This article keeps them distinct: the outward gate is the Symplegades at the Bosphorus; the Planktai belong to the return's western seas. The actual Bosphorus entrance has conspicuous rocks and a genuinely dangerous set of currents, and the Greeks who colonised the Black Sea shores from the seventh century BCE passed it in fact; that the myth's gate stands exactly where Greek sailors met the Pontus's threshold is a correspondence of mythic to real geography, and it is recorded as a correspondence, not as a memory of any single voyage.

Beyond the Rocks, Apollonius's Book 2 becomes a coasting periplus — a sailor's itinerary raised to epic — down the southern shore of the Black Sea (2.648–1259, paraphrased in summary). Among the Mariandyni, whose king Lycus received the Argonauts as deliverers from Amycus's oppression, the expedition paid its toll in its two quiet deaths: Idmon the seer, killed by a boar in a marsh — the death he had foreseen and sailed to meet — and Tiphys the helmsman, dead of sickness, with Ancaeus taking the helm (Apollodorus 1.9.23). Past the Thermodon, where the Amazons' country lay — the poem gives their three cities and their customs a wary, admiring paragraph and sails on — past the Chalybes, who live by ironworking and never plough, the Tibareni, and the Mossynoeci in their wooden towers, whose customs Apollonius reports with an ethnographer's straight face inverted from Greek norms. At the island of Ares, the company beat off the bronze-beaked, arrow-feathered birds that haunted it — Stymphalian birds of the same brood Heracles had scattered — by the concerted clash of shields, and on the island met the voyage's most useful castaways: the four sons of Phrixus, wrecked on their way from Colchis to Greece to claim their grandfather Athamas's inheritance. Taken aboard, fed, and questioned, they became what the expedition had lacked since Phineus: guides with Colchian knowledge — and kin to Medea, whose half-sister Chalciope was their mother. Their leader Argus (a second Argus, distinct from the shipwright) undertakes to intercede. The poem ends its second book as the Argo enters the Phasis and the heroes see the Caucasus and, circling above Prometheus's crag, hear the Titan's groan and the eagle's wings — the edge of the world announcing itself by its most famous prisoner.

// THE PERIPLUS AND THE MAP //

Apollonius's Black Sea coast is the real coast, in real order — Sinope, the Thermodon, the peoples Herodotus also catalogues — described by a poet working in the city that housed the ancient world's best geographical scholarship. The accuracy is Hellenistic, not Bronze Age: it tells us what a third-century Alexandrian knew about the Pontus, and it is one of the strongest reasons for refusing to read the poem as a fossilised log of archaic exploration. The saga's route is old; its map, in our surviving epic, is new.

XI. Colchis: Medea, the Trials, and the Winning of the Fleece

Book 3 of the Argonautica opens not in Colchis but on Olympus, and the disposition of its first scene is the whole Colchian story in embryo (3.1–166, paraphrased). Hera and Athena, Jason's patrons, conclude that force and diplomacy alike will fail with Aeëtes, and that the quest's only instrument is the king's daughter. They descend on Aphrodite; Aphrodite, found combing her hair, bribes her son Eros — a spoiled, cheating child, caught dicing with Ganymede — with the promise of a marvellous golden ball, to shoot Medea with a love-arrow. Eros flies down, invisible, and shoots her in her father's hall as Jason's embassy enters: the shaft, Apollonius says, burned deep in her heart like a flame, and she kept glancing at Jason, her wits gone, her soul melting with the sweet pain. The machinery is divine and the register half-comic; the consequence is the most serious thing in the poem. Everything at Colchis follows from that arrow — and Apollonius's achievement is that, within a hundred lines, the arrow stops being an explanation. Medea's love becomes a deliberated, argued, chosen catastrophe, conducted in the longest interior monologues Greek epic had yet given any character.

The Embassy and the Setting of the Trials

Jason's approach to Aeëtes is diplomacy before it is defiance (3.167–438, paraphrased). The sons of Phrixus present him; Jason states the errand plainly — Pelias's command, the Fleece, a request, with service offered in return: he will fight the king's enemies, the Sauromatae, as payment. Aeëtes' answer is rage managed into a test. He suspects — correctly — that the sons of Phrixus have conspired to bring these strangers against his throne, and he sets the condition that is the saga's most famous ordeal: Jason shall do what Aeëtes himself does. He shall yoke the king's two fire-breathing bulls — bronze-hoofed, a gift of Hephaestus — plough the field of Ares with them, sow the field with dragon's teeth (Aeëtes holds half the teeth of the Theban dragon, Athena's gift; Cadmus sowed the other half at Thebes), and destroy the armed men, the Earthborn, who will spring from the sowing. Then, and only then, he may take the Fleece — a promise Aeëtes makes, Apollodorus and Apollonius agree, with no intention of keeping it: the king is already planning to burn the Argo and kill its crew whatever the outcome (Apollodorus 1.9.23). Jason accepts, in Apollonius with a composure the poem immediately undercuts: back at the ship, alone with the reality of the task, he is again amēchanos, at a total loss — and the poem leaves him there, and goes to Medea.

Medea's Night, and the Meeting at Hecate's Temple

What follows (3.439–824, paraphrased) is the passage on which the poem's fame rests. Medea cannot sleep. She argues with herself in a sequence of monologues that Greek literature had never attempted at this length for a woman: shame at betraying her father wars with the certainty that Jason will die without her; she resolves to help him and then to kill herself; she takes out her drug-box — and puts it back; she imagines the Colchian women's mockery if she fails and her own death if she succeeds in shaming herself; she dreams, and the dream (a Freudian millennia before Freud, as commentators tirelessly note) shows her yoking the bulls herself, easily, and choosing a husband against her parents' will. Meanwhile her sister Chalciope, terrified for her own sons — Aeëtes has threatened the Phrixus boys for their part in the embassy — begs Medea's help for them, and gives Medea the respectable errand she needs: she will meet Jason, she says, for her nephews' sake. The meeting is set at the temple of Hecate, the goddess of whose priestess Medea, in the tradition, is — the Colchian chapters consistently place her knowledge under Hecate's patronage, and the drug-lore of Section XIV flows from that spring.

At the temple (3.876–1145, paraphrased), the two meet with a company of attendants dismissed, and Apollonius stages the saga's decisive transaction as a love scene conducted in the language of supplication and oath. Medea gives Jason the Promethean drug: a plant that sprang, the poem says, from the ichor that dripped from Prometheus's liver where the eagle tore it, a saffron-flowered root of terrible power, harvested by Medea herself in a rite of bathing, dark clothing, pits dug at midnight, and the howling of Hecate's hounds underground. Anointed with it — and his shield and spear anointed too — Jason will for one day be proof against fire and iron, tireless, and of godlike strength. She gives him also the Earthborn stratagem: when the armed men spring up, throw a great stone into their midst unseen; they will fall on each other over it like dogs over food, and in that confusion he can cut them down. And she asks, in return, the two things the saga's moral bookkeeping will never stop auditing: that he remember her when he is home in Iolcus, as she will remember him — and that he take her with him, in marriage, out of Colchis. Jason swears it. Apollonius lets the scene close on Medea's line, in paraphrase, that she will follow him even to Greece, since fate and a shameless god have so decided — the poem holding, in one breath, her choice and her compulsion.

The Trials

The ordeal itself (3.1246–1407, paraphrased) is staged as a public spectacle: Aeëtes in his chariot, the Colchian people on the hillsides, the Argonauts watching from the ship. Jason bathes, anoints himself and his arms, and goes to the field visibly changed — the drug makes him, in the poem's handling, radiant and terrible. The bulls charge out of their underground stable breathing flame; he takes the fire on his shield unharmed, seizes the first bull by the horn, drags it to the yoke, forces it down, does the same with the second, and ploughs the field of Ares — four acres of it — driving the fire-breathing team with a goad and finishing the ploughing by afternoon. He sows the teeth from a bronze helmet. He withdraws to drink from the river while the crop grows — the Earthborn rising from the furrows, some to the waist, some to the shoulders, shields and spears bright in the sun — and then, remembering Medea's counsel, hurls the great stone into their midst from a distance, unseen. The Earthborn turn on each other; Jason falls on the survivors and cuts them down as a farmer cuts a crop, until the field of Ares is harvested in both senses. Aeëtes, in Apollonius's closing stroke, rides home in silence to plot how he may yet cheat the strangers of the prize. Pindar's version of the same ordeal, two centuries older, is terser and differently weighted: Aphrodite herself, with the wryneck bird bound to its wheel — the iynx, Greek love-magic's standard instrument — teaches Jason the charms that make Medea forget her parents' honour, and Medea mixes the drugs with oil for his anointing (Pythian 4.211–223, paraphrased). The trials are constants of the tradition; the psychology that delivers them is each poet's own.

The Winning of the Fleece

The Fleece is not given; it is taken, the same night (Apollonius 4.100–182; Apollodorus 1.9.23; paraphrased). Aeëtes, the trials accomplished, holds council to burn the Argo and seize its crew; Medea, warned by her sister's household of the plot, comes to the ship in the dark, alone, calling Jason by name from the shore — the poem gives her the saga's most exposed moment, a princess in flight crying out to foreign sailors — and leads Jason at once to the grove of Ares. There the Fleece hangs on its oak, and beneath it the dragon keeps its sleepless watch: in Apollonius's description a monster of coiling length, hissing, its eyes unclosing. Medea advances on it singing, sprinkles its eyes with a drug cut fresh from juniper, and calls on Sleep and Hecate; the dragon's coils slacken, its head sinks; Jason steps over the coils, lifts the Fleece from the oak — its golden wool lighting his face and hands, in the poem's most famous image, like a flame — and carries it back to the ship, where the Argonauts lift it in wonder, passing it from hand to hand. The quest is complete. It has been completed, the narrative insists at every step, by Medea's knowledge, Medea's drugs, Medea's treason, and Jason's obedience to her instructions. What the expedition does next with that debt is the rest of the story.

XII. Flight, Apsyrtus, and the Return Routes

The flight from Colchis is where the saga's versions diverge most consequentially, because it contains the act by which the whole return is morally governed: the death of Medea's brother Apsyrtus. The sources give two incompatible accounts, and this article sets them side by side rather than harmonising them, because the difference between them is the difference between two Medeas.

The Two Deaths of Apsyrtus

Apollodorus's version (1.9.24, paraphrased from Frazer) is the one the later popular tradition adopted. Apsyrtus is a child. Medea brings him aboard the Argo in the flight; when Aeëtes' pursuit draws close, she murders the boy, cuts him limb from limb, and throws the pieces into the sea. Aeëtes stops — a father gathering his son's limbs from the water — and the Argo escapes. He buries what he recovered at a place he names Tomi, Tombs, and sends the Colchians out in squadrons to continue the search on pain of suffering Medea's punishment themselves. In this version the act is Medea's alone, performed with her hands, on a child, as a calculated delay — the deed Euripides' Medea will be reproached with, and the deed whose shadow the whole Corinthian story lives under.

Apollonius's version (4.224–481, paraphrased) is deliberately, elaborately different. Apsyrtus is a grown man, Aeëtes' son by a Caucasian nymph, commanding the swifter Colchian squadron, which out-sails the Argo by taking a straighter course and blockades her in the Ister's mouth at an island of Artemis. Trapped, the Argonauts negotiate: the Fleece, won fairly by the trials' terms, they will keep; Medea's fate shall be referred to a neutral king's arbitration. Medea, furious at Jason for entertaining her surrender even as a feint, is reconciled to a counter-plot of her own devising: she sends Apsyrtus gifts and a false message, luring him to a parley at Artemis's temple by night, alone, to plan (she pretends) her betrayal of Jason and return. Jason waits in ambush. As Apsyrtus arrives, Jason springs out and cuts him down — Apollonius's simile is of a butcher felling a great-horned bull — while Medea stands by with her veil turned away. Jason then performs, over the body, the archaic rite for spilt kindred blood: he licks the dead man's blood three times and spits it out, cuts off the corpse's extremities — the maschalismos, the ritual mutilation meant to disable a victim's vengeance — and the Argonauts fall on the leaderless Colchian escort and slaughter them. In this version the killing is Jason's, by Medea's contrivance, of an armed adult commander, under truce. Frazer's note to Apollodorus 1.9.23 records the tradition's third placement: in Sophocles' Colchian Women, on the scholia's testimony, Apsyrtus was murdered in Aeëtes' own palace — and Euripides, at Medea 1334, has Jason reproach Medea with killing her brother at the hearth, which aligns the tragedian with the palace version or with Apollodorus's child-murder, not with Apollonius's ambush. The versions cannot all be true of the same fiction, and the tradition never made them agree. What they agree on is the function: Apsyrtus dies so the Argo can escape, his death is kin-killing by or for Medea, and it is the one act of the voyage that the gods themselves mark for expiation.

The Return Routes: A Problem the Tradition Set Itself

Colchis, in Greek geography, is at the far end of an enclosed sea. A ship that has entered the Black Sea by the Bosphorus can leave, in reality, only by the Bosphorus — back through the Clashing Rocks, past every shore it has already sailed. But the saga's return cannot simply retrace the outward route: the Colchians are behind them, the story needs new wonders, and — the deeper problem — the return had already, in the tradition's older strata, acquired episodes that belong to the western Mediterranean: Circe's island, the Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, the Phaeacians, Libya. Those are Odyssey waters. Getting the Argo from the Phasis to Circe is a geographical impossibility on the real map, and the tradition solved it, characteristically, by redrawing the map. The solutions on record, assembled in Frazer's notes and the scholia and summarised in the older encyclopaedic tradition, are these.

Apollonius's Danube solution rests on a genuine piece of ancient geographical doctrine: the belief, respectable in Hellenistic geography, that the Ister divided, one branch flowing to the Black Sea and another to the Adriatic — an error generated by the real proximity of the Danube's tributaries to the Adriatic head and by the amber trade routes that crossed between them. His return is thus not whimsy but scholarship: the poem routes its heroes through the best map its century had, errors included. The deeper point belongs to the saga's transmission. The return's western episodes are older than any map that could accommodate them — Circe, in the Odyssey, is already Medea's aunt, her island already in the fabulous west — and each age's geography strained, visibly, to keep the episodes and lose the impossibility. The strain is the evidence: the episodes came first, and the routes were invented to reach them.

XIII. Circe's Purification, the Phaeacians, Libya, and Talos

Zeus's Anger and the Speaking Ship

The return's first governing event is theological (Apollonius 4.557–591; Apollodorus 1.9.24; paraphrased). Zeus, angered by the treacherous murder of Apsyrtus, drives the Argo off her course with storms; and the ship's Dodona timber speaks — in Apollodorus's plain report, the ship said that Zeus's wrath would not cease until Jason and Medea had journeyed to Circe and been purified by her for the murder. Apollonius gives the beam a full oracular utterance to the same effect and adds the threat that drives the rest of the book: without that purification, the voyage home will pass through Hades' own waters. Purification — katharmos — is the Greek institution for exactly this condition: homicide, above all kin- homicide, pollutes (miasma), and the polluted are cut off from altars, cities, and fair weather until a qualified purifier cleanses them with the established rites — water, sacrifice, the blood of a suckling pig let flow over the hands. The institution is historical Greek religion, attested across law, oratory, and tragedy; its appearance here, at the saga's moral centre, is register (b) of this article's method operating inside register (a): the myth is told in the real ritual grammar of the culture that told it.

Circe at Aeaea

Circe is Medea's paternal aunt — Aeëtes' sister, a child of Helios — and the purification scene at her island of Aeaea (Apollonius 4.659–752, paraphrased) is among the poem's most quietly terrible. Jason and Medea come to her hall as suppliants, sitting at her hearth in silence with eyes downcast, Jason's sword — the sword that killed Apsyrtus — planted in the earth before them: the ritual posture of blood-stained supplication, which Circe reads at a glance. She performs the rite in full: sacrifices, libations, the suckling pig's blood over their hands, prayers to Zeus the Cleanser and to the Erinyes, asking that their anger be laid to rest. The pollution is lifted. Then, the rite complete, she questions them — and Medea answers in the Colchian tongue, telling the whole story; and Circe, who has cleansed them as the law of suppliants required, dismisses them from her island with contempt for what the story contains: a daughter who betrayed her father, a kin-killing, a flight. She will not be a party to Medea's counsels, she says; let them go, and take their suffering with them. Apollonius's point is exact and worth stating in the article's own voice: purification removes pollution; it does not remove consequence. The cleansed pair sail on into a book that will end at Corinth. Apollodorus (1.9.24) compresses the whole matter to its ritual fact — they came to Aeaea, supplicated Circe, and were purified — and the compression itself shows what the handbook tradition thought essential: not the aunt's judgement, but the rite's performance.

The Western Gauntlet and the Phaeacian Wedding

From Circe's island the return runs the Odyssey's gauntlet in reverse (Apollonius 4.753–1057; Apollodorus 1.9.25; paraphrased). Past the Sirens — where Orpheus saves the ship as Phineus's chart had promised music would, striking up a counter-song on his Bistonian lyre that drowns the Sirens' voices; only Butes leaps overboard, and Aphrodite, who has her own plans for him, rescues him to Sicily. Through the strait of Scylla and Charybdis and past the Wandering Rocks, where Thetis and the Nereids, sent by Hera, take the ship through hand to hand like a ball in a dance — Peleus's wife repaying, in person, the expedition that carries her husband. Past Thrinacia and the Sun's cattle, untouched. And so to Corcyra, the Phaeacians' island, where the saga's second Colchian squadron — the searchers Aeëtes had sent out under threat — overtakes them at last and demands Medea's surrender from King Alcinous. Alcinous's judgement, in Apollodorus's crisp form (1.9.25), turns on the marriage bed: if Medea is still a maiden, she returns to her father; if she already knows Jason as a wife, she stays with him. Queen Arete, anticipating the verdict, has Jason and Medea married that night — in Apollonius, in the cave that Corcyra still showed as Medea's cave, on a bed spread with the Golden Fleece itself. The wedding the saga has promised since Hesiod is thus performed in haste, under legal threat, on the prize of the quest: the poem means the reader to notice all three of those things. The disappointed Colchians, afraid to go home empty-handed to Aeëtes, settle among the Phaeacians and in Illyria — another aition, planting Colchian colonies on the Adriatic map.

Libya: The Ship Carried

The return's last great ordeal is its strangest (Apollonius 4.1225–1629; paraphrased in summary). Driven south by a storm from the Peloponnese, the Argo is thrown into the Syrtes, the shoal-water off Libya where the tide strands ships in a wilderness of sand and shallow sea with no exit — a death, in ancient seamanship, of thirst in sight of land. The heroes carry the ship — on their shoulders, for twelve days and nights, across the desert to Lake Tritonis, exactly as Pindar's older version had them do. In the desert Mopsus the seer dies of a serpent's bite; the Argonauts meet the Hesperides, mourning Heracles who had passed this way the day before and killed their dragon; and at the lake, the god Triton, in the guise of a young man named Eurypylus, gives Euphemus the clod of Libyan earth — the seed of Cyrene, the gift Pindar's ode exists to celebrate — and, taking the Argo's steering-oar and a tripod in return, tows the ship out through the lake's hidden channel to the open sea. The episode is the tradition's own claim about its geography of memory: the return through Libya is how the saga explained a Greek colony in Africa. It is also the last time the whole company acts as one body. From Libya the poem is a coda of single episodes, and the first of them is Crete.

Crete and Talos

Crete was guarded, in the saga, by the Bronze Age's own sentinel (Apollonius 4.1638–1693; Apollodorus 1.9.26; paraphrased). Talos was a bronze man — in Apollodorus's variants, a survivor of the Bronze Race, or a bronze bull; in the main tradition, a figure made by Hephaestus and given by Zeus to Europa, or to Minos, to guard the island — who ran the circuit of Crete three times a day and drove off landing parties by hurling rocks. The Argo, desperate for water and anchorage, came under his barrage. Talos's construction is the saga's most famous piece of anatomy: he had a single vein, running from his neck to his ankle, carrying the ichor — the gods' lifeblood — and closed at the ankle by a single bronze nail. What Medea does to him is the return voyage's last demonstration of her art, and the versions divide instructively. Apollonius has her work from the ship, at a distance: she calls on the Keres, the death- spirits, fixes Talos with the evil eye of her incantations, and maddens him; as he heaves a rock, he grazes his ankle on a sharp stone, tears the membrane, and the ichor runs out of him like molten lead until he falls like a great pine. Apollodorus gives the alternatives in a mythographer's row: Medea drove him mad with drugs, or she promised to make him immortal and drew out the nail herself, so that the ichor gushed out and he died — or, some say, Poeas shot him in the ankle. The variants agree on the essential: the bronze giant, invulnerable to the heroes' weapons, is killed by the woman's knowledge of the one point where his making can be undone. It is the Colchian pattern repeated at the voyage's end — and the last service Medea performs for the Argonauts. After a night ashore, the company sails for Aegina, and then, in Apollodorus's summary, between Euboea and Locris to Iolcus: the whole voyage, he says, was completed in four months. The handbook's flat sentence, after four books of epic storm and wonder, is its own kind of aition — the tradition insisting, against all its own marvels, that this was a journey men could have made.

XIV. Medea the Engine: Magic, Pharmaka, and the Cauldron

Strip the saga of Medea and it stops. Without her drug, Jason burns at the plough; without her stratagem, the Earthborn kill him; without her night-knowledge, the dragon keeps the Fleece; without her contrivance, Apsyrtus's squadron retakes the ship; without her art, Talos keeps Crete. No other figure in Greek myth — not Circe, not Helen, not Athena herself in the Odyssey — carries a heroic enterprise so completely. This section examines what, exactly, her power consists of in the texts, because the texts are precise about it in a way the popular image of “the witch” is not.

What Medea Knows: Pharmaka

Medea's art, in the Greek, is pharmakeia: the knowledge of pharmaka — drugs, in the full Greek breadth of the word, which covers medicine, poison, dye, and charm without distinguishing them, because the knowledge that distinguishes them is the knowledge itself. Apollodorus, in the plainest sentence any source gives on the subject, calls her simply a witch in Frazer's rendering — the Greek is a practitioner of pharmaka — daughter of Aeëtes and Idyia (1.9.23). Her attested repertoire, across the sources, is consistent and specific. She knows protective unguents: the Promethean drug of Apollonius Book 3, one day's proof against fire and iron, harvested in a midnight rite under Hecate. She knows soporifics: the sprinkling that puts the sleepless dragon to sleep — a dragon whose whole definition is that it does not sleep. She knows herbs and their gathering: Sophocles' lost Root-Cutters showed her at the work, and the verb rhizotomein, root-cutting, became the ordinary Greek word for the professional gatherer of medicinal plants — the rhizotomist was a real trade, and Medea is its mythical patroness. She knows incantation: the songs with which, in Apollonius, she calls on Sleep and Hecate over the dragon and fixes Talos from the ship. And she is placed, consistently, under two divine patrons: Hecate, whose priestess the tradition calls her and at whose temple she meets Jason, and her own grandfather Helios, the Sun, from whom her dragon-drawn chariot will come at Corinth. Graf's analysis, followed here, adds the structural point: Medea is an enchantress from afar — her power is inseparable from her foreignness. Colchis is where Greek geography put the edge of the world; a princess from the Sun's own house at that edge is, by the map's own logic, the person in the story who knows what no Greek knows. Her magic is the East's knowledge, as the saga's Greece imagined it: older, darker, vegetable, female, and indispensable.

// THE PROMETHEAN DRUG, AS THE POEM GIVES IT //

Apollonius 3.844–868 (paraphrased): the plant grows on the Caucasus crags where Prometheus's liver drips ichor; it has a saffron-coloured flower and a root like freshly cut flesh; the earth shook and Prometheus groaned when Medea first cut it. She harvested it after bathing in seven perennial streams, calling seven times on Brimo (Hecate), dressed in black, at midnight, digging a pit — and the goddess's hounds bayed underground as she worked. It is stored in a casket among her drugs. The passage is the fullest herb-gathering rite in surviving Greek epic, and its details — night, running water, dark dress, invocation, the plant's resistance — match the working lore of the historical rhizotomists as Theophrastus and the later herbals record it. Myth and trade practice are, at this point, describing the same ceremony from opposite ends.

The Cauldron: Rejuvenation, the Ram, and the Daughters of Pelias

Medea's most famous instrument is not a wand but a pot. The rejuvenation sequence — told entire by Ovid (Metamorphoses 7.159–349) and in handbook form by Apollodorus (1.9.27) — belongs to the story's Iolcus chapter (Section XV), but its logic belongs here, because the cauldron is where her art shows both its faces in a single operation. In Ovid's full telling (paraphrased, with one quotation below): Jason, home from the voyage, finds his father Aeson alive but sinking under extreme old age, and begs Medea to take years from his own life and give them to his father. She refuses the transfer and undertakes the greater work: nine nights of gathering in her dragon chariot across the herbal geography of Greece — Othrys, Olympus, Ossa, Pelion, Oeta — then the rite itself, at twin turf altars to Hecate and Hebe (Youth), with black sheep, milk, wine, and invocations to the powers of earth not to hasten the old man's end. Aeson's throat is cut, the old blood drained, and Medea's brew — a cauldron of roots, seeds, stones, frost gathered by moonlight, the wings of a screech-owl, the entrails of a werewolf, and a stag's liver among its catalogue of ingredients — is poured into his mouth and his wound. His white hair and beard turn black as he sits up; leanness and pallor leave him; he stands up a man forty years younger. Ovid's catalogue of ingredients is Augustan poetry revelling in its own excess, and is paraphrased here as such; the rite's structure — drain the old blood, refill with the potent brew — is the tradition's constant core.

Then the same operation is run again, as a demonstration and a trap. Medea, feigning a quarrel with Jason, takes refuge with Pelias, wins his daughters' confidence by telling them of Aeson's renewal, and offers their aged father the same gift. To prove it, she stages the saga's most quietly horrifying experiment. In Brookes More's translation of Ovid (Metamorphoses 7.297–321, Perseus text), she tells the daughters, in the words quoted here in part:

“That you may have strong confidence in this my promised boon, the oldest leader of your flock of sheep shall be changed to a lamb again by my prized drugs.” — OVID, METAMORPHOSES 7, TRANS. BROOKES MORE (1922)

The oldest ram of the flock is brought — horns curled round hollow temples, barely a stain of blood when her Thessalian knife cuts its throat — and plunged into the bronze cauldron with her potent herbs. The limbs shrink, the horns melt away with the years, a thin bleating sounds from inside the pot, and a lamb leaps out and runs frisking to find an udder. The daughters, amazed, press her to perform the same for their father. Three nights later, Medea sets over the fire a cauldron of clear water and herbs of no potency. Pelias and his guards are sunk in a magic sleep. The daughters, brought to his bedside, are exhorted by Medea — in Ovid's staging, with a speech that weaponises their filial love: draw your swords, let out the old blood, that she may refill the veins with young. The daughters cut their father to pieces and boil him. Nothing rises from that cauldron. Apollodorus (1.9.27) gives the same sequence in five clauses — she persuaded the daughters to make mince-meat of their father and boil him, promising to make him young again by her drugs, and to win their confidence she cut up a ram and made it into a lamb by boiling — and the handbook's flatness is, if anything, colder than Ovid's poetry.

This article states the episode's structure plainly, because the brief of the hub requires honesty about it: the ram demonstration is a true demonstration of a real power, used as the credential for a murder. Medea can do the thing. She has done it, for Aeson, in earnest. The Pelias operation is not a fake that fails; it is the same technique, deliberately run with inert ingredients, on a man she intends to kill, by the hands of the people who love him most. The horror the Greeks found in Medea is not the horror of a charlatan. It is the horror of competence without loyalty — knowledge that works, placed entirely at the service of the knower's grievances. That is also, for this archive's alchemy wing, the reason Medea matters beyond mythology: the cauldron that renews, the brew that transforms a base old thing into a young precious one, is the mythical template the Alexandrian art will inherit — and the alchemical tradition's own reading of Medea and the Fleece is treated, as this hub's plan provides, in Alchemical Allegory, not here.

// MEDEA AND AESON, BEFORE OVID //

The rejuvenation of Aeson is older than Ovid by half a millennium. The lost epic Nostoi (Returns) is credited in the scholia with Medea's boiling of Aeson back to youth; Pherecydes and Simonides are named in the same testimonia; and a red-figure stamnos of about 470 BCE, noted in the Corinth scholarship consulted for this article, already shows Medea with the ram before Pelias's daughters. The ram-and-cauldron scene is thus archaic-classical iconography, not a Roman embroidery — Ovid gives the fullest text of a story Greek art had been telling for five hundred years.

XV. Iolcus and Corinth: Pelias, and Euripides' Medea

The Return to Iolcus

The homecoming is an anti-climax the sources do not soften (Apollodorus 1.9.27, paraphrased). While the Argo was away, Pelias, despairing of its return, had moved against Jason's family: Aeson, forced toward death, asked leave to die by his own hand and drank the blood of a sacrificial bull; Jason's mother cursed Pelias and hanged herself; Pelias killed even the infant Promachus she left behind. Jason delivered the Fleece to the man who had murdered his house — and bided his time. He sailed with the chiefs to the Isthmus and dedicated the Argo to Poseidon — the saga's one great Argonautic dedication, weighed in Section XVII — and then set Medea to devise Pelias's punishment. The cauldron did the rest (Section XIV). Acastus, Pelias's son, buried his father, held the famous funeral games that Greek art loved to paint, and expelled Jason and Medea from Iolcus. The quest's terms are thus fulfilled to the letter and void in substance: the Fleece was fetched, the usurper is dead, and Jason is king of nothing — an exile in the city he delivered. The saga's political moral is exact: the errand was the punishment. Pelias sent Jason to die fetching the Fleece, and fetching it was the means by which Pelias died; and the instrument in both directions was Medea.

Corinth: The Ten Years and the End

Corinth gives the story its second act and its permanent form. Apollodorus (1.9.28, paraphrased) states the outline the whole tradition shares: Jason and Medea lived at Corinth happily for ten years; then Creon, king of Corinth, betrothed his daughter Glauce to Jason; Jason married her and divorced Medea. Medea invoked the gods by whom Jason had sworn — the oaths of Colchis, sworn at Hecate's temple and on the ship — and sent the bride a robe steeped in poison: Glauce put it on and was consumed by fierce fire, and Creon, rushing to embrace her, died with her. Then the children: Mermerus and Pheres, her sons by Jason, died — and here the tradition forks for the last time, and the fork is the subject of the next paragraphs. Medea fled Corinth in a chariot drawn by winged dragons, a gift of her grandfather the Sun, to Athens; married Aegeus; bore Medus; plotted against the returned Theseus; was expelled; and ended her Greek career, in Apollodorus's closing notice, back in Colchis, where she killed her uncle Perses, who had deposed Aeëtes, and restored her father to his throne. The woman who betrayed Colchis for Jason dies, in the handbook's geography, as Colchis's restorer. The saga's circle is closed with an exactness no novelist would dare.

Euripides' Medea (431 BCE): The Version That Ate the Others

In 431 BCE, at the City Dionysia in Athens, Euripides produced the Medea — as part of a tetralogy that placed third, last, in the competition. It is the single most influential telling of any part of the Argonaut saga, and it covers, in one day at Corinth, only the catastrophe. Its opening is the saga's own epitaph, spoken by the Nurse. In David Kovacs's Loeb translation, quoted here in its first lines:

“Would that the Argo had never winged its way to the land of Colchis through the dark-blue Symplegades! Would that the pine trees had never been felled in the glens of Mount Pelion and furnished oars for the hands of the heroes who at Pelias' command set forth in quest of the Golden Fleece!” — EURIPIDES, MEDEA 1–6, TRANS. DAVID KOVACS (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY)

Euripides' play (paraphrased in what follows) assumes the audience's knowledge of everything this article has narrated and spends its force on the moral accounting. Creon exiles Medea and her sons, fearing her cleverness and her drugs; she wins one day's grace. Aegeus of Athens, passing through Corinth on his way home from an oracle about his childlessness, promises her sanctuary in Athens in exchange for her drugs' help in fathering a child — the scene that secures her escape route and the play's Athenian audience's uncomfortable complicity. Her revenge is staged in ascending order: the poisoned robe and crown sent by the children's hands to the princess, whose death, and Creon's, a messenger narrates in the play's set-piece of horror; and then the act Euripides either invented or raised to centrality — Medea kills her own two sons, with her own hand, inside the house, after the great monologue (lines 1021–1080) in which she argues herself to the deed and back and to it again. She appears above the house in the Sun's dragon chariot, beyond Jason's reach, refuses him even the burial of the children — she will bury them herself in Hera Akraia's sanctuary, she says, and institute a solemn festival and rites in atonement — prophesies his squalid death (struck by a falling timber of the Argo, in the tradition the play invokes), and is carried away. Jason is left on stage with nothing: no wife, no bride, no sons, no city, no future.

The filicide requires the article's closest source-discipline, because the popular account — “Medea killed her children” — conceals a genuine scholarly problem. The Corinthian tradition before and beside Euripides told the children's deaths differently. Pausanias, describing Corinth in the second century CE (2.3.6–11, paraphrased from the testimonia assembled in the scholarship consulted), reports the grave of Mermerus and Pheres shown at Corinth and the local account: the children were stoned by the Corinthians — in one form, for bringing the fatal gifts to Glauce; and Apollodorus himself, immediately after giving Medea as their killer, records the other tradition (1.9.28): that she left the infants as suppliants on the altar of Hera Akraia, and the Corinthians took them from the altar and killed them. A third Corinthian account, attached to the early epic poet Eumelus, has Medea herself hide her children in Hera's temple as each was born, believing that so hidden they would become immortal; the hope failed, the children died, Jason discovered it and sailed away in anger, and Medea left Corinth. A scholium on the Medea preserves the ancient counter-charge in its rawest form: that the Corinthians, whose own ancestors did the killing, bribed Euripides with five talents to transfer the deed to Medea. The bribe story is anecdote, not evidence; but the multiplicity of Corinthian versions is evidence, and the scholarly consensus reported by Gantz and by Donald Mastronarde's commentary is that Euripides' deliberate, willed filicide — the mother killing her sons as an act of revenge, knowing and choosing it — is in its full form Euripides' own terrible innovation, or at least his radical transformation of materials that had distributed the guilt among Corinthians, accident, and failed immortalisation. The innovation conquered the tradition so completely that Seneca's Roman Medea, and every Medea since, works in its shadow. This article records the conquest and the prior distribution together, because the hub's rule is that a version's victory does not retroactively make it the myth.

// WHAT EURIPIDES DID NOT INVENT //

Medea the drug-worker, the bride's death by Medea's gift, the dragon chariot, the flight to Aegeus's Athens: all are older than 431 BCE, attested in the testimonia and the art. What the play adds is not the machinery but the will: a Medea who debates her revenge in full view, names its cost to herself as greater than its cost to Jason, and pays it anyway. Earlier Medeas are dangerous. Euripides' Medea is dangerous and right about her grievances — the play gives her the saga's best arguments (her speech on the lot of women, lines 214–266, paraphrased here, remains its most quoted) — and that combination, not the body count, is why the play has never stopped being performed.

XVI. The Fleece Debated in Antiquity: Gold-Washing and the Other Rationalisations

The question “what was the Golden Fleece, really?” is not a modern impertinence. Antiquity asked it, in print, with named authors and competing answers, and the debate is part of the saga's history. This section reports the ancient rationalisations as what they are — ancient authors' explanations of their own inheritance, register (c) of this article's method — and keeps them strictly separate from the question of what, if anything, happened. A rationalisation is evidence for the rationaliser's world. It is not, without more, evidence for the myth's origin.

Strabo: Fleeces in the Gold Rivers

The most durable of the ancient explanations comes from the geographer Strabo, writing under Augustus and Tiberius. In Book 11 of the Geography (11.2.19), describing the Soanes — the Svans of the high Caucasus, in the hinterland of Colchis — he reports their method of winning the gold that the mountain torrents bring down. In H. L. Jones's Loeb translation, quoted here in its central sentence:

“It is said that in their country gold is carried down by the mountain torrents, and that the barbarians obtain it by means of perforated troughs and fleecy skins, and that this is the origin of the myth of the golden fleece.” — STRABO, GEOGRAPHY 11.2.19, TRANS. H. L. JONES (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY)

The technique Strabo describes is real and long-lived: a fleece, or a sheepskin stretched in a sluice or laid in a gold-bearing stream, traps heavy gold particles in its wool as the water washes the gravel through; dried and shaken or combed out, the fleece yields its gold. Ethnographic reports from Svaneti describe the practice surviving into modern times, and modern geological fieldwork in the region — the studies of the Svaneti gold sands published by Georgian geologists in the 2010s, consulted in summary for this article — confirms the premise on which Strabo's whole passage rests: the rivers of the Colchian hinterland do carry placer gold, in quantities worth working, and Colchis's fame for gold in the Greek world was a fact of trade, not of fable. Strabo himself draws the economic moral a few sections earlier (11.2.17, paraphrased): the wealth of the Colchian region in gold, silver, iron, and copper is motive enough for the expedition of the Argonauts and for Phrixus's journey before them — Jason, in the geographer's dry handling, was after the country's metals all along. Appian, the second-century CE historian, gives the same fleece-in-the-stream explanation in his Mithridatic Wars (paraphrased), which shows the rationalisation's currency in the imperial period.

The discipline required here is the one this hub applies to every attractive correspondence. Strabo's account proves three things: that gold-washing with fleeces was practised in the Caucasus in his day and reported by him as the myth's origin; that Colchis was genuinely rich in metals and genuinely traded with the Greek world; and that educated antiquity preferred an economic kernel for its most famous quest-object. It does not prove that the myth arose from the practice. The golden ram is already in the saga's oldest recoverable stratum, the fleece is golden because the ram was a marvel — golden-woolled, winged, speaking in some versions — and a marvellous ram needs no sluice-box to explain him. The direction of influence may equally run the other way: a region famous for a golden fleece in myth may have had its ordinary gold-washing noticed, named, and remembered precisely because the myth taught travellers what to look for. This article records Strabo's explanation at its full ancient authority and leaves the derivation undecided, as the evidence leaves it.

The Other Ancient Explanations

Strabo's was not the only ancient answer, and the alternatives — collected in the mythographic tradition and in modern surveys of the fleece's interpretation, among them the study of the Colchian gold tradition in the journal Phasis (13–14, 2010–2011), consulted for this article — map the range of what antiquity was willing to believe about its own story. The guardian made literal: in one euhemeristic line, preserved in the later mythographers, the “dragon” was a man — a Taurian named Draco, set by Aeëtes to guard the treasure — and the Fleece itself a royal treasury's emblem; the marvels dissolve into court personnel. The statue: another Byzantine-preserved explanation made the Fleece a golden statue which the historical Phrixus — who reached Colchis by boat, not by ram — presented to Aeëtes. The book on skin: an anonymous mythographic collection of the second century CE, reported in the Phasis survey, held that the Fleece was in reality a book written on a skin — a parchment — containing the rules of the chemical art of making gold, and that this was the true object of the expedition. That last explanation is the seed of the alchemical reading of the whole saga, and by this hub's division of labour it is flagged here and treated where it belongs: the alchemists' own reading of the Fleece is treated in this hub's Alchemical Allegory article, which follows the fleece-as-book from its late antique seed through the medieval and early modern allegorists. Diodorus Siculus (4.47–48, paraphrased) deserves separate mention as antiquity's most systematic Argonaut rationaliser: his prose version keeps the voyage and strips the wonders into plausibility — Medea's drugs become the plausible skills of a king's daughter versed in poisons and antidotes, the marvels become reputations — producing a saga a Hellenistic gentleman could believe without embarrassment. Diodorus is valuable to the historian precisely as Strabo is: not as a window on the Bronze Age, but as a document of the moment when the myth had to be defended against its own incredibility, and was defended by translation into economics and pharmacology.

ATTESTED AS PRACTICE

Gold-washing with fleeces and perforated troughs in the Caucasus streams: reported by Strabo (11.2.19) and Appian; the technique is ethnographically long-lived in Svaneti; the region's placer gold is geologically real.

ATTESTED AS ANCIENT OPINION

That the practice explains the myth: Strabo's stated derivation. That the Fleece was a statue, a guarded treasure, or a book on skin containing the rules of gold-making: later mythographic explanations, each attested as an opinion held, none attested as a fact established.

NOT ESTABLISHED

That the myth originated in the gold-washing practice; that any historical expedition underlies the saga in a recoverable form; that the fleece-as-book preserves an archaic truth rather than a late antique reader's ingenuity. The correspondences are real. The derivations are arguments, and are labelled as such wherever this hub meets them.

XVII. Cult and Historical Footing: Iolcus, Corinth, and the Dedications

This section states the saga's footing in cult and history at its real strength, which is this: real places, real rites attached to the story's persons, real dedications attached to the story's objects — and no evidence that the voyage occurred. The three clauses are equally important, and the hub's rule is that none of them may be dropped to make the others more comfortable.

Thessaly and Iolcus: The Saga's Home Port

The saga is, by its oldest attachments, Thessalian and Minyan. Iolcus, Orchomenus in Boeotia, and the Minyai — the people whose name Apollonius gives the Argonauts themselves — form its home triangle: Jason is Iolcan, the Phrixus story is Orchomenian-Boeotian in its Athamas half, and the crew's core is Thessalian. Thessaly's claim on Medea is of the same kind: the Thessalian tradition remembered her, in the scholarship's summary, as a beneficent healer — the rejuvenator of Aeson — and Thessaly in historical times was proverbial in Greece as the land of pharmaka and of women skilled in them, the witches of Thessaly being a standing figure of classical literature. The correspondence between the saga's drug-queen and the region's historical reputation is noted here as a correspondence: it shows the myth and the folklore of a place reinforcing each other, and it does not date either. Of cult practice at Iolcus itself tied to Jason, the record is thin — hero-cult of the saga's persons in Thessaly is asserted in the local traditions Pausanias and the scholia retail, but no Argonaut sanctuary at Iolcus has the documentary or archaeological standing of, for example, the Eleusinian Telesterion in this hub's Mysteries article. The article says so plainly rather than inflating the dossier: the saga's Thessalian footing is literary and folkloric, strong as tradition, weak as cult archaeology.

Corinth: Medea's Children and Hera Akraia

Corinth is the saga's strongest cult site, and the evidence is of the best kind this subject offers: a named sanctuary, a named rite, and a topographer who saw the monuments. Pausanias, in his Corinthian book (2.3.6–11, paraphrased from the testimonia in the scholarship consulted), describes the grave of Medea's children, Mermerus and Pheres, shown at Corinth near the Odeon, and retails the aetiology the Corinthians attached to it: the children were stoned by the Corinthians; their deaths brought a plague on Corinth's infants; and an oracle ordained expiation — annual sacrifices to the dead children, and a statue of Deima, Terror, set up in their honour. The scholia on Euripides add the rite's most distinctive detail (scholion on Medea 264, paraphrased): seven boys and seven girls of Corinth's noblest families served a year in the sanctuary of Hera Akraia — Hera of the Height, whose great archaic sanctuary stood across the gulf at Perachora — wearing black, with their hair cut, in expiation for the children's deaths; the observance, the testimonia say, lasted until the Roman destruction of Corinth in 146 BCE. Euripides' own play ends by instituting the rite from the stage: Medea, departing, announces that she will bury the children in Hera Akraia's precinct and establish a solemn festival and rites for the time to come, in atonement for the killing (Medea 1378–1383, paraphrased). Francis Dunn's study of that ending (“Euripides and the Rites of Hera Akraia”, 1994), consulted for this article, draws the careful conclusion the evidence supports: the play's aition and the Corinthians' actual rites for Medea's children are genuinely connected, but the connection is complicated — the rites honoured children whose deaths the local tradition did not lay on their mother, and Euripides' aition annexes a real Corinthian expiation to his own, different, version of the deaths. Cult here is not the myth's illustration. It is the myth's rival and elder: a rite for dead children at Corinth, of real antiquity, around which the competing stories crystallised.

Corinth's other Medea attachments are of the same topographical kind. Pausanias also reports the Fountain of Glauce at Corinth, where Creon's daughter was said to have thrown herself to escape the poisoned robe's burning — a named water-source carrying the story in the city's fabric. And the Corinthian epic tradition, Eumelus's Korinthiaka, made the city's claim genealogical as well as ritual: Aeëtes himself, in the Corinthian version, was originally king of Ephyra — Corinth's ancient name — who received the land from his father Helios and emigrated to Colchis; Medea, invited back by the Corinthians as Helios's heir, came from Iolcus with Jason and ruled Corinth as its queen, with Jason as her consort. Simonides (fr. 545 in the testimonia) is cited for the same Corinthian queenship. The local tradition, in short, did not know Euripides' refugee Medea at all: it knew a hereditary queen, a benefactor whose children died in Hera's temple in a failed rite of immortalisation. That the same city could hold the queen's genealogy, the children's expiatory cult, and (in Athens' theatre) the filicide, without any authority to reconcile them, is the normal condition of Greek myth in its cult setting, and this article's Corinthian dossier is its best illustration.

Dedications and Monuments of the Voyage

The saga generated dedications the way it generated aitia, and the record, stated at its real strength, is as follows. The Argo herself: in Apollodorus's account (1.9.27), Jason, returned, sailed with the chiefs to the Isthmus of Corinth and dedicated the ship to Poseidon — the god of the Isthmian sanctuary and games. Other traditions kept her elsewhere: a long later tradition placed the Argo's remains on display, and the constellation Argo Navis carried her into the sky in the catasterisms. A dedication is a thing a story says was made; no timber of the Argo was ever produced for inspection, and the article records the dedication as the saga's internal act, not as a museum record. The Hylas rite at Cius in Mysia (Section IX) is the dossier's strongest item of the other kind: an annual ritual search, with a priest calling the lost boy's name at the spring, attested in sources independent of the epic and still performed in the Roman period — a rite whose aition is the saga and whose existence is not merely the saga's claim. The anchors and altars of the route — the stone anchor of the Argo shown at Cyzicus in the local tradition Apollonius retails (1.955–960, paraphrased: the Argonauts left their first anchor stone under Athena's direction and took a heavier one), the altar of Apollo of Embarkation at Pagasae, the altar of Radiant Apollo founded at Anaphe on the return (Apollodorus 1.9.26) with its rite of women's ritual jesting — belong to the middle category: local monuments and customs of the historical Greek world, attached to the saga by the communities that kept them. A Greek city that showed the Argo's anchor was making a claim about itself — that the heroic age had touched its shore — and the claim, not the anchor, is the historical fact.

// THE HISTORICAL QUESTION, ANSWERED AT ITS REAL STRENGTH //

Was there a voyage? The honest answer has three parts. First, Greeks really did sail to Colchis: from the seventh century BCE the Black Sea was ringed with Greek colonies, Colchian gold, linen, and timber really were traded, and a Thessalian ship reaching the Phasis was, by the archaic period, an ordinary event. Second, the saga as told — the fifty heroes, the dragon, the trials — contains no element that can be dated, located, or corroborated as an event, and its oldest witnesses already treat it as a story from the heroic generation before Troy, which is to say from a past already mythical to them. Third, the fashionable middle position — that the saga is a “memory” of Bronze Age or early archaic exploration of the Black Sea — is an interpretation, attractive and unprovable, of the correspondence between the saga's route and the real geography of Greek expansion. This hub's ruling, applied throughout, is Burkert's discipline applied to this dossier: myth is a traditional tale, told about a past the tellers did not witness; its correspondences with real geography, real metals, and real rites are data about the tale's long life in a real world, and they are not a log-book. No claim that the voyage happened is made anywhere in this article.

XVIII. Later Versions: Valerius Flaccus, the Orphic Argonautica, and the Allegorical Afterlife

Valerius Flaccus: The Saga in Flavian Rome

Rome retold the voyage in Latin epic once, and the retelling breaks off in the middle of the story. Gaius Valerius Flaccus composed his Argonautica in the Flavian period — the poem is dedicated to Vespasian (emperor 69–79 CE) and its author is generally placed in the decades around 70–90 CE; Quintilian, writing about 95 CE, already mourns him as a recent loss (Institutio Oratoria 10.1.90, paraphrased). Eight books survive, of which the eighth stops in mid-course, shortly after the winning of the Fleece, with the Colchian pursuit under way and Medea wavering — the poem is unfinished, whether by the author's death (the usual inference) or by interruption, and the return voyage was never written. Valerius works from Apollonius as his base text and rewrites him in Virgil's shadow: the divine machinery is Romanised (Jupiter delivers, in Book 1, a prophecy of Roman history that frames the voyage as the first opening of the seas, with empire as its distant consequence), the storm and battle pieces are expanded in the Flavian epic manner, and Medea's falling in love, in Books 6–8, is re-staged with Juno taking a hand in person — disguised as Medea's sister Chalciope — where Apollonius had used the arrow of Eros at a distance. The chief interest of the poem, as its modern readers from the Loeb editor J. H. Mozley onward have agreed, is precisely that Medea: Valerius slows Apollonius's psychology down still further, and his unfinished eighth book leaves her, uniquely in the tradition, suspended forever between Colchis and Corinth — the epic breaking off inside her hesitation. The poem was lost to the Latin Middle Ages and recovered by Poggio Bracciolini from a manuscript at St Gall in 1416–1417, an event of the Renaissance recovery of antiquity; Renaissance editors even supplied the missing ending from Apollonius, in Giovanni Battista Pio's continuation of 1519, before scholarship learned to prefer the fragment as it stands.

The Orphic Argonautica: Orpheus Tells It Himself

The last ancient Argonaut epic is a impersonation. The Orphic Argonautica (Argonautica Orphica) is a Greek poem of 1,376 hexameter lines, narrated in the first person by Orpheus himself, addressed to his disciple Musaeus, and composed — on the scholarly dating associated with M. L. West's study of the Orphic poems (1983) — no earlier than the fourth century CE, and possibly later: a work of late antiquity wearing the mask of the saga's own singer. Its narrative follows Apollonius's in outline — it is, as Mooney's commentary on Apollonius put it in a judgement the scholarship has kept, in all probability an imitation of Apollonius by a versifier of the early Christian era — with the emphases an Orphic narrator requires: Orpheus is first in the catalogue in fact as Apollonius had made him first in form; his songs do the voyage's heavy work (it is Orpheus's singing, in this telling, that masters the Sirens, stills storms, and moves the natural world at every crisis); the Argo is claimed as the first ship ever built; and the return is routed by the wild northern course — up the Tanais and round the outside of the world — that Section XII's table records. The poem's value to this article is documentary in a specific sense: it shows the saga, in its last ancient century, being absorbed into the Orphic corpus — the body of poems, hymns, and theogonies that circulated under Orpheus's name and that late antique religious readers treated as revealed antiquity. The Argonauts end their ancient career as an Orphic scripture's supporting cast, which is a fate the Mysteries article's account of Orpheus prepares the reader to understand. Jason Colavito's complete English translation (2011), the first full professional one, is the form in which the poem is now most accessible, and its introduction's account of the poem's strange reception — prized for centuries as among the oldest Greek poems, demoted by nineteenth-century analysis to late antiquity — is the cautionary tale of the whole dossier in miniature.

The Allegorical Afterlife, in Its Place

Beyond the retellings stands the third register: the saga read as a coded something else. Antiquity began it — Section XVI's fleece-as-book is a second-century seed — and the Byzantine and medieval centuries grew it: the Fleece as the prize of the soul's voyage, Jason as everyman or as Christ-figure, Medea as Nature or as the art of arts, and, most consequentially for this archive, the whole expedition as an allegory of the alchemical work, with the Fleece as the gold-making secret won from the dragon-guarded East. Pernety's mytho-hermetic dictionary of the eighteenth century, which reads Greek fable systematically as veiled alchemy, gives the Argonauts one of its fullest entries. By this hub's standing division of labour, that entire tradition — the alchemical allegory of Jason, Medea, and the Fleece, from the late antique book-on-skin to Pernety and beyond — is treated in the hub's Alchemical Allegory article, and is flagged here, once, so that the reader knows where in the archive it lives and that its absence from these sections is a shelving decision, not a judgement. What belongs to this article's closing is the literary line: Virgil's Dido, formed on Apollonius's Medea; Seneca's Medea, which out-Euripides Euripides in cold deliberation; Ovid's heroine, whose letter to Jason (Heroides 12) gives the abandoned Medea her own first person; and the unbroken stage and operatic line from the Renaissance to the present, in which Medea — not Jason — is the saga's surviving protagonist. The tradition's verdict is inscribed in that survival. The quest gave the story its name and its ship. The Colchian princess gave it its life.

// JASON'S OWN AFTERLIFE //

For completeness, the hero's end as the tradition gives it (Pausanias 2.3 and the scholia, paraphrased): Jason, ruined at Corinth, returned to live obscurely, and died as Medea had prophesied in Euripides' play — struck by a rotting timber that fell from the beached Argo as he slept or sat in her shadow. The ship that Athena built and Dodona's oak spoke through ends the saga as it began it: the vessel outlasts the captain, and has the last word.

A closing note on the evidence, in the archive's usual form. The narrative of this article is a narrative of texts: Apollonius's poem, Apollodorus's handbook, Pindar's ode, Euripides' tragedy, and the Roman and late antique versions, each identified at the point of use and each kept distinct where they disagree — on the crew, on Apsyrtus, on the return route, and on the deaths of the children. The cult dossier of Section XVII rests on Pausanias's topography, the Euripidean scholia, and the Hylas and Samothracian testimonia, and is stated at the strength those sources bear. The rationalisations of Section XVI are ancient opinions, quoted and paraphrased as opinions. Nothing in this article asserts that Jason sailed; everything in it asserts that the Greeks told, for a thousand years and in their greatest poems, that he did — and that the telling, examined closely, is evidence enough for the hub that houses it.
