I. One Leaf: What This Article Is About

Every other major monument of Greek alchemy is a book, or the fragment of one: Zōsimos of Panopolis survives in memoirs, letters, and visions; pseudo-Democritus in excerpts; the commentators in lectures. The Chrysopoeia of Cleopatra — the “Gold-making of Cleopatra”, Greek Chrysopoiia Kleopatras — survives as one leaf: folio 188 verso of the Codex Marcianus graecus 299 in the Biblioteca Nazionale Marciana in Venice, the oldest surviving Greek alchemical codex, copied at the end of the tenth or the beginning of the eleventh century. The leaf contains no continuous prose at all. It contains symbols, drawings, and captions: a serpent devouring its own tail around the words ἓν τὸ πᾶν, “one is the all”; a second, concentric-ring diagram carrying the same doctrine at greater length; small star and crescent signs; and drawings of distillation apparatus — a two-armed still of the dibikos type and a device of the kērotakis type. Nothing else in the Greek corpus looks like it, and no image from the corpus has been reproduced more often. The ouroboros on this leaf is, for most modern readers, simply the ouroboros: alchemy’s universal emblem enters the visual record here.

This article treats the leaf at the resolution that fame deserves and that evidence permits — which are not the same resolution, and keeping them apart is the article’s method. The leaf will be read element by element: the codex that carries it, the editions that printed it, the serpent as drawn, the three words inside it, the double ring beside it, the apparatus sketches, the star signs, and the texts — a dialogue and a teaching treatise — that travel under the same name in the same codex. Then the name itself will be examined, because “Cleopatra” is not the Ptolemaic queen and the leaf proves nothing about any queen. Then the serpent will be followed backward to its first attested appearance, on a shrine in the tomb of Tutankhamun more than a millennium and a half earlier, and forward through the Byzantine copies that degraded its inscriptions, the Renaissance engravings that multiplied it, and the modern readings that have made it mean everything from the unity of matter to the structure of the psyche. The article closes where it must: on the discipline of the single witness. Everything said here rests on one page, copied in the tenth or eleventh century from a lost original of unknown date, and the hub’s claims are sized to that fact throughout.

ATTESTED IN THE RECORD

The leaf exists: Marcianus graecus 299, folio 188v, in Venice. Its contents — the ouroboros enclosing ἓν τὸ πᾶν, the concentric-ring diagram with its two inscriptions and the central signs for gold, silver, and mercury, the star and crescent signs, and the apparatus drawings — are described consistently from Berthelot’s first reproduction (1885) through Sheppard’s figure-by-figure study (1962) to the present literature. The codex that carries it is dated, by handwriting, to the end of the tenth or beginning of the eleventh century.

INFERENCE, LABELLED AS SUCH

The leaf’s lost original is conventionally placed in the Alexandrian milieu of the first to third centuries CE — the formative stratum of Greek alchemy, the world of Maria and the pseudo-Democritus tradition. The placement is an inference from the leaf’s company in the corpus and from the doctrine its inscriptions share with datable texts. No colophon, no dated exemplar, and no ancient citation fixes the Chrysopoeia itself to any century.

INTERPRETATION, LABELLED AS SUCH

What the serpent means — the unity and self-sufficiency of the matter of the work, the endless cycle of the process, the return of the many into the One — is interpretation, ancient and modern. H. J. Sheppard, in the standard study of the symbol, states the limit plainly in paraphrase: neither the early alchemists nor their later commentators gave explicit reasons for adopting the ouroboros. Section VIII keeps every reading under its own label and its own date.

NOT RECOVERABLE

The original leaf; its author; its date; the hand that first drew the serpent; and any biography whatsoever of the “Cleopatra” whose name the leaf bears. What survives is a Byzantine copy of a diagram, a name, and a milieu. This article claims exactly that much, and no more, for its subject.

A word on the title’s key term. Chrysopoeia is Greek for gold-making: the production of gold, most commonly by the transmutation of base metals such as lead, with argyropoeia, silver-making, as its twin. It is the defining ambition-word of the art from Zōsimos to the seventeenth century, and the hub’s origins article treats chrysopoeia as a workshop practice — the yellowing of metals, step by step — in its Section X. The leaf that bears the word as its title contains no recipe for making gold. It contains the doctrine under which gold-making was thought possible, drawn as pictures, and the instruments with which the work was done, drawn beside it. Doctrine and instruments on one page: that compression is the leaf’s character, and the reason a single folio can stand, as it does in this hub, for the whole Alexandrian art in miniature.

II. The Codex: Marcianus graecus 299 and the Leaf’s Place in It

The leaf cannot be understood apart from its book, and its book has already been given a full treatment in this hub: the transmission article, Sections V and VI, describes the Byzantine codices and the Marcianus in detail — its dating by handwriting to the end of the tenth or beginning of the eleventh century, its decorated opening, its table of contents listing the collection’s pieces (fifty-two entries in the count commonly reported from that table), the quires it lost and the quires its binder inverted, and the later hands that annotated it for centuries. This section does not repeat that treatment. It takes up the narrower question the leaf itself poses: where does folio 188v sit inside the codex, what surrounds it, and what does its position among working recipes tell us about what the leaf was for?

The codex is an anthology, not an author’s book. Its contents span the three levels into which the scholarship divides the Greek corpus. From the oldest, pseudepigraphic stratum: works and fragments connected with pseudo-Democritus and Hermes, the lists of alchemical symbols, and the Dialogue of the Philosophers and Cleopatra. From the second level: the works of Zōsimos, dispersed through the codex in the interwoven manner the transmission article describes — the Authentic Memoirs in part, the chapters to Eusebia and to Theodorus, the Book of Sophe and Final Count materials. From the third level: the commentators, including Olympiodorus, Christianus, and the nine alchemical lectures of Stephanus of Alexandria. The Chrysopoeia leaf stands inside this company, on folio 188v, as one item among the anthology’s fifty-two — and its neighbours matter. The codex around it is, in large part, a working book: recipes for colouring metals, instructions for apparatus, procedures with charges, heats, and durations. The leaf’s mysticism, such as it is, does not float in a devotional miscellany. It sits among bench instructions, in a book compiled for readers who also needed to know how long to heat a vessel and what to seal it with.

That placement is the first fact to hold against a persistent modern misreading. Because the ouroboros became famous as a free-floating symbol — detached, reproduced, tattooed, printed on book covers — the leaf is easily imagined as a piece of pure symbolism, a mandala avant la lettre. In its codex it is nothing of the kind. It is a page of apparatus and mystical designs, in H. J. Sheppard’s description of folio 188v — his words, paraphrased here from his 1962 study, call the Chrysopoeia a page of apparatus and designs of which the serpent figures form part. The drawings of the dibikos and the kērotakis-type device are not decoration added to a symbol. They are the leaf’s other half: the same page that asserts the unity of the All also shows the vessels in which the All was to be worked. Symbol and bench, on one leaf, in one hand — the conjunction is the document, and any reading that keeps only the serpent has kept only half of it.

// THE LEAF’S ADDRESS //

Venice, Biblioteca Nazionale Marciana, Codex Marcianus graecus 299 (cited in the scholarship as M; fuller shelfmark Marcianus graecus Z. 299), folio 188 verso. The codex reached Venice among the Greek manuscripts gathered by Cardinal Bessarion (1403–1472) and bequeathed by him to the Venetian Republic — the founding collection of the Marciana. A later copy of the Chrysopoeia material is reported at Leiden University in the modern reference literature; the Marcianus leaf remains the primary and oldest witness, and it is the witness every description in this article follows.

One further codicological fact governs everything that can be said about the leaf’s drawings, and the transmission article states it for the codex’s apparatus illustrations generally: the pictures in M are Byzantine copies of late-antique exemplars, drawn by scribes who copied what they saw rather than what they used. Between the lost original of the Chrysopoeia — whenever in the first centuries CE it was composed — and the leaf in Venice stand perhaps seven centuries of copying that no surviving intermediate witness lets us inspect. The serpent’s scales, the ring inscriptions’ letter-forms, the stills’ proportions: all of it reaches us through copyists. Section III shows what happened to the leaf’s sister-images in the later codices, where the inscriptions can be watched degrading from copy to copy — Sheppard’s word for the later forms is “degenerate” — and the lesson transfers: folio 188v is the best witness to the Chrysopoeia, not a transparent window onto its original. Best is not the same as perfect, and the distinction is load-bearing for Sections V to X.

Finally, the leaf’s company includes the two prose texts that share its author’s name, and a reader of the codex meets all three Cleopatras in one binding. The Dialogue of the Philosophers and Cleopatra stands in the codex’s oldest stratum; the Comarius material, in which the philosopher and high priest Comarius teaches Cleopatra the divine art and she in turn instructs the philosophers, travels with the same tradition; and a short tract On Weights and Measures is transmitted under her name in the wider corpus. Section XI treats those texts on their own terms. Here it is enough to fix the codicological point: the Byzantine compiler who gathered this anthology understood the leaf, the dialogue, and the teaching as one dossier — a name, a doctrine in pictures, and a doctrine in words. The modern habit of treating the ouroboros as a stand-alone emblem is a habit the codex itself does not share.

III. How the Leaf Reached Print: Berthelot, Ruelle, and the Modern Descriptions

For roughly eight centuries after its copying, the leaf’s readers were the codex’s readers: the annotating hands of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, the copyists of the Paris manuscripts, and the Venetian custodians of Bessarion’s bequest. Its modern life begins, as the whole corpus’s modern life begins, with Marcellin Berthelot. In Les Origines de l’alchimie (Paris, 1885) Berthelot reproduced the leaf — Michèle Mertens’ bibliography of the codex records the reproduction precisely: “Cleopatra’s goldmaking” (M, fol. 188v), reproduced in Berthelot, Les origines de l’alchimie, plate I, and again in Mertens’ own edition of Zōsimos (plate II). Two years later Berthelot and Charles-Émile Ruelle published the Collection des anciens alchimistes grecs (Paris, 1887–88), the three-volume edition — introduction, Greek text, French translation — that made the corpus a public object; the Chrysopoeia is treated there at page 128 of the first volume in the standard citation, and the leaf’s line drawing, reproduced from the Collection’s introduction (p. 132, fig. 11 in the citation the American Research Center in Egypt gives the plate), became the form in which scholars who never saw Venice learned the page. The transmission article’s Section VIII gives the Collection its full assessment — a foundation with faults, made quickly from the principal codices — and this article simply registers the consequence for its subject: the most reproduced alchemical page in the world was, for its first modern century, known almost entirely through a nineteenth-century French line drawing of a tenth-century Byzantine copy of a lost ancient original. Three mediations deep, and famous at every depth.

The modern descriptive literature on the leaf is short enough to name whole, and naming it whole is the honest way to show how thin the leaf’s scholarship is compared to its fame. F. Sherwood Taylor’s survey of Greek alchemy (1930) placed the Chrysopoeia and its author in the corpus’s formative stratum and remains a reference point for the leaf’s dating discussion. Jack Lindsay’s The Origins of Alchemy in Graeco-Roman Egypt (1970) gave the Cleopatra material its fullest narrative treatment in the older literature, including the sentence on the ouroboros quoted in Section V. The single most important study of the leaf’s imagery is H. J. Sheppard’s “The Ouroboros and the Unity of Matter in Alchemy: A Study in Origins” (Ambix 10.2, 1962, pp. 83–96), which describes the manuscript ouroboroi figure by figure — the Marcianus serpent and ring diagram as his Figures 2 and 4, the Paris variants as Figures 3 and 5 — prints the ring inscriptions in Greek with translations, and argues the symbol’s Egyptian derivation. Sections V to VIII and XIII to XV of this article rest on Sheppard’s descriptions more than on any other single source, and cite him at each use. Lawrence Principe’s The Secrets of Alchemy (2013), the standard modern introduction, treats the leaf within its account of Graeco-Egyptian alchemy’s founding claims — the unity of matter above all — and is followed here for the framing of what the image meant to the tradition that made it. Matteo Martelli’s laboratory scholarship, cited throughout the companion Maria the Jewess article, supplies the apparatus context of Section IX.

Two cautions about this literature belong in the record. The first is that the leaf has never had a modern critical edition of its own in the way Zōsimos has: there is no Mertens for Cleopatra. The Greek of its inscriptions is read from Berthelot’s printing and from the descriptions of Sheppard and his successors, not from a dedicated edition with a full apparatus of variants — one reason this article quotes the inscriptions in Sheppard’s published translations, named as his, rather than offering fresh renderings dressed as established text. The second caution is about images. The reproductions in circulation — Berthelot’s line drawing, the cleaned-up ouroboros of a thousand websites — smooth the leaf. They regularise the serpent’s scales, tidy the letter-forms, and drop the apparatus sketches and star signs that share the page, until what circulates is a logo rather than a document. A reader who wants the leaf should go to a facsimile of folio 188v itself, or to Berthelot’s plate with its mediation confessed, and should treat every simplified ouroboros — including any mental picture formed from this article’s prose — as a copy of a copy, at one more remove.

Three features of the inventory deserve emphasis before the reading begins, because each one disciplines a temptation. First, the leaf is mostly pictures. V. The Ouroboros: The Serpent as Drawn

Begin with the animal. The serpent of folio 188v is drawn as a closed ring: the body curves full circle, the head meets the tail, and the tail is in the serpent’s mouth — the tail-devourer, which is what the Greek word means: ouroboros, from oura, tail, and -boros, eating. The drawing is, by the standards of the symbol’s later career, strikingly plain. Sheppard, describing it as his Figure 2, calls it — in the paraphrase this article uses for his description — a simple figure of the serpent devouring its tail, with the inscription in the centre. No wings, no legs, no crown, no second serpent, no dragon’s crest: the elaborations that the Paris copyists and the Renaissance engravers would add are all absent. What the Marcianus scribe drew is a snake, a circle, and three words.

Two features of the drawing carry interpretive weight, and both must be stated as features of this copy before they are read as meanings. The body is two-toned. In the drawing as the descriptions record it, one half of the serpent — the upper half, including the head — is dark, and the other half is light: black and white halves meeting at the two points where the circle’s halves join. The body is scaled and dotted. The scribe marks the serpent’s scales along its length, and the standard reproductions show the small marks — dots and star-like specks — that fleck the body and its field, the kind of detail a copyist preserves because his exemplar had it, whether or not he knew what it signified. Neither feature is captioned. The leaf does not say “the dark half is night” or “the dots are stars”, and this article does not say it either, in the leaf’s voice. What can be said is what the features are, and then, separately and labelled, what readers have made of them.

The making began early and has never stopped. The two-toned body has been read, in the modern literature, as a figure of duality within unity: the dark and light halves as the paired opposites — the tradition’s own vocabulary offers body and spirit, fixed and volatile, the two natures whose joining the work requires — held inside a single continuous creature that is devouring and nourishing itself at once. One widely repeated modern gloss proposes a Gnostic duality of existence for the black and white halves; the gloss is modern, its analogy to other two-toned cosmic diagrams is the glossator’s, and it is recorded here as a specimen of the reading the drawing invites, not as the drawing’s meaning. The scales and dots have attracted less commentary, and the honest treatment is the shorter one: they are the scribe’s rendering of a serpent’s body and its ornament, continuous with the way the later Paris serpents are given elaborate scale-work in red, yellow, and green (Section XV), and any finer significance — stars for a cosmic serpent, seed-dots for a scattered spirit — is speculation, labelled as such wherever this hub has met it and not adopted here.

“a snake curving around with its tail in its mouth (eating itself) is an obvious emblem of unity of the cosmos, of eternity, where the beginning is the end and the end is the beginning” — JACK LINDSAY, THE ORIGINS OF ALCHEMY IN GRAECO-ROMAN EGYPT (1970), ON THE CHRYSOPOEIA OUROBOROS

Lindsay’s sentence, quoted here from his published text, is the classic modern reading, and its confidence is worth examining as carefully as its content. “Obvious” is the word a symbol earns after two thousand years of readers have agreed to find it so; nothing about a snake eating itself is obvious until a tradition teaches the eye. What Lindsay’s reading gets right, and what the leaf itself supports, is the geometry: a closed curve has no beginning or end, and a creature that feeds on itself needs nothing outside itself. Those two properties — endlessness and self-sufficiency — are in the drawing, not imposed on it. Everything beyond them, including the word “cosmos”, is the reader’s contribution, Lindsay’s included. Section VIII gathers the contributions and dates them. This section’s serpent is the plainer animal: dark half and light half, scales and dots, mouth on tail, three words inside. The plainer animal is the evidence. The rest is the history of its readers.

VI. ἓν τὸ πᾶν: The Three Words Inside the Serpent

ἓν τὸ πᾶν hen to pan — “one is the all”; also Englished “the all is one”

Inside the serpent’s ring stand three Greek words: hen, one; to, the; pan, all. The phrase is the diagram’s own text — not a caption about the serpent but words the serpent encloses and, in the drawing’s logic, protects and proclaims at once. This article quotes the Greek directly, as the brief of the leaf permits and its fame requires, and follows Sheppard’s rendering, “the One is the All”, alongside the plainer “one is the all” used in its headings; the equally common Englishing “the all is one” reverses the Greek’s order to catch its sense, and the reader will meet all three forms in the literature. The differences among them are differences of English, not of doctrine: each renders a three-word assertion that the One and the All are the same thing stated twice.

The assertion was old when the leaf’s original was drawn, and its age matters to the reading, because the leaf is quoting a formula, not coining one. Sheppard’s study traces the formula’s pedigree through the Greek philosophical tradition: the doxographers attribute to Xenophanes of Colophon (sixth–fifth centuries BCE) the position that being is one and whole — Theophrastus’ summary of Xenophanes, preserved by Simplicius, is Sheppard’s exhibit — and Hippolytus preserves the compressed form of the claim, that the whole is one. A fragment Clement of Alexandria transmits as Orphic carries the same thought in verse: all things are one, and in that one all things revolve, fire and water and earth. By the second century CE the affirmation of cosmic unity was, in Sheppard’s summary, widespread: the Hermetic Poimandres has the form “all things are one”, and Gnostic works quoted by Clement carry their own variants. The leaf’s three words thus arrive with a half-millennium of philosophy behind them — Ionian monism, Eleatic argument, Stoic pantheism, Hermetic revelation — compressed into the space inside a snake. Whoever composed the Chrysopoeia did not need to explain the phrase. Its readers already carried it.

Within alchemy specifically, the formula has a second, nearer pedigree, and it is the pedigree that ties the leaf to the corpus around it. A saying in the same form is attributed, in works transmitted by Zōsimos, to the early authority Chymes (also written Chemes): in the modern summary of that attribution, the all is one, and through it the all has come into being; the all is one, and if the all did not contain all, the all would not have come to be. Olympiodorus, commenting on the tradition, quotes a like saying of Chemes and connects it with Parmenides. The leaf’s ring inscription, treated in the next section, is this Chymes-form of the doctrine expanded and set in a circle: the Chrysopoeia did not invent the One-and-All formula for alchemy; it gave the formula its enduring picture. The distinction is the section’s point. ἓν τὸ πᾶν is not Cleopatra’s coinage, and the hub does not credit it to her. What the leaf contributes is the placement: the oldest surviving image in which the formula of unity is locked inside the serpent that would carry it through fifteen centuries of alchemical books.

// WHY THREE WORDS CAN BE A DOCTRINE //

For the working alchemist, the unity of the All was not a metaphysical ornament. It was the licence for the work itself. If all metals are modifications of one underlying matter — differing by imposed qualities, colours, and proportions — then changing one metal into another is not a violation of nature but a rearrangement within it: strip the imposed qualities back to the common matter, and impose the qualities of gold. The three words inside the serpent are that licence, stated at the length of a breath. The hub’s origins article shows the licence at work in the recipes; this leaf shows it as an emblem.

A last philological note, because the leaf’s afterlife turns on it. The phrase is grammatically an equation without a verb: one — the all, the copula understood. Greek monist formulas of this shape assert identity, not resemblance: not “the one is like the all”, nor “the one rules the all”, but the one is the all. The serpent’s body performs the same assertion geometrically — one continuous creature whose end is its beginning — so that words and image say one thing in two media, each glossing the other. That mutual gloss is the leaf’s rhetorical engine, and it is why the emblem proved so portable: a reader who could not construe the Greek could still read the snake, and a reader who could construe the Greek found the snake already explained. Section XV follows the portability. Here the words stand as the scribe wrote them, inside the ring, unexplained by any caption — because in the leaf’s world, they were the explanation.

VII. The Double Ring: The Fuller Formula of the One and the All

Beside the serpent — in Sheppard’s ordering of the leaf’s figures, as his Figure 4 — stands the leaf’s second diagram, and it is the serpent translated into pure geometry. Sheppard describes it, in the account this section follows, as a stylised representation of the ouroboros consisting of three concentric circles. The creature has become rings; what the rings carry is text. The outer ring contains the long inscription; the inner ring contains a second, shorter inscription; and the central circle encloses the symbols for gold, silver — the silver sign, Sheppard notes in a copyist’s detail worth preserving, bearing a tail — and mercury. The diagram is a target of doctrine: two sentences in orbit around the three metals that matter most.

The outer inscription, in the Greek as Sheppard prints it from the Marcianus, runs: ἓν τὸ πᾶν καὶ δι’ αὐτοῦ τὸ πᾶν καὶ εἰς αὐτὸ τὸ πᾶν, καὶ εἰ μὴ ἔχοι τὸ πᾶν, οὐδέν ἐστι τὸ πᾶν. Sheppard’s published translation, quoted here in full because it is the rendering this article has verified in his text, reads:

“One is All and through it is All, and to it All, and if it has not All, All is nothing” — H. J. SHEPPARD, AMBIX 10.2 (1962), TRANSLATING THE OUTER RING INSCRIPTION OF MARCIANUS GR. 299, FOL. 188V

The sentence is the three words of Section VI unfolded into a cycle. The One is the All; the All comes through it; the All returns to it; and the conditional clause closes the system with a threat: if the One has not the All — if the unity fails to contain everything — then the All is nothing. Read as process rather than as metaphysics, which is how the leaf’s bench context invites it to be read, the sentence describes a circulation with no remainder: everything in the work derives from the one matter, everything returns to it, and nothing may be lost on the way — a doctrine of the sealed vessel stated as a doctrine of the cosmos. The Chymes saying of Section VI is the same sentence in prose dress, and the resemblance is not coincidence: the ring is the tradition’s formula, versified by geometry. The renderings in circulation differ in small ways — “by it is All” for “through it”, “toward it” for “to it” — and the differences are recorded here rather than silently harmonised, because the hub’s rule is that a famous formula is quoted in a named translation or not at all.

The inner ring’s inscription is stranger, and it is the leaf’s most distinctive sentence. In Sheppard’s printing of the Greek: εἷς ἐστιν ὁ ὄφις ὁ ἔχων τὸν ἰὸν μετὰ δύο συνθέματα — in his translation, quoted here:

“One is the Serpent having its venom according to two compositions” — H. J. SHEPPARD, AMBIX 10.2 (1962), TRANSLATING THE INNER RING INSCRIPTION

The ring diagram, that is, explains the serpent diagram. The serpent is one; its venom — ios, the word that also names the green corrosion on bronze and stands in the art’s colour vocabulary near the final purple stage, iōsis — is disposed according to two compositions, two arrangements or compounds. Sheppard’s interpretation of the sentence, given here as his interpretation and labelled as such, is that the cyclic nature of the unity is dualistic in structure: the All separates out of the One and returns into the One, and the serpent’s twofold venom figures the two phases of that cycle, the going-forth and the return — the ambivalence of a symbol that is at once the agent of dissolution and the agent of perfection. Other readers have taken the two compositions more chemically, as the two tincturing agents or the paired volatile substances of the work; the leaf does not decide between them, and this article reports Sheppard’s reading as the standard one without adopting any reading as the inscription’s settled sense. What the sentence establishes, on any reading, is that the leaf’s author thought of the serpent as one thing with a twofold action — which is also, not accidentally, a description of the art’s central reagent, the divine water of the Zōsimos tradition, one agent that both kills and revives the metals it touches.

And at the centre of the rings, the point of the whole construction: the signs for gold, silver, and mercury. The placement repays a slow look. The two sentences — the All circulating through the One, the serpent’s twofold venom — do not enclose an abstraction. They enclose the work’s materials in their sign forms: the perfect metal that is the goal, the white metal that is the waystation, and the volatile spirit-metal that is the agent. Doctrine outside, materials inside; the formula as a wall around the bench. A modern reader who knows the planetary-metal correspondences of the later art will recognise the triad’s other face — sun, moon, and Mercury — and the leaf permits that recognition without requiring it: the signs are the metals’ signs, in a corpus whose symbol lists, preserved in the same codex, key those signs to the metals plainly. The ring diagram is thus the leaf’s thesis in its most compressed form: the One-and-All doctrine is not about the cosmos in general; it is about these three substances, and what may lawfully be done among them. That last sentence is this article’s reading, offered as a reading. The diagram itself says only what its rings say — which, Sections VI and VII have now given entire, in the leaf’s own words, at the leaf’s own length.

VIII. What the Image Asserts: Interpretation, Ancient and Modern

This section gathers the interpretations of the leaf’s imagery — its own section, under its own label, because the hub’s method requires that what the image asserts be filed separately from what the image shows. Sections V to VII described. This section interprets, and every paragraph names whose interpretation it is.

The Ancient Frame: Unity of Matter

The interpretation nearest the leaf is the one its own inscriptions perform. The serpent and the rings assert, in words, the unity of the All and its circulation through the One; and Sheppard’s study, whose very title joins the two terms — “The Ouroboros and the Unity of Matter in Alchemy” — argues that the symbol is to be understood in general as figuring the essential unity of matter, and in particular the Work itself, the alchemical process conceived as having neither beginning nor end. On this reading the image is a premise drawn as a picture: because matter is one, transformation is possible; because the process is a cycle, it must be run whole, to its return. Sheppard is careful, and this article repeats his care as its own: the early alchemists and their commentators left no explicit statement of why the ouroboros was adopted, so the unity-of-matter reading is an inference from the inscriptions the symbol carries and from the doctrine of the texts around it — a strong inference, held by the field, and an inference still.

The Process Reading: The Cycle of the Work

A second family of readings, ancient in spirit and modern in formulation, takes the serpent as a picture of the process rather than of the matter: the work as a circulation in which the material is dissolved and reconstituted, killed and revived, driven off as vapour and returned as condensate, until the cycle has perfected it. The reading draws strength from the leaf’s own bench context — the kērotakis of Section IX is a machine for exactly such a circulation, its vapours rising, condensing, and returning for hours on a governed fire — and from the outer ring’s insistence that nothing be lost: if the One has not the All, the All is nothing, which at the bench means that a charge whose spirit has escaped is a charge ruined. Principe’s account of the early art, in The Secrets of Alchemy, frames the founding claims of Graeco-Egyptian alchemy in these terms — one underlying matter, worked by cyclical operations toward perfection — and this article follows that frame here, as interpretation with a named modern sponsor, consonant with the inscriptions and not entailed by them.

The Self-Sufficiency Reading: A System That Feeds on Itself

A third reading fastens on the mouth and the tail. The serpent does not hunt, graze, or receive: it consumes itself and is consumed by itself, a closed economy of one creature. Read against the work, the figure asserts the self-sufficiency of the matter of the work — the prima materia that contains its own agent, its own solvent, its own perfection, needing nothing imported from outside the vessel. The later tradition’s maxims run in this channel — the stone is found in the matter itself; nature is perfected by nature — and the origins article’s Section XVIII treats the most famous of them, the aphorism that nature delights in nature, nature conquers nature, nature masters nature, in the pseudo-Democritus tradition. The leaf’s serpent is that aphorism’s zoological form. Whether the leaf’s composer intended the maxim or merely the geometry, the tradition read the two together, and the reading is recorded here as the tradition’s.

// READINGS THIS ARTICLE DOES NOT ADOPT //

Three popular readings are excluded from the leaf’s dossier, and the exclusions are stated so that a reader who meets them elsewhere knows their standing. The serpent as a portrait of the philosopher’s stone personified: a later emblematic development, not stated on the leaf. The two halves as a deliberate counterpart to East Asian twofold cosmic diagrams: an analogy of modern writers, with no historical channel proposed, let alone shown. And the serpent as a coded chemical recipe, dot by dot and scale by scale: no ancient or Byzantine witness reads it so, and the leaf’s own captions — the only authorised gloss — speak of unity, not of charges and heats. The leaf is dense enough without decryption.

What survives the exclusions is a layered but disciplined picture. At the centre, the inscriptions’ own claim: one is the all, circulating, self-contained, ruinous to lose. Around that, the ancient frame the corpus supplies: the unity of matter as the work’s licence. Around that, the readings of the tradition and its modern historians: the cycle of the process, the self-sufficiency of the matter, the Work without beginning or end. And at the outside, clearly marked as the outermost layer, the twentieth century’s psychological reading — Carl Jung’s ouroboros, the archetype of a wholeness that devours and renews itself, treated in Section XV as part of the emblem’s afterlife. The layers are not rivals. They are strata, and the hub’s whole method, here as everywhere, is to let each stratum keep its date.

IX. The Apparatus Drawings: Stills and Vessels on the Same Leaf

Turn from the diagrams to the drawings, and the leaf changes register. On the same folio as the serpent and the rings, the scribe drew apparatus: in the standard summaries’ wording, a dibikos and an instrument similar to a kērotakis. The transmission article’s description of the leaf names the same pair — drawings of the dibikos and of a kerotakis-type apparatus — and the companion article on Maria the Jewess treats both device families in full: the dibikos as the two-armed member of the still family in its Section XIII, the kērotakis as the sealed reflux chamber in its Sections X and XI. This section’s business is narrower: what the presence of these two drawings, on this leaf, beside these diagrams, establishes — and what it cannot establish.

THE DIBIKOS — THE TWO-ARMED STILL

The dibikos is the still of Maria’s family with two delivery arms instead of the tribikos’ three: a cucurbit or pot below, a still-head — the ambix, ancestor of the word alembic — above it, and two tubes delivering condensed vapour to two receivers. Zōsimos distinguishes the two-beaked and three-beaked forms explicitly in the texts Maria’s article follows. On Cleopatra’s leaf the form is drawn in outline: the earliest surviving picture, as distinct from description, of the still family at work.

THE KĒROTAKIS-TYPE DEVICE

The kērotakis is the sealed reflux apparatus in which a volatile charge — sulphur, mercury, an arsenic compound — is heated below a suspended metal leaf or plate, so that the rising vapours bathe the metal, condense on it, and return, for hours, in a closed cycle of tincturing. The leaf’s second apparatus drawing is of this type: a vessel-form answering to the descriptions Zōsimos transmits under Maria’s authority. Its presence beside the ouroboros is the leaf in miniature — the cycle as a machine, drawn next to the cycle as a serpent.

The kinship with Maria’s apparatus is real, and it must be stated at the right strength. The dibikos and the kērotakis are the two signature devices of the tradition Zōsimos expounds under Maria’s name; their appearance on a leaf attributed to Cleopatra places the leaf inside that same Alexandrian apparatus culture — the circle the sources call the school typified by Maria and Comarius, in which Cleopatra’s name also moves. It does not make Cleopatra the inventor of either device, and the later tradition’s habit of crediting her with the invention of the alembic itself is recorded in Section XII at its proper standing: an attribution, not a record. What the drawings certify is membership, not priority. The leaf’s author — whoever hid behind the royal name — worked in a milieu where these vessels were the common instruments of the art, and expected the leaf’s readers to recognise them at outline scale, without labels worth the name. A diagram that sketches a still the way a modern chemist sketches a flask is evidence of a readership fluent in glassware.

The drawings are also the leaf’s strongest internal evidence against reading it as pure mysticism, and the point deserves its full weight. A page that intended only contemplation would not need a two-armed still. The apparatus drawings anchor the leaf’s doctrine in operations: the unity of the All, asserted by the serpent, is to be demonstrated in vessels where vapours rise and return; the serpent’s twofold venom has its bench counterpart in the paired arms and paired receivers of the dibikos; the ring’s warning — lose the All and the All is nothing — is the operator’s first rule for a luted joint. None of these correspondences is stated on the leaf, and this paragraph offers them as the reading the leaf’s composition invites, not as captions it contains. But the composition itself is a fact: mysticism and bench-work share the folio because, for the tradition that made the leaf, they were one document’s two hands. The hub’s Egyptology division makes the same point from inside Zōsimos’ corpus, where the visions meditate on the processes the apparatus performs; the leaf makes it in a single glance.

// HAZARD NOTE — THE DRAWINGS ARE NOT PLANS //

The apparatus of this tradition concentrated mercury vapour, sulphur fumes, and arsenic compounds in sealed, heated vessels; those agents are cumulative poisons, and the kērotakis in particular was designed to maximise a metal’s exposure to them. The drawings on folio 188v are Byzantine copies at outline scale, without dimensions, materials schedules, or seals specified. They are historical evidence for the forms of the devices, as the Maria article’s Section XIV establishes for the whole drawing tradition. They are not buildable plans, and nothing in this article should be read as instruction to construct or operate such apparatus.

A final limit, from the companion scholarship, applies to both drawings equally. No physical example of a dibikos or a kērotakis survives from the centuries of Maria or Cleopatra or Zōsimos; the evidence for the devices is textual and pictorial only, and the pictures — as Section II noted — are copies made by scribes who drew what they saw. The leaf’s stills therefore certify the forms the tradition remembered, at the fidelity Byzantine copying affords: family resemblance, named parts, the volatile-below and body-above arrangement. Within those limits they are precious — the earliest pictures of chemical apparatus that survive anywhere in the Greek tradition, on the same leaf as its most famous symbol. Beyond those limits, a drawing that is asked to certify dimensions or performance is being asked for testimony it cannot give.

X. Stars, Crescents, and the Eight-Banded Star

The leaf’s remaining population is its smallest: a set of star and crescent signs, grouped on the page among the diagrams and drawings. The standard descriptions record an eight-banded star — an eight-pointed or eight-rayed star form — among Cleopatra’s symbols on the leaf, and crescent shapes set above or beside the star groupings. Small as they are, these signs have attracted a reading disproportionate to their size, and the reading must be given with its label on: in the modern account from which the descriptions descend — the survey of women in chemistry by Marianne Offereins and Renate Strohmeier, summarising the leaf — it is believed that the drawing of the star symbols and the crescent shapes is a pictorial depiction of turning lead into silver. Believed by whom, on what argument, the summary does not fully say; the reading is plausible in shape — crescents for the moon’s metal, silver; a star-grouping for a process staged toward it; lead as the art’s universal starting body — and it remains a reading. This section records it as the leaf’s most repeated piece of small-scale exegesis, at exactly that strength.

What can be said without the reading is still worth saying, because the signs are not random ornament. The Greek corpus possessed a developed notation for the metals and substances of the work — the symbol lists preserved in the Marcianus itself key the signs for gold, silver, mercury, lead, and the rest — and the planetary correspondences that the later art would systematise, seven planets to seven metals, are already the notation’s background in the Alexandrian milieu: gold under the sun’s sign, silver under the moon’s crescent, mercury under its own. A leaf that sets crescents and stars among its diagrams is writing in that notation, at the level of a practitioner’s shorthand: the signs are the vocabulary in which the bench kept its books. The eight-banded star itself resists a single gloss in the sources this article has read, and the resistance is reported rather than overcome: renewal and regeneration are the senses modern popular accounts assign it, without ancient warrant that this article can verify, and the hub does not adopt them. Sometimes a sign on a copied leaf is a sign whose key its copyists have lost. The honest inventory entry is the one Section IV gave: present, grouped, read by some as a lead-to-silver depiction, unexplained by any caption.

// HOW TO WEIGH A SMALL SIGN //

Ask three questions of any mark on the leaf. Is it in the notation the corpus itself keys — the symbol lists in the same codex? Then it may be read at the list’s strength. Is it glossed by a text that travels with the leaf — the dialogue, the Comarius material? Then it may be read at that text’s strength. Or is its sense supplied by a modern writer’s sense of fitness? Then it is that writer’s reading, and it travels under his name, not the leaf’s. The lead-to-silver reading of the stars and crescents passes the first test in part — crescent and metal signs are keyed vocabulary — and the third test wholly. It is filed accordingly.

The small signs also complete the leaf’s portrait as a working page. Serpent for the doctrine, rings for the formula, stills for the operations, stars and crescents for the materials and their celestial keys: the folio is a one-page aide-mémoire for a practitioner, the whole art on a leaf that could be pinned above a bench. That characterisation is this article’s synthesis, offered as such. It explains the leaf’s compression better than the alternative — a devotional icon that happens to include glassware — and it matches the codex’s own placement of the leaf among working texts. A page meant to be used wears its meaning the way folio 188v does: in pictures a fluent reader could unpack at a glance, and no reader since has fully unpacked.

XI. The Texts Around the Pictures: Dialogue, Comarius, and the Promises

The leaf does not travel alone under its name. Three texts are related to Cleopatra in the tradition, and two of them stand in the same codex; this section treats them because the leaf’s first readers met them together, and because the dialogue in particular supplies the verbal world — the idiom of waters, corpses, and awakenings — in which the leaf’s pictures were read. The three are: the Chrysopoeia itself; a tract On Weights and Measures transmitted under her name, the tradition’s most practical Cleopatra, concerned with the standardisation of the art’s quantities; and the Dialogue of the Philosophers and Cleopatra, the substantial prose work of the dossier. The dialogue’s attribution must be stated first and plainly: the reference literature is explicit that the dialogue, though it exists and though Cleopatra is its speaker, cannot be attributed to her as an author. It is a text in which Cleopatra teaches, composed by the tradition that revered her — the same distinction the Maria article draws for the Dialogue of Maria and Aros, and the same distinction Section XII generalises for the whole dossier.

The Dialogue: Cleopatra Instructing the Philosophers

The dialogue’s frame is a school scene raised to liturgy. A company of philosophers — Ostanes, the Persian sage of the pseudo-Democritus legend, among them, anachronistically as the Egyptological summaries note — gathers before Cleopatra as before a throne, and puts to her the art’s central riddle in the form of a question about the dead. In F. Sherwood Taylor’s published translation, made from Berthelot’s French, the philosophers ask — quoted here in part, in Taylor’s rendering — how “the blessed waters visit the corpses lying in Hades fettered and afflicted in darkness”, and how the medicine of life reaches them and wakes them as if from sleep. The corpses are the metals: dead bodies in darkness, imprisoned spirits within them, awaiting the waters that will visit, dissolve, and revive them. Cleopatra’s answer, in the same translation, begins not with metals but with plants: she bids the philosophers look at the nature of plants, whence they come — some down from the mountains, some up from the valleys — at the seasons and days for gathering them, at the air that ministers to them and the divine water that gives them drink. The teaching method is analogy from growth: as the plant dies into the earth and rises in its season, so the body in the vessel dies under the waters and rises in colour. Her summary of the waters’ action, again in Taylor’s rendering, is the dialogue’s most quoted sentence in the scholarship: “The waters, when they come, awake the bodies and the spirits which are imprisoned and weak.”

Jack Lindsay’s verdict on the dialogue is the other sentence of the scholarship that this article quotes, because it has governed the text’s modern reputation: he calls it, in his published words, “the most imaginative and deeply felt document left by the alchemist”. The verdict is Lindsay’s, and a cooler description is available — a Byzantine-shaped redaction of an early dialogue, in which a legendary teacher expounds the death-and-revival of metals in the idiom of vegetation and resurrection — but Lindsay’s warmth is itself evidence of what the text does to its readers, and the hub records both. What the dialogue establishes for the leaf is narrower and firmer than either verdict: in the Cleopatra dossier’s own prose, the work is figured as waters visiting imprisoned bodies and waking them — dissolution and revival, the two phases of the serpent’s twofold venom, the going-forth and return of the ring’s formula. The leaf’s pictures and the dialogue’s words are one teaching in two media, whoever wrote either of them.

Comarius: The Teacher Behind the Teacher

Behind Cleopatra, in the dossier’s own genealogy, stands a teacher. The Book of Comarius presents itself — in the full style the tradition gives it — as the teaching of Comarius, philosopher and high priest, who taught Cleopatra the divine and sacred art of the philosopher’s stone. Its structure, as the modern summaries describe it, is a relay: Comarius instructs Cleopatra in the mystical philosophy of the work — the elements, the metals, the colours, the apparatus — and Cleopatra then communicates what she has received to the assembled philosophers, becoming in her turn the teacher of the dialogue. The text calls her, in the tradition’s epithets, the divine and the learned — Cleopatra the Wise, the style under which the ARCE’s survey of the alternative Cleopatra tradition also records her. A Christian prayer that heads the treatise in its transmitted form is judged, in Georg Luck’s assessment as the summaries report it, a later addition — a Byzantine copyist’s edifying frame for a pagan text — and the judgement is recorded here as the standard caution for the whole Comarius material: what survives is a layered document, early teaching in later dress.

The “you will find” promises belong to this section, because they are the dossier’s characteristic speech-act and the leaf’s implied one. The Cleopatra texts, like the art’s teaching literature generally, proceed by assurance: attend to the plants and the seasons; join the natures in the vessel; govern the fire; and you will find — the waters will wake the bodies, the colours will come in their order, the hidden glory will appear when the fire has tested the work. The dialogue’s Cleopatra, in Taylor’s translation as the scholarship excerpts it, tells her hearers that the materials they take from their places only appear mature until the fire has tested them, and that their hidden glory appears when they are clothed in the glory and shining colour of the fire. Read at the level the words can bear — the level of the dialogue’s own analogies — the promises instruct in three things: in attention (observe natures in their seasons and places), in procedure (the waters, the vessel, the tested fire), and in expectation (the work announces itself in colour, as spring announces itself in flowers — the dialogue’s own simile for the revived bodies putting on divers glorious colours). What they do not instruct in is quantities, charges, or timings: the Cleopatra dossier, leaf and dialogue together, contains no recipe a bench could run. Its teaching is a way of seeing operations whose recipes live elsewhere in the codex. That division of labour — Cleopatra for the vision, the recipe collections for the work — is the dossier’s real shape, and it is why the leaf, with its doctrine in pictures, is the dossier’s fitting emblem.

XII. “Cleopatra”: Not the Queen

She is not Cleopatra VII. The statement belongs at the head of the section, in the plainest form this hub can give it, because the confusion is ancient, profitable, and wrong. Cleopatra VII, the last Ptolemaic monarch, died in 30 BCE, three centuries before the milieu to which the alchemical Cleopatra’s dossier belongs. The alchemist Cleopatra — Cleopatra the Wise, in the Comarius tradition’s style — is a figure of Roman Egypt’s Greek alchemical literature: a name attached to a leaf, a dialogue, a weights-and-measures tract, and a teaching legend, conventionally placed by modern reference works in Alexandria in the third century CE, or the third-to-fourth, or — at the widest honest bracket, and the bracket this article uses — the first to third centuries of the formative Alexandrian stratum, before or beside Zōsimos. The reference literature’s own formulation is the one this hub adopts: Cleopatra is a pseudonym for an unknown author or group of authors. Whether a single historical woman stands behind the name, as a historical Maria may stand behind hers, the evidence does not let anyone say. What the name certainly is, is a choice — and the choice is the evidence.

For the choice belongs to a habit, and the habit is Wing II’s normal condition. The hub’s method, stated in its index and applied throughout the alchemy wing, is that pseudepigraphy is the library’s ordinary state, not an embarrassment: works travel under the names of Democritus, Hermes, Moses, Aristotle, and Cleopatra that their named authors did not write, and the attribution is itself part of the historical evidence. A royal name did specific work for an alchemical text. It claimed antiquity — a Ptolemaic queen predates every Greek philosopher of the Roman period. It claimed Egyptian authority — the queen of Egypt, in the Greek-Egyptian tradition’s own memory of her, was already a figure of learning, and the alternative tradition surveyed by the American Research Center in Egypt shows Greek and then Arabic sources of the first centuries CE depicting a Cleopatra who is a scholar and teacher, knowledgeable in medicine, magic, and alchemy, exchanging learning with philosophers — a portrait with her love life and appearance wholly absent, and wholly unlike the Roman tradition’s seductress. And it claimed, for a woman’s teaching voice, the highest female authority the culture remembered. The alchemical Cleopatra is that portrait become an author: the scholar-queen of the Egyptian tradition, seated at last in her own schoolroom, with Ostanes at her feet. To ask whether she “really” wrote the leaf is to ask the tradition’s question in the record’s grammar. The record answers only: the name was worth choosing.

The confusions that gather around the name can now be sorted, briefly, each under its label. Cleopatra VII herself: not the alchemist, for the chronological reason given above; the identification is nevertheless made by later works — the seventeenth-century Basilica Philosophica of Johann Daniel Mylius (1618) pictures her seal among the philosophers’ seals with a motto on the divine hidden from the people, in the queen’s imagined person — and the Egyptological survey is careful to note that the early tradition’s Cleopatra may be identified with the Ptolemaic queen by that tradition itself: Isis, Hermes, Maria, and Cleopatra stand together in it as ancient practitioners and authorities, with Zōsimos citing such figures as sources. The identification is the tradition’s claim about itself, precious as such, and not a biography. Cleopatra the Physician: a separate figure of the medical tradition, credited with writings on women’s medicine and cosmetics and cited in that capacity, with whom the alchemist is also conflated in the later literature, on the strength — the reference summaries suggest — of a shared grandeur of imagery. Two Cleopatras of the learned tradition, neither of them the queen, periodically merged into one another and into her: the sorting above is the current reference position, and it is held here at that strength. The inventor of the alembic: some writers credit Cleopatra with the invention of the distillation apparatus itself. The leaf draws a dibikos; it does not claim an invention, and the still family is older than any of its describers, as the Maria article establishes from Dioscorides onward. The credit is later tradition’s, and it is filed there.

What, then, can be claimed? The Arabic tradition’s respect, first: the Kitāb al-Fihrist of Ibn al-Nadīm (987 CE), the great Baghdad catalogue, mentions Cleopatra with great respect among the art’s authorities — evidence that by the tenth century her name carried weight in a second civilisation’s library, independently of the Byzantine codex. Michael Maier’s early seventeenth-century canon, second: Maier names her among the four women who knew how to make the philosopher’s stone — Maria the Jewess, Medera, Paphnutia, and Cleopatra — a Renaissance measurement of her standing, taken a millennium and a half after her dossier formed. And the dossier itself, third and last: a name, a leaf, a dialogue she speaks but did not write, a teaching she receives from Comarius and transmits to philosophers, a set of weights and measures, and a milieu — Alexandria, the apparatus culture of Maria’s circle, the formative centuries of the art. No birth, no death, no face, no sentence certainly hers except, at one remove more, the inscriptions of a leaf copied seven centuries after her time. A name, a leaf, a milieu — no biography. The sentence is this article’s whole position on its author, and every earlier section has been written inside it.

// THE ROYAL PSEUDONYM, IN CONTEXT //

Cleopatra’s case is one cell in Wing II’s table of borrowed names. Pseudo-Democritus borrows the atomist’s prestige for workshop recipes (see the origins article, Section V); the Hermetica borrow Hermes-Thoth for a revelation (see Hermetica); the Jabir corpus, in the Arabic sequel, borrows a master’s name for a whole school. The borrowing is not fraud in the modern sense. It is the ancient literature’s way of filing a work under the authority it claims descent from — which is why the hub cites such works as ascribed to their names, and why the ascription, honestly labelled, loses none of its historical interest.

XIII. Before Cleopatra: The Ouroboros in Egypt

The serpent is far older than the leaf, and its first attested appearance is the most consequential single fact in its long history. One of the earliest known ouroboros motifs — in the standard formulation, the earliest known representation of the ouroboros — is found in the Enigmatic Book of the Netherworld, an ancient Egyptian funerary composition preserved in KV62, the tomb of Tutankhamun, in the fourteenth century BCE. The composition is inscribed on the gilded shrines that enclosed the king’s sarcophagus — the second shrine in the usual description — and its subject is the mystery the Egyptology division of this archive treats as the netherworld books’ centre: the actions of Ra and his union with Osiris in the underworld, the sun god’s nightly death and rebirth through the lord of the dead. The archive’s treatment is at Netherworld Books, Section XIX, on the Enigmatic Netherworld Books of the Solar-Osirian Unity, with the cryptographic writing that gives the books their name treated at Enigmatic Writing: compositions in cryptographic hieroglyphs, on Tutankhamun’s shrines and in the tombs of Ramesses VI and Ramesses IX, edited in modern scholarship by John Coleman Darnell (2004). It is inside that encrypted composition, around that unified god, that the tail-eating serpent first survives.

The Egyptian image differs from Cleopatra’s in ways a careful reader must hold onto, because the differences mark what the Greek transformation changed. There are two serpents, not one. Each holds its tail in its mouth; one encircles the head and upper chest of an enormous divine figure, the other surrounds its feet. The figure they frame may represent, in the standard reading, the unified Ra-Osiris — Osiris born again as Ra — and the whole divine figure represents the beginning and the end of time. Both serpents are manifestations of the deity Mehen, the coiled serpent who in other funerary texts protects Ra on his underworld journey. The function here is enclosure and protection: the serpents bound and guard the god in whom beginning and end are one body, as — in the wider Egyptian usage the reference literature summarises — the ouroboros-form elsewhere figures the formless disorder that surrounds the ordered world and is involved in that world’s periodic renewal. No words stand inside these serpents. No doctrine is inscribed in their rings. They are guardians at the border of a mystery, drawn in gold on a king’s shrine, fifteen centuries before Alexandria.

Sheppard’s study argues the Egyptian derivation of the alchemical symbol on broader grounds than the shrine alone, and his argument is recorded here at its proper description: a scholarly argument, from the serpent’s deep Egyptian dossier. Following R. T. Rundle Clark’s Myth and Symbol in Ancient Egypt (1959), Sheppard assembles the tail-eater’s Egyptian pedigree — the primeval serpent of the creation traditions, the serpent who in the Coffin Texts bends right around himself, encircled in his coils, and utters the creative word from within them; the serpent Sito of the Book of the Dead, who dies and is reborn every day; the huge serpent biting its tail that symbolised the world in the Hermopolitan tradition as Clark reports it; and pictorial witnesses including a mummy-like figure on Tutankhamun’s shrine ringed above and below by two encircling serpents. Sheppard’s conclusion, in his own measured terms, is that it seems probable the early Egyptian form was adopted by both the Gnostics and the alchemists of Greco-Roman Egypt — probable being his word, and the word this article keeps. Probability is not a chain. No intermediate witness carries the serpent, step by dated step, from the shrine to the leaf, and Section XIV assembles what the intervening fifteen centuries actually offer: not a chain, but a scatter of sightings — magical, Gnostic, astrological — through which a motif demonstrably present in Roman Egypt can be watched approaching the alchemists’ page.

THE EGYPTIAN SERPENT

Two serpents; tails in mouths; encircling head and feet of the unified Ra-Osiris; manifestations of Mehen; protective enclosure around the mystery of solar rebirth; no inscription; gold on a royal shrine; fourteenth century BCE.

THE ALEXANDRIAN SERPENT

One serpent; tail in mouth; two-toned body; enclosing a verbal formula, ἓν τὸ πᾶν, and paired with a ring-diagram that glosses it; drawn among apparatus on a practitioner’s leaf; ink on parchment, in a copy of the tenth–eleventh century from a lost original of the first centuries CE.

The transformation between those two cards is the Greek contribution, and it can be stated in one sentence: Egypt supplied the serpent; Greece put words inside it. The encircling guardian becomes a proposition. The mystery protected in silence becomes a doctrine stated in three words and expounded in two ring inscriptions. And the cosmic enclosure — the serpent as the border between the ordered world and the formlessness outside — becomes a laboratory enclosure: the sealed vessel, the circulating vapour, the matter that feeds on itself and is perfected in the feeding. Whether the Alexandrian who first drew the alchemical ouroboros knew the shrine image, or knew the motif only as Roman Egypt’s common visual property, cannot be shown; the motif was in the air of the country, on gems and talismans as Section XIV records, and the leaf’s composer needed no antiquarian research to reach for it. What he did with it — the words, the rings, the stills beside it — is the leaf’s originality, and it is enough.

XIV. Between Egypt and Alexandria: Gems, Papyri, and Gnostic Serpents

The fifteen centuries between the shrine and the leaf are not empty of serpents; they are empty of documentation, which is a different thing, and this section assembles the sightings the record does offer, in their order, with the standing of each stated as it is given. The purpose is disciplinary as much as narrative: a reader who sees the scatter will stop imagining the chain.

The magical gems and talismans. The ouroboros persisted in Egypt into Roman times, in the reference literature’s summary, frequently appearing on magical talismans, sometimes in combination with other magical emblems. Sheppard’s study makes the same point from the alchemical side: the tail-eating serpent engraved on the so-called Abraxas stones of the Gnostics carries the simple inscriptions of the type the leaf would later elaborate. The gem-serpent is the motif’s Roman-Egyptian common form: small, portable, apotropaic, inscribed — already a serpent with words, though the words are names and vowels of power rather than doctrines. The Leyden papyri. Sheppard records the earliest mention of the ouroboros in connection with alchemy in the Leyden Papyri V and W (c. 250–350 CE) — with his own caution attached, that the associations there are magical rather than alchemical. The Theban papyri are the workshop-magical collections the origins article treats in its Section XVI, recipe books of the same Greco-Egyptian world as the leaf; that the serpent appears in their orbit, at the leaf’s own conventional date, is the nearest thing to a contemporary sighting the record affords. The Gnostic texts. In Gnosticism, the serpent biting its tail symbolised eternity and the soul of the world; the Pistis Sophia (c. fourth century CE) describes the outer darkness as a huge dragon with its tail in its mouth, surrounding the world completely, and elsewhere the disk of the sun as a great dragon with its tail in its mouth — the serpent at both poles, darkness and light, in a text of Egyptian provenance. Sheppard collects the kindred witnesses: the apocryphal Acts of Thomas, with its offspring of the serpent who encircles the sphere, tail in mouth; the Ophite diagram described by Origen, where the dragon’s orbit circumscribes the seven planetary spheres.

Two learned witnesses complete the scatter, one astrological and one philosophical, and both reach us through the commentators. The Hellenistic astrologers, in Sheppard’s account following Franz Cumont, assigned the encircling serpent to the ninth, starless sphere that encloses the heavens and the earth — the draco caelestis, the celestial dragon, controller of eclipses: the ouroboros as the sky’s own rim. And Olympiodorus, the sixth-century commentator of this hub’s transmission article, preserves the tradition’s most explicit ancient gloss on the alchemical serpent: in the modern summary of his text, Agathodaimon conceived the beginning of the cosmos as residing in the end, and vice versa, symbolised by the ouroboros-serpent that eats its own tail — and Olympiodorus adds that the sacred scribes of Egypt saw the ouroboros as the image of the cosmos, and engraved it with hieroglyphic characters on obelisks. The gloss is late, and it is a commentator’s construction of the symbol’s meaning, not a report from the leaf’s century; but it shows the tradition, by the sixth century, already reading its serpent exactly as Section VIII reads it — beginning in end, the cosmos in a ring — and already crediting Egypt with the image. The fourth-century Latin commentator Servius, finally, knew the Egyptian use of the symbol and glossed the tail-biting snake as the cyclical nature of the year: the serpent had become, by late antiquity, a commonplace of learned symbolism on both sides of the Mediterranean.

// WHAT THE SCATTER PROVES //

Presence, not descent. The sightings prove that the tail-eating serpent was available — in Egypt above all, and across the Roman world’s magical, Gnostic, and astrological literatures — throughout the centuries before and around the leaf’s composition. They do not prove that the leaf’s composer copied any one of them, and no two sightings are linked by a witness that names its source. The hub’s formulation, here as in the transmission article, is that the alchemical ouroboros is the Egyptian motif in its Greek transformation, with the transformation documented and the transmission inferred. A stronger claim would need a document nobody has found.

XV. After Cleopatra: The Serpent Becomes Alchemy’s Emblem

The leaf’s afterlife begins, fittingly, with its copyists — and with their errors, which are the afterlife’s first documents. The Paris manuscripts preserve the tradition’s later ouroboroi, and Sheppard describes them figure by figure; his descriptions, followed here, let the degradation be watched in detail. In Parisinus graecus 2327, folio 196 (his Figure 3), the serpent has become three concentric rings of creature: the outermost scaly, its head and three ears depicted in bright red, its eye white with a black pupil; the middle ring also scaly, coloured yellow; the inner ring, bearing four feet, portrayed entirely in green. The manuscript’s own accompanying description, as Sheppard reports it, glosses the anatomy: the four feet represent the four bodies, the tetrasōmia — the quartet of base metals that form the work’s body — and the three ears the sublimed vapours, possibly sulphur, mercury, and orpiment. In the Parisinus 2325 (folio 82) and 2327 (folio 220) variants (his Figure 5), the serpent has become two concentric inscribed rings, and Sheppard’s judgement on their texts is the transmission history of the leaf in one sentence: the inscriptions are probably degenerate forms of those in the Chrysopoeia — the outer one incomplete, the inner one a confused version of the serpent sentence — from which he concludes that the Chrysopoeia doubtless served as a model for later copyists. Yet another variant in Parisinus 2327 (folio 80) drops the concentric circles, bears the same axioms in red, and shows again the signs for gold, silver, and mercury — accompanied now by those for lead and cinnabar, or the Egg. The direction of travel is unmistakable and entirely typical: the emblem grows more elaborate as its text grows less accurate. Colour multiplies; wording decays; the symbol detaches from its sentences and begins its career as a free image.

One copyist’s serpent deserves its own sentence, because it is the afterlife’s most famous single artefact after the leaf itself. The Parisinus graecus 2327 was copied in June 1478 at Candia in Crete by Theodoros Pelecanos of Corfu — the transmission article’s Section VII gives the codex its full treatment — and Pelecanos’ own drawing of the ouroboros, dated by the codex to 1478, renders the symbol in a fifteenth-century hand: faithful in outline, contemporary in execution, the emblem as a Renaissance scribe understood it. Between the Marcianus serpent and Pelecanos’ stands the whole Byzantine millennium of the symbol’s life inside the codices; after Pelecanos comes print, and with print the serpent escapes the codices altogether. The fifteenth-century Aurora Consurgens already shows the ouroboros among the symbols of sun, moon, and mercury. The early modern emblem books take it up as a standard device: an engraving by Lucas Jennis in the 1625 alchemical tract De Lapide Philosophico shows the wyvern-like ouroboros serving as a symbol for mercury; Mylius’ Basilica Philosophica (1618) had already set Cleopatra’s own seal among the philosophers’ seals, the author and her emblem canonised together; Maier’s canon of the four women who knew the stone (Section XII) belongs to the same decades. By the seventeenth century the serpent no longer needs its leaf: it is alchemy’s coat of arms, available to any title page, and its three Greek words have become a motto that printers could set without knowing they were quoting a Byzantine copy of an Alexandrian diagram.

The modern centuries completed the detachment and then, characteristically, re-attached the serpent to new doctrines. Carl Jung made the ouroboros a central archetype of his reading of alchemy — in the passage of his Collected Works most often quoted on the symbol, the alchemists’ paradox of a creature that slays itself and brings itself to life, fertilises and gives birth to itself, figures the integration of opposites and the secret of the prima materia — a reading this article files, as it files Lindsay’s, under its author’s name and century: the twentieth century’s stratum, laid over the leaf like the Paris copyists’ colours. The chemist’s version of the same afterlife is the famous anecdote of August Kekulé, who in a retrospective account described the structure of benzene coming to him as a snake seized hold of its own tail before his eyes — an anecdote told by Kekulé of himself, decades after the event, and repeated here as the afterlife’s folklore, not as the history of chemistry. And at the popular level the serpent now circulates without any doctrine at all: jewellery, tattoos, logos, the universal clip-art of cyclical renewal. Every stage of the journey from folio 188v to the tattoo parlour is a stage of the same process the Paris variants began: the image survives by shedding its text. This article’s whole labour has been to reverse one step of that shedding — to put the serpent back on its leaf, among its rings and stills and stars, with its sentences restored and its copyists’ names attached. The emblem will outlive the effort, as it has outlived every previous one. The leaf, meanwhile, remains in Venice, saying its three words to whoever opens the codex.

XVI. One Witness: What a Single Leaf Can and Cannot Prove

The article closes on its method, because the leaf is the purest case in the hub of the condition the whole archive works under. Consider the arithmetic of the evidence. One leaf. A few dozen words of Greek, of which the doctrinal content is two inscriptions and a three-word motto. Two apparatus outlines. A cluster of small signs, one of them unexplained. A title that is an attribution rather than a signature. A codex copied at the end of the tenth or beginning of the eleventh century, from exemplars lost, of an original composed at an unknown date within a conventional bracket of three centuries. No second ancient witness to the leaf itself; the Paris variants descend from it or from its near relations and add colour while subtracting text. That is the whole of it. Everything else — the Egyptian shrine, the Chymes saying, the dialogue’s waters, Maria’s apparatus, Olympiodorus’ gloss, Jung’s archetype — is context, analogy, pedigree, and afterlife: real knowledge, honestly come by, about everything around the leaf. The leaf itself says almost nothing, and says it in pictures.

THE STANDARD STUDY

H. J. Sheppard’s “The Ouroboros and the Unity of Matter in Alchemy” (Ambix 10.2) describes the manuscript figures, prints and translates the ring inscriptions, and argues the symbol’s Egyptian derivation — the account this article principally follows.

What, then, does the leaf prove? It proves that by the time its exemplar tradition formed, the Alexandrian art possessed a doctrine in pictures: that the unity of the All could be stated as an emblem, glossed by concentric sentences, and set beside the instruments of the work on a single page meant for fluent readers. It proves that the ouroboros — an Egyptian image at least fifteen centuries old by then — had been taken up into the art and married to the monist formula that licensed transmutation. It proves that the tradition credited this synthesis to a woman’s name, and to a queen’s. And it proves, by its survival, the narrowness of the channel through which Greek alchemy reached us: had the Marcianus burned, as so many of its fellows did, the most famous image in alchemy would not exist, and no text would describe it well enough to miss.

What it cannot prove is the longer list, and the article ends by reciting it without flinching. It cannot prove who composed it, or when, within three centuries. It cannot prove that its serpent was the first alchemical ouroboros — only that it is the first to survive. It cannot prove what its two-toned body, its scales, or its star signs meant to its composer, beyond what its inscriptions say. It cannot prove a recipe, a practice, or a result: no gold was ever made by this leaf, and none was claimed by it beyond the claim of its title. It cannot prove a biography for Cleopatra, a school she founded, or a sentence she wrote. And it cannot prove the Egyptian descent it so strongly suggests: the shrine and the leaf are two sightings of one serpent, fifteen centuries apart, with the space between them filled by probability and scatter. The hub’s claims have been sized to that list throughout — the record, the tradition, and the telling kept apart, in the formula of this division’s footer. One leaf, one serpent, three words. Read at their own weight, they are enough: the oldest picture of the idea that made alchemy thinkable, drawn on the same page as the vessels that made it workable. Nothing in the archive says more for a single folio, and nothing honest could.
