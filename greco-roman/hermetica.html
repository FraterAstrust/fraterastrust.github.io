I. Two Families Under One Name: Philosophical and Technical Hermetica

This article belongs to the Alchemy wing of the Greco-Roman hub, but it is not, strictly speaking, an article about alchemy. It is the wing's philosophical sibling. The alchemical texts treated elsewhere in this hub — above all in the article on Zosimos of Panopolis — were written in the same cities, in the same centuries, and under the same presiding name as the texts treated here: Hermes Trismegistus, Hermes the Thrice-Greatest. To understand why a furnace manual and a dialogue about the divine Mind could both be ascribed to Hermes, the reader first needs the distinction on which all modern scholarship of the subject rests.

Writings ascribed to Hermes fall, in the conventional scholarly division, into two families. The philosophical Hermetica are religious and philosophical treatises — dialogues, discourses, hymns, and prayers — concerned with God, the making of the cosmos, the nature and fate of the human soul, and the knowledge (gnōsis) by which the soul finds its way back to its source. The technical Hermetica are practical works: astrological treatises and catalogues, alchemical recipes and allegories, magical procedures, and writings on the hidden properties of plants, stones, and animals. The Cyranides, the astrological works that circulated under Hermes' name from the Hellenistic period onward, and a large body of alchemical pseudepigrapha all belong to the second family.

The division is a modern filing system, not an ancient wall. Garth Fowden, in The Egyptian Hermes (1986), argued persuasively that both families are best understood as responses to a single milieu — the complex Greco-Egyptian culture of Ptolemaic, Roman, and early Christian Egypt — and later scholarship has repeatedly noted that the two kinds of text shared readers, copyists, and assumptions. A reader who copied a philosophical dialogue about rebirth might also copy an astrological catalogue; the alchemist Zosimos, writing around 300 CE, appears to quote or echo the philosophical treatises even as he writes about apparatus and operations. The families are distinguished here because their histories differ — the philosophical treatises have a datable Greek manuscript tradition, a famous Renaissance translation, and a famous scholarly redating, and that history is this article's subject — not because ancient readers kept them in separate rooms.

THE PHILOSOPHICAL HERMETICA

The Corpus Hermeticum; the Latin Asclepius; the Hermetic excerpts preserved by Stobaeus, including the Kore Kosmou; the Vienna fragments; the Armenian Definitions; and the three Coptic Hermetica of Nag Hammadi Codex VI. Theology, cosmology, anthropology, and the way of knowledge and rebirth.

THE TECHNICAL HERMETICA

Astrological, alchemical, magical, and medical writings under Hermes' name, some of them older than the philosophical treatises — astrological Hermetica may reach back to the second or third century BCE. Practical in aim: elections, remedies, operations, and the sympathies of things.

The technical family has its own article in this hub: The Technical Hermetica treats the Kyranides, Hermetic astrology and iatromathematics, and the alchemical writings under Hermes' name. This article keeps the philosophical family.

A note on method, because this subject attracts invention as few others do. No direct quotation from a Hermetic treatise is given in this article unless it is explicitly attributed. In practice, the article paraphrases throughout, and every paraphrase of a treatise's contents is labelled as paraphrase in the text or follows the standard modern account in Brian P. Copenhaver's Hermetica (Cambridge, 1992) — the Greek Corpus Hermeticum and the Latin Asclepius in a new English translation, with notes and introduction — which is the backbone translation and scholarly introduction for everything that follows. Where a famous phrase is discussed, it is discussed as a phrase, not smuggled in as a quotation. Readers who want the treatises' own words should go to Copenhaver; this article's job is the texts' identity, date, and afterlife.

One further framing point. The Hermetica are routinely described, in popular writing, as “ancient Egyptian wisdom”. That description is itself a historical artefact — it is what late-antique, medieval, and Renaissance readers believed, and the belief had enormous consequences — but it is not what the evidence supports about the texts' composition. The treatises were composed in Greek, in Roman Egypt, in the second and third centuries CE. Holding both facts at once — what the texts are, and what they were taken to be — is the whole discipline of this article, and Sections V, XII, and XIII return to the tension between them from three directions.

II. What the Corpus Hermeticum Is

The Corpus Hermeticum (conventionally abbreviated CH) is a collection of seventeen short Greek treatises ascribed to Hermes Trismegistus. The name is slightly misleading, and Copenhaver's introduction is among the many scholarly accounts that say so: corpus normally suggests the whole body of an author's surviving work, whereas this collection is a small selection from a much larger Hermetic literature, most of it lost. The individual treatises were quoted by authors from the second and third centuries onward; the collection as a collection is first attested much later, in the writings of the Byzantine philosopher Michael Psellos (c. 1017–1078), and it was Byzantine editors who gathered the treatises into the form in which the Renaissance would receive them.

The Numbering, Stated Accurately

Modern editions number the treatises I–XVIII, but there are seventeen of them, because number XV is vacant. The gap is an artefact of early-modern editing, not a lost or suppressed book: an early printed edition placed extraneous material — excerpts drawn from Stobaeus and other sources — at that point in the sequence, and later editors removed it while leaving the surrounding numbers undisturbed. The collection therefore runs I–XIV and then XVI–XVIII. Any account that treats “the missing Book XV” as a mystery to be explained by concealment has mistaken a printer's and editor's accident for a fact about antiquity.

The treatises are not chapters of one book. They differ in length, form, speaker, and — as Section VII sets out — in doctrine. Most are dialogues or discourses in which Hermes instructs a disciple: his “son” Tat (whose name derives, by a Greek route, from Thoth himself), Asclepius (the Greek name under which the deified Egyptian sage Imhotep was known), or King Ammon (the god Amun in human, royal form). One treatise is spoken by Mind (Nous) to Hermes; one is a letter; one, the last, is a panegyric of kings in which Hermes does not appear at all. The standard critical Greek text is the Budé edition prepared by A. D. Nock and A.-J. Festugière (four volumes, 1945–1954), on which Copenhaver's English translation rests.

Two consequences of that table deserve emphasis. First, the Corpus is an anthology assembled over time, not a work composed at a sitting: its treatises were probably written by different hands, and their gathering into one collection was the work of later editors. Second, the Renaissance division of labour follows directly from the numbering: Marsilio Ficino translated treatises I–XIV; the remaining three, XVI–XVIII, were translated later by Lodovico Lazzarelli (1447–1500). When a Renaissance or early-modern source speaks of Ficino's Pimander, it means the fourteen treatises of his 1463 translation — not the whole modern Corpus (Section X).

III. Beyond the Corpus: Asclepius, Stobaeus, Vienna, and Nag Hammadi

The Corpus Hermeticum is the most famous part of the philosophical Hermetica, but it is not the whole of it. Four further bodies of material complete the picture, and each matters to a different part of this article's story: the Asclepius is the text the Latin Middle Ages actually knew; the Stobaeus excerpts preserve Hermetica that would otherwise be lost; the Vienna fragments show the literature still being copied on papyrus in Roman Egypt itself; and the Nag Hammadi texts, recovered only in 1945, changed modern scholars' sense of who read Hermes and where.

The Latin Asclepius

The Asclepius is a long Hermetic dialogue that survives complete only in Latin. It is a translation of a lost Greek original known as the Perfect Discourse (Greek: Logos teleios), and it was transmitted in the Middle Ages among the works of Apuleius of Madaura, to whom it is not now attributed. In paraphrase: Hermes instructs Asclepius on God, the cosmos, and the human being — presented, in a celebrated passage, as a great wonder, a being who joins the mortal and the divine — and the dialogue contains two passages that dominate its later history. The first describes the Egyptian art of making gods: drawing powers into statues so that the images become, in the text's terms, animated bodies inhabited by invited spirits. The second is a prophecy, cast as a lament, that Egypt will one day be abandoned by its gods and its temples left desolate. Augustine quoted both at length (Section VIII); the first made the Asclepius the most theologically dangerous Hermetic text in the Latin tradition, and the second let Christian readers hear, in a pagan voice, a foretelling of paganism's end.

The Stobaeus Fragments and the Kore Kosmou

In the fifth century CE, the anthologist John of Stobi — Stobaeus — compiled a vast anthology of extracts from Greek authors for the education of his son. Among its contents are dozens of Hermetic excerpts, conventionally cited as the Stobaean Hermetica (SH). Some duplicate material known from elsewhere; many preserve Hermetic writing found nowhere else. The most substantial is the Kore Kosmou — the title means, roughly, the Virgin (or Pupil, or Maiden) of the World — preserved as Stobaean excerpts XXIII–XXIV in the usual numbering. In paraphrase: the goddess Isis instructs her son Horus in how souls were created and embodied — made to assist in the ordering of living things, grown proud and transgressive, and in consequence bound into bodies, with a promised return for those who live worthily of their origin. The Kore Kosmou is the most overtly mythological of the surviving philosophical Hermetica, and its Isis–Horus frame is a standing reminder that the literature's Egyptian dramatis personae are not confined to Hermes himself.

The Vienna Fragments

The Vienna Hermetica are four short Greek fragments on papyrus — papyri now in Vienna, dating to the late second or third century — preserving parts of what was once a collection of Hermetic discourses of Hermes to Tat, one of them conventionally titled On Energies. Their importance is out of proportion to their size. They are physical witnesses from Roman Egypt itself, close in date to the treatises' composition, and they show the dialogues circulating as a collection of discourses — not only as isolated tracts — already in antiquity. Related witnesses include the Definitions of Hermes Trismegistus to Asclepius, preserved in an Armenian translation, which some scholars regard as among the earliest Hermetic philosophical material, possibly reaching back toward the first century CE.

The Nag Hammadi Hermetica

In 1945, near Nag Hammadi in Upper Egypt, a buried jar yielded thirteen codices of mostly Gnostic and Christian-related writings in Coptic. Codex VI contains three Hermetic texts, all translated from Greek originals:

NH VI,6 — THE DISCOURSE ON THE EIGHTH AND NINTH

A previously unknown initiatory dialogue: a teacher leads a disciple, through prayer and sung vowel sequences, toward a vision of the eighth and ninth spheres above the seven planetary zones. The most explicitly initiatory Hermetic text to survive whole.

NH VI,7 — THE PRAYER OF THANKSGIVING

A Hermetic prayer of gratitude for saving knowledge, also known independently in Greek (in the Greek Magical Papyri) and in Latin (at the close of the Asclepius). A scribal note records that those who prayed then embraced and shared a meal.

NH VI,8 — ASCLEPIUS 21–29

A Coptic version of a central portion of the Asclepius (the Perfect Discourse), covering — among other material — the prophecy of Egypt's desolation. A control on the Latin text, and proof the dialogue circulated in Egypt in Coptic dress.

The find mattered for two reasons. It proved, in physical form, that Hermetic writings were copied and read in fourth-century Egypt alongside Gnostic and Christian texts — the readers were neighbours, and in some cases evidently the same people. And the Discourse on the Eighth and Ninth, with its guided ascent, its hymnody, and its instruction that the teaching be written up and deposited in a temple, gave scholars their strongest single exhibit for the argument — associated above all with Jean-Pierre Mahé's work on the Hermetica of Upper Egypt and developed by Fowden and others — that the philosophical Hermetica reflect a lived devotional practice, not merely a literary game played with Egyptian scenery. How far that argument extends to the Corpus treatises themselves remains debated (Section V); that the debate exists at all is a post-1945 fact.

IV. Hermes-Thoth in Brief

This section is deliberately brief. The figure behind the treatises — how the Greek Hermes and the Egyptian Thoth became one thrice-greatest sage — is treated at length, from the Egyptian side, in the Egyptology division's Hermeticism article, and the treatises' Egyptian frame is the subject of its Corpus Hermeticum article. What follows is only what a reader of the Greek tradition needs in hand.

When Greeks settled in Egypt after Alexander's conquests, they practised what modern scholarship calls interpretatio graeca: reading foreign gods through Greek equivalents. The Egyptian Thoth (Egyptian Ḏḥwty) — ibis-headed god of writing, reckoning, and sacred knowledge, scribe of the gods, who in Egyptian myth records, mediates, and heals — was identified with Hermes, the Greek messenger, herald, and patron of speech and interpretation. Thoth's chief cult city, Khmunu, became in Greek Hermopolis, the City of Hermes. The compound figure, Hermes Trismegistus — Hermes the Thrice-Greatest, the epithet generally explained as rendering the repeated Egyptian superlatives (“great, great”) applied to Thoth — emerges in the Hellenistic and Roman periods as author, revealer, and patron of all sacred learning.

The identification carried a curriculum with it. Around 200 CE the Christian writer Clement of Alexandria described, in paraphrase, a procession of Egyptian priests carrying forty-two books of Hermes held to be indispensable to the cult — books on hymns, ritual, astronomy and astrology, geography, medicine, and the training of priests. Clement's list is a Christian author's report, not a temple catalogue, but it shows the shape of the reputation the Hermetica inherited: Hermes was not the author of a book but of a library, and a library administered by priests. That reputation is the seed of everything later — of the technical Hermetica as much as the philosophical, since astrology, medicine, and ritual all stood on Clement's shelves — and it is why new writings on any sacred subject could plausibly appear under Hermes' name for centuries. Authorship, in this tradition, is authorisation.

// ONE FIGURE, THREE REGISTERS //

God: Thoth-Hermes, worshipped at Hermopolis and across Roman Egypt. Sage: the legendary priest-king and lawgiver whom Cicero's catalogue of five Hermeses already sends fleeing to Egypt with letters and laws. Author-function: the name on the title of any text claiming revealed, Egyptian, and therefore ancient authority. The treatises move among all three registers without marking the transitions, which is one reason their later readers could find in them a prophet, a philosopher, or a magus, as the reader required.

V. Dating and Milieu: Greek Texts from Roman Egypt

The scholarly consensus that Copenhaver's Hermetica represents — and that the standard reference literature shares — dates the philosophical Hermetica to the first three centuries CE, chiefly the second and third, composed in Greek in Roman Egypt. No philosophical Hermetic treatise is generally accepted as earlier than the turn of the era, and the Definitions (Section III), sometimes placed toward the first century CE, mark the early edge of the range rather than an exception to it. The technical Hermetica are a different chronological story — some astrological Hermetica may go back to the second or third century BCE — and conflating the two families' dates is the single most common error in popular accounts of “Hermes”.

The evidence for the dating is cumulative, and Section XII shows it being assembled, in essence, as early as 1614. The treatises' Greek is the Greek of the Roman imperial period, with the vocabulary of later Greek philosophy. Their intellectual furniture — the divine Mind, the mediating Word, the craftsman, fate working through the planetary spheres, the soul's kinship with God — is the furniture of the period's popular Platonism. No author before the Christian era quotes or mentions them. Their earliest certain readers are the writers of the second and third centuries and the copyists of the Vienna papyri (Section III). And their world — temples still standing, statues still animated in cult, a priestly culture writing in Greek under Roman rule — is the world of Roman, not pharaonic, Egypt.

The Ingredients

MIDDLE PLATONISM

The dominant ingredient: a supreme God beyond easy predication; Mind (Nous) as first principle and revealer; a craftsman figure ordering the cosmos; the soul's descent into body and its possible return. The Hermetica are unintelligible except as popular, devotional Platonism of the imperial period.

STOICISM

A living cosmos pervaded by divine reason and breath; fate (heimarmenē) as the connected order of causes; sympathy among the parts of the whole. Stoic physics supplies much of the treatises' cosmology even where their soteriology is Platonic.

JEWISH AND BIBLICAL ECHOES

The Poimandrēs creation account (Section VI) works with material closely parallel to the Greek Genesis — light separated from darkness, a creative Word, the command to increase and multiply. Scholarship generally explains this through the shared Alexandrian world of the Septuagint and of Philo, not through direct dependence in either direction between the treatises and the New Testament.

EGYPTIAN RELIGION

The dramatis personae, the temple settings, the statue animation of the Asclepius, the Isis of the Kore Kosmou. How deep this ingredient goes — living priestly tradition or literary colouring — is the field's central live debate, summarised below.

How Egyptian? The Fowden Question

A.-J. Festugière, whose edition still underlies the modern text, read the Hermetica as essentially Greek philosophy in Egyptian dress: the Egyptian names and settings, on his account, lend sanctity to doctrines drawn from the Greek schools. Fowden's The Egyptian Hermes (1986) mounted the counter-case that has structured discussion since: that the Hermetica, philosophical and technical together, arise from a genuinely Egyptian, if thoroughly Hellenised, milieu — the world of the Egyptian temples and their priestly intellectuals under Roman rule, for whom Greek was a working language and Greek philosophy a usable idiom, but whose institutions, practices, and gods were their own. The Nag Hammadi evidence (Section III), the Demotic and temple parallels assembled by Mahé and others, and more recent work — notably Christian Bull's The Tradition of Hermes Trismegistus (2018), which presses the Egyptian priestly continuity further still — have moved the centre of gravity toward Fowden, without dissolving the question. The position this article adopts, because it is the position the evidence currently supports, is the double one stated in Section I: Greek in language and philosophical idiom, Roman in date, Egyptian in milieu and in more than decoration. Casaubon (Section XII) was right about the date. He was not required to be right about everything else.

// ALEXANDRIA, WITH A WARNING //

Alexandria is the conventional shorthand for the Hermetica's world, and this article's title keeps it — the city was Roman Egypt's intellectual capital and the natural home of its Greek-Egyptian-Jewish exchanges. But the treatises name no city, and the Nag Hammadi and Panopolis evidence points up the Nile as well as to the Delta. “Alexandria” in Hermetic contexts should be read as the name of a culture, not as a proven street address.

A last point of milieu, easily missed because the texts are so bookish: the dialogues presuppose circles. A teacher, a small company of disciples — Tat, Asclepius, Ammon — secrecy enjoined, hymns sung in common, a meal after prayer in the Nag Hammadi note. Fowden called the pattern, in paraphrase, a way of Hermes: a graded spiritual path pursued in small Hermetic communities. Whether those communities were formal lodges, temple circles, or reading groups gathered around a charismatic teacher cannot be recovered, and this article does not pretend otherwise. But the social form inscribed in the texts — master, initiates, silence, hymn — is itself evidence, and it is the form the later Hermetic tradition, from the Renaissance academies onward, instinctively reproduced.

VI. Poimandrēs (CH I): The Vision, the Making, and the Way Back

The first treatise is the Corpus's most famous, and the one from which the whole collection took its Renaissance name: Ficino titled his translation the Pimander after it. Its Greek title names its revealer: Poimandrēs, the being who appears to the narrator. The name's own etymology is disputed — a Greek reading takes it as “shepherd of men”; a widely discussed modern proposal derives it from an Egyptian phrase for the knowledge of Re — and the dispute is a miniature of the whole Hermetic question (Section V). What follows is a paraphrase of the treatise's thirty-two short sections, following the account in Copenhaver's translation and the standard scholarly summaries. It is not a quotation, and no sentence in it should be cited as the treatise's wording.

The Vision (§§1–3, in paraphrase)

The narrator relates that once, when his thought was turned toward the things that are, his understanding rose to a great height while his bodily senses were held back, as in the heaviness of sleep after food or labour. A being of boundless size appeared, called him by name, and asked what he wished to hear and see. Asked who he was, the being named himself Poimandrēs, the Mind (Nous) of the sovereign power, and offered to teach what the narrator desired. The narrator asked to learn the things that are, to understand their nature, and to know God. The revelation that follows is the answer.

The Making of the Cosmos (§§4–11, in paraphrase)

The narrator saw an unbounded light — serene and joyful — and then, in counterpoint, a darkness coiling downward that became a moist, troubled nature, smoking and giving out an inarticulate sound. From the light a holy Word (Logos) came upon the moist nature; pure fire leapt up from it, light and eager; air followed; earth and water remained mingled below. Poimandrēs explained, in paraphrase: the light is Mind, the narrator's god, older than the moist nature that emerged from darkness; the Word that comes from Mind is the son of God; and the two are not separate from the life they give. Then Mind — described as male-and-female, being life and light — brought forth by a word a second Mind, a craftsman (demiurge), who formed seven governors (administrators) enclosing the sensible world in circles; their government is what is called fate. The Word rose to join the craftsman Mind, its kin; the heavy elements left below produced living creatures without reason; and the ordered cosmos stood complete: elements below, governors above, and all of it held within Mind.

The Human (§§12–19, in paraphrase)

Mind, the father of all, then brought forth a Human (Anthrōpos) in his own image, beautiful and bearing the father's likeness, and loved him as his own child, granting him authority over the craftsman's works. The Human, entering the craftsman's sphere, received from each of the seven governors a share of its nature, and then broke through the vault of the spheres and leaned down through the cosmic framework to show the form of God to downward-tending nature. Nature saw the reflection of that form in her own element and smiled with love; the Human, seeing in nature a form like his own, reflected in her waters, loved it and willed to dwell in it — and act and will coincided: he took up residence in the form without reason. Nature embraced her lover, and they were mingled. Hence the treatise's anthropology, in paraphrase: the human being alone among living creatures is double — mortal through the body, immortal through the essential Human; subject to fate, yet carrying within an authority above the framework of the spheres. In time, the first androgynous generation was divided into male and female, with the divine command to increase and multiply; and the treatise draws its ethical line at once: whoever has recognised himself knows where he comes from and returns to life and light, while whoever has loved the body that came from the error of desire wanders in darkness.

The Way Back (§§20–32, in paraphrase)

The return reverses the descent. At death, in paraphrase, the body is surrendered to alteration, the senses return to their sources, and the soul rises through the framework of the spheres, giving back at each of the seven zones what it had received: at the first, the energy of growth and decline; then, in succession, the scheming of evil, the deceit of desire, the arrogance of rule, unholy daring, the evil impulses of wealth, and the falsehood that lies in wait. Stripped of all the governors' workings, the soul enters the eighth region — the ogdoadic nature — where it joins the powers in hymning the Father, and those who have ascended are made powers themselves and come to be in God. That, the treatise says in paraphrase, is the good end for those who have knowledge: to be made god. The vision closes in mission and worship: Poimandrēs mingles with the powers; the narrator, empowered, goes out to preach to those sunk in drunkenness, sleep, and ignorance of God, calling them to sobriety and to a share in immortality; some mock and depart, others are taught; and the treatise ends with the narrator's hymn and prayer — thanksgiving for knowledge received, and a plea not to fall away from it and to bring light to those of his kind still in ignorance.

// TWO READING WARNINGS //

First: “fall” is the interpreter's word, not the treatise's. The Human of §§12–15 is not expelled and does not rebel; he leans down in love, and the text's tone toward the descent is wonder as much as lament. Second: the seven zones of the ascent are not named as planets in the text; calling them the planetary spheres rests on their identification with the seven governors of §9 — a standard and reasonable identification, but an identification all the same. Both warnings are the kind of distinction Copenhaver's notes exist to preserve, and both are routinely lost in popular retellings.

Why this treatise, of the seventeen, carries the tradition: it contains the whole Hermetic plot in miniature — revelation by Mind, a cosmos made through Word and craftsman, a humanity double in nature, salvation as self-knowledge, and an ascent that undoes the descent zone by zone. Every later Hermetic text, and most later Hermetic readers, are footnotes to one or another of its movements.

VII. A Constellation, Not a System: The Other Treatises

Read straight through, the Corpus Hermeticum does not agree with itself, and honest accounts — Copenhaver's introduction among them — say so plainly. Some treatises speak warmly of the cosmos as a beautiful, living, divine order, a “second god” through whom the first God can be contemplated; others speak of the body and the material world as a burden and a prison from which the soul must escape. Scholarship has long described these as an optimistic and a pessimistic strand within the literature — world-affirming and world-fleeing Hermetism — with the pessimistic strand standing nearest to Gnosticism. The inconsistency is not a defect to be explained away. It is the expected product of an anthology of different authors writing devotional philosophy for different circles across two centuries, and Fowden's suggestion (Section V) — that some apparent contradictions mark stages of a graded path rather than rival doctrines — can be held alongside it. What the Corpus offers is a constellation of related treatises, not a system; the system was supplied later, by its readers.

Three treatises beyond the first repay naming here, because later sections and the companion articles lean on them. CH IV, the Mixing Bowl (in paraphrase): God sent down a great bowl — a krater, the mixing vessel of a Greek symposium — filled with Mind, and a herald invited all who could to immerse themselves; those who did gained knowledge and became complete in mind, while those who refused the message remained at the level of reason alone. CH X, the Key, offers the Corpus's most compact overview of its own teaching, a summary dialogue that ancient and modern readers alike have used as a map of the whole. CH XIII, On Rebirth — a secret dialogue on a mountain — is the Corpus's initiatory centrepiece (in paraphrase): Hermes leads Tat through a rebirth (palingenesia) accomplished in silence and contemplation, in which twelve tormentors that bind the soul to matter — ignorance, grief, lust, injustice, greed, deceit, envy, anger among them — are driven out and ten divine powers enter in their place, and the reborn Tat answers with a hymn. Together with the Nag Hammadi Discourse on the Eighth and Ninth (Section III), CH XIII is the chief evidence that Hermetic knowledge was imagined not as information but as transformation.

The alchemist Zosimos of Panopolis writes in Upper Egypt, echoing the philosophical Hermetica inside alchemical teaching — the two families in one reader. See Zosimos of Panopolis.

VIII. Ancient Readers: Lactantius, Augustine, and the Prisca Theologia

The Hermetica survived antiquity because two kinds of reader preserved them: the pagan and Hermetic circles who copied them, and the Christian authors who quoted them — in admiration, in refutation, and usually in some mixture of the two. Those Christian readings fixed the terms in which Europe would receive Hermes for more than a thousand years, and they built, between them, the chronological claim on which the entire Renaissance reading rested: that Hermes was a sage of the deepest antiquity, a contemporary — or near-contemporary — of Moses.

Lactantius: Hermes as a Witness

Lactantius (c. 250–c. 325), rhetorician and later tutor to a son of the emperor Constantine, quoted Hermes repeatedly and warmly in his Divine Institutes (written c. 304–313). In paraphrase of his use: Hermes had known and spoken of the one God, and of a divine Son — a Word — and could therefore be summoned as a pagan witness, outside Scripture, to truths Christians held by revelation. Lactantius quoted Hermetic material in Greek, including passages of the Perfect Discourse (the Greek original of the Asclepius) and of treatises and fragments known to him — quotations that are now themselves evidence for the texts' circulation in his century. His Hermes is the first great instance of the pattern: an ancient pagan authority, older than the Greek philosophers, who had foreseen Christian truth. The pattern requires the antiquity. A Hermes of Lactantius's own era could witness to nothing.

Augustine: Hermes Under Suspicion

Augustine of Hippo (354–430) read the same dialogue and reached the opposite valuation. In Book VIII of the City of God (chapters 23–26, in the standard division), Augustine discusses Hermes Trismegistus at length in the course of his argument against the worship of demons as mediators between gods and men. He quotes the Asclepius extensively — in paraphrase of his treatment: Hermes taught that alongside the gods made by the supreme God there are gods made by men, visible images inhabited by invited spirits, animated bodies with power to harm or to fulfil their worshippers' desires — and Augustine condemns this god-making as idolatry and demon-work, the more dangerous for the learning of its author. Yet the same chapters preserve, in Augustine's own quotation, the Asclepius prophecy of Egypt's abandonment (Section III): Hermes bewailing in advance the destruction of his forefathers' religion. Augustine reads the lament, in paraphrase, as an unwilling prophecy — Hermes foreseeing, and grieving, the abolition of the very superstitions he practised — and so even his refutation enrols Hermes among the witnesses.

Augustine also supplied the chronology that would govern the tradition. In the City of God's later books (Book XVIII, chapter 39, in the standard division) he places Hermes — the Egyptian Trismegistus, grandson in his account of the elder Hermes — long before the Greek philosophers and sages, while still after Moses in the reckoning Augustine is there concerned to defend. Later readers, less careful than their source, routinely promoted Hermes to Moses' contemporary or predecessor. The point for this article is structural: by the fifth century, the two moves that define the next millennium of Hermetic reading are both in place. Hermes is ancient — ancient enough to stand at the head of philosophy — and Hermes is double-valued: a prophet of Christian truth to Lactantius, a learned idolater to Augustine. Admiration and suspicion never thereafter travel separately.

The Prisca Theologia Frame

The frame that holds both moves is the prisca theologia — the “ancient theology”: the conviction that a single true wisdom about God was revealed in the remotest antiquity and transmitted, in fragments and under veils, through a succession of ancient sages — Hermes, Zoroaster, Orpheus, Pythagoras, Plato among the usual names — until its full disclosure in Christianity. The frame is older than the Renaissance that made it famous: it is assembled from the Church Fathers' own strategies for valuing pagan wisdom, from late-antique Platonists' genealogies of their tradition, and from the Hermetica's self-presentation as the teaching of Egypt's most ancient sage. Within the frame, the Hermetica are not one literature among others. They are the first link of the chain — the oldest recoverable statement of the theology that Plato would later philosophise and Christianity fulfil. Everything in Sections X and XI follows from that placement, and everything in Section XII follows from its collapse: remove the first link's antiquity, and the chain does not shorten. It ceases to be a chain.

// WHY THE DATE WAS THE DOCTRINE //

Modern readers often treat the Hermetica's date as a bibliographical detail. For their pre-modern readers it was the doctrine's warrant. A teaching about God, Mind, and rebirth carries one weight as the work of a sage older than Moses, and quite another as the work of anonymous devotees of the second century CE writing centuries after Plato and after Christ. The Renaissance did not merely misdate the treatises; it built an authority-structure on the misdating. That is why a philological argument, in 1614, could land like a demolition.

IX. The Long Middle: One Dialogue in the West, a Corpus in Byzantium

Between Augustine and Ficino, the Hermetica led two separate lives. In the Greek East, the Corpus treatises continued to be copied in Byzantium; the anthology Stobaeus had made was transmitted; Psellos, in the eleventh century, knew and used the collection (Section II); and Byzantine scholarship preserved the manuscripts from which the Renaissance copies descend. In the Latin West, by contrast, the Greek Corpus was effectively unknown. What the Latin Middle Ages possessed was the Asclepius alone — the one philosophical Hermetic dialogue available in Latin — travelling under Apuleius's name, read, excerpted, and argued with by medieval authors who knew its author chiefly through Lactantius's praise and Augustine's warning. To that slender textual base the medieval West added a large penumbra of technical Hermetica — astrological, alchemical, and magical writings under Hermes' name, many of them arriving through Arabic intermediaries — so that the medieval Hermes is at once a shadowy theological authority and a working patron of the occult sciences, with very little of the philosophical Corpus visible in between.

The Arabic route deserves its sentence here, because the hub's Transmission article treats it in full: Hermes — identified in Arabic tradition with the prophet Idrīs, and multiplied in some accounts into three Hermeses — presided over a substantial Arabic Hermetic literature of astrology, alchemy, and talismanic magic, through which technical Hermetic material reached both the Islamic world and, by translation, Latin Europe. The philosophical dialogues, however, did not travel that road into the Latin West. They came by the other road: a Greek manuscript, carried west in person. That is the event of the next section.

X. Ficino, 1463: Hermes Before Plato

Around 1460, a monk named Leonardo da Pistoia, one of the agents whom Cosimo de' Medici employed in the search for ancient manuscripts, returned to Florence from Macedonia with a Greek manuscript containing fourteen Hermetic treatises — CH I–XIV, the bulk of the Byzantine collection. The manuscript, a fourteenth-century copy, survives in the Laurentian Library. Cosimo — in his seventies, and, as the tradition consistently reports, conscious of his remaining time — directed his young translator Marsilio Ficino (1433–1499) to set aside the work then in hand, the translation of Plato, and to translate Hermes first. Ficino complied. A manuscript colophon records the completion of the Latin translation in April 1463. It was Ficino's first book.

The episode is usually told as an anecdote about priorities. It is better read as the prisca theologia (Section VIII) acting as a work order. On the genealogy Ficino himself sets out in the prefatory Argumentum to his translation — his only extended critical reflection on the Hermetica, assembled, as he presents it, from the testimony of Cicero, Lactantius, and Augustine — Hermes stands at the head of the line of ancient theologians that runs through Orpheus, Aglaophemus, Pythagoras, and Philolaus to Plato (Ficino's later lists place Zoroaster at the head of the line). Plato, on that scheme, is Hermes' heir at several removes. An elderly patron who wanted the source before the derivative, and who might not live to read both, was not indulging a curiosity; he was reading in chronological order as his century understood chronology. Hermes came before Plato because Hermes came before Plato.

THE TRANSLATION

Ficino's Latin version of CH I–XIV, completed April 1463, titled from its first treatise the Pimander (Latin: Mercurii Trismegisti Pimander — in its printed form, the book of Hermes Trismegistus on the power and wisdom of God). Circulated first in manuscript; Cosimo died in August 1464, having received it.

THE PRINTING

The editio princeps was printed at Treviso by Gerardus de Lisa on 18 December 1471. The Pimander became one of the publishing successes of the incunable age, reprinted repeatedly across Europe for two centuries — the vehicle through which Latin Europe actually read the philosophical Hermetica.

What Ficino's readers received, it should be noted, was not quite the modern Corpus. They received fourteen treatises, in Ficino's Latin — a Latin that Christianises at the margins, as his translation choices render the Greek's divine sonship and regeneration in vocabulary resonant with the Church's — prefaced by an Argumentum certifying the author's Mosaic antiquity, and accompanied by the already-familiar Latin Asclepius. The remaining treatises, XVI–XVIII, reached print later in Lazzarelli's translation. The Hermetica of the Renaissance are therefore a specific textual object — Ficino's Pimander plus the Asclepius — and arguments about “the Hermetic influence” that do not specify that object are, as Section XIII shows, arguments at risk of vagueness. Ficino himself went on to the Plato translation Cosimo had interrupted, to the Platonic Theology, and to the Three Books on Life (1489), whose third book's astral medicine and talismanic material — drawn, as modern scholarship has established, chiefly from Plotinus, Proclus, and the medieval astrological tradition rather than from the Corpus — would later be Exhibit A in the case for a Hermetic Ficino. The distinction matters, and Section XIII returns to it.

XI. The Renaissance Afterlife: Pico, Bruno, and the Republic of Letters

For roughly a century and a half after 1471 — the span from Ficino's printed Pimander to Casaubon's redating — Hermes Trismegistus stood in learned Europe where Ficino's Argumentum had placed him: at the head of the ancient theologians, an Egyptian sage of Mosaic antiquity whose writings authorised a programme of recovering a pristine, pre-scholastic wisdom in which Plato, Moses, and Christ could be read as agreeing. The programme's adherents were not a sect. They were, at various moments, much of the Republic of Letters: humanists, court philosophers, physicians, astrologers, friars, and printers, for whom citing Hermes was a normal scholarly act. Two figures dominate the modern telling, and both need careful statement.

Pico della Mirandola: An Honest Accounting

Giovanni Pico della Mirandola (1463–1494) composed his famous Oration in 1486 as the introduction to a public defence, planned in Rome, of nine hundred theses — the Conclusiones — drawn from every philosophy and theology he could read. The popular claim, repeated in countless textbooks, makes the Oration a Hermetic manifesto: man's dignity, on this telling, is Hermes' teaching proclaimed. The claim needs the correction that Copenhaver and other Pico scholars have supplied. Pico certainly honoured Hermes and cited the Hermetica — the Asclepius's praise of man as a great wonder stands behind the Oration's opening anthropology, in paraphrase — and Hermetic theses appear among the nine hundred. But the Oration's operative magic, and the bulk of its novel learning, are Kabbalistic, not Hermetic: Pico's great import into Latin philosophy was Christian Kabbalah, learned from Jewish teachers and from the convert Flavius Mithridates, who translated Kabbalistic texts for him; and his defence of natural magic draws on the same broad Neoplatonic and medieval sources as Ficino's. In Copenhaver's judgement — stated here as his scholarly position, in paraphrase — Pico was even less a Hermetic thinker than Ficino, and the Oration is not about Hermetic magic, though it recommends natural magic and promotes Kabbalah. The honest sentence is the compound one: the Hermetica are present in Pico — honoured, cited, woven into the syncretic programme — and they are not the programme's engine. Kabbalah is nearer to that honour, and any account that reverses the two has inherited Yates (Section XIII) without her footnotes.

Bruno: The Strongest Case

With Giordano Bruno (1548–1600) the Hermetic strand is genuinely central, and the texts are not shy about it. In the Italian dialogues of his London years — among them the Spaccio de la bestia trionfante (The Expulsion of the Triumphant Beast, 1584) — Bruno, in paraphrase of his argument, sets the religion of Egypt as the Asclepius laments it above a corrupted present: a natural religion in which the divine was worshipped in the living world, against which the intervening centuries figure as decline. His memory arts, his magic of bonds and images in works such as De vinculis in genere, and his infinite, animated cosmos all draw sustenance from the Hermetic-Neoplatonic atmosphere Ficino's translations had created. Bruno was burned at Rome in 1600 after trial on charges centred on theological heresies — denial of doctrines including the Trinity and transubstantiation, among many counts — and not, as popular telling has it, simply for Copernican astronomy or for Hermes. Frances Yates (Section XIII) made Bruno the hero of her Hermetic Renaissance: a Hermetic magus whose Copernicanism itself was, on her reading, a religious symbol — the sun restored to the centre as in the Egyptian religion. Later Bruno scholarship keeps the Hermetic commitment and qualifies the thesis built on it: Bruno's debts to Lucretius, to Nicholas of Cusa, and to the Neoplatonic tradition are at least as load-bearing as his Hermetism, and his Copernicanism has mathematical and philosophical motives that a purely Hermetic reading flattens. Bruno remains the strongest case for a Renaissance Hermeticism that is more than a citation habit. He is also, in the modern literature, the case that teaches the difference between Hermetic material in a thinker and a thinker explained by Hermeticism.

Around these two, the afterlife ramified: Lodovico Lazzarelli, translator of the Corpus's last treatises and author of the Hermetic dialogue Crater Hermetis, stands in modern reassessment (notably Wouter Hanegraaff's) as the period's most genuinely Hermetic philosopher — a reminder that the canon of “great Hermeticists” is itself a historiographical choice; Agostino Steuco gave the ancient-theology programme its enduring name in De perenni philosophia (1540), the “perennial philosophy”; Hermetic citations thread through sixteenth-century natural philosophy, medicine, and antiquarianism; and the Corpus in Greek reached print in Adrien Turnèbe's Paris edition of 1554. By 1600 Hermes was less a discovery than a furniture of the learned world — which is precisely the condition that made 1614 a shock.

XII. Casaubon, 1614: The Redating

Isaac Casaubon (1559–1614), the Genevan Huguenot scholar then in the service of James I of England, published at London in 1614 his De rebus sacris et ecclesiasticis exercitationes XVI — sixteen exercises on sacred and ecclesiastical matters — directed against the Annales Ecclesiastici of the Counter-Reformation cardinal Cesare Baronio (Baronius), whose church history had, among much else, cited Hermes as an ancient pagan prophet of Christian truth. The Hermetic argument occupies a small portion of the book (Exercitatio I, chapter 10, in the usual citation). It is among the most consequential few pages in the history of classical scholarship.

The Argument

Casaubon's method was philology, and his argument, in paraphrase, runs in converging lines. The Greek of the treatises is not the Greek of remote antiquity but of the Roman imperial centuries: its vocabulary includes words and usages unattested in early authors, and its philosophical terminology is that of the later schools. The treatises show knowledge of doctrines, persons, and artefacts of a late age — Casaubon pointed, among his exhibits, to material inconceivable from a contemporary of Moses, including references consonant with a world that knew Plato's successors and, in his reading, Christian ideas. No genuinely early author quotes or mentions the treatises: the silence of the pre-Christian record, where a sage of Hermes' supposed eminence should have been famous, is itself testimony. And the treatises were therefore not translations from an ancient Egyptian original but compositions in Greek, of the Christian era — Casaubon's own placement, in the form in which later scholarship reports it, put them in the centuries after Christ, in the neighbourhood of the second and third centuries that modern dating (Section V) confirms. He took the authors for half-Christian forgers trading on Plato and the Gospel; the forgery charge has not survived, but the date has.

WHAT THE REDATING DESTROYED

The Mosaic dating, and with it the prisca theologia's first link. Hermes could no longer stand before Plato as his source, before Moses as his contemporary, or behind Christianity as its pagan prophet. The authority-structure built on the antiquity (Section VIII) lost its foundation, and Hermetic citation drained out of mainstream learned argument across the following generations.

WHAT IT DID NOT DESTROY

The texts. The treatises remain what Casaubon's own argument showed them to be: genuine works of late antiquity — second- and third-century religious philosophy from Roman Egypt, copied in Byzantium, translated in the Renaissance, and still read. Casaubon changed their date, not their existence; and his further charge — that they were Christian-influenced fabrications — has been replaced by the modern reading of them as authentic products of Greco-Egyptian religious circles.

Two qualifications keep the episode honest. First, the demolition was slower and less total than the textbook version suggests: Hermetic and prisca-theological reading persisted through the seventeenth century — in Athanasius Kircher's Egyptian antiquarianism, in Ralph Cudworth's defence of ancient theology, in Isaac Newton's private prisca sapientia manuscripts — and Casaubon's book itself, a vast Latin polemic left unfinished at his death in the year of its publication, persuaded by accumulation rather than by decree. Second, Casaubon was arguing as a controversialist as well as a philologist: his target was Baronius and the Catholic use of pagan prophecy, and his Protestant patrons had commissioned a refutation. That his argument was nonetheless right — right on evidence that any reader of Greek could check — is the episode's enduring lesson, and the reason modern scholarship, which has corrected his estimate of the texts' character, has never needed to correct his date.

XIII. The Yates Thesis and Its Correction

In 1964 the Warburg historian Frances A. Yates published Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic Tradition, and Hermetic studies — and, for a generation, Renaissance studies — reorganised themselves around it. The thesis, stated fairly and in paraphrase: that the recovery of the Hermetica in Ficino's translation was not a sideshow of the Renaissance but one of its engines; that it created a “Hermetic tradition” of magi — Ficino, Pico, Bruno at their head — for whom magic was a dignified, operative engagement with a living cosmos; that Bruno's Hermetism explains his philosophy and his Copernicanism; and that this Hermetic, magus-centred current, with its will to operate on nature, stands in the ancestry of the Scientific Revolution itself. Yates wrote with learning and imaginative sympathy, she restored the occult philosophies to serious historical attention after decades of dismissal, and her Bruno remains a permanent provocation. No account of the field is honest that does not begin by granting the book its power.

The Correction

The correction, when it came, was textual before it was ideological, and its leading figure is the translator of Section I. In work beginning with his 1988 critique and carried through the introduction to Hermetica (1992) and his later Magic in Western Culture (2015), Brian Copenhaver argued — stated here in paraphrase as his scholarly position — that Yates had mislocated the magic. The philosophical theory of magic in Ficino and his successors derives, on close reading of their sources, from the Neoplatonists — Plotinus, Porphyry, Iamblichus, Synesius, Proclus — and from medieval astrological and medical traditions, not from the Corpus Hermeticum, in which operative magic is marginal: the treatises are works of theology, cosmology, and contemplative soteriology whose path is knowledge, prayer, hymn, and rebirth, not talismanic operation. The one great exception — the statue animation of the Asclepius — proves the rule by its notoriety: it was the passage Augustine had already singled out, and Renaissance magi handled it with visible caution. Around Copenhaver's core argument the wider reassessment gathered: the category “Hermetic tradition” itself, inherited by Yates from Paul Oskar Kristeller's coinage of 1938, was charged with vagueness — stretched, in György Szőnyi's summary of the critics' case, until it could cover Platonism, Kabbalah, alchemy, and natural magic indifferently, and so explain everything and nothing; the supposed Renaissance rupture was softened by medievalists who found the continuities (the Latin Asclepius had never been lost); Lazzarelli was recovered as the period's exemplary Hermetic thinker at the expense of Yates's starring cast (Section XI); and Hanegraaff's re-examination proposed the deliberately modest label “Renaissance Hermetism” for a specific, text-bound phenomenon, in place of a world-historical Hermeticism. The link from Hermetic magic to experimental science, the boldest span of Yates's bridge, is the span least of her structure still standing.

// THE MODERN POSITION, IN ONE PARAGRAPH //

The Corpus Hermeticum is philosophy and religion — a devotional Platonism of Roman Egypt whose centre is gnōsis, rebirth, and hymn — with magic at its margins, chiefly in the Asclepius and in the technical literature that shares its author's name. Renaissance thinkers revered it, cited it, and built genealogies on its supposed antiquity; their operative magic came mostly from elsewhere. Yates was right that the Hermetica mattered enormously to the Renaissance imagination, and wrong about the mechanism: they mattered as theology and as authority, not as a magician's manual. That is the position Copenhaver's scholarship represents, it is the position of the current reference literature, and it is the position of this article.

A closing fairness. The correction of Yates is not a return to the pre-1964 dismissal of the occult philosophies as embarrassments. Yates taught two generations of historians to take Ficino's world seriously as its inhabitants understood it; her critics work inside the field she opened, with the texts she taught them to read. The sequence — Festugière's edition, Yates's synthesis, Fowden's milieu, Copenhaver's translation and critique — is the normal shape of scholarship working as it should, and it is the same shape this hub applies, at its other sites and in its other divisions, to claims of every kind.

XIV. Reading the Hermetica Today — and Why They Sit Beside Alchemy

The modern reader meets the Hermetica in a better position than any reader since Byzantium: a standard Greek text (Nock–Festugière), a standard English translation with full notes (Copenhaver, 1992), the Stobaean material and the Nag Hammadi texts in modern editions, and a scholarly literature that has settled the date, mapped the milieu, and argued the Egyptian question to a productive draw. The treatises repay reading in the order the Corpus gives them — the Poimandrēs first, for the plot; the Key (CH X) for the map; the Mixing Bowl (CH IV) and On Rebirth (CH XIII) for the practice of transformation; the Asclepius last and whole, for the dialogue that carried Hermes through the Latin centuries — with the Kore Kosmou and the Discourse on the Eighth and Ninth as the essential excursions beyond the Corpus itself. Readers coming from this archive's Egyptology division will find the same texts wearing their other aspect in its Corpus Hermeticum and Hermeticism articles; the two treatments are designed to be read together.

And the placement of this article, finally, explains itself. The Hermetica sit in the Alchemy wing because alchemy, in its Alexandrian birthplace, is unintelligible without them: Zosimos read Hermes as this article's readers now can; the technical Hermetica supplied alchemy with its patron, its authorising antiquity, and much of its vocabulary of transformation; and the philosophical treatises supplied the frame — a cosmos alive with Mind, a humanity double in nature, a knowledge that is itself a rebirth — inside which turning lead toward gold could present itself as a sacred science rather than a craft secret. The traffic ran both ways for fifteen hundred years, and the hub's Transmission article follows it east and west from Alexandria. The sibling relationship is the historical fact: one family theological and contemplative, one operative and practical, one name over both doors. Hermes kept both houses. This article has kept to the quieter one — the house of dialogues, hymns, and a date that changed the world by being wrong.
