I. The Phase Change: From Workshop Letter to Lecture Hall

Every literature has a moment when it stops being written by its practitioners alone and starts being taught. For Greek alchemy that moment has a date-range, a city, and a cast. The date-range is the fourth to the seventh century CE. The city is Alexandria — until, at the end of the story, it is Constantinople. The cast is the generation modern scholarship calls, simply, the commentators: Synesius in the fourth century, commenting on pseudo-Democritus; Olympiodorus in the sixth, commenting on Zōsimos; the Christian philosopher known as Christianus, commenting on the whole early canon; and Stephanus of Alexandria in the seventh, lecturing on the art entire from an imperial chair. Around them stand an Anonymous Philosopher and four verse poets, and behind them all stands the figure who made their work possible and necessary at once: Zōsimos of Panopolis (fl. c. 300 CE), the last great author of the art’s first age and the first of its exegetes.

The change can be stated as a change of literary form, and the form is the fact. Zōsimos wrote letters and memoirs: urgent, personal, addressed to a named patroness, Theosebeia, full of furnaces, visions, warnings against charlatans, and quotations from the ancients deployed in argument. The commentators wrote commentaries and lectures: impersonal in address, systematic in order, addressed to students, a court, or posterity, in which the ancients — Democritus, Hermes, Maria, and now Zōsimos himself — are no longer argued with but expounded from, lemma by lemma, as a school expounds Plato. Cristina Viano’s standard survey of the period, “Byzantine Alchemy, or the Era of Systematization” in the Oxford Handbook of Science and Medicine in the Classical World (2018), gives the generation its scholarly characterisation, followed throughout this article: a generation of commentators tied to the Neoplatonic milieu, whose writings were designed primarily to clarify the ideas of the previous generations — Democritus and Zōsimos above all — and which represent, in her judgement, the most advanced stage of ancient alchemical theory. Systematisation is the right word. What the workshop knew as operations, the lecture hall rebuilt as doctrine: with a history, a canon, a technical vocabulary fixed by definition, and a philosophical frame — above all the unity of matter — inside which every recipe could be discussed as an instance of a principle.

This article treats the commentators as thinkers and teachers. Its companion in this hub, Transmission, treats them as carriers — the first link in the chain of codices, Syriac versions, Arabic translations, and the Latin threshold of 1144 by which the corpus survived. Both treatments are true, and they are not the same. A carrier passes a parcel; a teacher interprets it, in public, to students who will remember the interpretation as the thing itself. The commentators did not merely hand Greek alchemy to Byzantium. They decided what Greek alchemy meant — which ancients were canonical, which of their disagreements were merely verbal, which words were cover-names for which substances, and what kind of knowledge the art was: a craft, a philosophy, or, in Christianus’s daring formulation, a divine gift administered by method. Without those decisions, the codices of the transmission article would have had far less to carry, and what they carried would have meant far less to everyone who received it. The lecture hall is where the corpus acquired its table of contents in the mind before any scribe gave it one on parchment.

A last framing observation belongs at the threshold, because it explains the article’s title. A lecture hall is not a neutral container. It has a curriculum, a method, an audience to be formed rather than merely informed, and an institution — a chair, a city, an emperor — that decides the teaching will continue next term. When alchemy entered that container, it gained order, method, continuity, and prestige; it also submitted to the container’s impositions: the text before the furnace, the authority before the experiment, the reconciliation of the ancients before the correction of any one of them. The four centuries of this article are the record of that bargain — on balance the making of the tradition, and, in respects Section XIV states plainly, a freezing of it. Both truths are the commentators’ legacy.

II. What a Commentary Is: Lemma, Theōria, Lexis

To understand what the commentators did to alchemy, the reader first needs the instrument in hand: the late-antique philosophical commentary itself, as the Alexandrian school had perfected it for Plato and Aristotle. The form was by the fourth century centuries old and highly standardised. A commentary takes a base text — the lemma text — that the school has already agreed is authoritative, and it proceeds through that text in order, in short quoted portions. Each portion, the lemma, is set down verbatim; the commentator then unfolds it. The unfolding, in the mature Alexandrian practice described in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s account of Olympiodorus the philosopher, was itself divided: a lecture (praxis) opened with the theōria, a general exposition of the passage’s meaning, scope, and place in the author’s system, and continued with the lexis, a close reading attending to wording, grammar, variants, and difficulties of detail. Before the text came the prolegomena: a standard set of introductory questions — the aim of the work, its authenticity, its order in the curriculum, its division into parts — answered for Aristotle’s treatises in a scheme the school of Ammonius had fixed. The commentary was, in short, a complete technology for keeping a dead author present as a living teacher: his words on the desk, his meaning in the professor’s voice, his system in the students’ notebooks.

Three features of that technology matter for alchemy, and each will recur through this article. First, the lemma preserves by excerpting. A base text quoted inside a commentary survives in the commentator’s chosen portions, in the commentator’s order, surrounded by the commentator’s sense. Where the base text is later lost — as Zōsimos’s On Action is lost — the commentary becomes its ark and its editor at once: we have the treatise exactly as far as a sixth-century teacher needed it for his lessons, and no further. Second, commentary reconciles. The Alexandrian school held, as a working axiom, that its authorities did not fundamentally disagree: Plato and Aristotle were to be harmonised, and apparent contradictions resolved by distinguishing senses, levels, or audiences. Transferred to alchemy, the axiom became the commentators’ central labour: Democritus, Hermes, Maria, and Zōsimos name the divine water differently and describe it differently, and the commentator’s task — Christianus’s above all — is to show the one thing beneath the many names. The reconciliation is not a footnote to the doctrine. It is the doctrine, in the form the schools could teach. Third, commentary is delivered from a voice. Many Alexandrian commentaries survive as a student’s record from the voice (apo phōnēs) of the master: lectures heard, noted, and circulated. Stephanus’s alchemical works keep the lecture form in their very title — praxeis, lessons — and their openings and prayers keep the lecture room’s air. These are not treatises that happen to be didactic. They are courses.

The Commentator’s Four Offices

It helps, before meeting the men, to name the offices the form conferred on whoever held it, because the commentators exercised all four over the alchemical corpus and the corpus still bears their exercise. The commentator was an editor: he chose the base text, established its wording as he quoted it, and decided — by quotation, abbreviation, or silence — which parts of the inheritance would be read. He was a canon-maker: to comment on Democritus and Zōsimos, and not on the recipe-papyrus or the workshop notebook, was to declare where the art’s classics lay, and the declaration stuck for a millennium. He was a systematist: the prolegomena questions forced on the art the shape of a discipline — its aim, its parts, its order of study, its relation to the other sciences — whether or not the workshop had ever conceived itself in that shape. And he was a glossator of names: the lexis, the close reading of words, made him the heir and the fixer of the art’s cover-names, the Decknamen under which substances hid; his glosses, gathered, became the glossaries with which the Byzantine codices open. Section XIV takes up each office’s profit and its price. Here it is enough to see that none of the four is passive. To comment, in Alexandria, was to govern.

// HOW TO READ A COMMENTATOR //

Ask of any commentary three questions. What base text does it quote, and does that text survive anywhere else? What disagreements does it reconcile, and is the reconciliation the author’s or the tradition’s? And what did the commentator leave out? The answers locate the fragment in the chain: a sentence of Zōsimos may reach us only because Olympiodorus needed it for a lesson, in the form Olympiodorus’s lesson required. (The rule is this hub’s, stated in the transmission article and applied throughout this one.)

One more feature of the form distinguishes the alchemical commentators from their philosophical colleagues. The philosopher commenting on Aristotle could assume his base text was trying to be clear: obscurity in Aristotle is a difficulty, not a design. The alchemist commenting on Democritus assumed the opposite. The alchemical authorities were held to have written obscurely on purpose — to hide the art from the unworthy and to exercise the minds of the worthy — and the commentators say so as a principle: Synesius attributes the training purpose to pseudo-Democritus himself, Olympiodorus and Stephanus repeat it, and the verse poets versify it (the dossier is Merianos’s, cited in Section X). A tradition whose founding axiom is that its texts mean more than they say holds a licence no Aristotelian exegete enjoyed: to find, in a recipe’s dark sentence, a philosophy its author may never have intended — and to find it honestly, by the school’s rules. Much of what is most impressive in the commentators, and much of what is most suspect, flows from that licence — including Dufault’s finding (Section VII) that the general transmutation theory is Olympiodorus’s own, projected by the form’s logic into the founders.

III. The Alexandrian School: The Room the Commentators Taught In

The lecture hall of this article’s title was a real place, with a succession of occupants whose names frame everything the alchemical commentators did. Alexandria in late antiquity was the Mediterranean’s school city: rhetoric, medicine, law, mathematics, and philosophy taught to an international student body, pagan and Christian together — the world Michael Griffin’s study of Ammonius and the Alexandrian school reconstructs in detail. The succession that matters here runs as follows. Hierocles of Alexandria (first half of the fifth century), a pupil of Plutarch of Athens, taught a providential, ethical Neoplatonism and commented on the Pythagorean Golden Verses — proof that commentary on a revered, riddling, verse authority was already an Alexandrian habit. Hermias, who had studied at Athens under Syrianus, held a publicly funded chair at Alexandria and commented on Plato’s Phaedrus from his master’s voice. His son Ammonius, son of Hermias (c. 435/445–517/526) inherited the chair and made the school: a pupil of Proclus, teacher of Damascius, Simplicius, Eutocius, Olympiodorus — and of the Christian John Philoponus (c. 490–570), the milieu’s emblem. Philoponus began as Ammonius’s pupil and editor, publishing his master’s lectures; he ended by turning the commentary’s own tools against Aristotle’s eternity of the world. A school in which a Christian pupil could publish a pagan master’s lectures, then refute the master’s cosmos in the master’s genre, was not a confessional enclave. It was a method with a building.

That method’s religious flexibility is the milieu’s second lesson for the alchemical story. The Alexandrian school taught pagan philosophy to predominantly Christian students, for careers in the clergy and the imperial service, and it survived — as Athens’ school, closed by Justinian around 529, did not — by a practiced conciliation: the Stanford Encyclopedia’s account of Olympiodorus stresses how his lectures accommodate his audience, rendering the gods as powers within the first cause, treating myth as language to be explained rather than doctrine to be believed, and keeping the school’s positions teachable inside an officially Christian empire. Olympiodorus the philosopher (c. 500–after 565), Ammonius’s heir and probably the last pagan to hold the Alexandrian chair without even nominal Christianity, taught students who would staff a Christian world; his own pupils — the shadowy Elias and David, and Stephanus — bore Christian names and carried the school’s pedagogy into the seventh century and to Constantinople. The alchemical commentators belong to exactly this ecology. Synesius writes to a priest of Serapis in a city whose Serapeum would fall in 391. Christianus writes as a Christian inside a corpus of pagans. Stephanus lectures with prayer, under a Christian emperor, on Hermes and Democritus. None of this is paradoxical in Alexandria. It is the school’s normal weather, and the commentators’ ease in it — their assumption that Democritus and the Gospel can be expounded by the same man, in the same week, by the same method — is Alexandrian before it is alchemical.

The curriculum is the third lesson. Alexandrian study was ordered: prolegomena first, then Aristotle’s logic in its set sequence, then physics and mathematics, then Plato, toward theology as the crown; lectures were divided into praxeis, each with its theōria and lexis. Two consequences follow for alchemy. First, the commentators present the art curricularly: with prolegomena questions (its aim? its founder? its order of reading?), a canon arranged for study, and a place among the recognised disciplines — Stephanus sets it beside medicine, mathematics, astronomy, and music, the company Christianus also claims for it by method. Second, the school’s habit of writing a discipline’s history as a succession of authorities was ready-made for an art that treasured its lineage: Olympiodorus’s doxography (Section VIII) is the curriculum’s historiography applied to Hermes and Zōsimos.

It remains to place the alchemical chairs — if chairs they were — inside this room honestly. No evidence shows alchemy as a funded subject of the Alexandrian school with a succession of its own; what the evidence shows is alchemical commentary written by the school’s people, in the school’s forms, for the school’s audiences: Synesius in dialogue with a temple priest, Olympiodorus (whoever exactly he was) in full prolegomena-and-doxography dress, Stephanus in titled lectures with the help of God. Viano’s formulation — a generation tied to the Neoplatonic milieu — is the correct strength, and this article keeps it. The tie is not institutional decoration. It is the explanation of the commentators’ method, their canon, their reconciliations, and their confidence that a dyeing recipe of the first century could bear, and deserved, the same exegetical reverence as the Timaeus. The workshop had produced texts. Only the school could have produced commentators. With the room furnished, the article turns to the texts on the desk — and to the first man who taught the school, by example, how to expound them.

IV. Before the Commentators: Bolos, Pseudo-Democritus, and Zōsimos the First Exegete

A commentary needs a classic, and alchemy’s classic was ready-made: the Four Books circulating under the name of Democritus — on the making of gold, on the making of silver, on precious stones, and on purple dyeing — gathered as the Physika kai mystika, the Natural and Secret Questions. The hub’s adjudicated position on the authorship behind that name is followed here exactly, as it is stated in full in Greek Alchemy: Origins. Bolos of Mendes, the Hellenistic compiler of the sympathy-and-antipathy tradition, flourished in the third to second century BCE and is a distinct figure from the alchemical author. The alchemical pseudo-Democritus — the anonymous writer (or writers) who composed the Four Books under Democritus’s name — is placed by Matteo Martelli’s edition (The Four Books of Pseudo-Democritus, 2013) in the second half of the first century CE, most likely c. 54–68 CE. The older literature’s simple identification of the two — one Mendesian author, c. 200 BCE, fathering both traditions — is not followed; the dispute is stated openly wherever the hub touches it, and it is stated here: Bolos is the formative predecessor, pseudo-Democritus the Neronian-era heir who reworked the older Democritean tradition, and the commentators of this article expound the heir’s books. Nothing in their exegesis requires the two to be one man, and their own citations never settle the question modern scholarship has separated.

What the Four Books bequeathed the commentators, beyond recipes, was a voice: oracular, compressed, and programmatic. Its most famous sentence is the aphorism the books ascribe to Democritus’s legendary Persian master Ostanes. This article quotes it once, verbatim, from the published translation in Martelli’s edition of the pseudo-Democritean tradition as preserved by Synesius — the one ancient wording in the article that meets its quotation rule, named here as its source: “Nature delights in nature, nature conquers nature, nature masters nature.” The sentence’s career is the commentary tradition in miniature. Zōsimos quotes it as the art’s charter. Synesius expounds it. Olympiodorus repeats Synesius’s exposition so closely — Martelli’s study of the indirect tradition shows the verbal dependence in detail, down to Olympiodorus’s apparent misreading of Synesius’s word for Democritus’s starting-points as a title of the Four Books themselves — that the chain of exegesis can be watched, link by link, in the Greek. An aphorism that no one could apply without help had created, by the sixth century, a profession of helpers.

The profession’s founder, however, is not Synesius. It is Zōsimos himself. The hub’s Zōsimos article establishes the point and this section only draws its consequence: Zōsimos (fl. c. 300 CE) is the first known commentator of alchemy. His Authentic Memoirs — the corpus as Michèle Mertens delimited it in Les alchimistes grecs, Tome IV.1 (1995) — proceed by quotation and unfolding: a sentence of Democritus, an aphorism of Maria, a saying of Hermes or Agathodaimon is set down and then expounded, corrected, and applied, with rival readers refuted by appeal to the canon. Zōsimos even preserves the names of exegetes before him — a lost commentary on the Four Books by an alchemist Petasius is known because the tradition cites it — which warns the reader that “first known” means first surviving: the commentary impulse is as old as the canon it serves, and Zōsimos is its earliest extant master, not necessarily its inventor. What he invented, or at least perfected, is the posture the next four centuries inherited: the living teacher who stands between the student and the ancients, quotes them exactly, and tells the student what they meant. When Synesius, Olympiodorus, and Stephanus later perform that office for Zōsimos’s own sentences, they are paying him the precise compliment he paid Democritus. The chain has no first link we can see. It has a first link we can read, and his name is Zōsimos.

That is the lecture hall’s family tree — and, Section XV will argue, the corpus’s.

V. Synesius to Dioscorus: Unfolding Democritus Term by Term

The earliest surviving work of the exegete generation is a dialogue. Its transmitted title, in the form the editions give it, presents it as the philosopher Synesius’s notes on Democritus’s book, addressed to Dioscorus; its opening greeting, as Martelli’s work on the indirect tradition establishes, salutes Dioscorus as priest of the great Serapis in Alexandria, with God’s approval — a pagan temple’s priest, greeted in a Christianising formula, by a philosopher expounding a pagan classic. The whole Alexandrian settlement of Section III is in that one salutation. The text itself stands in Berthelot and Ruelle at CAAG II, pp. 56–69, and in Martelli’s 2011 Italian edition of the pseudo-Democritean writings with Synesius’s commentary, on which the modern study of the work rests. Its date is fixed by its addressee’s temple: the Alexandrian Serapeum was destroyed in 391 CE, and a commentary addressed to its serving priest belongs before the fall — Martelli and Viano both place the work at the beginning of the fourth century, in Zōsimos’s near aftermath, on the strength of its close affinities with his treatises as well as the dedication. The hub gives the dating in that form: fourth century, before 391, probably early in the century. A more precise year is not available and none is pretended.

The dialogue’s programme is declared at its head, and Viano’s summary of the declaration is the one this article follows: Synesius states his exegetic intention openly — to disclose Democritus’s thought, the sequence of his teachings, and his processes and materials. Each element of the triad does work. Thought: the commentary’s business is not the recipes alone but the doctrine they encode. Sequence: Democritus is presented as a teacher with an ordered course, whose books unfold in a deliberate pedagogical order the commentator can recover — the curriculum idea of Section III, projected onto the founder. Processes and materials: for all its philosophy, the commentary keeps its hands on the substances; Synesius’s surviving exegesis attends chiefly, as Martelli’s study of the divine water in pseudo-Democritus shows, to the lists of solid and liquid substances the Four Books transmit, expounding their names one by one — often by a pseudo-etymological reading of the name itself, as if the word, rightly divided, would confess the thing. That is the lexis half of the Alexandrian lecture, applied to a recipe list: term by term, name by name, the master’s obscurity unfolded into a teachable inventory.

Deliberate Obscurity, and the Glossary in Embryo

Synesius’s most consequential doctrine is not about any substance. It is about Democritus’s style. The commentary insists — in the passages Merianos’s study collects and Martelli’s edition prints — that Democritus wrote obscurely by design: the riddling, scattered, cover-named manner of the Four Books was composed, in Synesius’s presentation of the master’s own defence, to exercise the minds and wits of its readers — to train the intelligence that could unfold it, and by the same act to exclude the intelligence that could not. The doctrine does three things at once, and the commentary tradition lived on all three for four centuries. It excuses the text: no difficulty in Democritus can count against him, since difficulty is his method. It authorises the commentator: if the meaning is deliberately folded, the unfolder is not an interloper but the text’s intended second author. And it obliges the glossary: a text that hides substances under cover-names, on purpose, permanently requires a key, and the commentator who unfolds term by term is manufacturing that key in public. Synesius is accordingly one of the chief ancient witnesses behind this hub’s Decknamen Glossary: his name-by-name exegesis of the Democritean substance lists — the divine water’s kinds distinguished, the solids and liquids identified — is among the earliest surviving strata of the cover-name tradition the glossaries of the codices would eventually freeze into lists. The glossary begins as a lecture.

The dialogue form is itself a teaching decision. It lets the commentator stage the student’s difficulties as a character’s questions — Dioscorus asks, doubts, and is corrected, and every later reader is invited into Dioscorus’s chair — and it flatters the addressee into the tradition: a priest of Serapis, the great temple that was Alexandria’s pagan heart, shown learning Democritus, a generation before the temple’s destruction made the scene unrepeatable. Whether the dialogue records real conversations or invents them cannot be known; its literary fact is enough. The first alchemical commentary is a conversation between philosophy and the temple, held in the temple’s last decades, about a book already three centuries old.

// SYNESIUS AS ARK //

The commentator’s second service is preservation. Synesius quotes pseudo-Democritus extensively — including the substance lists and the divine-water material Martelli edits from his fragments — and preserves, in quotation, matter the codices’ epitome of the Four Books abbreviates or drops. To read pseudo-Democritus today is, in part, to read Synesius’s quotations of him: the earliest commentator is already, unavoidably, one of his author’s manuscripts.

What Synesius does not do is equally diagnostic, and Dufault’s study (Section II) supplies the measure. For all his unfolding, Synesius articulates no general theory of transmutation: his Democritus gilds, colours, and doubles, and the commentary’s philosophy stays close to the names and the lists. The mystical reading of the older recipes as true transmutations — a reading the 1911 Encyclopaedia Britannica’s alchemy article already attributed to Synesius’s changed attitude — is, on the current scholarship, more than the text performs: the theory arrives with Olympiodorus, two centuries later, and is then read back into the whole succession. Synesius’s real historical work is quieter and more durable than a theory. He fixed the posture: Democritus as classic, obscurity as design, exegesis as the art’s second practice, the glossary as its necessary instrument. Every later commentator stands in that posture, including the ones who out-theorised him. It remains only to ask who he was — and that question, for Synesius, is a trap with a famous name in it.

VI. Two Synesii: The Alchemist and the Bishop of Cyrene

There are two Synesii in the late-antique record, and the tradition has spent centuries merging them. The first is the subject of Section V: an alchemical exegete of the fourth century, known only from his dialogue commentary on pseudo-Democritus. The second is one of the best-documented lives of the age: Synesius of Cyrene (c. 373–c. 414), aristocrat, Neoplatonist, pupil of Hypatia at Alexandria in the 390s, ambassador to Constantinople, hymn-writer, letter-writer of genius, and from 410 the Christian bishop of Ptolemais in Libya — a man whose surviving correspondence lets us watch him think about everything from barbarian invasions to the construction of an astrolabe. The identification of the alchemist with the bishop was made early, repeated by Berthelot’s generation, and is still repeated in popular accounts and even in reference entries that should know better: the bishop’s own article in general encyclopaedias has carried the alchemical commentary among his works. It must be handled head-on, because the hub’s position — following Martelli, Viano, and Dufault — is that the alchemist is probably not the bishop, and the reasons are instructive beyond this one name.

The case for the identification is not nothing, and fairness requires it first. The name matches, and it is not a common name. The dates match: a commentary written before 391 by a young Synesius who had studied at Alexandria in the 390s is chronologically tight but not impossible — though it requires the bishop to have written the dialogue as a very young man, essentially in his student years, to a serving priest of a temple still standing. The interests match, at a distance: the bishop’s letters show real scientific curiosity — the hydrometer passage, the astrolabe treatise, the request to Hypatia for instruments — and a Neoplatonist of his formation would have found Democritean natural philosophy congenial. And the prestige matches the tradition’s habit: an anonymous or obscure commentary gains immeasurably by attachment to a famous name, and the manuscript tradition of Greek alchemy attaches famous names as readily as any literature of the age. That last point, however, cuts both ways — it explains the identification as easily if the identification is false.

The case against rests on the dedication, and Viano’s statement of it is the one this article follows: the commentary is addressed to Dioscorus, a pagan priest of Serapis, in terms that present him as the author’s senior interlocutor in the art — and that dedication, she judges, makes the identification with the Christian bishop of Cyrene difficult to sustain. The difficulty is not that a Christian could not know a pagan priest; Alexandria was full of such friendships, and the bishop’s own devotion to the pagan Hypatia is the age’s famous example. The difficulty is cumulative and professional: nothing in the bishop’s large, self-revealing surviving corpus — letters, hymns, treatises, speeches — mentions alchemy, Democritus’s books, or anything like the commentary’s exegetical programme; the commentary’s manner, its substance lists and etymologies, belong to the workshop-exegetical world of Zōsimos’s successors rather than to the literary-philosophical world of Hypatia’s circle; and Martelli’s edition treats the alchemical Synesius throughout as a distinct figure, an author of Zōsimos’s near aftermath whose work Olympiodorus used — the relative chronology of Section IV, which the identification with the bishop (born c. 373) would strain to breaking. Dufault’s footnote on the question states the current resting point in the form this article adopts: Synesius “the alchemist” was probably not the well-known Synesius of Cyrene. Probably not: the evidence excludes certainty in either direction, and the article’s discipline is to carry the probability as a probability.

Why does the distinction matter enough for its own section? Because the merged Synesius does historiographical damage in both directions. Credit the bishop with the commentary, and alchemy acquires a false pedigree — a Hypatia-taught, court-connected bishop-philosopher expounding Democritus — that inflates the art’s institutional standing in the fourth century beyond what any other evidence supports. Credit the commentary’s obscurity-doctrine and substance lists to the bishop’s biography, and the bishop acquires a false workshop past that his letters nowhere corroborate. The unmerged record is more interesting than either inflation: an otherwise unknown Alexandrian exegete, working in Zōsimos’s shadow in the Serapeum’s last years, whose dialogue was good enough to be quoted for three centuries and borrowed, at some point, a famous contemporary’s identity — or lent his own work to that identity’s gravity. The commentators’ history is full of such gravity wells. Olympiodorus, to whom the article now turns, is the largest of them.

VII. Olympiodorus: On the Sacred Art, a Commentary on Zōsimos

Two centuries after Synesius, the commentary tradition produced its most ambitious surviving work. The treatise transmitted under Olympiodorus’s name — cited from Berthelot and Ruelle, CAAG II, pp. 69–106, and known by its short title On the Sacred Art — presents itself, in its full transmitted title, as a commentary on Zōsimos’s Kat’ energeian (On Action, or According to Action) together with what was said by Hermes and the philosophers. The base text is lost: Zōsimos’s On Action survives, so far as it survives, inside this commentary’s lemmata, a circumstance Section II’s rule exists for. The commentary’s date is the sixth century, on the succession evidence of Section IV — it quotes Synesius extensively and is quoted by Stephanus — and its author names himself, in the manuscripts’ attribution, Olympiodorus the Alexandrian philosopher. Who that attribution truly denotes is Section IX’s question, held open there with both cases stated. This section treats the work itself, which is substantial enough to need no borrowed biography: the most philosophical writing in the Greek alchemical corpus, and the hinge on which the art’s self-understanding turned from craft tradition to philosophy of nature.

The work’s shape already teaches. It begins as a commentary should — a passage of Zōsimos expounded in detail — and then, as the Stanford Encyclopedia’s survey puts it, continues in the vein of a didactic letter to an unnamed fellow alchemist, gathering quotation upon quotation: Zōsimos at length, Hermes, Petasius, Pelagius, the divine Maria, Agathodaimon, Chymes. The form is the commentary grown anthological — lemma, exegesis, and florilegium in one — and its effect is to constitute, inside a single treatise, the canon it expounds. A student who had read Olympiodorus had met the art’s authorities, in Olympiodorus’s excerpts and Olympiodorus’s order, whether or not he ever opened their books. The subjects the exegesis works through are the art’s standing problems: how scattered parts of gold are joined, how metals are purified, and above all the divine water — the dissolving, active principle that Viano’s survey identifies as the era’s central research object, the agent of transformation that acts by dissolution, named also as sulfur water, mercury, chrysocolla, or raw sulfur in the tradition’s several vocabularies, with mercury understood at once as dyeing agent and as the common metallic substrate, the principle of liquidity itself. To expound Zōsimos, for Olympiodorus, is to organise the whole art around that agent and that substrate — around, that is, a theory.

The Neoplatonic Frame: The Work as Purification

The frame within which Olympiodorus sets that theory is the frame of his school, and it is the feature of the commentary the brief for this article rightly insists on: the alchemical work is expounded as a process with a double register, in which the purification of the metals and the purification of the worker’s soul are not metaphors for each other but parallel instances of one pattern — the separation of the pure from the gross, the drawing of spirit out of body, the return of a scattered nature to its unity. The pattern is Zōsimos’s own before it is Olympiodorus’s: the Visions’ priest dismembered and become spirit, the memoirs’ doctrine of sōma and pneuma, are the Panopolitan’s native teaching, treated in full in the hub’s Zōsimos article (Sections X–XII). Olympiodorus’s contribution is to give that pattern its philosophical credentials: to state it in the vocabulary of the schools, to align it with the first principles of the earliest Greek physics (Section VIII), and thereby to present the furnace work as the laboratory form of the soul’s own ascent — matter perfected as the soul is perfected, by the removal of what is foreign to its nature and the actualising of what is its own. Later centuries would call this spiritual alchemy and build systems on it; the hub’s alchemical allegory article treats that long afterlife. Its Greek warrant begins here, in a commentary that refuses to let the metals’ purification be only about metals — and the refusal is a commentator’s act, performed on a base text, in a lecture’s form.

Two cautions discipline the admiration. The first is Dufault’s: the general theory of transmutation that later tradition credits to “the Greek alchemists” wholesale is articulated, among the texts he examines, by Olympiodorus’s commentary alone — Zōsimos works by analogy, aphorism, and dream, Synesius by name and list. The systematising is an event, not a summary: the moment the art acquired something approaching a unified doctrine of how one metal becomes another — by reversion to the common substrate and re-imposition of form, in Dufault’s analysis. The second caution is textual, and Section IX develops it: the transmitted commentary shows seams — shifts of genre, blocks of excerpt, a Christian citation of Paul (2 Corinthians 3:6) attributed to “the Lord” — that mark the work as layered, a core reworked by a compiler. Its ideas can be expounded with confidence; its every sentence cannot be assigned with the same confidence to one historical man.

To give a school a doxography was to declare it a philosophy with a past. VIII. The Doxography of the Founders: Nine Monists and the Unity of Matter

The centrepiece of Olympiodorus’s commentary, and the passage on which its modern study turns, is the doxography of On the Sacred Art §§18–27, in the analysis Cristina Viano devoted to it: “Olympiodore l’alchimiste et les Présocratiques: une doxographie de l’unité” (in Kahn and Matton, eds., Alchimie: art, histoire et mythes, 1995). A doxography, in the schools’ usage, is a structured survey of opinions — doxai — arranged so that a discipline’s history appears as the progressive articulation of a single truth. Olympiodorus’s survey sets the doctrines of nine monist Presocratic philosophers — the early Greek thinkers who had each proposed one principle (archē) of all things: Thales’ water, Anaximander’s boundless, Anaximenes’ and Diogenes of Apollonia’s air, Heraclitus’s fire, and their fellows, Melissus among them — beside the principles of the alchemical art, and reads the two series as one. The comparison is the argument, and Viano’s study shows it following the standard pattern of Neoplatonic surveys of opinion: this is school historiography, executed by the school’s rules, on the school’s new subject.

The argument’s nerve is the unity of matter. If each Presocratic archē can be read as an early, partial naming of the one underlying substrate — the common matter that receives all metallic forms — then the alchemists’ founding claim follows with philosophical honours: metals differ not in substance but in imposed qualities on a shared substrate, and what differs by qualities can be changed in its qualities. Transmutation ceases to be a workshop marvel and becomes a corollary of the oldest Greek physics. Viano’s analysis catches the exegesis in the act at its most granular: Olympiodorus reads even Melissus — the Eleatic philosopher of motionless, infinite Being — into the series by identifying his principle, in the commentary’s alchemical register, with sulfur, the yellow unburnt sulfur that the tradition counts among the divine water’s kindred agents (the passage stands at CAAG II, p. 81, in the lines Viano edits and Martelli’s divine-water study quotes). A reader may find the identification strained; that is beside the historical point, which is the method: no ancient is to be left outside the consensus, because the consensus is the doctrine. Beside the nine philosophers Olympiodorus enrols the art’s own founders — Hermes, Chymes, Agathodaimon, and Zōsimos himself — as the same succession continued by other means: the alchemists are the physicists’ heirs, and their furnaces are the succession’s current address.

One absence in the doxography is as eloquent as its inclusions, and the recent scholarship notes it: Democritus is missing from the nine. The atomist whose name heads the alchemical canon does not appear among Olympiodorus’s Presocratic monists — because, as the discussion in the Cultural History of Chemistry in Antiquity (2022) observes, Democritus figures throughout the commentary not as the founder of atomism but as the author of the four pseudepigraphic books on dyeing (on the dating of which, c. 54–68 CE on Martelli’s position, see Section IV). The doxography’s Democritus-shaped hole is thus a precise instrument: it shows Olympiodorus distinguishing, in practice, the historical philosopher from the alchemical authority — filing the first with the physicists he omits for plurality’s sake (atomism is no monism), and the second with the art’s founders whom the commentary exists to expound. The distinction the modern scholarship labours to state — Democritus of Abdera against pseudo-Democritus of the Four Books — is already operative, silently, in the sixth-century lecture room.

The doxography’s afterlife justifies its central place here. It is the first surviving attempt to write alchemy’s history as philosophy writes history — and every later history of the art, from the Anonymous’s list to the codices’ order to Berthelot’s own narrative, works in the genre it founded: a succession of named founders, each holding a fragment of the one doctrine, reconciled by the surveyor. That the survey reconciles by force — Melissus must become a sulfur theorist for the series to close — is the genre’s known vice. That it preserves, orders, and dignifies a scattered craft literature into a tradition teachable for a thousand years is its known virtue. The nine monists are the lecture hall’s foundation charter for the art: partly invented, wholly effective, and still legible in the charter’s own text.

IX. One Olympiodorus or Two? The Identity Debate Stated Fairly

No question in this article has been more argued, or better illustrates its method, than whether Olympiodorus the alchemist is Olympiodorus the Neoplatonist. The philosopher is a solid historical figure (Section III): Olympiodorus the Younger of Alexandria, c. 500–after 565, Ammonius’s heir, commentator on Plato’s Alcibiades, Gorgias, and Phaedo and on Aristotle’s Categories and Meteorology, astrologer-commentator on Paulus Alexandrinus, teacher of Elias, David, and Stephanus’s generation, and — the Stanford Encyclopedia’s phrase — one of the last pagans to teach philosophy at Alexandria. The alchemist is a name on four manuscripts of the Zōsimos commentary, styled in them “Olympiodorus, the Alexandrian philosopher.” Older scholarship, Berthelot’s generation included, moved between several Olympiodori — even attaching the work, in some accounts, to Olympiodorus of Thebes, the fifth-century historian and diplomat. The modern debate, in the form the Stanford Encyclopedia’s Olympiodorus article (Wildberg) surveys and this section follows, is a debate between two positions and a synthesis, and the article’s duty is to state all three fairly before giving its resting point.

The Case Against Identity

The separation case, associated above all with H. D. Saffrey (2005) and endorsed in the Encyclopedia’s survey, rests on four mismatches. Religion: the philosopher is a pagan, the Alexandrian chair’s last uncommitted holder; the alchemical text reads as Christian — most pointedly in its citation of Paul’s “the letter kills, but the spirit gives life” (2 Corinthians 3:6), attributed in the commentary (CAAG II, p. 94) to “the Lord” in a manner the survey judges uncharacteristic of the philosopher’s careful classroom practice. Learning: the philosopher is a professional Platonist-Aristotelian of deep, if unoriginal, erudition; the alchemist, in the survey’s blunt summary, knows a smattering of the Presocratics — whom he regards as alchemists — and next to nothing of Plato and Aristotle, while quoting the alchemical authorities at a length the philosopher’s works never approach. Style: contents, diction, and rhetorical ambience differ strikingly between the two corpora (the survey cites Opsomer 2011 and Griffin 2017 in support). Genre: the alchemical work will not stay a commentary — it drifts into didactic letter and florilegium — in a way the philosopher’s disciplined praxis-structure, theōria then lexis, never does. On this case, the manuscripts’ attribution is the corpus’s usual borrowed lustre (compare Synesius, Section VI): a commentary by an unknown Christian alchemist, dignified with the great contemporary professor’s name and city.

The Case For Identity — and the Synthesis

The identity case is Cristina Viano’s, argued from inside both texts: Viano edited Olympiodorus’s commentary on Book IV of Aristotle’s Meteorology — the “chemical” book, on the generation and corruption of sublunary matter — and her La matière des choses (2006) rests on striking similarities of structure and language between that commentary and the alchemical treatise. A professor who had systematised Aristotle’s chemistry of mixture and concoction, the argument runs, is exactly the professor who might lecture on Zōsimos; the doxography’s school pattern (Section VIII) is his professional signature. Viano’s mature position, as the Encyclopedia reports it (2006; 2021), is however a synthesis, and it is the synthesis this article adopts: Olympiodorus indeed wrote a commentary on Zōsimos, but the commentary as transmitted was later altered and expanded by a compiler. The Christian colouring and anthological drift — the separation case’s best evidence — belong to the expansion; the school structure and the Meteorology-kin language to the core. The work is neither simply the philosopher’s nor simply a forgery: in the survey’s phrase, a mixture of genuine attribution, intellectual appropriation, and literary expansion.

A methodological moral closes the section, because the debate’s shape recurs across the whole commentary tradition. In a literature transmitted by excerpt, expansion, and borrowed names, the question “did X write this?” often has no yes-or-no answer the evidence can fund; the fundable question is “which strata of this text belong to which hands, and what did each hand do?” Olympiodorus’s commentary, read stratigraphically, stops being a problem and becomes a specimen: the philosopher’s school method at its core, the tradition’s anthological appetite at its surface, the two fused by copyists who saw no seam. The commentators, who taught the schools to read Democritus stratigraphically — design beneath obscurity, doctrine beneath recipe — are now read the same way themselves. There is a justice in it they would have recognised, and probably taught.

X. Christianus: Alchemy Baptised in the Lecture Hall

The third principal commentator has no biography at all — not even, in the end, a name. The manuscripts style him ho Christianos, the Christian, with the article: an epithet, not a baptismal name, as Merianos’s study (Arys 20, 2022) establishes from the Marcianus’s table of contents, where his is the only author’s name so preceded at first mention. This article uses Christianus, the hub’s established form, for what was most likely an anonymous philosopher designated as the Christian of the corpus — the author whose work presented a truly Christian view of the art. His surviving work stands at CAAG II, pp. 395–421: On the Consistency of Gold and the thirty chapters On Making Gold, dispersed across the Collection’s pages in an order Letrouit’s reconstruction attempts to restore. In Viano’s formulation it is what its companions are: a compilation with commentary, built on pseudo-Hermes, Zōsimos, and pseudo-Democritus, organised by themes and questions. Its ruling theme is the commentators’ theme in its purest form: the divine water, and the scandal that the ancients name it many ways while meaning, he argues, one thing.

That argument deserves its moment, because it is the reconciliation office of Section II performed at full stretch. Christianus takes up a set-piece objection — his chapter headings include, in the survey’s rendering, an Objection That Divine Water Is One According to Species — and answers it with the school’s dialectic: where pseudo-Democritus speaks in general of a unique kind and Zōsimos of multiple material kinds, Christianus undertakes to show that all multiplicity leads back to the one unity; the ancients’ apparent discord about the divine water’s names and kinds is a discord of expression over an accord of doctrine. His instruments are Plato’s own: the method of division and reunion by genera and species, applied to the operations for clarity, and — in a figure the survey notes — the comparison of the art’s treatments (oikonomiai) to plane geometrical figures, recalling the Timaeus and Stephanus’s practice. Exegesis here is not commentary on the way to something else. The reconciliation is the contribution: a canon that agrees with itself can be taught, and a canon that cannot, cannot. Christianus is the commentator as harmoniser, and his thirty chapters are the sound of the early corpus being tuned to a single pitch.

The Gift of God: Donum Dei, Virtue, and Method

What distinguishes Christianus from every other commentator — and earns him his epithet — is the theological frame he builds around that harmonising labour, analysed in full in Merianos’s study, which this section follows. For Christianus, alchemical knowledge is a gift of God (donum Dei): the mind knows, in his exposition, by participation, not by nature — man is not God by nature but the image of God — and the art’s knowledge descends as every good gift descends, from the Father of lights, to be sought by asking, seeking, and knocking, in the Gospel’s terms his text weaves through Paul and James. Two prerequisites, intertwined, govern the reception of the gift. The first is virtue: Christianus describes the qualities the philosopher-alchemist must possess — purity of conscience, conduct, and intention, prayer in a clean conscience — and makes moral fitness a demarcation between true and false pursuers of the knowledge; the oath-bound, ethically examined adept replaces the workshop’s merely initiated one. The second is mathematical method: formed by the Neoplatonic mathematisation of philosophy, Christianus develops a precise method for defining and classifying alchemical productions on a mathematical basis, presenting the art as sharing the traits of the quadrivial sciences — arithmetic, geometry, music, astronomy — and the mathematical road itself as a path illuminated by God, by which the worthy philosopher-alchemist partakes in divine knowledge. His one use, noted by Merianos, of the term theia epistēmē — divine knowledge, a term with a Platonic and then a Dionysian and Maximian pedigree — for the art states the claim in two words. This is not a Christian gloss pasted on pagan material, Merianos argues, and the article adopts the argument: it is a refoundation. The art is baptised in the lecture hall — its epistemology rewritten as gift and participation, its practitioner rewritten as a virtuous, oath-bound, mathematically disciplined philosopher — while its texts, its operations, and its divine water remain, lemma by lemma, the ancients’ own.

Christianus also preserves some of the corpus’s most concrete workshop matter — and with it, the sharpest modern debate about his date. His procedures include an extended treatment of Indian lac — the secretion of the scale insect Kerria lacca, source of lac dye and shellac — which Marco Bellini’s study (Ambix 72, 2025) analyses as one of the earliest Mediterranean descriptions of lac-dye and shellac manufacture. The dating debate turns on such details and on his addressee, a Sergius: Berthelot identified him with Sergius of Reshaina (d. 536); Saffrey proposed instead Sergius I, patriarch of Constantinople under Heraclius (610–638). Letrouit’s argument from the lac — that the dye was unattested in Egypt before the Arab conquest, placing Christianus in the seventh to eighth century — has since been weakened from two sides: pseudo-Democritus already names the ingredient in the first century CE, and archaeometric work has detected lac colorants on a third-century BCE vessel from Canosa and on Roman-Byzantine textiles. Bellini argues, on the evidence reassessed, for the early seventh century, in Heraclius’s reign. Around the argument stands the reference tradition’s wider bracket — sixth century to eighth in the authorities Merianos collects (Letrouit and Mertens to the seventh–eighth; Viano between the sixth and eighth). This article gives both: broadly sixth to eighth century; early seventh argued on recent evidence; not settled. A commentator whose name is an epithet and whose century is a bracket is, for this tradition, not an embarrassment but a type: the lecture hall preserved his course and let the man go.

XI. Stephanus of Alexandria: Nine Lectures On the Great and Sacred Art

The commentary tradition ends — in the sense that after him it compiles, versifies, and annotates, but does not again systematise at full scale — with the most institutionally splendid figure in Greek alchemy. Stephanus of Alexandria (fl. c. 580 – c. 640) studied at Alexandria in the circle of Olympiodorus’s Christian successors — Elias is named as his probable teacher in the tradition the hub’s transmission article follows — and was teaching and writing commentaries there in the 580s: on Plato and Aristotle (a commentary on the De interpretatione survives under his name), on astronomy and astrology, on medicine, and on the quadrivial subjects. His alchemical work survives as nine lectures (praxeis) On the Great and Sacred Art of making gold, with a letter to Theodore interpolated between the second and third. The lectures’ transmitted titles, in F. Sherwood Taylor’s translation of Ideler’s edition, display the course’s architecture at a glance: the author styled “Stephanus of Alexandria, universal philosopher and teacher of the great and sacred art”; Lecture I On the Making of Gold; Lecture III on the entire world; Lecture IV on that which is in actuality; Lecture VIII on the division of the sacred art; and Lecture IX as a Teaching addressed to the Emperor Herakleios — each lecture headed, in the manuscripts’ formula, with the help of God. A course that begins with gold, rises through the cosmos and being itself, orders the art by division, and ends before an emperor is not a recipe collection with pretensions. It is the Alexandrian curriculum — prolegomena, physics, division, theology — rebuilt with the furnace at its centre.

The lectures’ manner matches their architecture. Viano’s characterisation, followed here, calls the style highly rhetorical: Stephanus expounds the ancient alchemists in periodic, declamatory prose, opening with prayer — Maria Papathanassiou’s study of Stephanus (in Magdalino and Mavroudi, eds., The Occult Sciences in Byzantium, 2006) notes that the treatise On the Great and Sacred Art of Making Gold begins and ends with prayers strongly influenced by the early Christian fathers — and moving through the authorities in sequence, quoting Synesius and Olympiodorus verbatim, commenting on pseudo-Democritus, and harmonising as he goes. Taylor, who translated the first three lectures against the Marcianus, defended the manner against the authorship-sceptics of his day with a teacher’s argument this article preserves: a declamatory and rhetorical style may simply have been thought appropriate to lectures on an arcane subject, and such a style is found throughout the Greek alchemical corpus — rhetoric is not, by itself, evidence of a late date. What the rhetoric is evidence of is the setting: these are performances for a hall — for students, for a court, for an emperor named in the final lecture’s title — by a professor whose other lectures filled the same hall on Aristotle in the morning. The art is expounded, for the first time in its history, as one subject among the subjects of a general philosophical professorship, and expounded as philosophy: its theory linked explicitly with medicine, astrology, mathematics, and music, the system of knowledge Stephanus’s chair existed to teach.

Stephanus’s place in the chain is fixed by his quotations, whatever is doubted about his biography. He quotes Olympiodorus and Synesius verbatim and comments on pseudo-Democritus, so that the whole succession of Sections IV–VII stands inside his text in order. The Anonymous Philosopher, a generation or two later, already cites Stephanus with Olympiodorus as the famous ecumenical commentators (Section IV): by the time the corpus was gathered, Stephanus was not a contemporary but a classic. And his text’s own transmission carries the tradition’s signature irony, stated at length in the transmission article: Stephanus was not printed in Berthelot and Ruelle’s Collection. Ideler’s edition (Berlin, 1841–42) was made from a faulty copy of the Marcianus, breaks off where the Marcianus’s binding had lost leaves, and appends the end of an entirely different text as if it were the end of Lecture IX. Taylor’s re-edition of 1937–38 covers only the first three of the nine lectures; Papathanassiou’s new edition appeared only in 2017. The corpus’s last great Greek systematiser is still, in large part, read through a compromised nineteenth-century text.

// THE NINE PRAXEIS, AND A CAUTION //

Papathanassiou’s structural study of the alchemical work (1996) argues that the transmitted division into nine lectures and a letter redistributes an original course of seven lessons: Lectures I–II as the first lesson, the Letter and Lecture III as the second’s matter, and so on to Lecture IX as the seventh lesson. The ninefold division is the manuscripts’ and Ideler’s, and this article cites it as such; the seven-lesson reconstruction is the modern editor’s argument, reported here as an argument.

What Stephanus teaches, beneath the rhetoric, is the commentators’ completed doctrine. For Zōsimos the art was divine and sacred by dreams and demons resisted; for Synesius, by the founder’s designed obscurity; for Olympiodorus, by enrolment among the physicists; for Christianus, by gift and participation. For Stephanus it is divine and sacred institutionally: taught by the universal philosopher, ordered among the disciplines, opened and closed in prayer, and laid, in its final lecture, before the emperor. The lecture hall has absorbed the temple, the workshop, and the throne room into a single audience. That is why the tradition made him its bridge-figure: the Morienus story of his pupil carrying the art east is filed in the transmission article as the tradition’s own account, precious and unverified — but the documented bridge is the system itself, a Greek corpus ordered by his generation into a teachable whole that the Syriac and Arabic worlds could read as a workshop notebook could never have been read. Systems travel. Stephanus built the one that did.

XII. Stephanus and Heraclius: The Chair, the Capital, and the Doubts

The traditional biography, in the form the hub’s transmission article gives it, is splendidly definite. Stephanus, Alexandrian professor, is summoned or migrates to Constantinople shortly after the accession of the emperor Heraclius in 610; he takes up a chair as oikoumenikos didaskalos — ecumenical, or universal, teacher — at the imperial school of the capital; he teaches Plato, Aristotle, the quadrivium, astrology, and alchemy there; his ninth alchemical lecture is addressed to Heraclius himself; astronomical matter in his work has been used to date the Constantinopolitan activity to about 617; among his students is Tychicus of Trebizond, teacher in turn of the Armenian polymath Anania Shirakatsi, so that the Alexandrian curriculum radiates from the capital across the Christian East; and he dies before Heraclius’s own death in 641. Viano’s survey adds the corpus’s own honorific: Stephanus is named with Olympiodorus among “the masters famous everywhere and worldwide, new exegetes of Plato and Aristotle,” in her rendering of the tradition’s phrase, and Heraclius is said to have appointed him worldwide professor of the imperial school. It is a complete, satisfying, imperial story. This section’s duty is to state, as the brief for this article requires and the scholarship demands, how much of it is doubted, and on what grounds — without letting the doubts dissolve a figure the textual evidence, at least, leaves entirely solid.

The Doubts, in Order of Depth

First, the dedication. The Heraclius connection rests principally on Lecture IX’s transmitted title — a Teaching addressed to the Emperor Herakleios — and on the alchemical and astrological manuscripts’ styling of their author. Titles in this corpus are scribal as often as authorial (Section IX’s Olympiodorus is the standing warning), and a dedication can be a copyist’s upgrade as readily as an author’s address. The lecture’s dating consequence — 610–641, the years of the reign — is therefore exactly as strong as the title, no stronger. Second, the chair. Mossman Roueché’s study “Stephanus the Philosopher and Ps. Elias: A Case of Mistaken Identity” (Byzantine and Modern Greek Studies 36, 2012) argues that Hermann Usener’s influential hypothesis — the philosopher summoned by Heraclius to a newly founded imperial institution — rests on an anachronistic use of the titles oikoumenikos didaskalos and katholikos didaskalos as they appear in some astrological and alchemical manuscripts, and concludes that there is no evidence for an imperial institution of higher education under Heraclius of the kind Usener imagined. The same study dismantles Wanda Wolska-Conus’s identification of Stephanus with the anonymous author (Ps.-Elias) of the Prolegomena Philosophiae and the Porphyry commentary: the two men’s treatments of diairesis differ so completely that the identity, Roueché argues, must be abandoned. Third, the authorship of the lectures themselves. Krumbacher, Usener, and Kind held the alchemical works pseudepigraphal, Usener on the ground that a public professor in the palace would not have dared teach an art Diocletian had proscribed — an argument Taylor already answered: the proscription’s own evidence is slender and late, and an edict three centuries old would have carried little weight with Heraclius. Kind placed the work in the ninth century without, in Taylor’s judgement, new evidence. Fourth, the style — the rhetorical manner of Section XI — has been urged both ways for a century, which is usually a sign it can settle nothing.

Against the doubts stand the anchors. The identification of the alchemical Stephanus with the Alexandrian professor is, in Mertens’s formulation as the transmission article reports it, not absolutely certain but quite probable; the current scholarly trend, in Viano’s survey, is to accept it. The lectures’ verbatim quotation of Synesius and Olympiodorus fixes them after the sixth century. The planetary configuration described in a passage of the lectures, as Papathanassiou’s analysis shows, could have been observed at Constantinople in the Heraclian period; her biography, building on Wolska-Conus, has Stephanus teaching at Alexandria before moving by 617 to Constantinople; and Taylor accepted the attribution outright. The article’s resting position is therefore double. The lectures are Stephanus’s, and Stephanus is the Alexandrian professor, with high probability; the Constantinople chair, the imperial summons, and the Heraclius dedication in their full traditional splendour rest on titles and a lecture heading whose authority modern scholarship — Roueché above all — has shown to be weaker than Usener’s generation assumed. An imperial university under Heraclius may be a mirage. A professor lecturing on the sacred art in the early seventh century, in a course ending with an address to the emperor, is a text — and the text survives.

XIII. The Minor and Anonymous Voices: Poets, Compilers, and Names Without Faces

Around the four principals stands a penumbra of lesser voices, named here briefly and honestly: for most of them a name, a title, and a date-bracket are nearly all that survives. First is the Anonymous Philosopher — the Anepigraphos — whose writings Berthelot gathered into three works at CAAG II, pp. 421–441: On Divine Water, On the Gold-Making Procedure, and On Music and Alchemy, sometimes divided (Letrouit) between a first and a second Anonymous; his date is given as c. 600–800. He preserves one of the oldest lists of the alchemists — Hermes, pseudo-Democritus, Synesius, Zōsimos, John the Archpriest, Christianus summarised, and then, in the phrase the Encyclopedia of Ancient Natural Scientists quotes from CAAG II, p. 425, “the famous ecumenical philosophers, the commentators on Plato and Aristotle, who employed dialectic, Olympiodorus and Stephanus” — the canon of Section IV, written out as a canon. He also works the tradition’s problems with its tools: mixture by liquids and without fire, and an analogy between musical instruments and the parts of the alchemical science. The Anonymous is the tradition become self-aware: a compiler who knows he is compiling a tradition, and says who is in it.

Beside him stand the four alchemical poems On the Divine Art, transmitted under the names of Heliodorus, Theophrastus, Hierotheus, and Archelaus, in iambic verse, dated by Viano’s survey to the seventh century, close to Stephanus (some datings run into the eighth). They were not printed in Berthelot’s Collection; their edition is Goldschmidt’s Heliodori carmina quattuor (1923), and Browne published an English metrical translation of Theophrastus’s poem, with commentary, in 1920. Their family resemblance is so close that Reinesius took all four for one author’s versification of Stephanus’s prose — the poems imitate Stephanus’s style and language unmistakably, whoever held the pen. That is their meaning: verse was the schools’ mnemonic and ceremonial register, and an art with iambic poems in its corpus is an art being memorised and celebrated, not merely consulted. They versify the commentators’ commonplaces — Hierotheus’s and Archelaus’s verses carry the obscurity-as-training topos of Section V — and Archelaus’s poem, the longest, is the verse summa of the lecture hall: Stephanus set to metre by an admiring hand.

The remaining names are thinner still, and the article gives them at their true thickness. Theophilus survives in the corpus’s penumbra as an attributed name among the minor Byzantine alchemical writers — a title and an ascription rather than a readable author; no work of substance under his name has the modern study the principals have received, and none is pretended here. Sergius belongs to the lecture hall not as an author but as its most consequential addressee: the Sergius of Christianus’s dedication (Section X), whose two candidate identities — Sergius of Reshaina (d. 536) and Sergius I, patriarch of Constantinople (610–638) — between them span the commentators’ whole world. That a single ambiguous dedication can swing a commentator across a century is the minor voices’ lesson in its sharpest form: in this tradition, even the audience is partly conjectural. After the corpus’s gathering, the exegete line continues by other means — Michael Psellos in the eleventh century, the monk Kosmas, Nikephoros Blemmydes in the thirteenth: readers and compilers rather than new system-builders. The lecture hall empties, across the Byzantine centuries, into the scriptorium.

XIV. What Commentary Did to the Art: Canon, Glossary, Philosophy — Gains and Losses

The assessment requires both sides stated: what commentary did to the art was a transformation with genuine gains and genuine losses. Begin with the gains. Commentary fixed a canon. Before the commentators, the art’s literature was a drift of pseudepigraphic treatises, recipe books, and letters, authoritative in proportion to the antiquity of their borrowed names. The commentators sorted the drift into a succession — founders, systematiser, exegetes — with Democritus and Zōsimos at its head. A canon can be read in sequence, cited, and argued from; a drift cannot. Every later alchemy that presents itself as a tradition inherits the commentators’ sorting as its table of contents. Commentary created theory. The unity of matter as a stated doctrine, the divine water as a defined research object, transmutation as an articulated problem — these are, on the current scholarship (Viano; Dufault), the commentators’ constructions, Olympiodorus’s above all, and they proved fertile for a millennium. Commentary gave the art an institution. A discipline expounded from chairs, beside medicine and the quadrivium, under an emperor’s name, gets copied by institutions — and institutional copying, not workshop use, is what carried the corpus through the seven centuries between Zōsimos and the Marcianus.

The fourth gain is the one this hub’s structure most depends on: commentary froze a vocabulary into glossaries. The art had always hidden its substances under cover-names — Decknamen — and signs: the Leiden papyrus already writes the sun and moon signs for gold and silver, and closes with a small lexicon excerpted from Dioscorides. The commentators’ lexis — the term-by-term unfolding of Section II — turned that scattered practice into a discipline of definition: Synesius expounding Democritus’s substance lists name by name, Olympiodorus distinguishing the divine water’s kinds, Christianus reducing its names to its unity. Gathered and copied at the head of the codices, such exegesis became the corpus’s standing apparatus: the lists of signs and names — over two hundred symbols with their substances in the Parisinus graecus 2327’s list alone — and the lexica that Byzantine, Syriac, and Arabic scholars alike would go on compiling, in the entangled tradition Matteo Martelli’s study of the Syriac alchemical lexica traces. The hub’s Decknamen Glossary stands at the end of that line: a modern glossary of the cover-names, whose chief ancient witnesses are the commentators themselves. A vocabulary frozen is a vocabulary preserved — the student in Baghdad or Toledo could decode Democritus because a professor in Alexandria had first decoded him aloud. That is the gain. Its price is the first of the losses, and it is real: a frozen vocabulary stops growing with the workshop. Names fixed by sixth-century exegesis were still being glossed, unchanged, when the substances and processes behind them had drifted — the glossary preserving the word and embalming the thing.

The Losses, Stated Plainly

Commentary subordinated the furnace to the text. In the workshop letter, authority is the operation that works; in the commentary, authority is the ancient who is quoted. The reconciliation axiom — the ancients cannot fundamentally disagree — discounted the possibility that a founder was simply wrong: where Democritus and Zōsimos conflict on the divine water, Christianus’s duty is to dissolve the conflict, not to test it. The Greek art’s later centuries show the cost: immense exegetical energy, very little new operation. Commentary allegorised the recipes. The licence of Section II let every dark recipe bear a philosophical sense that tended to displace the procedure — the drift the older scholarship, from the Britannica of 1911 onward, described as the practical parts obscured and the processes mystically reinterpreted. The hub’s alchemical allegory article treats that drift’s long career; its Greek root is here. Commentary excerpted what it preserved. Berthelot’s verdict on Byzantine compilation — it conserves fragments and dismembers texts in one operation — applies to the commentators first: Zōsimos’s On Action survives as Olympiodorus’s syllabus required it, and Petasius’s commentaries survive as titles in other men’s lists. The canon was saved. The library was not.

An art that exists as commentary is an art that has become scholastic — portable between cities, teachable to strangers, and dependent for its survival on institutions that copy books. That dependence is the precondition of everything the corpus became. // AFTER THE TRANSMISSION ARTICLE, §II — THE BARGAIN STATED FROM THE CARRIERS’ SIDE //

The balanced verdict is therefore not a compromise but a sequence. Commentary was the art’s second practice, arising when the first practice’s texts had grown too old, too riddling, and too prestigious to be merely used. It did to alchemy what it had done to Plato and Aristotle: it made the founders immortal and their books into curricula, at the price of making the curriculum, not the furnace, the art’s living centre. A workshop tradition without commentators leaves papyri in tombs, as the Theban recipe papyri were left, to be found by chance in the nineteenth century; a tradition with commentators leaves a corpus in libraries, argued over in three languages for a thousand years. The commentators chose — or rather, being schoolmen, could not have chosen otherwise — the library. This archive records the choice without pretending it was costless.

XV. The Commentators as Editors: How Their Choices Became the Corpus

The last subject is the one where this article and its transmission companion meet, and it can be treated briefly because the companion treats it at length. The Greek alchemical corpus — some forty authors, from the pseudo-Democritean books of the first century to the systematists of the seventh — is not a library that survived but an anthology that was made, most plausibly at Constantinople in the seventh or eighth century, in the world the commentators had created. The makers’ choices — order, attributions, inclusions — are the commentators’ choices at one remove: the succession the exegetes had taught (founders, Zōsimos, exegetes, poems) is the succession the anthology embodies, opening in the Marcianus, characteristically, with Stephanus himself, the last systematiser enthroned at the corpus’s head in a decorated pylē of four colours. The commentators had spent four centuries deciding what the art’s classics were. The compilers believed them, and bound the belief in parchment. Everything the Byzantine, Syriac, Arabic, and eventually Latin traditions knew of Greek alchemy, they knew through that anthology and its descendants — or, in the Arabic case, through Greek exemplars older than the anthology’s surviving copies, the luckier story the transmission article tells in its Sections X–XII.

The modern editions repeat the pattern at a further remove. Berthelot and Ruelle’s Collection (1887–88) printed the commentators from the great codices — Synesius at CAAG II, pp. 56–69; Olympiodorus at pp. 69–106; Christianus at pp. 395–421; the Anonymous at pp. 421–441 — and omitted Stephanus, who waited in Ideler’s faulty text until Taylor and, at last, Papathanassiou. The Budé series Les alchimistes grecs has been replacing the Collection text by text: Halleux’s papyri (1981), Mertens’s Zōsimos (1995), Martelli’s pseudo-Democritus with Synesius (2011; 2013). Each modern editor, like each Byzantine compiler before him, selects, orders, and reconciles; each edition is a commentary on the commentators by other means. The scholarship this article rests on — Viano, Saffrey, Merianos, Bellini, Roueché — is the lecture hall’s latest session, still dividing the text into theōria and lexis, still reconciling the ancients, still arguing about the names. The form has outlived every institution that housed it. It is housing this article.

Their lemmata are our texts: Zōsimos’s On Action in Olympiodorus, pseudo-Democritus in Synesius, the canon in the Anonymous’s list. To edit the founders today is to edit the commentators’ excerpts first.

// THE LECTURE ENDS //

A priest of Serapis takes his seat to hear Democritus explained. A professor enrols Hermes among the physicists and Zōsimos among the founders. An unnamed Christian tunes the ancients to a single pitch and calls the tuning a gift of God. An imperial lecturer opens with prayer, divides the sacred art, and lays the last lecture before an emperor.

None of them invented the art. All of them decided what it meant — and because they taught their decisions in halls, in courses that institutions copied, their decisions are still the order in which the art is read. The workshop made alchemy. The lecture hall made it a tradition.
