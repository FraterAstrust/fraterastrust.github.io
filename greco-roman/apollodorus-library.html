APOLLODORUS AND THE LIBRARY

Athens (attributed) · the Greek world under Rome · 1st–2nd century CE

// ONE HANDBOOK :: A BORROWED NAME :: THE SYSTEM BEHIND THE MYTHS //

No ancient book is cited more often in this hub, and none is trusted more cautiously. The Bibliotheca — the Library — is the one comprehensive handbook of Greek myth to survive from antiquity nearly whole: three books of plain prose that run from the first gods to the death of Odysseus, arranging everything by genealogy. It is also a book whose author we cannot name, whose date we can only bracket, and whose ending we possess only in summary. This article is the source-article the rest of the hub leans on: who wrote it, when, out of what, how it reached us, and how far it can be believed.

I. A Handbook That Swallowed a Tradition

Begin with the scale of the dependence. Across this hub's Mythology Wing, the Library is cited several hundred times — for the order of Heracles' labours, for the Theban genealogies, for the passenger lists of the Argo, for who married whom and who killed whom in houses whose own poems are lost. That dependence is not a local habit. It reflects the state of the evidence itself. Greek myth was told, for a thousand years, in epic, lyric, tragedy, local history, and cult aetiology, almost all of it now fragmentary or gone. What survives entire, besides the great poems, is this one prose summary — a work with no literary pretensions at all, written in a Greek so plain that its translator J. G. Frazer could call it, in words quoted verbatim in Section XIV, generally pure, always clear, simple, and unaffected. The plainness is the point. The Library does not perform the myths. It files them.

The Greek title is simply Bibliothēkē — “library”, a storehouse or book-case. Whether the title is the author's own is not known; the work is undivided in its manuscripts, and even its division into three books is an inheritance from its first printed edition (Rome, 1555, edited by Benedetto Egio, Latinised as Benedictus Aegius). Photius, the ninth-century patriarch of Constantinople, is the first writer known to mention the book, in his own Bibliotheca — a coincidence of titles that has confused cataloguers ever since — and Photius preserves an epigram that once stood at the work's head. The epigram is paraphrased here, not quoted, because this article quotes only wordings verified in the editions named in Section XX: in paraphrase, it bids the reader draw knowledge of the past from this book alone, and not trouble Homer, elegy, the tragic muse, or the Cycle, since everything the world contains is to be found in these pages. As advertising, it overshoots. As a description of what the book became — the single shelf on which the whole tradition was consulted — it is close to the truth.

References to the Library follow the standard numeration: book, chapter, section (thus 2.5.1), with the Epitome cited as E. plus its own chapter and section.

Three cautions govern everything that follows, the hub's standing discipline applied to its most-used source. First, the Library is a literary compilation, not a record of cult: a myth's presence in it proves a textual tradition, not a rite. Second, it is a Greek work of the Roman period, separated from most of its material by centuries; its systematising is late work on early matter. Third, where later readers — above all the alchemical tradition of Wing II — read these myths allegorically, that reading is theirs, not the compiler's: the Library explains nothing and allegorises nothing. It is a card index that outlived the library it indexed. The task of this article is to show whose handwriting is on the cards.

THE WORK IN ONE VIEW

Bibliotheca (“Library”), three books plus an epitome of the lost ending, in plain scholarly prose. Scope: from Uranus and Gaia to the death of Odysseus — the whole mythical history of the Greek world, ordered by descent.

THE AUTHOR IN ONE VIEW

Unknown. The manuscripts name “Apollodorus the Athenian, the grammarian”; scholarship since Carl Robert's dissertation of 1873 treats that identification as false and calls the writer pseudo-Apollodorus (Sections II–IV).

THE DATE IN ONE VIEW

After the middle of the 1st century BCE (it cites Castor of Rhodes) and, on linguistic grounds, most probably in the 1st or 2nd century CE. The range, and the arguments, are set out in Section V.

THE TEXT IN ONE VIEW

One principal manuscript, Parisinus Graecus 2722 (14th century), breaking off mid-Theseus; the lost ending recovered in substance from two Byzantine epitomes published in 1891 (Sections VIII–IX).

II. The Author Problem: Two Men Named Apollodorus

The problem can be stated in a single sentence: antiquity knew a real Apollodorus who really did write systematically about the gods and about mythical chronology, and the Library is not his work. Everything else in this section and the next two is the unpacking of that sentence — who the real man was, why the attribution was made, and why it fails.

The attribution itself is old and respectable. Photius, in the ninth century, calls the author “Apollodorus the Grammarian”; the manuscripts describe him as Apollodorus the Athenian, grammarian. No ancient writer earlier than Photius is known to name the book at all — Frazer notes that the author seems to have been ignored by his contemporaries and by posterity for many generations, surfacing by name in Photius, then in the Byzantine scholars John and Isaac Tzetzes in the twelfth century, and anonymously in the scholia to Homer, Sophocles, and Euripides and in material absorbed into Zenobius' collection of proverbs. A work can be heavily used and rarely credited; the Library was excerpted the way dictionaries are excerpted, and the name that travelled with the excerpts was the famous one.

That the famous name was available to be attached is itself explicable. The minor scholia on Homer — the scholia minora, schoolroom notes of uncertain date — preserve references to a comprehensive mythological work by the genuine Apollodorus of Athens, and Aubrey Diller's study of the Library's text history (1935) traced the false ascription to a conflation arising in that scholia tradition: two repertories of myth, one by the grammarian and one anonymous, drawn together under one name. The suggestion has also been made — by Fritz Graf and others, as a possibility rather than a proof — that the compiler really was named Apollodorus: it was a common Greek name, and coincidence needs no conspiracy. This hub follows the standard convention. The author is cited as pseudo-Apollodorus, the work as the Library or Bibliotheca, and the bare name “Apollodorus” in a citation is always to be read with that silent pseudo- understood.

// A NOTE ON THE NAME //

Nothing in this article's use of “Apollodorus” as shorthand implies the Athenian grammarian's authorship. Where the grammarian himself is meant, he is always “Apollodorus of Athens”. Where the Library is meant, its author is pseudo-Apollodorus, whoever he was. The distinction is not pedantry: the grammarian's real works, described in the next section, took positions the Library contradicts, and arguments built on the false attribution — dating the handbook to the 2nd century BCE, or reading its silences as a Stoic's programme — collapse with it.

It is worth pausing on how complete the compiler's anonymity otherwise is. His persona is almost wholly suppressed: no preface in his own voice apart from the epigram Photius transmits (itself unattributed), no dedication, no reference to a patron, a city, or an event of his own time. A handful of passages speak in the first person or pass judgement on a variant, and those few sentences are the entire surviving trace of a personality. We know his library better than we know him — Sections X–XI argue that this is the right way round: the man is recoverable, if at all, as a set of working habits visible in the text.

III. The Grammarian of Athens: On the Gods and the Chronicle

Apollodorus of Athens (c. 180 – after 120 BCE) was a scholar of the first rank, and the Library's borrowed glory is his. He was a pupil of Aristarchus of Samothrace, the great Alexandrian editor of Homer, and he worked in the orbit of the Pergamene court as well as Athens; his writings, all lost except in fragments and citations, ranged across Homeric scholarship, geography, etymology, and chronology. Two of them matter here, because each is close enough to the Library's territory to explain the confusion — and different enough from it, in method and in detail, to disprove the identity.

On the Gods

The Peri theōn, On the Gods, ran to twenty-four books. From its surviving fragments — collected in Müller's Fragmenta Historicorum Graecorum and now in Jacoby's Fragmente der griechischen Historiker (no. 244) — its method is plain. It was a work of interpretation: it explained the gods' natures and epithets, working freely with etymology, and it resolved the deities, in the manner Frazer summarises from the fragments, either into personified powers of nature or into deified dead men and women — the twin instruments of Hellenistic rational theology, physical allegory and euhemerism. Whatever one thinks of that method, it is a method: the grammarian had a theory of what gods are, and he argued it epithet by epithet. The Library, by contrast, contains no theory of anything. It does not explain a single epithet, resolve a single god into a power or a person, or pause over a single cult title. Frazer's contrast, paraphrased here, is exact: nothing could be further from the spirit of the mythographer — who summarises the traditional mythology without the smallest attempt to explain or criticise it — than the dissections of On the Gods.

The Chronicle

The Chronika, Chronicle, was a versified universal chronology in four books, composed in iambic trimeters — a metre chosen, as later readers noted, because it is easy to memorise — and dedicated to a king Attalus, generally identified as Attalus II of Pergamum. It ran from the fall of Troy (1184/3 BCE by the era it used) down to its author's own century: the original edition reached 144/3 BCE, and the work was later continued to about 119 BCE. Its importance for Greek chronology was enormous; later chronographers built on its framework for centuries. Here, if anywhere, is the seed of the false attribution: the scholia knew Apollodorus as author of a comprehensive, ordered repertory touching mythical history, and a comprehensive, ordered repertory of myth circulated under his name. But the Chronicle was a dated framework, verse, organised by years. The Library is undated prose organised by descent, and never once converts a genealogy into a year-count. The resemblance is of subject, not of book.

There is a further, quieter point. Apollodorus of Athens wrote as a known scholar inside known institutions, and his fragments show a man arguing with named predecessors and correcting them. The Library's compiler argues with nobody: where his sources disagree he notes the disagreement and moves on (Section XI). The two temperaments — the controversialist grammarian and the self-effacing filer — are not impossible in one life, but nothing requires us to unite them, and the hard evidence of the next section forbids it.

IV. Why the Library Is Not His: The Case for Pseudo-Apollodorus

The identification was dismantled in full by Carl Robert, De Apollodori Bibliotheca (Berlin, 1873), and Frazer's Loeb introduction accepts Robert's arguments in the main, as does the scholarship since. The case has four strands, of increasing weight.

1 · METHOD

On the Gods interprets; the Library only reports. A scholar whose life-work was explaining the gods by etymology, nature-allegory, and euhemerism does not plausibly produce, in the same field, a handbook containing no explanation at all (Section III).

2 · DETAIL

Robert catalogued specific discrepancies and contradictions between the grammarian's certainly attested fragments and the Library's statements on the same points of genealogy and myth. The fragments and the handbook do not merely differ in manner; at checkable points they differ in fact.

3 · STYLE

The Library's plain, correct, slightly clogged prose — Frazer remarks on its accumulations of participles — belongs to the scholarly koine of the Roman period, with occasional late usages (Section V). It is not the instrument of Aristarchus' pupil, and its poetic tags are quotations from sources, not the versifying habit of the Chronicle's author.

4 · THE CASTOR CITATION

Decisive by itself. At 2.1.3 the Library cites the chronicler Castor of Rhodes — a contemporary of Cicero whose chronicle was brought down to 61/60 BCE, roughly a century after Apollodorus of Athens. A book cannot quote a writer not yet born.

The Castor passage deserves a moment of care, because the whole dating of the work hangs from it. The citation occurs in the Argive genealogy at the opening of Book 2, where the compiler, giving the line of kings, invokes Castor's authority for the list — the passage is registered as a fragment of Castor (FGrHist 250). Frazer's reasoning, paraphrased from his introduction, is the standard one: there is no good ground to dispute either Castor's date or the genuineness of the reference, so the middle of the 1st century BCE stands as the earliest possible date for the Library's composition. Apollodorus of Athens, dead by about 110 BCE at the latest on any reconstruction of his life, is excluded not by taste but by arithmetic.

Two escape routes from this conclusion have been tried and should be recorded, since this hub labels scholarly dispute where it exists. The first treats the Castor citation as a later interpolation. Against it: the citation sits inside a king-list of exactly the kind Castor compiled, the text shows no seam, and a theory that must delete its only fixed point to save an attribution has the burden of proof backwards. The second accepts the date and detaches the man, proposing that the compiler was a different, otherwise unknown Apollodorus. That proposal is unfalsifiable and harmless: it changes nothing about the book. What scholarship means by pseudo-Apollodorus is precisely this residue: a competent professional of the Roman-period Greek world, working from good sources, whose name, city, and circumstances are irrecoverable.

V. The Date: Between Castor and the Scholia

The external evidence fixes only the window's near edge. Castor gives a terminus post quem of about 60 BCE. The far edge is embarrassingly distant: the first certain evidence of the book's existence is Photius, in the 9th century CE, and the passages of the Library absorbed anonymously into scholia and into Zenobius cannot be dated closely enough to help — Zenobius worked under Hadrian, but whether the Apollodoran material entered his collection in his own time or later is not demonstrable. Frazer states the resulting position with admirable bleakness, here paraphrased: on the external evidence alone, the author might have written at any time between the middle of the 1st century BCE and the beginning of the 9th century CE. Everything narrower than that must come from the language and the contents.

The linguistic argument is the one the field actually rests on. The Library's Greek is, in the main, correct and clear scholarly prose; against that background, a small set of usages points late: the subjunctive with hina where classical usage prefers the infinitive, occasional rare words and words in unusual senses, and a few constructions paralleled in Polybius and later writers but not in classical Attic. Jean-Claude Carrière and Françoise Massonie, in the Budé edition's study of the language (1991), concluded for composition in the 2nd or even 3rd century CE. Ken Dowden, reviewing the question for the standard discussions of Greek mythography, holds that a 1st-century CE date cannot be excluded. Robert himself inclined to the earlier part of the 2nd century CE. The positions differ by a century or two; they agree in placing the book in the Roman imperial period, and the hub's working range — 1st to 2nd century CE, with the 2nd the centre of gravity — is the range the handbooks standardly print.

The silence about Rome deserves a sentence of analysis, because it is the one internal fact that pulls against the consensus. Frazer pressed it hard: the compiler narrates Heracles driving Geryon's cattle the length of Italy, and the settlement of Greek heroes in Italy after Troy, without once naming Rome or Aeneas' landing in Latium — though he had told Aeneas' birth and escape. Frazer, paraphrased, could draw no inference but deliberate abstention, and confessed it puzzling in a subject of the Empire. The modern resolution is less dramatic: the Library is a handbook of Greek myth built from Greek sources, and its silence is its sources' and its genre's, not a political act. Roman foundation legend has its own article in this hub's plan (Roman Founding Legends); the date stands where the language puts it — an imperial Greek of the first two centuries CE, filing a tradition already a millennium old.

VI. Structure: Three Books on a Genealogical Spine

The Library opens with the words — in paraphrase — that Heaven was first ruler of the whole world, and it ends, in its complete form, with the death of Odysseus and the return of the Heraclidae. Between those points it tells, in principle, everything: every god's birth, every hero's parentage, marriage, deeds, and death, every royal house's succession. The instrument that makes “everything” organisable is genealogy. The book is not arranged by theme, by place, or by the chronology of events. It is arranged by descent: a family tree is stated, and the stories hang from its branches in the order the branches generate them. A myth enters the book when its hero is born into the narrative, and it leaves when his line is exhausted or hands over to another stem. To read the Library is to walk a vast pedigree, and most of its peculiarities — its repetitions, its doublets, its sudden compressions of famous stories into two lines while obscure marriages get a paragraph — follow from that single decision. The pedigree is the plot.

The model is Hesiodic. The Theogony had already organised the divine world by generation and birth — see this hub's Theogony article — and the Hesiodic Catalogue of Women had extended the same architecture over the heroic world, arranging the heroes by the women who bore them to gods. The Library is that architecture translated into prose and completed: where the Catalogue survives in fragments, the handbook survives whole, and its skeleton is recognisably the older poem's (Section XV). Within the divine section the compiler even preserves the theogonic order — Uranus, the Titans, the Olympians, the Giants, the distribution of honours — before passing, by the bridge of Prometheus and the flood of Deucalion, into the human stems.

The Stems

After the gods, the heroic world grows from a small number of great stems. From Deucalion, the flood survivor, descend the Aeolian houses and so, by way of Athamas and the golden ram, the Argonauts. From Inachus of Argos descends the line of Io, Danaus, Perseus, and — by the book's longest single arc — Heracles. From Atlas descend the Pleiades' houses, including the Spartan and Trojan royal lines. The Athenian kings, the Theban houses, and the Pelopid house are worked through in Book 3. The Trojan War, when it comes in the Epitome, is not a new subject but the collision of stems already planted: Electra's descendants hold Troy, the descendants of Pelops lead the attack, and the compiler can narrate the war as a family event because he has spent three books building the family.

A word on the book-divisions, since citations depend on them. The manuscripts transmit the work undivided; the division into three books is editorial, descending from Aegius' first edition of 1555, and modern scholarship has noted that it need not reflect any ancient articulation of the text — the break between Books 1 and 2, for instance, falls in the middle of the Argive material's natural run. The lost final portion, recovered through the epitomes (Section IX), is cited as the Epitome (E.), and a minority discussion in the scholarship treats that portion as, in effect, a fourth book's content; the hub follows the standard convention — three books, then Epitome — and so do both English translations discussed in Section XIV. Chapter and section numbers within the books are likewise editorial (Wagner's Teubner text of 1894 fixed the numeration in general use), which is why a citation such as 2.5.1 is a modern address, not an ancient one — an address, none the less, that every modern work on Greek myth shares.

Two features of the map repay emphasis. The first is the sheer weight of Book 2. The Heracles cycle occupies a whole book — the only hero so honoured — because Heracles is the genealogical crossroads of the heroic world: Perseus' descendant, Argos' champion, the Heraclidae's ancestor, and a figure whose journeys let the compiler sweep up dozens of local traditions (monsters, kings, foundations) as episodes of one life. The Library's ordering of the twelve labours is the ordering the tradition, and this hub, treats as canonical; the labour article examines what that means in Heracles' Labours, including the two labours — the Hydra and the Augean stables — whose counting the compiler himself reports as disputed by Eurystheus.

The second feature is what is not there. There is no Roman material (Section V); almost no cult — a tomb, an altar, or a rite is mentioned when a genealogy requires it, never described for its own sake; no natural philosophy, no allegory, no etymological play of the kind the real Apollodorus practised; and remarkably little wonder as wonder — a metamorphosis is reported in the same flat sentence as a marriage. Readers who come for the myths' poetry will find the book airless. That airlessness is a scholarly virtue misread as a defect: the refusal to perform is what lets a reader see the tradition's joints, and it is why the hub cites the Library for structure and reserves its poetry for the poets.

VIII. Survival I: Parisinus Graecus 2722 and the Broken Ending

The Library very nearly did not survive at all. Its medieval transmission narrows, at the critical point, to a single damaged codex: Parisinus Graecus 2722 (siglum R), a 14th-century manuscript now in the Bibliothèque nationale de France, Paris. The codex is a miscellany in disordered condition — Wagner's preface describes its Apollodoran leaves as fragments bound in confusion, some headed only “fragment of Apollodorus, as it seems” by a later hand (paraphrased here from Wagner's Latin). From this manuscript, through a copy made in the 15th century for Cardinal Bessarion, the other surviving manuscripts descend: the whole modern tradition of the text hangs from R, and where R is damaged or wrong, the text is damaged or wrong with it, unless the epitomes or a scholion can correct it.

And R breaks off. Its text of the Library runs to 3.16.2 — into the Athenian section of Book 3, in the middle of the Theseus narrative, during the hero's journey from Troezen to Athens and his clearing of the road of its monsters and brigands. The sentence stops; the leaves that carried the rest are lost. Everything the complete work contained after that point — the remainder of Theseus, the whole Trojan War, the returns, the death of Odysseus, the return of the Heraclidae, which Photius' description shows to have been the work's true extent — was, in the direct tradition, simply gone. For the first three centuries of print (1555 to the 1880s), editors published a Library that ended, without explanation available to them, on the road to Athens.

// WHAT “INCOMPLETE” MEANS HERE //

The loss is a loss of ending, not of bulk: Books 1, 2, and most of 3 survive in the compiler's own continuous prose. But the lost portion is disproportionately load-bearing for this hub, since it contained the handbook's Troy, on which the Trojan Cycle article partly rests. Every citation of the Library for Troy is a citation of the Epitome, and should be read with the next section's cautions attached.

A second caution belongs here. Several scholars have argued that even the books we possess in full may have come down in an abridged state — that the transmitted Library is itself, in part, an epitome of a fuller original. The argument rests on the work's extreme compression at points where its sources were expansive, on abrupt transitions, and on details preserved in scholia that cite the Library but are absent from our text. The position is a scholarly hypothesis, labelled as such: it is not provable from the single-manuscript tradition, and the hub does not assume it. But it should temper any argument from the Library's silence. A silence in an epitome is not a silence in its source.

IX. Survival II: The Two Epitomes and the Recovery of 1891

The ending was recovered — in summary, which is a different thing from recovered — at the end of the 19th century, from two Byzantine collections of excerpts, both published in 1891.

EPITOMA VATICANA

Excerpts preserved in Vaticanus Graecus 950 (14th century), Vatican Library. Published by Richard Wagner in 1891. The collection has been ascribed — on grounds Wagner set out and later scholars have debated — to the circle, or the hand, of the 12th-century scholar John Tzetzes, who used the Library heavily in his own work. It covers the lost portion from the Theseus narrative through the end.

FRAGMENTA SABBAITICA

Excerpts preserved in Sabbaiticus Graecus 366 (13th century), from the monastery of Mar Saba, in the library of the Orthodox Patriarchate of Jerusalem. Published by Athanasios Papadopoulos-Kerameus in 1891. Independent of the Vatican excerpts, drawn likewise from a complete Library, and carrying valuable scholia of its own.

Wagner conflated the two collections — checking each against the other, and both against the Tzetzes scholia on Lycophron, which quote the lost portion extensively — into the composite Epitome printed in his Teubner edition, Mythographi Graeci I (Leipzig, 1894), the edition on which Frazer's Loeb rests. Frazer translated the Epitome as the work's continuation, and it is in that form — Wagner's Greek, Frazer's English — that most readers meet the Library's Troy. The excerptors' accuracy, where it can be tested against the surviving books, has been judged good: the epitomes are faithful in substance, and their mutual agreement, from independent extraction, gives the reconstruction a double witness for much of the lost ground.

But fidelity of substance is not identity of text, and the limits must be stated as plainly as the achievement. An epitome compresses: it keeps names, successions, and outcomes, and it drops connective tissue, variant-notices, and exactly the small qualifying phrases by which a reader judges a compiler's care (Section XI). The Epitome's Greek is the excerptors' Greek, at one or more removes from the compiler's. Where the two epitomes differ, the editor chooses, and the choice is a reconstruction. And the Epitome as printed is a composite: no ancient or Byzantine reader ever held precisely the text Wagner assembled. This hub's practice follows from those facts. Citations of the Epitome are cited as E., never silently as a book number; claims resting on the Epitome alone are one grade weaker than claims resting on Books 1–3; and where the Epitome's compression flattens a variant that the prose systematizers or the scholia preserve, the variant is reported. The ending of the Library is a well-attested summary of a lost text. It is not the lost text.

X. The Compiler's Sources: A Library Behind the Library

Pseudo-Apollodorus invented almost nothing, and that is his value. Behind the handbook stands a shelf of earlier Greek mythography and poetry, most of it lost, which he excerpted with a fidelity that — where checking is possible, Section XIII — proves generally high. Reconstructing that shelf is the work of two centuries of scholarship, from Robert's dissertation to R. L. Fowler's Early Greek Mythography, and its main holdings can be named with reasonable confidence.

Two disciplines of reading follow from this table. The first is that the Library is a secondary source even at its best: behind nearly every sentence stands an earlier telling, and the handbook's sentence is evidence for that telling, filtered through excerpting. When the hub writes “Apollodorus says”, the accurate expansion is usually “the tradition Apollodorus excerpted says” — and where the earlier telling survives (a fragment of the Catalogue, a scholion naming Pherecydes), the earlier witness outranks the handbook. The second discipline is the reverse, and equally important: the compiler's sources were, in large part, good — classical and early Hellenistic works of real learning, not late-antique improvisation. A handbook of the 2nd century CE that transmits Pherecydes is a 2nd-century object carrying 5th-century cargo. Date of compilation is not date of content, and the Library is the hub's standing proof of the distinction.

What the compiler did not use is also instructive. There is no sign that he worked from cult records, local guides, or autopsy of any kind; Pausanias, his rough contemporary, walked Greece interviewing priests, and the Library shows no trace of that world. Nor does he use the paradoxographers, the allegorists, or the schools. His library was a library. The tradition he transmits is the bookish tradition of Greek myth — the tradition the poets and tragedians also used, and precisely not the whole of what Greeks believed and practised. The hub's rule — literary myth distinguished from cult practice — is, for this source, a description of the compiler's desk.

XI. The Compiler's Method: Variants, Silence, and Light Rationalising

A compiler who never speaks in his own voice can still be known by his habits, and pseudo-Apollodorus' habits are consistent enough across three books to amount to a method. Five of them matter for any reader who cites him.

1 · The Variant-Note: “Some Say”

The handbook's signature move is the registered alternative: a main version is narrated, and a variant is attached with a formula — “some say”, “others say”, or a named authority — without adjudication. Parentages are the commonest site (a hero's father is Zeus in the main line, a mortal king “according to some”), but deaths, marriages, and the counting of the labours all attract the note. The habit is easy to mock as indecision and is in fact the handbook's chief scholarly virtue: it preserves, inside a systematising work, the plurality that systematisation usually destroys. Where the hub reports “Apollodorus gives X, but notes that some gave Y”, the Y is often the older or the local version, and the variant-note is the only reason we have it. The compiler files his disagreements instead of resolving them, and Greek myth is richer for his refusal to tidy.

2 · Genealogical Arbitration

Where variants cannot both stand — a hero cannot descend from two incompatible stems — the compiler must choose, and his choices are governed by the architecture: the version that keeps the great stems coherent is preferred. The arbitration is silent, and it is the point at which the handbook stops transmitting and starts constructing. Contradictory doublets survive — the same figure entered under two stems with two parentages — precisely where the arbitration failed or was never attempted, and those doublets are the reader's best evidence that the compiler excerpted more faithfully than he harmonised.

3 · Compression Without Commentary

Famous stories are cut to their genealogical skeleton. The Library's Oedipus occupies a fraction of the space Sophocles gives one afternoon of the hero's life; its Medea is a sequence of marriages, murders, and removals. Motive, speech, and scene are stripped away, because the handbook's unit is not the episode but the fact: birth, union, offspring, deed, death. The compression is so regular that its exceptions are informative — where the compiler suddenly expands (the labours, the Argonaut catalogue), the expansion marks a source followed closely or a subject the architecture required in full.

4 · Light Rationalising Touches

The compiler is no Palaephatus (Section XVI): he does not explain marvels away, and monsters stand in his pages unapologised. But a light rationalising pressure is visible at the edges. Divine parentage is sometimes doubled with a mortal alternative — a hero is the son of a god “in story”, of a king in fact, a formula that lets both registers be filed. Etymological and aetiological notes occasionally anchor a marvel to a place or a custom. And the narrative quietly prefers versions in which events have human-scale causes where his sources offered them. These touches are inherited as often as they are his own — Pherecydes and the tragedians had already rationalised much — and the hub accordingly treats them as the tradition's pressure, not the compiler's philosophy. He had no philosophy. That was Section III's point about the other Apollodorus, and it holds.

5 · The Suppressed Persona

Finally, the method includes its own erasure. The compiler almost never says “I”, never names his sources except Castor, never dates, never moralises, never wonders. The effect is of a tradition speaking for itself — an illusion the reader must resist. Every line was chosen. The Library's authority in later centuries, and its danger as a source, both flow from that illusion of authorlessness: a handbook with no visible author is easily mistaken for the myth itself. Sections XII–XIII exist to correct the mistake with evidence.

XII. Reliability I: Where the Library Is the Only Witness

The hardest cases for a source-critic are not the false ones but the unverifiable ones, and the Library is full of them: stretches where it is not the best witness to a canonical form but the only one, so that the form the world knows — the form in every modern handbook, including the modern handbooks this hub competes with — is Apollodorus' form because no other survives. The reader should know the main districts of that territory.

THE LABOURS, AS A NUMBERED SET

The twelve labours of Heracles existed individually across archaic and classical art and poetry, and earlier lists existed (Peisander of Rhodes is credited with a Heracles epic). But the canonical sequence — the order, the count of twelve, the disqualification of two — is transmitted whole only here (2.5.1–12). The hub's labours article follows Apollodorus' order for that reason, and says so.

THE THEBAN SAGAS, CONNECTED

The plays survive; the connective genealogy — Cadmus to the Spartoi to Oedipus to the Seven to the Epigoni, with the marriages and minor figures that join them — is the handbook's construction from Pherecydes and the tragedians, and for several links it is our only continuous account. See The Theban Cycle.

THESEUS' ROAD AND EARLY DEEDS

The ordered itinerary of the young Theseus — Periphetes, Sinis, the Crommyonian sow, Sciron, Cercyon, Procrustes — as a single progress from Troezen to Athens is a systematiser's arrangement; individual brigands are older, the parade is the handbook's (3.16, continued in E. 1). The break in R falls inside this very passage: our fullest Theseus is half epitome.

MINOR FIGURES, WHOLESALE

Hundreds of minor figures — brides, sons, victims, founders — are attested only in the Library, where they exist to complete a stem. Some are genuine tradition rescued from lost sources; some may be the system's own mortar, names generated by the need of a genealogy for a link. There is often no way to tell, and the hub marks single-attestation figures as such where it matters.

The right attitude to sole-witness material is neither of the easy ones. Credulity — treating the handbook's form as the myth — forgets that the compiler chose, ordered, and sometimes harmonised. Dismissal — treating unverifiable as worthless — forgets Section X: the cargo is early even when the ship is late, and where the sole witness can be spot-checked at its edges (a name in a fragment, a scene on a vase, a scholion's parallel), it checks out often enough to earn provisional trust. The hub's rule is therefore: report such material as the Library's version, in the Library's name, and let the attribution carry the caution. “Apollodorus says” is not a weakness in a sentence. It is the sentence's evidence-tag.

One sole-witness district deserves separate warning: the neatness of divine machinery. The handbook's accounts of the Gigantomachy, of Typhon, of the division of the cosmos among Zeus, Poseidon, and Hades, and of Prometheus' career are the fullest connected versions we have, and they read as settled doctrine. They are, in fact, settlements — archaic poems and local traditions disagreed on the details, and the fragments prove it where they survive. The hub's Olympians and Makers and Fire articles therefore treat the handbook's divine narratives as one systematisation among others, checked against Hesiod wherever Hesiod speaks. A myth that exists in only one orderly version should raise the suspicion that the order is the version's, not the myth's.

XIII. Reliability II: Where the Library Can Be Checked

Where independent witnesses survive, the handbook can be marked like an examination, and two centuries of scholarship have marked it. The overall verdict is favourable — the Library has been called, in a judgement standardly quoted from the older literature, the most valuable mythographical work to survive from antiquity — but the marks are uneven in instructive ways, and the pattern of its agreements and errors is the best guide to trusting it where it cannot be checked.

Against Homer

The compiler follows Homer where Homer speaks, and diverges — instructively — where Homer is silent or where the later tradition had moved on. His Troy, in the Epitome, is the Cycle's Troy, not the Iliad's: the handbook needs the whole war, and Homer deliberately gives a few weeks of it (see The Homeric Epics, Section II). His genealogies sometimes correct Homer's by later systems, and sometimes preserve Homeric detail the later tradition dropped. The lesson is that “Apollodorus versus Homer” is rarely an error on either side: it is two strata of the tradition, and the hub cites each for its own stratum.

Against Pindar and the Lyric Tradition

Pindar's odes presuppose versions the handbook often supplies in prose — and sometimes contradicts, because Pindar himself revised tradition for his patrons' houses and his own theology. Where handbook and Pindar agree against later sources, the agreement is strong evidence for an early version. Where they differ, the difference is frequently Pindar's known innovation rather than the compiler's error: Pindar announces, in his own voice, when he refuses a traditional detail (his Pelops, in Olympian 1, is the famous case), and the handbook's retention of the refused detail marks it as the conservative witness. Conservative is the operative word for the whole comparison: the compiler's instinct, everywhere it can be tested, is to keep the received version, not to improve it.

Against Tragedy

The tragic check is the most delicate, because the tragedians were innovators by profession and the handbook drank deeply from the tragic tradition, largely through Asclepiades' Tragodoumena (Section X). Where a surviving play and the handbook agree — the outline of the Orestes story, the house of Oedipus' later generations — the agreement may mean only that the compiler used the play's tradition, not that two independent witnesses concur. Where they disagree, the disagreement is gold: it can expose a pre-tragic version taken from the genealogists, or a post-tragic conflation in which two plays' incompatible plots have been filed as one story. The hub's Tragedy article treats the plays on their own terms; the handbook is a witness to the tragic tradition at one remove, and must be cross-examined as such.

The Errors, and What They Teach

The compiler errs, and his errors have a taxonomy. There are conflations: figures of the same name merged, or two versions of one event spliced into a single impossible narrative. There are genealogical impossibilities: descents that make a grandfather younger than his grandson when the stems are cross-checked — the price of excerpting sources that counted generations differently. There are misreadings of sources, rare but real. And there are transmission errors that are not his at all: proper names are the most corruptible element in any manuscript tradition, and some “errors of Apollodorus” in older discussions are errors of 14th-century copyists, correctable from the epitomes or the scholia. The taxonomy matters because each kind of error has a different blast radius. A conflation poisons one story; a garbled name, one stem. But no error yet found is systematic — there is no agenda, no theology, no patron's house being flattered — and it is the absence of system in the errors that grounds the favourable verdict. The compiler is a careless filer at times. He is never a propagandist.

XIV. Editions and Translations: Frazer, Smith and Trzaskoma, and How This Hub Cites

The modern text rests on a short shelf. Aegius' editio princeps (Rome, 1555) printed the defective tradition and fixed the three-book division. Hieronymus Commelinus improved the text at Heidelberg in 1599; Christian Gottlob Heyne's Göttingen edition (1782–83) was the first built on a real comparison of manuscripts. The foundation of everything modern is Richard Wagner's Teubner text, Mythographi Graeci I (1894): the first edition to exploit Parisinus 2722 properly, the first to print the conflated Epitome from the two 1891 publications, and the source of the chapter-and-section numeration in universal use. A newer critical edition by Manolis Papathomopoulos (Athens, 2010) revisits Wagner's text with corrections and is now the reference Greek text for specialists; Wagner's numeration, however, remains the citation standard, and nothing in this hub depends on the differences.

Frazer's Loeb (1921)

Apollodorus, The Library, translated by Sir James George Frazer, Loeb Classical Library, two volumes (London: Heinemann; New York: Putnam, 1921) — the standard English text for a century, and the edition this hub cites first. Its virtues are three. The translation is close and unadorned, matching the original's plainness; the Greek faces it, so any sentence can be checked in seconds; and the notes are a work of scholarship in themselves — Frazer filled them with parallels from the whole of Greek literature and from comparative folklore, so that the Loeb Library functions as a concordance to Greek myth, not merely a translation. Its introduction remains the most readable statement of the authorship and dating case (Sections II–V lean on it, and say where they do). Two cautions: Frazer's comparative notes reflect the anthropology of 1921 and are used here for parallels, not for theory; and his text is Wagner's, so the Epitome in his second volume is the composite of Section IX, in English dress. Frazer on the compiler's style can stand as this section's one verbatim quotation, verified in the Loeb introduction: the style of Apollodorus is, he writes, “generally pure and always clear, simple, and unaffected, except in the very rare instances where he spangles his plain prose with a tag from one of his poetical sources”.

Smith and Trzaskoma (Hackett, 2007)

Apollodorus' Library and Hyginus' Fabulae: Two Handbooks of Greek Mythology, translated with introductions by R. Scott Smith and Stephen M. Trzaskoma (Indianapolis: Hackett, 2007) is the modern alternative, and this hub's second citation. It offers the first complete English translations of both surviving handbooks in one volume — a pairing whose value Section XVIII explains — in a contemporary English more readable than Frazer's, with introductions that state the current scholarly positions on authorship and date (including Smith's dating of the Fabulae to the later 2nd century CE) and with full indices of persons, places, and cited authors. It has no facing Greek and nothing like Frazer's mass of parallels; its notes are guidance, not a concordance. The hub's rule of thumb: Frazer for checking, Smith and Trzaskoma for reading — and for Hyginus, where Frazer has no equivalent, Smith and Trzaskoma throughout.

Other English versions exist and are used in the hub only where named: Keith Aldrich's translation (1975), serviceable and literal; Robin Hard's Oxford World's Classics version, The Library of Greek Mythology (1997), readable, with useful notes. The hub cites the Library in the form Apollodorus, Library 2.5.1 (Frazer) — book, chapter, section, translation named — and the Epitome as E. 3.21 and so on. A citation without a named translation, anywhere in this hub, is a defect to be reported, not a convention to be copied. For the systematising tradition as a whole, the hub's companion article is The Prose Systematizers, and for the poetic systematiser who answers Apollodorus from Rome, Ovid's Metamorphoses.

XV. The Tradition Before It: The Catalogue's Prose Afterlife

The Library did not begin the systematising of Greek myth. It ended a tradition of it — a tradition eight centuries long, which this section and the three that follow survey, because a reader who knows the tradition can see the handbook's choices as choices. The hub's Heroes and Epic article treats the poetic side; what follows is the prose line.

The fountainhead is the Hesiodic Catalogue of Women (the Ehoiai, from its refrain ē' hoiē, “or such as she”), a pseudo-Hesiodic epic in five books, composed probably in the 6th century BCE, which organised the heroic world genealogically through its heroines. The Catalogue survives in fragments, but its architecture is recoverable, and it is the Library's architecture: the great stems, the descent from Deucalion, the marriage-links between houses. When the epic tradition died as a living form, the Catalogue's system passed into prose. The early prose mythographers — Acusilaus, Pherecydes, Hellanicus (Section X) — rewrote genealogy as history-like prose in the 5th century BCE. Asclepiades of Tragilus added the tragic corpus in the 4th century; Hellenistic scholarship added chronology and commentary. By the time pseudo-Apollodorus sat down, every element of his book existed as a separate scholarly genre. His achievement was consolidation: one desk, one system, one book. The Works and Days and Homeric Hymns articles treat the other archaic deposits the system drew on; the line of descent runs Catalogue → genealogists → Tragodoumena → Library, with the handbook as the tradition's surviving summa.

Calling it a summa should not suggest a cathedral. The prose systematising tradition was always a working literature — handbooks for poets, commentators, teachers, and the reader who needed to know whose son Telemachus was — and it stayed working literature: epitomised, excerpted, rebound with other handbooks, mined by scholiasts. Its survival pattern is the proof. Of the whole tradition, what survives entire is the Library and the Fabulae; what survives in bulk fragments is the Catalogue and the genealogists; the rest is titles and testimonia.

XVI. Palaephatus and the Rationalising Method

One branch of the tradition refused the handbook's founding bargain — report everything, judge nothing — and it produced the strangest and most modern-feeling of the mythographic works: On Unbelievable Tales (Peri apistōn), ascribed to Palaephatus. The name may itself be a speaking pseudonym (“ancient utterance”), the author's identity and home are disputed in the ancient notices, and the work as we have it is probably an abridgement of a larger original; its core forty-five chapters are generally placed in the late 4th century BCE, in the intellectual wake of Aristotle. What survives is a preface and a series of short entries, each performing the same operation on a marvel: state it, reject it as impossible, and declare what really happened.

The preface sets out the method's two premises, here paraphrased from Jacob Stern's translation (1996), the standard English version. First: every story derives from some event — if it had not happened, it would not be told. Second: a principle of uniformity — the kinds of things that exist now are the kinds that existed then, so a creature or transformation unattested in the present world did not occur in the past either. From those premises the entries proceed by a small kit of devices: a metaphor literalised (Actaeon, the spendthrift hunter, was “devoured” by the upkeep of his hounds, and the saying became the story); a name mistaken for a thing (Europa was carried off not by a bull but by a man named Tauros; Cadmus fought not a dragon but a king named Draco); a place or a machine misdescribed (Scylla was a pirate vessel with a dog-device on its prow); a new sight misreported (the Centaurs were the first horsemen, young men of a Thessalian village whose unfamiliar mounted figures fused, in report, into one creature). The one verbatim sentence this article quotes from Palaephatus, in Stern's translation, is his Actaeon chapter's flat axiom: “Artemis can do whatever she wants, yet it is not true that a man became a deer or a deer a man” (§6). The piety is real and so is the refusal: the gods may be able; the event, being uniform with nothing, did not happen.

Palaephatus matters to this hub for two reasons. First, he proves that by the 4th century BCE, educated Greeks already read their myths with exactly the disbelief modern readers bring, and that “rationalising” is not a modern imposition but an ancient genre — one the Library's compiler conspicuously declined to practise beyond the light touches of Section XI. Where Palaephatus rewrites, pseudo-Apollodorus files, and the filing is why we can still recover what Palaephatus would have dissolved. Second, the caution: Palaephatus' explanations are, as evidence, worthless — invented events offered without argument, frequently sillier than the marvels they replace — and his modern students repeat his error whenever a plausible kernel is mistaken for a recovered fact. The hub's rule follows: a rationalisation is evidence for the rationaliser's world, never, by itself, for the event it claims to recover. Palaephatus is a witness to the 4th century BCE. He is not a witness to Actaeon.

XVII. The Collectors: Parthenius, Conon, Antoninus Liberalis

Between Palaephatus and the Library, and alongside it, stand the collectors: mythographers who gathered not the whole system but a single vein of it, chosen for a use. Three survive well enough to characterise, and together they show what the handbook tradition looked like at working scale.

Parthenius: Stories for a Poet

Parthenius of Nicaea, brought to Rome as a prisoner of war in the 1st century BCE and freed for his learning, compiled the Erotica Pathemata — Sufferings for Love — thirty-six prose summaries of unhappy love stories, dedicated to the poet Cornelius Gallus as raw material: the preface, paraphrased here, offers the tales as a storehouse from which Gallus might work them up into epic or elegy. The collection is thus a poet's quarry, frankly instrumental. Its scholarly treasure is its margins: the manuscript preserves source-notes naming the authority for tale after tale — Hellenistic poets and local historians, most of them lost — so that Parthenius functions, for modern scholarship, as a card-index to a vanished literature of love-myth. Several of his stories are attested nowhere else. The hub cites him for the erotic tradition's variants, with his purpose in mind: these are stories selected for pathos, not for typicality.

Conon: Narrations for a King

Conon, a contemporary, composed fifty Diegeseis, Narrations, dedicated to Archelaus Philopator, king of Cappadocia (reigned 36 BCE – 17 CE). The original is lost; the work survives in the summary Photius gives it (codex 186) — the same Photius who preserves our first notice of the Library, a reminder of how much of this field hangs on one Byzantine reader. Conon's tales mix myth, local legend, and aetiology, with a taste for foundations and for the marvellous reported straight: the genre at the moment before pseudo-Apollodorus, miscellaneous and arranged by no system stronger than the collection itself. The hub cites Conon with the double filter — Photius' summary of a lost original — stated.

Antoninus Liberalis: The Metamorphosis File

Antoninus Liberalis, probably of the late 2nd or early 3rd century CE — the Library's rough contemporary — compiled a Collection of Metamorphoses: forty-one brief prose tales, every one ending in a transformation. His sources, named in the manuscript's source-notes, are the Hellenistic poets of transformation above all: Nicander's lost Heteroeumena and the Ornithogonia ascribed to Boeus (or Boios). Many of his tales are found nowhere else, and some may be his own or his sources' inventions — a possibility the hub flags where it cites him alone. The text hangs by a thread even thinner than the Library's: a single manuscript, the late-9th-century Palatinus Graecus 398 at Heidelberg, from which three leaves have been lost since Xylander printed the editio princeps at Basel in 1568 — so that for those passages Xylander's print is itself a primary witness. Francis Celoria's translation with commentary (Routledge, 1992) is the standard English version. Set beside Ovid, Antoninus is the same genre at the opposite pole of art: Ovid makes the file into a poem; Antoninus leaves it a file — and for the lost Hellenistic originals, the file is the more faithful witness.

The collectors teach one lesson in common. Each is a selection, and each selection has a principle — love, narration, transformation — that silently shapes what “Greek myth” means inside its covers. The Library's principle, genealogy, is less visible only because it is more comprehensive. There is no principle-free handbook.

XVIII. Hyginus and the Roman Side: The Fabulae

Rome produced one surviving handbook of its own, and it is the Library's necessary counterpart: the Fabulae, 277 short Latin entries of myth and genealogy, transmitted under the name Hyginus. The name conceals the same trap as Apollodorus'. The famous Hyginus — Gaius Julius Hyginus, Augustus' freedman, librarian of the Palatine library, a scholar of the 1st century BCE — is not, on the modern consensus, the author: the attribution appears in the work's first printed edition (Jacob Micyllus, Basel, 1535), and the text's Latin, its sources, and its handling of them point much later. R. Scott Smith, in the Hackett edition, argues for an author of the later 2nd century CE — a pseudo-Hyginus to match pseudo-Apollodorus, the two handbooks near-contemporaries, the Greek and the Roman sums of the same tradition. A minority of scholars still defends the Augustan authorship; the hub reports the consensus and labels the minority where it matters.

The work's own transmission is as narrow as its Greek counterpart's: a single manuscript of about 900 CE, from Freising, in Beneventan script, now lost after serving Micyllus for the 1535 edition — so that the print is a primary witness. Its contents open with a theogony of a peculiar kind — it begins with Mist, Caligo, before Chaos, a figure Hesiod does not know, and the prefatory genealogy may be a later attachment — and proceed through narratives and lists in an order no one has called elegant: stories, then catalogues of killers and killed, of suicides, of the pious and impious, the whole reading like the notes from which a tidier book might have been written.

The Notorious Errors

Hyginus' reputation in the older scholarship was savage. H. J. Rose, his editor, characterised the author in Latin words this article paraphrases rather than quotes: an ignorant, half-learned youth. The errors that earned the verdict are real and instructive. They are, in the main, translation-and-epitome errors: a Greek source compressed by a compiler whose Greek was imperfect, so that names are confused, relationships inverted, Greek words misread as other Greek words, and two stories' figures exchanged. Whole entries contradict other entries. The genealogies of the prefatory material tangle lines the Library keeps straight. A reader who used Hyginus alone would possess a Greek mythology with the wiring crossed at dozens of points — and for centuries of the Latin Middle Ages and the Renaissance, Hyginus was used nearly alone, which is one reason the mythography of that age is full of confident phantoms.

The Real Value

And yet the Fabulae is indispensable, for the reason Rose himself conceded: it is built, however clumsily, from Greek sources now lost — above all from the tradition of Greek tragedy and its scholarly digests, and from scholia-material on Apollonius of Rhodes. For a number of lost plays, a Hyginan entry is the fullest surviving plot-summary; for a number of myths, his version, checked against the Library and the fragments, preserves the Greek original's outline under the Latin errors. The working rule the hub applies is symmetrical to its rule for the Epitome: Hyginus is a witness of the second rank — never sufficient alone for a name, a relationship, or a sequence; often decisive as corroboration; and precious exactly where he is the only surviving summary of a lost Greek telling. Smith and Trzaskoma's pairing of the two handbooks in one volume (Section XIV) is the modern form of that rule: the Greek summa and the Roman summa, read against each other, correct each other's characteristic failures — the one too smooth, the other too garbled. For Rome's own myth-making, which neither handbook serves, see Roman Religion and the hub's Aeneid article.

XIX. Why Systematisation Matters: How a Handbook Fixes a Myth

It remains to say why any of this matters beyond the scholarship — why a hub of mythology needs an article about a handbook at all. The answer is that systematisation is not a neutral act performed on myth from outside. It is one of the forces that made what we call Greek mythology: a single, cross-referenced, canonically ordered body of story, in which each figure has one parentage and each hero one sequence of deeds. The Greeks of the archaic and classical periods did not possess mythology in that sense. They possessed myths — plural, local, competing, told differently in Argos and in Athens, and revised by every poet who touched them. The unity is the systematizers' work, and the Library is its most complete surviving instrument.

Canon-formation by handbook works through three quiet mechanisms. The first is selection by architecture: a genealogical system can only file what descends, so myths that fit the stems are preserved, while unattached local traditions wither out of the written record. The second is fixing by order: once a handbook numbers the labours, sequences the brigands on Theseus' road, and lists the Argonauts, the order becomes the myth's shape in every later telling. The third is authority by anonymity: a compilation with no visible author (Section XI) is received as the tradition itself, so its choices stop looking like choices. By late antiquity, and overwhelmingly in the centuries when the Library and the Fabulae were the available sums, “Greek myth” meant the systematised version. Modern handbooks inherited the fixation, and this hub inherits it too — the several hundred citations counted in Section I are the proof — which is why the hub's discipline exists: to cite the handbook as a handbook, and to keep the seams showing.

There is a final, Wing II reason, stated in the open. When the later alchemical tradition read the Greek myths allegorically — when Zosimos and the commentators, and after them the Renaissance mythographers and Pernety, found the Work figured in the labours of Heracles or the fleece of the Argonauts — the texts before them were, overwhelmingly, the systematised ones: the handbook versions, with their fixed orders and settled genealogies. The alchemical reading treated in Alchemical Allegory is therefore, among other things, a reading of pseudo-Apollodorus' filing system — a fact that does not diminish it, but does locate it. The allegory is the alchemists' reading, of the systematizers' text, of the poets' myths, of traditions older than any of them. Four layers, and this article has been about the second.

// THE ARTICLE IN ONE RULE //

Trust the Library as you would a good index: completely, for finding; provisionally, for believing. Its order is evidence about the tradition's shape in the Roman period. Its facts are evidence about its sources, which are older and better than its date suggests. Its silences are evidence about nothing at all. Cite it by name, check it where checking is possible, and never let its neatness persuade you that the myths were ever neat.
