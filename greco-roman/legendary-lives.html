I. Legend Is Not Myth: The Genre and Its Three Layers

The other articles of this hub’s first wing deal in myth: stories of gods and heroes, set in what Latin scholarship calls in illo tempore — “in that time”, the undatable time before history, when Ouranos ruled and Kronos swallowed his children. No one asks when, in what year, the castration of Ouranos happened, because the story does not claim a year. Its persons are not the kind of beings who have biographies. The Theogony article of this hub treats Hesiod’s poem accordingly: as a text with a date, carrying a story without one.

This article deals in legend, and legend is a different genre with a different contract. A legend attaches wonders to a named person in datable time: a man who was born on Samos, taught at Croton, and died — somehow — in the Greek West around 490 BCE; a philosopher from Acragas in Sicily who flourished in the 440s BCE; a wandering sage from Tyana in Cappadocia who stood, or is said to have stood, before the emperor Domitian; a king who really did conquer the Persian Empire and really did die at Babylon in June 323 BCE; a slave on Samos whose killing the people of Delphi really did, on our earliest evidence, have to pay for. The wonders are not the difference. Gods work wonders in myth, and these men work wonders in legend. The difference is the frame: legend insists that its wonder-worker had a birthplace, a floruit, contemporaries who could have contradicted the story, and a grave — or, in the most telling cases, a conspicuously missing one.

That frame is what makes legend, paradoxically, the more historical of the two genres — not because its wonders are true, but because its growth is datable. A myth arrives whole, from a past that cannot be stratified. A legend arrives in layers, and the layers have authors and dates. We possess, for Pythagoras, a mocking couplet by a contemporary, a paragraph of sober report from Aristotle’s lost work on the Pythagoreans, a reincarnation memoir retailed by a pupil of Plato, and three full Lives written seven to eight centuries after the man died — and the wonders grow, measurably, at every step. The accretion is itself a document. Read in sequence, the sources for a legendary life are a core sample through the religious imagination of eight hundred years, and each stratum can be dated by the hand that laid it down.

LAYER (a): THE RECOVERABLE PERSON

What the earliest and least interested evidence supports: a birthplace, a school, a public career, a death in roughly datable circumstances. This layer is always thinner than readers want it to be, and this article reports it at its real evidential strength — including, where the strength is low, saying so.

LAYER (b): THE LEGEND’S GROWTH

The wonders, in the order the sources add them, each with its author and its date. A golden thigh in a fragment of Aristotle is a different kind of fact from a golden thigh in a Life written in 300 CE, and the article never lets the second borrow the authority of the first.

LAYER (c): WHAT THE LEGEND WAS FOR

Legends are made by communities that need them: a school proving its founder was more than human, a city claiming a sage, a court commissioning a pagan holy man, a conquered people making the conqueror their own king’s son. The function is not a debunking afterthought. It is, most of the time, the explanation of why the story exists at all.

The five lives chosen here were not chosen at random, and their order is an argument. Pythagoras is the type case: a founder about whom almost nothing contemporary survives, whose legend can be stratified with unusual precision thanks to Walter Burkert’s Lore and Science in Ancient Pythagoreanism (German 1962; English 1972), the study on which all modern work on the question rests. Empedocles is the harder case: a philosopher whose own surviving verses present him as a fallen god, so that the legend is not simply added to the man — the man supplied its script, and his biographers quarrelled over his death already in antiquity. Apollonius of Tyana is the commissioned case: his legend survives essentially in one magnificent, problematic book, written to order for an empress more than a century after his death. Alexander is the maximal case: the best documented life of the ancient world, and also the most thoroughly fictionalised, in a Romance that out-circulated every sober history ever written about him. Aesop is the limiting case: a person whose recoverable core shrinks to four or five facts, and whose legend is therefore almost pure — the storyteller absorbed into his own genre. The Egyptology division of this archive holds the great Egyptian parallel, a historical official of the Third Dynasty whom later ages made a god: Imhotep. The discipline is the same on both sides of the archive, and it is stated once here so that it does not have to be re-argued in every section: a legend is evidence — not for the wonders it reports, but for what its tellers needed the sage to be.

// A WORD ON “LEGEND” //

The word is used in this article in its scholarly sense, from Latin legenda, “things to be read” — a narrative tradition attached to a person, neither certified history nor free invention. It carries no verdict on any single episode. Some legendary episodes preserve real events in costume; most do not; the genre analysis does not depend on deciding each one, and this article decides only where the evidence actually decides.

One more preliminary, because the five lives share it. In every case the legend grows in the same direction: outward in space and upward in rank. The sage travels further than any contemporary could check — to Egypt, to Babylon, to India, to Ethiopia, to the Ocean and the ends of the earth — and he rises in ontological rank as he goes, from gifted teacher to prophet, from prophet to theios anēr, a divine man, from divine man to a being of a third order between gods and men, and finally, in the boldest tellings, to a god who has condescended to walk about in a body. The pattern is so regular that its exceptions are informative: where a source refuses the promotion — a historian who insists the sage died obscurely in exile, a biographer who hedges a resurrection with “apparently dead” — the refusal is datable too, and it is usually the sound of a rival community talking. Legend is a competitive genre. Schools, cities, and eventually religions bid against one another for the sage, and the bids are the evidence.

II. How a Legend Grows: Strata, Dates, and the Discipline of This Article

Before the lives, the method — because in this subject the method is most of the content. A legendary life is never one text. It is a tradition: a sequence of tellings, each retelling the last with additions, and the additions are rarely innocent. The working tool of this article is therefore stratigraphy, borrowed from the archaeologists: lay the sources out in chronological order, identify what each layer adds, and refuse to read a late layer’s material back into an early one. Applied consistently, stratigraphy dissolves most of the famous “mysteries” of these lives into ordinary literary history. Pythagoras did not become more mysterious as the evidence improved; the evidence got later, and later evidence is wonderfuller. The mystery is an artefact of reading the strata in the wrong order — beginning, as the late Lives invite us to begin, at the top of the pile.

The Five Strata, as They Recur in Life After Life

First stratum: contemporaries and near-contemporaries. Brief, oblique, often hostile or amused. Xenophanes mocks Pythagoras for recognising a friend’s soul in a beaten puppy; Heraclitus sneers at his learning as a patchwork; Herodotus mentions Aesop once, in passing, to date somebody else. This stratum is gold precisely because it is not trying to build a legend: a joke only works if the audience already knows the reputation it plays on, so a contemporary joke is evidence for the reputation, dated to the joker’s lifetime.

Second stratum: the fourth century BCE. The generation of Plato and Aristotle collects, systematises, and — crucially — already marvels. Aristotle’s lost treatise On the Pythagoreans, known from fragments, reported the golden thigh and the bilocation without endorsing them, in the tone of a man cataloguing what the school believed. His pupils and Plato’s — Aristoxenus, Dicaearchus, Heraclides Ponticus — wrote the first connected accounts, and each had a programme: Aristoxenus, himself from Pythagorean Tarentum, rationalised; Heraclides, a Platonist with a taste for the marvellous, embroidered. Almost everything in the later Lives that is not pure late-antique invention can be traced to this stratum’s workshop.

Third stratum: Hellenistic biography and the novel. Between roughly 300 BCE and 100 CE the lives become literature: Hermippus, Satyrus, Neanthes, and their kind supply teachers, travels, trials, last words, and deaths shaped to fit the subject’s philosophy — the phenomenon Ava Chitwood’s Death by Philosophy (2004) documents for the early philosophers, in which a thinker’s doctrines are mined to manufacture his biography, above all his death. The Life of Aesop and the Alexander materials assembled in this stratum belong as much to the history of the ancient novel as to biography.

Fourth stratum: the imperial Lives. In the second and third centuries CE the legendary life becomes a major literary form with religious stakes: Diogenes Laertius compiles his Lives of the Eminent Philosophers (early third century CE, dates debated); Philostratus writes Apollonius for the Severan court; Porphyry and Iamblichus write Pythagoras for a paganism that now has a rival. These texts are routinely mined as if they were archives. They are better read as arguments — each with a named author, a known milieu, and a discernible opponent.

Fifth stratum: translation and world conquest. Some lives escape the Greek world entirely. The Alexander Romance passes into Latin, Syriac, Armenian, Arabic, Persian, Ethiopic, Hebrew, and every European vernacular. Apollonius passes into Arabic as Balīnās, master of talismans, and into Byzantine civic folklore as the wards on Constantinople’s gates. Aesop passes into everything, everywhere, and is still passing. At this stratum the legend no longer needs the person at all; it has become a free-floating author-function, a name under which new wonders can be filed indefinitely.

c. 200–330 CE

THE GREAT COMPILATIONS

Within little more than a century: Diogenes Laertius compiles the philosophers’ lives; Philostratus completes the Life of Apollonius after Julia Domna’s death in 217; Porphyry (c. 234–c. 305) and Iamblichus (c. 245–c. 325) write their Pythagorean Lives; the Greek Alexander Romance reaches the form in which a Latin translation could be made of it (Julius Valerius, c. 320 CE). The legendary life becomes, in these decades, a dominant genre of Greek prose.

303 CE and after

THE LEGEND AS WEAPON

Sossianus Hierocles, an official of the Diocletianic persecution, sets Apollonius against Christ; Eusebius of Caesarea writes the rebuttal. From here the legendary lives are ammunition in a religious war, and their preservation — and much of their subsequent fame — follows from that use.

// THE STANDING CAUTION OF THIS ARTICLE //

A late source is not a lying source, and an early source is not an honest one; both are sources for their own moment. When Diogenes Laertius, writing around seven centuries after Pythagoras, reports a wonder and names a fourth-century authority for it, the report is good evidence that the fourth century told that wonder — which is a real and interesting fact. It is not evidence that the wonder occurred in the sixth century BCE. Every section below therefore cites wonders in the form “X reports, in [date], that…” wherever the distinction matters, and the reader should treat any sentence in any other book of the form “Pythagoras did…” followed by a marvel as a stratigraphic error until proven otherwise.

III. Pythagoras I: The Recoverable Man — Samos, Croton, and the School

Layer (a), the person. Pythagoras, son of Mnesarchus, was a Samian who emigrated to Croton in southern Italy at about the age of forty, around 530 BCE, and there founded the community that made him famous. His conventional dates, c. 570–c. 490 BCE, are modern scholarly estimates, not records — the heading of Diogenes Laertius’s Life in Hicks’s Loeb edition gives “c. 582–500 B.C.”, a reminder that even the frame dates have moved by a decade within modern scholarship. He wrote nothing that survives, and on the dominant modern view, following Burkert, he wrote nothing at all: the verses and treatises circulating under his name are the school’s later products, fathered on the founder by the normal pseudepigraphy of the tradition. What the earliest evidence — Plato, Aristotle, and the fourth-century writers who had access to the school’s own memory — supports is a founder of a way of life: Plato’s single mention of him, in the Republic (600a–b, paraphrased), says exactly that and nothing more marvellous: Pythagoras was loved for a manner of life that his followers, called Pythagoreans still in Plato’s day, continued to live by, distinguished from other men. It is the most valuable sentence about Pythagoras written in antiquity, because it is the least interested in him.

The way of life had a politics. At Croton, Pythagoras and his associates — Diogenes Laertius (8.3), in Hicks’s translation, gives the figure of nearly three hundred — were “held in great estimation” and governed so effectively, the same passage says, that the constitution was in effect an aristocracy, a government of the best. The Pythagorean societies of the Greek cities of Italy were political clubs as well as religious communities, and they ended as political clubs end: in violence. The tradition preserves two waves of anti-Pythagorean rising, one in Pythagoras’s own last years at Croton and a worse one around the middle of the fifth century BCE, when Pythagorean meeting-houses across southern Italy were burned and their members killed or scattered. The school’s dispersion is, ironically, part of why we know anything about it: Philolaus of Croton (c. 470–c. 385 BCE), a refugee generation’s heir, was the first Pythagorean to publish the doctrines in a book — the book Plato is said to have bought at a famous price — and it is Philolaus, not the founder, to whom the earliest attributable Pythagorean philosophy belongs.

What the founder himself taught, at the strength the evidence will bear, is this. He taught the immortality and transmigration of the soul: that the soul survives death and is reborn, in human and in animal bodies, and that conduct in this life answers for the next. He taught a discipline of ritual purity and dietary restriction, of which abstention from animal flesh — grounded in the kinship of all ensouled life — is the best attested and was already, in the fifth century, the school’s public trademark, mocked on the comic stage. He taught by authority rather than argument: the school’s later formula, autos epha — “he himself said it” — preserves the memory of a master whose pronouncements closed questions. And he founded a community bound by friendship, common property, and graded secrecy, in which candidates underwent a long probation — the tradition says five years of silence, listening without seeing the master — before full admission. Burkert’s stratigraphic verdict, which this article follows, is that this religious founder is the historical Pythagoras, and that the mathematician-philosopher of the handbooks — the theorem, the musical ratios as a system, “all things are number” — is a construction of the following centuries: Aristotle, our earliest systematic witness, attributes the number-doctrine not to Pythagoras but to “the so-called Pythagoreans”, and the doctrines he describes match the generation of Philolaus. The distinction is Burkert’s central one, between the lore — the acusmata, the ritual rules, the master’s charisma — and the science, which the school developed after him and the legend then donated to him.

Even his death comes in the two registers at once. Diogenes Laertius (8.39–40, paraphrased from Hicks’s translation) gives first the dramatic version: fleeing the burning of Milo’s house at Croton, Pythagoras reached a field of beans and stopped, saying he would be captured rather than cross it — the bean prohibition of Section VI made fatal — and his pursuers cut his throat. He then gives Dicaearchus’s version, in the next breath: Pythagoras died a refugee in the temple of the Muses at Metapontum, after forty days’ fasting. A death by his own doctrine, or a death in sanctuary exile: the tradition could not decide, because both deaths meant something, and meaning, in this genre, outbids fact. The Metapontine ending has the better early authority — Dicaearchus was Aristotle’s pupil — and the bean-field ending has the better story. The legend kept both, as it usually does.

IV. Pythagoras II: Already a Wonder-Worker — The Reports of Aristotle’s Generation

Layer (b) begins early — startlingly early. The standard picture of legend as a slow fungus on a sober original fails for Pythagoras in an instructive way: the wonders are not late. They are present, fully formed, in the fourth century BCE, within a long lifetime of the man’s death, in the reports of Aristotle himself. Aristotle wrote a treatise On the Pythagoreans, now lost; a famous fragment of it (fr. 191 Rose), preserved through later quotation — in Aelian, in Apollonius’s Mirabilia, and in Iamblichus’s Life — catalogues the school’s presentation of its founder, and the catalogue is worth giving in full, in paraphrase, because every later Life is an expansion of this single paragraph. Aristotle reported that the Pythagoreans divided rational beings into three kinds — gods, men, and beings like Pythagoras; that Pythagoras had a golden thigh, which he displayed; that he was seen on the same day and at the same hour at both Metapontum and Croton; that when a deadly snake bit him, he bit it back and killed it; that as he crossed a river — the Nessus in Diogenes’s version, the Casas in others — the river addressed him aloud, greeting him by name; and that the people of Croton identified him as the Hyperborean Apollo, Apollo come from the fabulous far north.

Diogenes Laertius, compiling seven centuries later, retails the core of the same catalogue in his own voice, and his wording — Hicks’s Loeb translation (1925) — may stand here for the tradition’s settled form:

“Indeed, his bearing is said to have been most dignified, and his disciples held the opinion about him that he was Apollo come down from the far north. There is a story that once, when he was disrobed, his thigh was seen to be of gold; and when he crossed the river Nessus, quite a number of people said they heard it welcome him.” — DIOGENES LAERTIUS, LIVES 8.11, TRANS. R. D. HICKS (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY, 1925)

Three observations keep this stratum honest. First, Aristotle reports; he does not endorse. The fragment’s frame is ethnographic — this is what the Pythagoreans say and teach about their master — and Aristotle elsewhere treats the school’s doctrines with cool philosophical distance. But reportage is not nothing: a sober catalogue of c. 330 BCE proves the wonders were the school’s public doctrine about its founder within two generations of his death, taught while men whose grandfathers had known him were still alive. The legend of Pythagoras is not a medieval or even a late-antique growth. It is, in its essentials, a product of the school itself, and nearly contemporary with the school’s founding generation. Second, the wonders are specific in kind. A golden thigh is not a generic marvel; in Greek terms it is a mark of the more-than-human, the body of a being whose substance is not ordinary flesh — the same logic by which heroes in epic have ichor, not blood, in their veins. Bilocation — Metapontum and Croton are some sixty kilometres apart — asserts freedom from the body’s ordinary limits. The speaking river asserts that nature itself recognises him. Together they do not say “this man was clever”; they say “this man was misclassified as human”, which is precisely what the three-kinds doctrine — gods, men, and such as Pythagoras — states in propositional form. Third, the Hyperborean Apollo identification ties the founder to Apollo’s cult geography: the Hyperboreans, the blessed people beyond the north wind, were Apollo’s own, and Abaris, their priest, was said to have flown to Greece on Apollo’s arrow. The later Lives make Abaris visit Pythagoras and hand him the arrow in recognition. The school’s founder is being slotted into an existing sacred map, not invented from nothing.

The contemporary stratum beneath Aristotle is thinner and ruder, and it corroborates the reputation from the other side. Xenophanes of Colophon (c. 570–c. 475 BCE), an older contemporary, wrote the puppy verses Diogenes preserves (8.36, in Hicks’s verse rendering): Pythagoras, passing a man beating a puppy, cries out — “Stay, smite not! ’Tis a friend, a human soul; I knew him straight whenas I heard him yelp!” The joke is datable evidence of the first class: by Xenophanes’ lifetime, Pythagoras’s transmigration doctrine was famous enough to be a punchline, and the punchline already casts him as the man who recognises souls across bodies — the seed from which Heraclides’ reincarnation memoir (Section V) will grow. Heraclitus of Ephesus (fl. c. 500 BCE), quoted by Diogenes at 8.6, attacks from another flank: Pythagoras practised inquiry beyond all other men, Heraclitus concedes in Hicks’s rendering, but made of his selections “a wisdom of his own, showing much learning but poor workmanship” — polymathy without understanding, the charge of a rival sage who plainly regards Pythagoras as the competition. And Empedocles, a generation later, praises in verse a man of surpassing knowledge whose mind reached across ten and twenty lifetimes of men — unnamed in the fragment (DK 31 B129), but identified by the ancient tradition, plausibly, as Pythagoras: the reincarnation doctrine reflected back as a compliment between wonder-workers. Hostile, amused, and admiring witnesses agree on the one thing that matters for layer (a): the reputation — soul-expert, master of a devoted school, claimant to knowledge beyond the human filing system — is contemporary. The golden thigh is the reputation, mineralised.

// WHY A THIGH? //

The golden thigh puzzled ancient rationalisers too, and the later tradition supplied occasions for its display: shown to Abaris as a credential, shown at the Olympic Games, bared by accident when Pythagoras was disrobed, as Diogenes has it. Burkert’s discussion treats the motif within the shamanic and Apolline complex of the early legend — the marked body of the man who travels between worlds. What can be said without theory is the stratigraphic fact: the thigh is in Aristotle’s fragment, and nothing in the tradition is older than the stratum that contains it except the jokes.

Layer (c), what this stratum was for, is already visible. A school that taught by autos epha — the master said it — needed a master whose saying settled things, and a master of the third kind, neither god nor man, settles things better than a clever Samian emigrant could. The wonders are the school’s constitution in narrative form. They also armed it against a specific vulnerability: Pythagoras left no writings, so his authority could not be checked against a text; it could only be embodied in a person, and the person had therefore to be extraordinary. Every founder-legend in this article solves an institutional problem of exactly this shape, and Pythagoras’s solves it first and most influentially.

V. Pythagoras III: Memory Across Lives — Heraclides Ponticus and the Reincarnation Chain

The single most influential addition to the legend was made by Heraclides Ponticus (c. 390–c. 310 BCE), a pupil of Plato and one of the most inventive writers of the fourth century — astronomer, literary critic, and retailer of marvellous narratives that antiquity itself already read with raised eyebrows. Diogenes Laertius (8.4–5) introduces the story with an attribution that deserves to be quoted in its grammar even in paraphrase: this is what Heraclides of Pontus tells us Pythagoras used to say about himself. A first-person memoir of previous lives, at two removes: Heraclides reports what Pythagoras claimed to remember.

The chain, as Diogenes retails it in Hicks’s translation (paraphrased here, with the structure kept exact), runs as follows. Pythagoras had first been Aethalides, a son of Hermes, and Hermes had offered him any gift except immortality; Aethalides chose memory — to retain, through life and through death, the recollection of everything he experienced. So the soul passed, with its memory intact, into Euphorbus, the Trojan hero whom Menelaus killed in the Iliad — and Euphorbus could still recount the soul’s earlier wanderings, its lives in plants and animals, and what it had seen in Hades. When Euphorbus died, the soul entered Hermotimus of Clazomenae, who proved the story empirically: travelling to the temple of Apollo at Branchidae, he identified the shield Menelaus had dedicated there on his voyage home from Troy, by then rotted away to its ivory facing alone. Hermotimus died and became Pyrrhus, a fisherman of Delos, who still remembered the whole sequence. Pyrrhus died, and the soul became Pythagoras — who remembered everything.

1. AETHALIDES

THE GIFT OF MEMORY

Son of Hermes. Offered any gift but immortality, he chooses unbroken memory across death — the device that makes the whole chain, and the doctrine behind it, narratable in the first person.

2. EUPHORBUS

THE TROJAN HERO

The soul in heroic time, killed by Menelaus (Iliad 17). The chain annexes the epic past: Pythagoras’s previous life is checkable, in principle, against Homer — and against a physical object, the dedicated shield.

3. HERMOTIMUS

THE PROOF AT BRANCHIDAE

Hermotimus identifies Menelaus’s shield, rotted to its ivory facing. The legend here invents its own verification scene — memory tested against an artefact in a temple, the ancient world’s nearest approach to a museum label.

4. PYRRHUS

THE FISHERMAN OF DELOS

A nobody between the hero and the sage. The humble incarnation matters: the chain does not only collect famous lives; it demonstrates the doctrine’s democracy of souls, high and low in alternation.

5. PYTHAGORAS

THE REMEMBERER

The present life, in which the chain becomes known — because this incarnation, uniquely, can tell it. The doctrine of transmigration acquires an eyewitness.

As narrative engineering, this is superb, and its functions (layer c) are legible. Transmigration is an unfalsifiable doctrine until someone remembers; Heraclides’ Pythagoras remembers, and the story supplies the memory with a mechanism (Hermes’ gift), a proof (the shield), and a pedigree that runs through Homer himself. The chain also quietly promotes its hero: his soul is not merely old, it is documented, with a heroic incarnation and a divine father in its past. And it converts the school’s central doctrine into the founder’s autobiography — the strongest form of authority a doctrine can have in a culture that trusts witnesses over arguments.

The stratigraphic discipline applies with full force. Heraclides wrote a full century and a half after Pythagoras died; he is also the source, in the Empedocles tradition (Section X), for a vanishing-and-apotheosis death, and ancient critics already classed him among the paradoxographers, the retailers of wonders. None of that makes the chain worthless as evidence. It makes it evidence of a precise thing: that by the later fourth century BCE, within Plato’s own circle’s penumbra, the Pythagoras tradition was producing first-person reincarnation narrative of a sophistication the founder’s contemporaries never record. Xenophanes’ puppy shows the doctrine was his; the chain shows what the doctrine became in the hands of a literary man. Between those two data points lies the whole growth of the legend’s second stratum — and the distance between them is exactly what this article means by a layer.

// HOW TO READ A CHAIN LIKE THIS //

Ask three questions of any such set-piece. Who first tells it, and how long after the events? (Heraclides; about 150 years.) What work does its machinery do? (It makes an uncheckable doctrine checkable-looking, by attaching it to a temple object and a Homeric name.) What did the teller’s other work look like? (Heraclides specialised in marvellous narratives of souls, heavens, and resurrections.) None of the answers refutes the story. Together they file it — correctly — as fourth-century school literature, not sixth-century memoir.

VI. Pythagoras IV: The Akousmata — A School Held Together by Riddles

Between the person and the wonders stands the stratum that Burkert taught scholarship to take most seriously, because it is the hardest to invent retrospectively: the akousmata, also called symbola — the short, strange oral precepts that initiates memorised and lived by. Diogenes Laertius (8.17) lists them in Hicks’s translation, and the list’s flavour is its own evidence: do not stir the fire with a knife; do not step over the beam of a balance; do not sit down on your bushel; do not eat your heart; do not help a man off with a load, but help him on; do not leave the pan’s imprint on the ashes — the list runs on: no swallows under the roof, no birds with hooked claws, do not turn back at the frontier when you go abroad. (Paraphrased from the list at 8.17–18; the individual precepts quoted are Hicks’s renderings.) Diogenes then immediately supplies allegorical explanations — do not stir the fire with a knife means do not provoke the passions of the powerful; do not step over the balance means do not transgress equity — and the explanations are themselves a stratum: Aristotle, in the material Diogenes excerpts at 8.34, already gives different, stranger explanations of the bean prohibition (beans are like the genitals; like the gates of Hades, being alone unjointed; like the universe; used in voting by lot, hence oligarchic), which suggests that even in the fourth century the precepts circulated as riddles whose original rationale the school itself glossed in competing ways.

The akousmata matter to this article for three reasons. First, they are the best evidence for what the early community actually did: a rule-bound common life of ritual prohibitions, dietary taboos, and disciplines of silence and memory — the recoverable school of Section III, preserved not as doctrine but as practice. Burkert’s analysis showed that many precepts belong to a wide Mediterranean and Near Eastern stock of folk-taboo and ritual rule, and that their presence in the tradition is very hard to explain as late invention: nobody forging a philosopher-founder in the age of Plato invents “do not sit on your bushel”. Second, the akousmata explain the school’s famous division, reported by the later tradition, between akousmatikoi, the hearers who kept the precepts, and mathēmatikoi, the learners who pursued the demonstrative sciences — a division Burkert used to model how lore and science coexisted in one institution, the lore old and authoritative, the science newer and eventually dominant in the school’s public image. Third, the precepts are the legend’s ballast. A tradition that preserves the founder’s table manners alongside his golden thigh is not a free fantasy; it is an institution’s memory, in which the marvellous and the mundane were filed together because both were his. The discipline of this article — layers, not dismissal — is nowhere more necessary: the same Diogenes passage that retails the river’s greeting also retails the rule about beans, and the historian who credits neither wholesale nor dismisses either wholesale has understood the source.

THE PRECEPT AS RIDDLE

“Do not stir the fire with a knife” survives with at least two ancient explanatory registers: the moral-allegorical (do not provoke the powerful) and the ritual-literal (a rule of the cult kitchen). The coexistence is the datum: the school itself no longer spoke with one voice about its own rules by Aristotle’s century.

THE BEANS

The bean prohibition is the akousma with the longest paper trail — Aristotle’s multiple explanations, the founder’s legendary death at the bean field (Section III), and a modern literature of its own (favism, the glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency common in Mediterranean populations, has been proposed as a material substrate; the proposal is modern and is labelled as such here). A taboo this odd, this early, and this consequential in the storytelling is bedrock tradition, whatever its origin.

SILENCE AND MEMORY

The five-year probation of silent listening (Diogenes 8.10, paraphrased) and the evening self-examination — “Where did I trespass? What did I achieve? And unfulfilled what duties did I leave?”, the verses at 8.22 in Hicks’s rendering — are the akousmatic life from inside: disciplines, not doctrines. They are also, not coincidentally, the parts of Pythagoreanism that later antiquity and the monastic traditions of two religions found most reusable.

“HE HIMSELF SAID IT”

The school’s citation practice — autos epha — is reported by the later tradition as the akousmatikoi’s answer to every question. Whether or not the formula is archaic, it names the institution’s real constitution: authority vested in a person, transmitted orally, guarded by initiation. The legend of the wonder-worker is that constitution’s necessary corollary.

VII. Pythagoras V: Egypt, Babylon, and the Late Lives — What the Travels Were For

The legend’s last and largest Pythagorean growth is geographical. The early strata keep Pythagoras in the Greek world — Samos, Croton, a descent into the cave of Ida on Crete in some tellings. The later strata send him to school with the whole ancient world: to Egypt, where the priests initiate him and he learns the language and the sacred lore; to Babylon, where the Chaldaeans and the Magi teach him, and where Porphyry’s Life has him attach himself to a master named Zaratas — a form of Zoroaster’s name — by whom he is purified and instructed in the principles of nature; to the Jews, the Arabs, the Phoenicians, and in Iamblichus’s maximal itinerary effectively everywhere wisdom was kept. The travels are datable as a tradition. The first surviving author to send Pythagoras to Egypt is Isocrates, in the Busiris (c. 390 BCE), and he does it in passing, as an exemplum in an argument about Egyptian wisdom’s prestige — which tells us the claim was already available, and already useful, in the early fourth century. Diogenes (8.2–3, paraphrased from Hicks) retails the developed version: Polycrates’ letter of introduction to Pharaoh Amasis, the Egyptian language learned from Antiphon’s account, the journeys among Chaldaeans and Magi, the Egyptian sanctuaries entered and their secret lore imparted. Porphyry (c. 234–c. 305 CE) and Iamblichus (c. 245–c. 325 CE), in the two surviving late Lives, complete the itinerary and systematise it: Iamblichus even supplies the mechanism — Pythagoras, studying in Egypt, is carried off to Babylon by the invading soldiers of Cambyses, and there, overjoyed, consorts with the Magi. A captivity narrative solves the chronology problem of how a Samian of the 530s BCE studied at both ends of the Near East, and the solution’s ingenuity is the surest sign that the problem was felt — by the tradition itself — to need solving.

What were the travels for? (Layer c, in its purest form.) In the Greek debate about wisdom’s origins, a sage’s authority could be underwritten by derivation: Greek wisdom was young, Egyptian and Babylonian wisdom was immeasurably old, and to have studied at Heliopolis and Babylon was to hold a doctorate from antiquity itself. Herodotus had already run the argument in reverse, deriving Greek rites from Egypt; Plato’s Timaeus has an Egyptian priest tell Solon that the Greeks are children. The Pythagoras of the travels is the Greek sage re-credentialed by the East — and the claim cuts both ways, which is why it flourished: it exalts Pythagoras (he alone of Greeks was admitted everywhere) and it exalts the schools that inherit him (their lore is not Greek improvisation but the perennial wisdom of the temples). The archive’s Egyptology division documents the real Egyptian priestly culture such claims traded on; the reader who wants the Egyptian side of the exchange should follow the legend’s own itinerary to Alchemy in Egypt and the House of Life, the temple scriptorium whose Greek reputation is the travels’ institutional backdrop. What the stratigraphy will not support is the itinerary as biography: no source earlier than Isocrates — three generations and more after the man’s death, in a rhetorical set-piece about Egypt — places Pythagoras east of Crete, and Burkert’s treatment files the travels with the legend’s programmatic growth, not with the recoverable life. The historical Pythagoras may of course have travelled; Samos traded with Egypt, and Naukratis was full of Samians. But may have is the ceiling of the evidence, and this article leaves it there.

The late Lives themselves — Diogenes’ compilation, Porphyry’s Life of Pythagoras, Iamblichus’s On the Pythagorean Life — close the Pythagorean dossier, and they should be read as what they are: works of the third and early fourth centuries CE, written when “Pythagoras” had become the figurehead of a whole religious philosophy. Porphyry, Plotinus’s pupil and editor, writes a comparatively restrained Life that still contains the travels, the wonders, and the Zaratas tutelage. Iamblichus writes a protreptic — an invitation to the Pythagorean life as his school practised it — in which the founder’s biography is the first book of a ten-book Pythagorean curriculum, and the wonders multiply accordingly: the river’s greeting, the golden thigh displayed to Abaris, the white bear of Daunia talked out of eating beans’ animal counterparts, the eagle called down from the sky. To cite Iamblichus for the sixth century BCE is a stratigraphic error of eight hundred years. To cite him for the fourth century CE — for what a Neoplatonist school needed its founder to have been — is to use a first-class source correctly. That sentence, mutatis mutandis, is the method of every section that follows.

// BURKERT’S VERDICT, IN ONE LINE //

Walter Burkert, Lore and Science in Ancient Pythagoreanism (1972): separate Aristotle’s testimony about the Pythagoreans from the Academy’s glorification of Pythagoras, and the historical figure who emerges is a charismatic religious founder — an expert on the soul, ritual, and the rule-bound life — onto whom the school’s later science, and Plato’s metaphysics, were retrospectively loaded. The verdict has been qualified at its edges (the founder’s mathematical interests are still argued over) but its stratigraphic core remains the working consensus this article follows.

A last Pythagorean datum belongs here because it is the legend’s own commentary on itself. The later tradition reports that the Metapontines, after his death, made Pythagoras’s house a temple of Demeter and his porch a shrine — Diogenes (8.15) retails it from Favorinus. A founder’s house becoming a temple is the institutional form of the three-kinds doctrine: the man of the third kind gets, in the end, a cult. The legend of Pythagoras does not merely describe his promotion from man to divine man. In the school’s practice, it performed it.

VIII. Empedocles I: The Philosopher of the Four Roots

Empedocles of Acragas (c. 494–c. 434 BCE) is the best-documented person in this article relative to the size of his legend, for a simple reason: he wrote, and his writing survives. Some 150 fragments of his hexameter poetry — roughly a tenth of the original, on the usual estimate — are preserved in quotation by later authors, and in 1999 the publication of the Strasbourg papyrus added primary papyrus evidence to the dossier. Layer (a) can therefore be built, unusually, from the man’s own words. He was a citizen of Acragas (modern Agrigento) in Sicily, the great Dorian city whose temples still stand in the Valley below the town; from a distinguished family — his grandfather, also Empedocles, won an Olympic horse-race in 496 BCE, on the testimony Diogenes collects (8.51–52, paraphrased). The tradition makes him a democratic champion who broke an oligarchic faction after the fall of Acragas’s tyranny, a physician of standing — Galen’s tradition counts him founder of the Sicilian school of medicine — and a public benefactor of the engineering kind: the sources credit him with draining or diverting the rivers at Selinunte to end a pestilence, and with stopping the crop-destroying etesian winds by stretching asses’ skins on the hills to catch them, the feat that earned him the nickname “wind-stayer” in Diogenes’ Timaeus material (8.60). The political and medical outline is tradition, not record; the poetry is the man.

The poetry falls, on the ancient division Diogenes reports, into two works. On Nature (the Physics) sets out the system that made him immortal among philosophers: nothing comes into being or perishes outright; what men call birth and death are the mixing and separation of four eternal “roots” — rhizōmata — earth, water, air, and fire, driven by two cosmic powers, Love (Philotēs), which unites, and Strife (Neikos), which divides, in an endless cycle of cosmic union and dissolution. Aristotle’s respect for the system is the measure of its standing: Empedocles is the first clear pluralist in Greek physics, the man who answered Parmenides’ denial of change by making change rearrangement — and Aristotle, who criticises him for hundreds of pages, criticises him as a physicist among physicists. The four roots became, as the four elements, the matter-theory of the Western world for two thousand years, and they are the reason Empedocles has a chapter in every history of philosophy ever written. The Purifications (Katharmoi) speak in another voice entirely — the voice of Section IX. Whether the two works are really two, or one poem read in two registers, is a live scholarly question (the Strasbourg papyrus, which places physical and daimonological material in uncomfortable proximity, sharpened it); this article uses the traditional titles as the tradition hands them down, and flags the question rather than settling it.

THE FOUR ROOTS, IN HIS OWN NAMES

In fragment B6 the roots are named as gods: bright Zeus, life-bearing Hera, Aidoneus (Hades), and Nestis — a Sicilian name of Persephone — whose tears are the source of mortal streams. Which god is which element was disputed already in antiquity; the identifications matter to Section X, because Peter Kingsley’s argued reassignment — Zeus as air, Hera as earth, Aidoneus as fire, Nestis as water — is one of the load-bearing claims of his reading of the whole tradition.

LOVE AND STRIFE

Not metaphors for attraction and repulsion, in the poem’s own presentation, but powers with cosmic office, waxing and waning in turn: under Love’s full dominion the roots form the Sphere, the blessed god of the physical poem; under Strife’s advance the world of separate things — our world — comes to be. The cycle has no final state and no creator. It is a physics that behaves like a theogony, which is why this hub files its author here, on the border of its two wings.

THE PHYSICIAN AND THE CITY

The benefactions the tradition retails — Selinunte’s waters sweetened, the winds checked, the woman Panthea cured after the physicians despaired (Diogenes 8.69, from Hermippus) — belong to the grey zone between layers (a) and (b): public works and medical fame are plausible for a rich, prominent Acragantine, and each story is also exactly the story a wonder-worker’s dossier requires. Section X shows the tradition itself quarrelling over the greatest of them.

WHAT HE DID NOT WRITE

No prose treatise, no biography, no letters survive or are credibly attested. The Empedocles dossier is: his verses, Aristotle’s critical engagement, the fourth-century historians (Timaeus of Tauromenium above all, Sicily’s own historian), and the Hellenistic biographical workshop — assembled, seven centuries on, by Diogenes Laertius at 8.51–77. Everything in Sections IX–X is one of those four things, and is labelled with which.

IX. Empedocles II: The Fallen Daimōn — The Purifications and the Self-Presentation

With Empedocles the legend’s direction of flow reverses, and the reversal is what makes him the most instructive case in this article. Pythagoras’s school wrote its founder’s divinity for him after his death. Empedocles published his own, in verse, in his lifetime — and the later legend is, to an extent true of no other figure here, a set of footnotes to his self-presentation. The Purifications open with an address to the citizens of Acragas that Diogenes already knew as the poem’s beginning. In M. R. Wright’s translation (Empedocles: The Extant Fragments, 1981), fragment B112 runs, in its famous central claim:

“I tell you I travel up and down as an immortal god, mortal no longer, honoured by all as it seems, crowned with ribbons and fresh garlands. Whenever I enter prospering towns I am revered by both men and women. They follow me in countless numbers, to ask where their advantage lies, some seeking prophecies, others, long pierced by harsh pains, ask to hear the word of healing for all kinds of illnesses.” — EMPEDOCLES, FR. B112 (PURIFICATIONS), TRANS. M. R. WRIGHT (1981)

The claim is precise, and its precision matters. Empedocles does not say he was born a god. Fragment B115 — the other pillar of the Purifications — tells the story that makes the claim a return: there is an oracle of Necessity, an ancient decree of the gods sealed with broad oaths, that any daimōn — one of the long-lived spirits to whom a portion of life among the blessed is allotted — who pollutes himself with bloodshed or false oath must wander from the blessed ones for three times ten thousand seasons, born through the age in every kind of mortal form, tossed from air to sea to earth to sun and back, hated by all the elements in turn. Then, in Wright’s rendering of the fragment’s turn: “I too am now one of these, an exile from the gods and a wanderer, having put my trust in raving Strife.” The speaker of the Purifications is therefore a fallen daimōn in the last act of his exile: the godhood of B112 is not megalomania bolted onto a physics, it is the physics’ companion doctrine — the soul’s cycle of fall and purification, matching the cosmos’s cycle of Strife and Love — announced by a man who presents his own biography as its proof. Add fragment B111, in which he promises his addressee Pausanias that he shall learn remedies against ills and old age, shall stop the winds and recall them, shall make rain and drought at need, and shall bring back from Hades the strength of a man who has died (paraphrased), and the dossier is complete: prophecy, healing, weather-working, necromancy, and personal godhood, all claimed in the first person, in public verse, around the middle of the fifth century BCE.

The scholarly question is what kind of speech-act this was, and it divides the modern literature in a way this article reports rather than resolves. On one reading — the rationalising tradition that runs from the nineteenth century through much twentieth-century scholarship — the Purifications are religious poetry in a conventional Orphic-Pythagorean idiom, and the “I am a god” is a persona, to be filed with the poem’s other voices. On the reading argued by Peter Kingsley in Ancient Philosophy, Mystery, and Magic: Empedocles and Pythagorean Tradition (1995), the persona reading is the evasion: Empedocles belonged to a real tradition of Greek healer-prophets — Kingsley’s word for the type is the iatromantis, the healer-seer — centred in the Greek West, practising incubation, descent to the underworld, and wonder-working as a vocation, and the poems mean what they say because the vocation was real. Kingsley’s Empedocles is, in the shorthand of his reviewers, a shamanic figure: a man whose philosophy is the verbal deposit of a practice of ecstasy, healing, and traffic with the dead. This article presents Kingsley’s reading as what it is — an argued position, built on formidable philology (his reassignment of the four roots’ divine names, his work on the volcanic and chthonic geography of Sicily and southern Italy), admired and contested in about equal measure by the specialists, and not the consensus of the field. What is not contested, and what the stratigraphy needs, is the floor beneath both readings: the self-presentation is Empedocles’ own, it is early, and it is extravagant. His biographers did not invent a wonder-worker. They inherited one, self-declared — and then had to decide, as Section X shows, what to do with him.

// THE MYSTERIES CONNECTION //

The Purifications’ world — the fallen soul, the cycle of births, the horror of bloodshed and meat-eating, the promise of return to the blessed — is the world of the Orphic gold tablets and the mystery traditions treated in this hub’s Mysteries article, where the same discipline is applied: the tablets are real objects, the eschatology they imply is debated, and the debate is reported as a debate. Empedocles is that world’s loudest surviving voice — and the only one who signed his proclamation with his own name and city.

One consequence follows for the whole Empedocles dossier, and Chitwood’s Death by Philosophy states its general form: where a philosopher’s works survive, ancient biography treats the works as autobiography and manufactures the life — above all, the death — out of the doctrines. A man who calls himself a god must die like one, or be caught failing to. A man whose physics enthrones fire must meet fire. The next section watches the tradition do both, simultaneously, and quarrel about it in the open.

X. Empedocles III: The Deaths — Etna, the Bronze Sandal, and the Sources Already Disagreeing

Diogenes Laertius’s account of Empedocles’ death (8.67–73) is the finest specimen in this article of a legend caught in the act of being several legends, because Diogenes — to his credit as a compiler — prints the quarrel instead of resolving it. Three endings stand side by side in his pages, each with its authority named.

First, the apotheosis. Heraclides Ponticus — the same Heraclides who authored Pythagoras’s reincarnation chain — told how Empedocles, after the cure of the woman in a trance (the case of the apparently dead woman restored, which had made him famous), was sacrificing at the field of Peisianax with his companions, Pausanias among them. After the feast the company dispersed to sleep; Empedocles remained where he had reclined. At daybreak he was gone. A servant reported that in the middle of the night he had heard an exceedingly loud voice calling Empedocles, and had seen a light in the heavens and a glitter of lamps, and nothing else. Pausanias sent men to search, then called the search off: things beyond expectation had happened, and it was their duty, Diogenes retails (8.67–68, paraphrased from Hicks), to sacrifice to him, since he was now a god. The B112 claim, enacted as a disappearance: no corpse, a voice, a light, and a disciple who closes the case with a cult act.

Second, the exposure. Hippobotus, a Hellenistic biographer, told it otherwise, and Diogenes (8.69) gives his version in words that became the legend’s most famous sentence. In Hicks’s translation:

“Hippobotus, again, asserts that, when he got up, he set out on his way to Etna; then, when he had reached it, he plunged into the fiery craters and disappeared, his intention being to confirm the report that he had become a god. Afterwards the truth was known, because one of his slippers was thrown up in the flames; it had been his custom to wear slippers of bronze.” — DIOGENES LAERTIUS, LIVES 8.69, TRANS. R. D. HICKS (LOEB, 1925)

The bronze sandal is the legend’s own tell — the detail that converts apotheosis into fraud inside the story itself. Its narrative function is exact: a disappearance requires the world to return no evidence, and the volcano returns one object, the most personal and least combustible one, a shoe. The story therefore contains its own debunking, and antiquity noticed: the Etna leap was already, in the sources, not a pious tradition but a hostile one, the version told by those who thought the god-claim a swindle. Horace fixed it for Latin posterity in the Ars Poetica (lines 464–466, paraphrased) — Empedocles, desiring to be thought an immortal god, calmly leapt into burning Etna — and the image has never left the culture: Hölderlin’s unfinished tragedy, Matthew Arnold’s Empedocles on Etna (1852), and a hundred paintings descend from Hippobotus’s paragraph, not from Heraclides’.

Third, the rebuttal. Timaeus of Tauromenium (c. 356–c. 260 BCE), Sicily’s own historian and Diogenes’s most-cited authority on Empedocles, rejected the whole marvellous dossier with a local’s contempt: the Etna story is false, he held (Diogenes 8.71–72, paraphrased), because Empedocles left Sicily for the Peloponnese and never returned — his later life is simply not Sicilian business at all. Neanthes supplied the corresponding plain death: a fall from a carriage, a broken thigh, death from the injury; a tomb was shown at Megara. Aristotle, characteristically, had contributed only a number — Empedocles died at sixty (Diogenes 8.74, paraphrased, from the Aristotelian tradition) — against other authorities’ 109 years. So the dossier’s own strata read: a self-declared god (his verses, c. 450 BCE); a vanishing with heavenly light (Heraclides, fourth century); a fraudulent leap exposed by a shoe (Hippobotus, Hellenistic); a historian’s denial and an exile’s grave (Timaeus, third century BCE, drawing on Sicilian memory). The sources do not merely fail to agree. They disagree on the record, by name, within the compilation that preserves them — the clearest demonstration in this article that ancient biography was a contested field, not a credulous one.

Kingsley’s reading (Section IX) re-enters here, and must be given its due as an interpretation of exactly this dossier. On his account, the Etna story is not a fraud anecdote that happens to have volcanic colouring: Sicily’s craters and hot springs were, in the religious geography of the Greek West, entrances to the underworld and sites of initiatory descent; the single bronze sandal belongs to the iconography of Hecate and of underworld initiation; and a tradition that sends the self-declared daimōn into the crater is preserving, in hostile or garbled form, an initiatory myth of apotheosis through fire — Empedocles’ own element of Aidoneus, on Kingsley’s reassignment of the roots. The reading is elegant, and it explains why the story has the details it has better than the fraud reading explains them. It remains, as Section IX flagged, an argued position: the same details can be read, with Chitwood, as the biographical workshop’s standard product — a death manufactured from the doctrines (the god-claim, the fire root) by writers who needed the life to rhyme with the work. The two readings are not equally supported on every point, but they agree on the fact that does the stratigraphic work: the Etna story is a construction around the poems, by hands later than the poems, for audiences who already knew the claim of B112 and wanted a verdict on it. Heraclides delivers the verdict guilty of being right; Hippobotus, guilty of fraud; Timaeus dismisses the court. The volcano is where three centuries of readers voted on Empedocles’ self-presentation, and the votes are preserved. That — not the leap — is the historical event.

// DID HE JUMP? //

At the strength of the evidence: unknowable, and the question is malformed. No contemporary source describes Empedocles’ death; the earliest surviving accounts are a century later and already legendary in opposite directions; the best-placed local historian denies the Sicilian setting of the famous version outright. What can be said is that he died, probably in his sixties on the Aristotelian figure, possibly in exile in the Peloponnese — and that his death became, within living memory of his city, the most contested sage’s death in Greek biography. The contest is the recoverable fact, and it is enough.

XI. Apollonius I: A Commissioned Life — Philostratus, Julia Domna, and the Notebooks of Damis

Apollonius of Tyana presents the opposite source-problem from Pythagoras. Pythagoras has too many sources, all late and layered. Apollonius has, in effect, one: the Life of Apollonius of Tyana (Vita Apollonii) by Flavius Philostratus (c. 170–c. 247 CE), a star sophist of the Severan court, in eight books. Layer (a), the recoverable person, must be quarried out of that single splendid quarry, and the quarrying is delicate, because the quarry was commissioned. Philostratus tells us so himself, in his first chapter (1.3, paraphrased throughout this and the following sections from the Life; the standard modern text and translation is Christopher P. Jones’s Loeb edition of 2005, which revised F. C. Conybeare’s 1912 Loeb version — the one short passage quoted verbatim in Section XII is Conybeare’s, and is named as his): the empress Julia Domna, wife of Septimius Severus and the presiding intelligence of a famous literary circle, charged him with the work and put into his hands its principal alleged source — the notebooks, or memoirs, of Damis of Nineveh (in Philostratus’s geography, of Hierapolis in Syria), Apollonius’s disciple and travelling companion, which a relative of Damis had brought to the empress’s attention. Julia Domna died in 217 CE; Philostratus completed the Life after her death, probably in the 220s or 230s. The sage had then been dead for roughly a hundred and twenty years.

What, beneath the commission, is recoverable? That Apollonius existed, modern scholarship is broadly agreed: a Neopythagorean teacher and ascetic from Tyana in Cappadocia, active in the first century CE, who travelled, taught in the cities of the Greek East, acquired a reputation for wisdom and for wonders, and attracted the hostile attention that eventually put a version of him on trial before Domitian. Independent attestation is thin but real: the historian Cassius Dio knows an Apollonius who, at Ephesus, was said to have visioned Domitian’s assassination at the moment it happened in Rome; a collection of letters circulates under Apollonius’s name, of mixed authenticity; a work On Sacrifices is quoted by Eusebius; and the Life itself names earlier, lost accounts — a book by Maximus of Aegae on the sage’s doings in his home city, and a biography by one Moeragenes, which Philostratus dismisses as ignorant. Even the frame dates will not sit still: Philostratus’s internal chronology yields a life from a few years before the common era to the reign of Nerva (96–98 CE) — a span of about a hundred years, itself a hagiographic round number — while modern reference works often print c. 15–c. 100 CE. This article reports both and builds on neither: a long first-century floruit, Tyana, the Pythagorean discipline (five years of silence in youth, abstention from animal food and wine, linen clothing, unshod feet, uncut hair — the Life’s consistent portrait, matching the school’s known uniform since Section III), fame in the cities of Asia Minor and Syria, trouble with authority. That is layer (a), and it is thinner than the eight books suggest — because the eight books are layer (b), entire.

The Damis Problem

Everything turns on Damis. If the notebooks were what Philostratus says — a companion’s day-book, kept across decades of travel from Babylon to Rome — then the Life rests on an eyewitness diary of extraordinary value, and its wonders are, at worst, a disciple’s testimony. If Damis is a literary device — the ancient novel’s found-manuscript trick, as old as the genre — then the Life is a work of the 220s CE wearing a first-century costume. Scholarship has divided on the question for a century, and the division is honestly reported here as unresolved. The sceptical case, associated above all with Ewen Bowie’s work on the Second Sophistic, notes that Damis appears in no source independent of Philostratus, that his narrative convenience is total (he is present at every wonder, absent from every documentable event), and that the found-diary is a recognised fiction-signal in ancient prose. The defence, mounted most fully by Maria Dzielska in Apollonius of Tyana in Legend and History (1986), holds that Philostratus worked from genuine earlier materials, however embroidered, and that the Life’s hard core of local and institutional detail resists explanation as free invention. A middle position — that a real, already-legendary Damis-text existed and Philostratus used it as novelists use sources — has its own advocates. The archive’s discipline is the one this article has used throughout: the Life is cited as Philostratus’s account, dated to the 220s CE, and its episodes are evidence, in the first instance, for what the Severan court’s commissioned sage looked like. Where an episode also preserves something older, the argument has to be made episode by episode — and in Section XII the most famous episodes are given with their Philostratean framing left visible, hedges and all.

// WHY A COURT COMMISSIONS A SAGE //

Layer (c) for the Life as a whole. Julia Domna was a Syrian empress presiding over a court that patronised Greek letters as imperial policy; her circle included the great sophists and the jurists of the Severan age. A Greek holy man from the Syrian borderlands, equally at home in Tyana, Babylon, and Rome, who rebukes tyrants and is vindicated — such a life, elegantly written, was cultural policy in narrative form: Hellenic wisdom, eastern depth, Roman vindication. Philostratus also states his own polemical brief at the outset (1.2–3, paraphrased): Apollonius has been maligned as a mere magician, a goēs, by those who knew only part of the record; the Life will present him as a philosopher and divine man whose wonders flow from wisdom, not sorcery. The distinction between sage and sorcerer is the book’s thesis, and every wonder in it is an exhibit.

The book’s shape follows the thesis. Books 1–3: formation, the Pythagorean vow, and the great eastern journey — Babylon, then India and the Brahmans, the legend’s standard credentialing itinerary (Section VII) now walked in full narrative, with Damis in attendance and the wonders beginning. Books 4–6: the Greek cities, Rome under Nero, Egypt and the naked sages of Ethiopia, Spain — the sage as public moralist, exorcist, and counsellor of governors and emperors-to-be. Books 7–8: arrest, imprisonment, and the trial before Domitian, the Life’s climax and its most deliberate literary construction (Section XIII). The itinerary is the argument: a man at home in every wisdom the empire contained, and at odds only with tyranny.

XII. Apollonius II: The Wonders — Plague, a Girl Restored, and the Ends of the Earth

The wonders of the Life are best read in Philostratus’s own register, which is not the register of a chronicle. He narrates as a sophist: scenes, speeches, and ekphraseis, with the sage’s wisdom displayed in dialogue as often as in miracle. Three episodes, chosen because the brief of this article and the tradition agree on their centrality, will stand for the whole.

The Plague Demon at Ephesus (Life 4.10)

Apollonius had warned Ephesus of a coming plague during an earlier visit, and been ignored (4.4, paraphrased). When the plague raged and no remedy checked it, the city sent a deputation to him at Smyrna, begging him to come as physician of their infirmity. In Conybeare’s Loeb translation (1912), the scene opens:

“And he thought that he ought not to postpone his journey, but said: ‘Let us go.’ And forthwith he was in Ephesus, performing the same feat, I believe, as Pythagoras, who was in Thurii and Metapontum at one and the same moment.” — PHILOSTRATUS, LIFE OF APOLLONIUS 4.10, TRANS. F. C. CONYBEARE (LOEB, 1912)

Note, before the wonder even starts, what Philostratus has done: he has cited Pythagoras’s bilocation as the precedent for his hero’s instantaneous journey — legend quoting legend, stratum leaning on stratum, the connection made by the author himself in the 220s CE. The scene that follows is the Life’s most famous. Apollonius assembles the Ephesians in the theatre, before the image of Heracles the Averter, and points out an old beggar, blind-seeming, ragged, with a wallet and a crust — and orders the crowd to stone him as an enemy of the gods. The crowd recoils from murdering a manifestly miserable stranger; Apollonius insists; the first stones fly; and the beggar’s blinking eyes open full of fire. The Ephesians recognise a demon, and stone him into a cairn. When the stones are removed at the sage’s command, the beggar is gone; in his place lies a hound, Molossian in form, as large as a lion, crushed and foaming (4.10, paraphrased from Conybeare’s translation). The plague, the narrative implies, is ended with its embodied cause.

The episode repays the three-layer reading precisely. As event (layer a), it is unverifiable and unrepeated in any independent source. As literature (layer b), it is a scapegoat rite narrativised — the city’s pollution located in a marginal body and expelled by communal violence, with the sage as the one who can see what the body really is; the story’s horror, which modern readers feel at the stoning of a beggar, is answered inside the story by the revelation that the victim was never human, an answer that tells us more about the story’s moral machinery than about Ephesus. As function (layer c), it is the Life’s thesis in one scene: the sage is not a magician hired to lift a plague but a philosopher whose purified perception reads reality correctly — he alone sees the demon — and whose authority the whole city then ratifies with its hands. Philostratus even leaves the aetiology standing in the landscape: the statue of the Averting Heracles, he says, marks the spot.

The Girl at Rome (Life 4.45)

At Rome, under Nero’s reign in the Life’s chronology, Apollonius meets a funeral: a girl of a consular family, dead — the narrative’s word — on the day of her marriage, her bridegroom following the bier, all Rome mourning with him. Apollonius orders the bier set down, asks the girl’s name, touches her, and whispers something over her in secret; and the girl speaks, and rises, and returns to her father’s house, as Alcestis returned when Heracles rescued her (4.45, paraphrased). The family’s offered reward he redirects as the girl’s dowry. And then Philostratus does the thing that separates him from a mere hagiographer: he hedges, in his own voice. Whether Apollonius detected some spark of life in her that her attendants had missed — it was raining, he notes, and steam rose from her face — or whether he truly rekindled a life that had departed, neither he nor the bystanders, Philostratus says, could clearly understand (paraphrased). The hedge is worth more to the historian than the wonder: it shows a third-century author managing his audience’s scepticism inside the scene, offering the naturalistic exit while keeping the marvellous door open — the exact posture of the whole Life, and a fair emblem of how educated antiquity actually consumed such stories.

The Ends of the Earth

Around these set-pieces the Life arranges the standard furniture of the developed sage-legend, at maximal scale: exorcisms (the empousa bride exposed at Corinth, 4.25); prophecies fulfilled (earthquakes, plagues, the deaths of emperors); the chains that fall from the prisoner’s leg in his cell and are replaced at his own instruction, lest the gaolers be blamed (7.36, paraphrased); discourses with the Brahmans of India, whose chief Iarchas discourses on the soul and on Apollonius’s own previous incarnation; audiences with kings of Babylon and emperors at Rome; and, in Egypt, the respectful cross-examination of the naked sages of Ethiopia, who are permitted — a fine Philostratean touch — to find the visitor’s Greek wisdom good but not complete. Each item can be paralleled in the Pythagoras tradition, and Philostratus knows it: his Apollonius is a self-conscious second Pythagoras, who had himself, the tradition said, written a Life of Pythagoras — the legend citing its own genre as its hero’s reading. For this hub’s purposes the point is structural. By the 220s CE the legendary life had a form, as fixed as a sonnet’s: eastern travels, wonders of healing and perception, a tyrant confronted, a death that will not produce a body. Philostratus did not invent the form. He gave it its most accomplished performance — which is why, when the form was next weaponised, it was his performance that was chosen as the weapon.

XIII. Apollonius III: The Trial Before Domitian — The Sage as Socratic Defendant

Books 7 and 8 of the Life are its climax, and its most openly literary construction: the arrest of Apollonius, his imprisonment at Rome, and his trial before the emperor Domitian (reigned 81–96 CE). The charges, as Philostratus frames them (7.20; 8.3, paraphrased), are the standard indictment of the dangerous holy man, drawn up by the informer Euphrates’ party: that Apollonius is a magician; that he has conspired with the senator Nerva — the future emperor — against Domitian; and, the capital specification, that he sacrificed a boy, cutting his throat to divine from the entrails whether Nerva would reign. Every element is diagnostic. Magic is the charge Roman law and Roman prejudice laid on the wonder-worker who would not stay in his place — the same charge Apuleius of Madauros, the Platonist novelist, had to answer in a real court a generation after Apollonius’s death, in the speech that survives as his Apologia; the man himself, novelist and defendant both, is treated in this hub at Psyche: Apuleius and the Golden Ass. Treason by divination — consulting omens about the emperor’s successor — was the one magical crime the principate prosecuted with genuine terror, because it was indistinguishable from planning the succession. And the child-sacrifice specification is the atrocity-charge that Mediterranean cultures kept in stock for the religious outsider, deployed in turn against Jews, Christians, and inconvenient philosophers.

Philostratus builds the defence as a Socratic echo, and the echo is deliberate to the point of quotation-by-structure. Like Socrates in Plato’s Apology, Apollonius refuses the postures of the ordinary defendant: he will not flatter, will not weep, will not produce his family, treats the court as an occasion for teaching, and answers the charge of impiety with an account of his whole manner of life. Philostratus even composes the speech Apollonius would have delivered at full length (Book 8.7) — a complete Apology on the Platonic model, which the narrative then, with a sophist’s flourish, declines to need. For the trial’s end is the Life’s most famous wonder. Apollonius, having addressed the emperor briefly and defiantly — the burden of his words, in paraphrase, being that no one shall kill him, because it is not his fate — vanishes from the court (8.5). The same day, at the hour of the trial, he appears to the waiting Damis and Demetrius at Dicaearchia (Puteoli), days of travel from Rome, smiling, and bids them keep the matter quiet (8.12, paraphrased). Socrates drank the hemlock and died proving the city wrong by his obedience; Apollonius leaves by the door the city cannot see, proving the emperor powerless by his absence. The difference between the two Apologies is the difference between classical Athens and the imperial third century: the sage no longer argues that the laws are owed obedience unto death. He demonstrates that the tyrant’s court is a theatre, and walks out of the play.

The death, when the Life finally reaches it, refuses to be an ending at all — the genre’s signature, by now familiar from Sections III and X. Philostratus (8.31, paraphrased) collects the versions with a compiler’s candour: some say Apollonius died at Ephesus, attended by two maidens, at an age past a hundred; some, that he ended at Lindos on Rhodes, entering the temple of Athena and vanishing within it; some, that in Crete the end was stranger still — seized by the priests of Dictynna’s temple as a night-prowling robber, he vanished from his bonds, the doors opened of themselves, and a choir of maidens was heard singing him upward, leave the earth, go to heaven — and Philostratus remarks that even after death a youth was said to have seen him, doubting, and been answered. No tomb is produced; the Life ends instead with a posthumous appearance. The stratigraphic reading writes itself: a historical teacher died, somewhere, around the end of the first century; the tradition, needing the third kind of being to exit as the third kind must, supplied the empty tomb and the ascending choir. Pythagoras’s school had invented the pattern. Philostratus perfected it. The next section shows what happened when a later age put the pattern to work.

XIV. Apollonius IV: Afterlives — Hierocles and Eusebius, the Talismans, and Balīnās

The Life had been in the world for some eighty years when it acquired the role that preserved it. Around 303 CE, at the opening of the Diocletianic persecution, Sossianus Hierocles — governor first of Bithynia, later of Egypt, and an architect of the persecution — published a work against the Christians, the Philalēthēs, the “Lover of Truth”, in which the comparison this article has been circling was made explicit and official: Apollonius against Christ. The argument, as it can be reconstructed from its refutation, ran on the wonders: the Christians make a god of a man on the strength of miracles attested by unlettered witnesses, Hierocles urged, while Apollonius’s equal or greater wonders stand in a polished history by a man of letters — and yet the pagans, more soberly, call Apollonius not a god but a man beloved of the gods (paraphrased from Eusebius’s report of the argument). It is the legendary life deployed as comparative religion, with the state behind it.

The rebuttal survives entire, which Hierocles’ book does not: Eusebius of Caesarea’s Against Hierocles (early fourth century CE), a work whose method will be familiar to any reader of this article, because it is stratigraphy turned into polemic. Eusebius does not deny that Philostratus wrote a book; he dismantles the book’s credentials — its romance character, its Damis, its anachronisms, its wonders’ dependence on a single interested source — and concedes, with tactical generosity, the Apollonius who remains when the dismantling is done: a philosopher of genuine parts, perhaps, whom his biographer’s fictions have made useless as a witness to anything but Philostratus. Lactantius mentions the comparison as a going concern of the persecution’s propaganda; Augustine, a century later, still knows Apollonius as the pagans’ exhibit. The debate’s afterlife is longer than either contestant could have wished: Charles Blount’s English translation of the Life’s first two books (1680), with its deist annotations, revived the Hieroclean comparison for the Enlightenment, and the “pagan Christ” reading of Apollonius — which the Life itself, written before Christianity was an imperial factor, does not sustain — has been periodically rediscovered ever since. The stratigraphic correction stands: Philostratus wrote a Pythagorean sage for a Severan empress; Hierocles, eighty years on, conscripted him; the conscription, not the book, is the comparison’s origin.

Meanwhile the sage had acquired a second, stranger afterlife, in stone and bronze. From the fourth century onward, the cities of the Greek East credited Apollonius with talismans — telesmata — public apotropaic devices set up under his authority against the local afflictions: at Byzantium and then Constantinople, against snakes, scorpions, mosquitoes, flooding rivers, and fractious horses; at Antioch, against winds and vermin; the chronicler Malalas, in the sixth century, already retails Apollonius’s talismanic visit to Byzantium as civic history. The objects were still being pointed out a millennium later: a bronze eagle grasping a serpent in the Hippodrome of Constantinople, universally ascribed to Apollonius’s ward against venomous snakes, was destroyed in the sack of 1204. Modern scholarship, following Maria Dzielska’s analysis, treats the talisman-tradition as a distinct stratum — the sage absorbed into the protective furniture of cities that had never read Philostratus — and the absorption has a logic the reader of Sections IV–VII will recognise: the man who in the Life sees the plague-demon no one else can see is the natural patron for a city’s invisible defences. The talisman is the wonder, municipalised.

In Arabic the sage changed his name and his profession together. As Balīnās (also written Balīnūs) — the name a worn-down Arabic form of Apollonius — he becomes the “Master of Talismans”, Ṣāḥib al-ṭilasmāt, and the named author of a library: the Great Book of Talismans, the Book of Stones, and, most consequentially for this archive, the Sirr al-khalīqa, the Secret of Creation, in whose closing pages the Emerald Tablet of Hermes makes its first datable appearance in world literature — presented, in the frame story, as a text Balīnās himself discovered in a vault beneath a statue of Hermes, in the sage’s own home country near Tyana. The hub tells that story in full at Emerald Tablet Roots, and the transmission that carried Balīnās westward into Latin at Transmission; the Hermetic milieu that made a Greek sage into Hermes’s discoverer and heir is treated at Hermetica. What belongs here is the stratigraphic marvel of it: a Cappadocian teacher of the first century, known to us through one commissioned Greek biography of the third, becomes — by way of talismans in Byzantine streets and Hermetic pseudepigraphy in Arabic — the frame-narrator of the most famous alchemical text in the world. No layer of that journey is evidence for the man. Every layer is evidence for the needs of the culture that laid it down: Severan Hellenism needed a sage; persecuting paganism needed a counter-Christ; Byzantine cities needed wards; Arabic Hermetism needed an initiate who had found the Tablet in the earth. The needs are datable. The sage, obligingly, fits them all.

XV. Alexander I: From Court History to Romance — Callisthenes and the First Fictions

Alexander of Macedon (356–323 BCE) is the control experiment of this article, because with him — uniquely — we can measure the legend against a documented life of the first richness. Layer (a) is not thin: the conquest of the Persian Empire between 334 and 323 BCE, the battles (Granicus, Issus, Gaugamela), the foundations (Alexandria above all), the march to the Indus and the army’s refusal at the Hyphasis, the death at Babylon in June 323, are anchored in a source tradition — Arrian, Plutarch, Diodorus, Curtius, Justin — that, however late its surviving forms, descends from eyewitness historians: Ptolemy the general, Aristobulus the engineer, Nearchus the admiral. No figure in this article is less in need of legend. And no figure got more of it. The Alexander legend is therefore the proof that legend is not a product of missing evidence. It is a product of surplus meaning: a life so large that every culture it touched required a version of it sized to its own needs.

The fictionalising began at court, and its first casualty was the court’s own historian. Callisthenes of Olynthus, a kinsman and pupil of Aristotle, accompanied the expedition as its official historian, charged with the Deeds of Alexander that would present the conquest to the Greek world. The fragments and the later tradition agree that his register was panegyrical from the start: the Pamphylian sea, in his account, withdrew before Alexander like a subject making obeisance — a courtier’s miracle, retailed with embarrassment by the later historians who quote it. Callisthenes then fell: implicated, justly or not, in the pages’ conspiracy of 327 BCE, after his public resistance to Alexander’s experiment with proskynēsis, the Persian court prostration, he was arrested and died in custody — executed, imprisoned until death, or dead of disease, the sources characteristically disagree. The historian became the first martyr of the Alexander story, and his name became its first false author: for the Romance that eventually consumed the tradition circulated under his name, as if written by the eyewitness historian of the campaigns. Scholarship therefore calls its anonymous compiler pseudo-Callisthenes — the standing type, in this hub’s usage, of pseudepigraphy as borrowed credibility: the legend files itself under the archive’s most trusted signature.

Between Callisthenes and the Romance stand the first fictions proper, and they are datable to the conqueror’s own generation. Onesicritus, who had sailed with Nearchus and fancied himself the expedition’s philosopher, already retailed the visit of the Amazons’ queen to Alexander’s camp — an episode the soberer tradition, citing the embarrassment of those who were there, rejected in antiquity itself. Cleitarchus (c. 300 BCE), whose history does not survive but whose influence saturates the so-called vulgate tradition, wrote of Alexander “rhetorically and dramatically”, as Cicero’s paraphrased verdict has it — not, the implication runs, truthfully. And around the king’s death there grew the pamphlet literature of the succession wars: a Death of Alexander tradition, complete with poison plots and a last will and testament dividing the empire, that served the propaganda needs of the generals who were tearing the empire apart. The pattern of Section II holds exactly: wonders and fictions accrete fastest where the political need is hottest, and in Alexander’s case the need began before the body was cold — the body itself, hijacked on its way to Macedon and installed at Alexandria by Ptolemy, becoming the first relic of the cult that the legend would serve.

// POTHOS: THE HISTORICAL SEED //

Richard Stoneman’s study of the legend (Alexander the Great: A Life in Legend, 2008) locates its germ in a trait the sober sources themselves attest: Alexander’s pothos — the yearning, as Arrian’s usage presents it, that drove him past every achieved limit toward the Ocean, the ends of the earth, the river no one had crossed. The Romance does not invent its hero’s defining drive; it removes the drive’s earthly limits. Legend, in the maximal case, is biography with the friction taken out.

Egypt’s contribution was the legend’s boldest stroke, and its most functional. In the Romance’s opening, the last native pharaoh, Nectanebo II — who had fled Egypt before the Persian reconquest of 343 BCE — arrives at the Macedonian court in disguise as a magician and astrologer, and by a trick of dream-sending and shape-shifting fathers Alexander on Olympias. The conqueror of Egypt is thus, by birth, Egypt’s own exiled king’s son: the foreign conquest rewritten as a restoration, Alexander naturalised as pharaoh by blood. No sober source knows anything of it; every Egyptian interest is served by it; and it is the perfect type of layer (c) — a conquered culture’s legend-work, performed on the conqueror, in the conqueror’s own language, in (on the usual view) the conqueror’s own Egyptian capital, Alexandria, where the Romance in its compiled form is generally held to have been put together. The hub’s Egyptology division treats the Egyptian Alexander — the temple reliefs that show the Macedonian sacrificing as pharaoh in good form — from the other side; the Romance is what that official iconography became in the hands of storytellers.

XVI. Alexander II: The Romance Itself — Letters, Marvels, and the Most Successful Life Ever Written

The Alexander Romance is not a book in the way the Life of Apollonius is a book. It is a compilation — an anonymous Greek assembly, put together in Alexandria on the usual account, from materials of very different ages: a historical skeleton descending, at whatever remove, from the Cleitarchan tradition; an epistolary novel of the Hellenistic age, built from the fictional correspondence of Alexander with Darius, with Porus, with his mother and his tutor; a pamphlet on the king’s death, with its will; Egyptian story-material of Nectanebo and of the pharaoh Sesonchosis; and free-standing marvel-collections, letters home from the edge of the world. The compilation’s date is debated — the materials are Hellenistic, the assembled Romance is usually placed in the second or third century CE, and it must in any case precede the Latin translation of Julius Valerius (c. 320 CE), through which the West first received it. It survives not in a text but in recensions — the Greek alpha recension and its divergent successors, conventionally lettered, differing in whole episodes — so that “the Romance says” is always shorthand for “a branch of the Romance tradition says”, a qualification this section observes. The standard English version is Richard Stoneman’s Penguin translation, The Greek Alexander Romance (1991), made from the alpha text with the tradition’s variants in view; the account below is paraphrase throughout, following Stoneman’s translation and his scholarship, and no wording is quoted from the Romance itself.

The Letter to Aristotle, and the Literature of the Edge

The Romance’s characteristic device is the letter, and its most influential letter is the one Alexander writes — in the tradition’s fiction — to his old tutor Aristotle, reporting the wonders of India: the monstrous births, the gold-digging ants’ greater cousins, the men with dogs’ heads, the trees that speak, the river of paradise glimpsed and refused. A version of the letter circulated independently, as the Letter of Alexander to Aristotle about India, in Greek and then in a Latin translation that made it one of the most copied texts of the early Middle Ages — the fountainhead of the whole medieval literature of the monstrous races and the marvels of the East. Its function (layer c) is transparent and rather beautiful: the conqueror reports to the philosopher. The man who classified all nature receives, from his own pupil at the world’s end, specimens that break the classification — and the fiction certifies both parties, Alexander as the eye of philosophy at the frontier, Aristotle as the mind to which frontiers report. Companion pieces do the same work in dialogue: the encounter with the Brahmans, the naked philosophers of India under their leader Dandamis, in which the conqueror of the world is cross-examined by men who own nothing and found, in the exchange’s pointed conclusion, to be the poorer party — philosophy’s revenge on power, filed inside power’s own biography, and preserved (in a separate treatise tradition) even by authors who never opened the Romance.

The Diving Bell and the Flying Machine: Accretion Caught in the Act

The brief of stratigraphy can be watched working, in the Romance tradition, on its two most famous machines. Alexander’s descent into the sea — in a glass vessel or barrel, lowered on chains to observe the fishes, in some versions with a cat, a dog, and a cock as a primitive air-supply and alarm system, in others betrayed by the queen who holds the chain — and his ascent into the air, in a basket or cage borne by griffins or great birds lured ever upward by meat held above them on spears, until a voice from heaven turns him back with the lesson that the earth, seen whole, is a small thing in a threshing-floor of sky: these episodes are not part of the earliest recoverable Romance. The flight, in the form the West inherited, enters the tradition late — its earliest descriptions are traced to Byzantine-era Greek transmission, with Talmudic parallels behind it, and both flight and descent flower in the medieval recensions, Latin, French, and Persian, and in the art of a hundred cathedrals and manuscripts. Stoneman’s scholarship treats them as the tradition’s own later growth, and this article follows him: they are given here not as the Romance’s original contents but as its most successful accretions — the proof, inside a single tradition, of Section I’s claim that accretion is datable. The machines also complete the hero’s conquest of the elements in a way the historical campaigns could not: the man who took earth’s kingdoms in fact takes, in legend, the sea’s floor and the sky’s ceiling, and is refused only heaven itself. The pothos of Section XV has become cosmology.

The Translations: History’s Most Successful Legendary Life

The Romance’s diffusion has no parallel among secular books before print. From the Greek recensions it passed into Latin twice over — Julius Valerius in the fourth century, and, decisively for the West, the version made in the tenth century by Leo, archpriest of Naples, from a Greek manuscript he found at Constantinople, whose descendants feed the great medieval compilation called the Historia de preliis and, through it, the Alexander books of every European vernacular: the French Roman d’Alexandre, which gave the alexandrine verse-line its name; the Middle English Kyng Alisaunder; the German, Spanish, Scandinavian, Slavonic, and Irish Alexanders. Eastward it passed into Armenian (fifth century), Syriac (by the seventh century), and from Syriac and Middle Persian into Arabic and Persian, where Alexander — Iskandar — becomes a prophet-king of the Persian national epic, the Shāhnāma of Firdawsī, and the hero-philosopher of Niẓāmī’s Iskandarnāma, and into Ethiopic and Hebrew besides. Ken Dowden’s introduction to the Romance in Collected Ancient Greek Novels (ed. B. P. Reardon, 1989) — paraphrased here — calls it antiquity’s most successful novel, its diffusion, in dozens of versions across more than twenty languages, exceeded only by the Bible’s; Stoneman’s survey reaches the same verdict by the longer road. The historical Alexander founded cities that mostly failed. The legendary Alexander founded a world literature that has never gone out of print. Any account of “Alexander’s influence” that confines itself to the sober sources is, by the numbers, an account of the smaller Alexander.

XVII. Aesop: The Fable-Teller Becomes a Fable

The last life is the smallest, and in some ways the purest. Aesop — Aísōpos — is the name attached to the Greek animal fable itself, and his recoverable core (layer a) can be stated in one paragraph, because our earliest source states it in one breath. Herodotus, writing in the fifth century BCE, mentions him once, in the course of correcting a Greek error about an Egyptian pyramid (2.134). The courtesan Rhodopis, Herodotus says, was no pyramid-builder’s contemporary but a woman of the reign of Amasis; and in A. D. Godley’s Loeb translation, the passage continues:

“She was a Thracian by birth, slave to Iadmon, son of Hephaestopolis, a Samian, and fellowslave of Aesopus the storywriter. For he also was owned by Iadmon; of which the chiefest proof is that when the Delphians, obeying an oracle, issued many proclamations inviting whosoever would to claim the penalty for the killing of Aesopus, none would undertake it but only another Iadmon, grandson of the first. Thus was Aesopus too shown to be the slave of Iadmon.” — HERODOTUS, HISTORIES 2.134, TRANS. A. D. GODLEY (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY)

That is the bedrock, and it should be contemplated before the legend covers it. From Herodotus, at the strength of his evidence, we have: a story-writer (logopoios) named Aesop; a slave, on Samos, of a man named Iadmon; a contemporary, therefore, of Rhodopis and of Amasis’s reign (570–526 BCE); a man killed by the Delphians, in circumstances already requiring an oracle and a public compensation — a blood-price, claimed in the end by his dead master’s grandson, which implies a killing Delphi acknowledged as wrongful. Four facts and an implication. Everything else — the ugliness, the wit, the masters, the travels, the death-scene’s dialogue — is strata.

The strata accumulate fast, and their dates are the familiar ones. By 422 BCE, Aristophanes’ Wasps (lines 1446–1449) can get a laugh from the Delphian ending alone: the Delphians, the joke runs, falsely accused Aesop of stealing a temple cup, and Aesop answered with the fable of the beetle and the eagle — the framed charge and the retaliatory fable already common property within a century and a half of the floruit Herodotus implies. Aristotle, in the Rhetoric (2.20), cites Aesop as a technician of argument, retailing the fable of the fox and the ticks, told — Aristotle says — in a real Samian court case, in defence of a demagogue on trial for his life: the fable as a working rhetorical instrument, and Aesop as a public advocate, a freedman’s career already implied. The fables themselves were first collected, the tradition says, by Demetrius of Phalerum (c. 350–c. 280 BCE), Aristotle’s school’s man at Athens — the collection is lost, the fact of it recorded in Diogenes’ catalogue of Demetrius’s works. Phaedrus versified the fables in Latin in the first century CE; Babrius, in Greek verse, probably in the second. And the life, when it finally arrives as a book, arrives as what by then it had to be: a novel.

The Life of Aesop: The Popular Novel

The anonymous Life of Aesop — also called the Aesop Romance, edited in its earliest recoverable recension (the “G” text, from a tenth-century Morgan Library manuscript) by Ben Edwin Perry in 1952, and composed, on the usual dating, in the first or second century CE, probably in Egypt — opens with the legend’s most quoted sentence-shape: Aesop is a slave of Phrygian origin, and he is, in the tradition’s lingering inventory, the ugliest of all men — pot-bellied, misshapen of head, snub-nosed, swarthy, dwarfish, bandy-legged, and, at the start, unable to speak at all (paraphrased from the Life’s opening chapters). A kindness to a wandering priestess of Isis earns him, from the goddess, the gift of speech and of story. Sold to the slave-dealer, then to Xanthus the philosopher of Samos, he spends the book’s long central panel doing to his master what the fables do to their lions and wolves: out-reasoning him in the market, in the bath, at table, in bed and out of it — the slave whose tongue is the only free thing in the household, purchasing his freedom at last by saving Samos itself, reading an omen for the Samians and answering Croesus’s demand with a fable. Then Babylon, in the expanded tradition, where he serves King Lycurgus as the wise counsellor of romance; then the return; and then Delphi.

The Delphian ending, in the Life and in the parallel tradition Plutarch retails, closes the circle Herodotus opened. Aesop comes to Delphi on Croesus’s business, with gold to distribute; finds the Delphians greedy and grasping; mocks them in fables; withholds the gold and sends it back. The Delphians plant a sacred cup in his baggage, convict him of temple-robbery on the planted evidence, and sentence him to be thrown from the cliff. He answers with fables to the end — the beetle, the frog and the mouse, story piled on story as the executioners wait — and is thrown, and dies cursing the city with the gods’ attention. Plague and famine follow; an oracle orders expiation; the compensation of Herodotus 2.134 is proclaimed, and claimed by Iadmon’s grandson. (Paraphrased throughout from the Life’s closing chapters and Plutarch’s On the Delays of Divine Vengeance.) The construction is transparent, and its transparency is its meaning. The teller of tales about the weak outwitting the strong is given a life that is one of his tales — the deformed slave out-talking philosophers and kings — and a death that is one of his tales too: the powerful, unable to answer the story, kill the storyteller, and are punished by the moral order the stories assert. The fable-teller has become a fable, complete with the moral appended by events. Layer (c) needs no excavation here: the Life is the ancient popular audience’s own theory of what a fabulist’s life must have been, written in the only genre adequate to it — his.

THE UGLINESS

No source before the Life describes Aesop’s body. Plutarch, who sets him dining wittily among the Seven Sages, jokes about his slavery and never about his face. The spectacular ugliness is the novel’s invention, and it is functional: the fable’s whole economy — wisdom in the despised vessel, the slave’s mind against the master’s status — required a body the audience could not mistake for authority. Later ages’ speculation on the name (Aísōpos glossed as “the Ethiopian”) and the tradition’s African Aesops descend from this stratum, and are reported here as its descendants, not as evidence about the sixth century.

THE TWO MASTERS

Herodotus knows Iadmon. The later tradition adds Xanthus the philosopher, the Life’s great comic butt, and reconciles the pair by making Aesop sold from one to the other. The reconciliation is typical legend-work: two strata, two names, one harmonising sale. Where a life presents two of everything — two masters, two deaths, two tombs — the stratigrapher reaches for the dating tools first.

PERRY’S SCEPTICISM

Ben Edwin Perry, the fables’ great modern editor, concluded in 1965 that Aesop’s traditional associations with Croesus and the Seven Sages cannot be chronologically sustained and must be reckoned literary fiction — a verdict this article extends, in form, to the whole post-Herodotean dossier, while noting that Perry’s scepticism stops where it should: at the four Herodotean facts, which no chronological problem touches.

WHY AESOP LASTS

The fables detached from the man so completely that “Aesop” became a genre-label — any beast-fable with a moral is Aesop’s, whoever wrote it. The legend performed the same detachment in reverse, manufacturing a man adequate to the corpus. Of the five lives in this article, Aesop’s is the only one in which the works demonstrably precede the life: first the stories, then — centuries of telling later — the storyteller.

XVIII. What Legend Is Evidence For: The Genre’s Lesson for This Archive

Five lives, five stratigraphies, one pattern — and the pattern, stated once more with the evidence now behind it, is this article’s conclusion. A legend is evidence. It is not evidence for the wonders it reports. No stratum of the Pythagoras tradition, read at its own date, certifies a golden thigh in the sixth century BCE; no stratum of the Empedocles tradition certifies a leap, a vanishing, or a shoe; Philostratus certifies no plague-demon; the Romance certifies no griffins; the Life of Aesop certifies no talking cure at Delphi. It is evidence — first-class, datable, quotable evidence — for what the tellers needed the sage to be. The Crotoniate school needed a founder of the third kind, and Aristotle’s fragment preserves its need in the act of supplying it. Empedocles’ readers needed a verdict on a man who had called himself a god, and Heraclides, Hippobotus, and Timaeus preserve the voting. The Severan court needed a Greek holy man for an imperial age, and Philostratus’s eight books are the need, bound. Persecuting paganism needed a counter-Christ and Byzantine streets needed wards and Arabic Hermetism needed a finder for the Tablet, and Apollonius was reissued for each. Egypt needed its conqueror to be its own king’s son, and the Romance opens with the adoption papers. The Greek popular audience needed the fable-teller’s life to be a fable, and the Life of Aesop is that need with a plot. Needs are historical facts of the hardest kind — they move courts, budgets, armies of copyists, and centuries of readers — and legend is their archive.

This is the discipline that governs how this hub reads every wonder-working name in its second wing as well, and the reader is invited to hold the wing to it. Maria the Jewess, the apparatus tradition’s foundress, is known only through quotation and attribution — Zōsimos cites her as an ancient authority for the tribikos and the kērotakis — and the hub’s article on her, Maria the Jewess, accordingly separates the attested attributions from the unattested woman, exactly as Sections III–VII separate the Crotoniate school from its founder’s thigh of gold. Cleopatra the Alchemist, author-figure of the Chrysopoeia with its famous drawing and its ouroboros, stands in the same posture as pseudo-Callisthenes: a name of maximum prestige — a queen’s, an eyewitness historian’s — under which real workshop knowledge filed itself for authority, treated in Greek Alchemy: Origins. The Antediluvian King Lists of the Before Kemet hub perform the same analysis on reigns of 36,000 years; the Zōsimos of the Egyptology division shows the one case in this archive where a wonder-worker of the tradition left enough of his own working text to quarried directly, visions and all. In every instance the rule is the rule of Section II: date the stratum, name the teller, ask what the telling was for — and the wonder, without being believed or debunked, becomes evidence.

Myth gives the gods a past. Legend gives the past a person — and then, century by century, gives the person back to everyone who needed him. The historian’s task is not to take the person away again. It is to read the gift receipts.

A closing word for the five themselves, since an archive that files its subjects under strata owes them a sentence unstratified. Pythagoras founded something that outlasted every wonder told about it: a way of examining a life nightly, in common, under rule. Empedocles wrote the verses that made his legend necessary, and they are still better than it. Apollonius, whoever precisely he was, frightened an emperor’s court enough — in fact or in the third century’s considered judgement of what such a man must have done — to be worth eight books and an empress’s commission. Alexander changed the map so fast that the map’s inhabitants spent two thousand years rewriting him as their own. And Aesop, the slave with four facts to his name, achieved the strangest immortality of the five: his stories are told in every schoolroom on earth, by people who could not say one true sentence about him — which is, when the strata are all laid out, exactly the immortality his legend describes: the voice surviving, the body — pot-bellied, bandy-legged, invented — nowhere to be found. The genre’s lesson and the genre’s justice are the same.
