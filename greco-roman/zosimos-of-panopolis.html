I. Two Articles, One Alchemist: The Division of This Archive

This archive treats Zōsimos of Panopolis twice, deliberately, and the reader is owed a plain statement of the division before anything else. The companion article, in the Egyptology division — Zosimos of Panopolis — is the biographical and practical profile. It covers his name and his city, his life and his addressee Theosebeia, the survey of the Cheirokmēta corpus, the Syriac and Arabic transmissions, the workshop world of the Leyden and Stockholm papyri, the recipes and what happens when they are repeated, the kerotakis and the tribikos step by step, the attribution of that apparatus to Maria, and the manuscript drawings. None of that ground is re-covered here. A reader who wants the man, the city, or the still should go there first, and this article will send them back at every point where the two subjects touch.

This article's ground is narrower and, in its own way, stranger. It treats Zōsimos as a Greek author: a writer of Greek prose, working in the Greek genre of the commentary, preserved in Greek codices, and read for a thousand years by Greek readers who cited him the way philosophers cite Plato. Its subjects are the Greek manuscript tradition and the modern editions built on it; the place Zōsimos holds in the commentary tradition that runs through Synesius, Olympiodorus, and Stephanus of Alexandria; the dream treatises known as the Visions, read closely as Greek allegorical text; his doctrine stated in its own Greek philosophical vocabulary — sōma and pneuma, the divine water, the tincture; and the religious landscape of his century, in which a workshop art and a philosophy of salvation occupied the same page. Where the companion article asks what Zōsimos did, this one asks what he wrote, and what his writing did to the readers who inherited it.

One convention governs the whole article and should be stated at the threshold. No wording from the Visions is placed in quotation marks in this article. The dream treatises are narrated throughout in labelled paraphrase, following the standard scholarly accounts — above all Howard M. Jackson's edition and translation of the Omega tractate and his work on the other treatises, Michèle Mertens' critical edition of the Authentic Memoirs, and the recent Oxford text, translation, and commentary on the Visions. The reason is the archive's standing rule: an ancient text is quoted only from a named published translation verified in the preparation of the article, and the Visions' English transmission — scattered across Berthelot and Ruelle's French, F. Sherwood Taylor's pioneering study, and the translation embedded in Jung's essay — does not offer a single rendering that this article can responsibly present as the text. Paraphrase, honestly labelled, loses the music and keeps the facts. The Sources section (XVI) names every rendering a reader may consult to recover the music for themselves.

A note on names and spelling, since this article's subject is a text and its words matter. The author is Zōsimos throughout, with the long vowel marked, following the transliteration practice of the scholarship this article rests on. Greek technical terms are transliterated consistently and glossed at first use: sōma (σῶμα), body; pneuma (πνεῦμα), spirit or breath; baphē (βαφή), a dyeing, hence a tincture; theion hydōr (θεῖον ὕδωρ), the divine — and sulphurous — water of Section XI. His floruit is given as c. 300 CE, the consensus bracket established by Mertens and refined by Jackson: he must postdate the authorities of the third century whom he cites, and he must predate the destruction of the Alexandrian Serapeum in 391 CE, which the Omega tractate mentions in terms that imply it still stood. Nothing in this article requires a more precise date, and none is pretended.

II. A Greek Author: Zōsimos the Commentator

To call Zōsimos a Greek author is not a remark about his passport. Panopolis was an Egyptian city, and the companion article treats its Egyptian depth in full. It is a remark about his genre. Zōsimos writes in the forms that Greek intellectual life had perfected over six centuries: the letter that is really a treatise, the memoir that is really a commentary, and above all the exegesis — the patient unfolding of what an ancient authority meant by words that have gone dark. His predecessors in the art, so far as they survive, wrote recipes framed as revelations. Zōsimos writes about those recipes: he quotes Democritus and glosses him, cites Maria and explains her, weighs Hermes against Agathodaimon, and corrects the readings of his contemporaries by appeal to the canon. The scholarship's sharpest formulation of the point — that Zōsimos is the first known commentator of alchemy — is also the key to his Greekness. Commentary is the characteristic literary act of the later Greek world, the form in which Alexandrian scholarship preserved Homer, in which the Neoplatonists preserved Plato and Aristotle, and in which, from Zōsimos onward, the alchemists preserved one another.

That placement has a consequence readers of the companion article should carry into this one. When later Greek writers cite Zōsimos, they do not cite him as a recipe-book. They cite him as an authority to be interpreted — which is to say, they treat him exactly as he treated Democritus. The commentary tradition is a chain of such treatments, and Section V follows Zōsimos's own link in it link by link. But the chain begins with his self-fashioning. A writer who presents his work as exegesis of the ancients is claiming two things at once: that the ancients are canonical, and that he is their authorised reader. Both claims were accepted. Within two centuries the exegetes were expounding Zōsimos's sentences with the same reverent ingenuity he had spent on the sentences of the Democritean books, and the Byzantine compilers who assembled the alchemical corpus placed his memoirs near its head — the position of a classic.

// COMMENTARY AS A GENRE //

A Greek commentary (hypomnēma) does not summarise its author; it proceeds lemma by lemma, quoting a phrase and then unfolding it — philologically, philosophically, and, in the alchemical case, operationally. Zōsimos's memoirs keep that shape even when their subject is a furnace: a sentence of Maria or Democritus is set down, and the memoir is the unfolding. His successors return the compliment in kind.

His Greek is the learned Greek of late-antique Egypt — the koinē of the schools, dense with the technical vocabulary of the workshops and increasingly coloured by the religious vocabularies of his milieu: Hermetic, Gnostic, and biblical turns of phrase all surface in his prose without any one of them owning it. That mixture is itself a datum of the textual tradition. Byzantine copyists, excerptors, and commentators were not neutral conduits for such a text: they abbreviated what bored them, expanded what puzzled them, and occasionally Christianised what scandalised them, and the modern editor's first task — Mertens' task, in the edition Section IV describes — is to distinguish the Panopolitan's voice from the chorus of his transmitters. The Zōsimos of this article is therefore, of necessity, the Zōsimos of the critical editions: a fourth-century author reconstructed from tenth- to fifteenth-century books by twentieth-century philology. The reconstruction is good. But the article never lets the reader forget that it is a reconstruction, because the distance between the dreamer of c. 300 and the codices of c. 1000 is precisely this article's subject.

One further Greek frame deserves naming here, because Sections X to XII depend on it. Zōsimos writes as a philosopher, and says so in the tradition's own terms: the practitioners of the art call themselves philosophers, and the art itself is styled the divine and sacred art. His matter-theory is not a workshop pidgin but a provincial dialect of the common philosophical language of his age — the language of body and spirit, of natures that act and are acted upon, of a substrate that receives form — which Plato's Timaeus, Aristotle's Meteorologica, and the Stoic physics of pneuma had made available to any educated Greek writer. When Olympiodorus, two centuries later, sets Zōsimos's authorities in a doxographical table beside Thales, Heraclitus, and the other early physicists (Section V), he is not flattering the alchemists. He is filing them where, by their own vocabulary, they had asked to be filed: among the investigators of nature, whose first principles were the business of philosophy.

III. The Greek Manuscripts: Marcianus Graecus 299 and the Codex Tradition

Every word of Zōsimos read in this article descends through a small family of Byzantine codices, and the family has a head. Marcianus graecus 299 — shelfmark M in the editions — is a parchment codex of the late tenth or early eleventh century, now in the Biblioteca Marciana in Venice, and by common consent the oldest and most important surviving manuscript of Greek alchemy. Mertens' study of the Byzantine reception of the art begins from it for a reason: M is not merely the earliest witness to the corpus, it is the most beautiful — its opening title set in a pylē, a magnificently decorated frame painted in four colours, its pages carrying the lavish illustrations for which the codex is famous. It is also a damaged book. It lost several quires in its long life, and some of the quires that remain were bound in the wrong order by a binder who did not know what he was handling. The oldest Zōsimos we possess is thus a splendid, mutilated, misbound Constantinopolitan anthology, copied some seven centuries after its author's death — a fact that should temper every sentence ever written about what Zōsimos “said”, including the sentences of this article.

The corpus M transmits was not Zōsimos's creation. The Greek alchemical corpus — some forty authors, from the pseudo-Democritean books of the first century to the Byzantine systematists of the seventh — was probably first assembled at Constantinople in the seventh or eighth century, when an unknown compiler (or a succession of them) gathered the art's classics into a single library between covers. Zōsimos stands near the centre of that library, and the Visions have a precise address within it: the dream treatises survive in more than forty manuscripts in total, but M preserves only the first of the three lessons, and the first complete copy of the Visions is the latest of the great witnesses — a point to which the textual history of Section VI returns. The hub's transmission article treats the codex tradition of the hub's texts as a whole; this section confines itself to what the Zōsimos reader needs in hand.

Three features of this tradition shape everything downstream, and each will matter when the Visions are read in Sections VII and VIII. First, excerpting: the Byzantine transmitters did not only copy Zōsimos, they abridged him, and Mertens' introduction is blunt that parts of the corpus under his name are more or less summarised extracts owed to Byzantine compilers — the author's connected argument replaced by a digest of its striking sentences. Second, dislocation: in the Authentic Memoirs as transmitted, the three sections of the work known as On Excellence — the Visions — are interrupted by shorter chapters on chemical procedures that are clearly not part of it, so that the dream treatise reaches us already dismembered by its own manuscript tradition, a circumstance Section VI takes up. Third, illustration: the codices carry the apparatus diagrams and the famous single leaf of Cleopatra's Chrysopoeia (Marcianus graecus 299, folio 188v), so that the Greek tradition transmits Zōsimos's world in pictures as well as words — the companion article treats the drawings and their limits, and this article simply notes that the pictures, like the words, are Byzantine copies of lost originals, not photographs of the fourth century.

How did such a book survive at all? The Marcianus reached Venice among the Greek manuscripts gathered by Cardinal Bessarion (1403–1472), the Byzantine scholar-cardinal who spent his last decades rescuing Greek books in the shadow of Constantinople's fall in 1453 and bequeathed his library to the Venetian Republic — the founding collection of the Marciana. The alchemical codex was part of that rescue. There is a fittingness the archive notes without labouring: a corpus assembled in Constantinople, copied in Constantinople, and carried west by a Constantinopolitan exile is the material form of the Greek tradition itself — continuous, endangered, and dependent at every stage on individual readers who thought the old art worth the parchment. The transmission article follows that thread through the hub's other texts; for Zōsimos, the thread runs M, B, A, and L, and then — in 1887 — into print.

IV. The Modern Text: From Berthelot and Ruelle to Mertens

The modern Zōsimos begins with a chemist. Marcellin Berthelot, the great French thermochemist, turned in the 1880s to the Greek manuscripts in Paris and Venice with a historian's question — what did the ancients actually know about matter? — and, with the philologist Charles-Émile Ruelle, produced the Collection des anciens alchimistes grecs (Paris, 1887–88): three volumes of Greek text with French translation that made the corpus generally readable for the first time. The Collection remains, more than a century on, the only edition of most of the corpus's treatises, and every later worker — Taylor, Jung, Jackson, Mertens — stands on it. It is also, by the standards Mertens would later set, an inclusive edition: Berthelot and Ruelle printed under Zōsimos's name much that travelled under it in the manuscripts, without the sustained attempt to separate the Panopolitan's authentic work from the extracts, summaries, and false attributions that a millennium of copying had attached to it. The reader of the Collection meets a Zōsimos larger, looser, and in places later than the historical author. That is not a reproach; it is the normal first state of a recovered literature, and the century of scholarship since has been the slow correction of it.

The correction, for Zōsimos, is Michèle Mertens' Les alchimistes grecs, Tome IV.1: Zosime de Panopolis: Mémoires authentiques (Paris: Les Belles Lettres, 1995) — Greek text, French translation, and an introduction of over a hundred pages that remains the foundation of all modern work on the author. Mertens sorted the manuscript jumble into four groups of works attributable to Zōsimos with certainty: the Authentic Memoirs; the Chapters to Eusebia; the Chapters to Theodorus; and the group formed by the Book of Sophe and the Final Account. The companion article sets out the four groups and their contents in full; for this article's purposes the load-bearing group is the first. The Authentic Memoirs — thirteen short treatises, also transmitted under the title On Apparatus and Furnaces — open with the programmatic On the Letter Omega and contain, as their tenth to twelfth memoirs, the three dream treatises known as the Visions. That single paragraph of orientation is all the corpus survey this article permits itself; the detail lives in the companion article, and the cross-link, not repetition, is the archive's method.

Alongside Mertens stands the one full English monograph-edition devoted to a single Zōsimos tractate: Howard M. Jackson, Zosimos of Panopolis on the Virgin: Zosimus' Tractate “On the Letter Omega” (Missoula: Scholars Press, 1978; Texts and Translations 14, Graeco-Roman Religion 5) — Greek text, English translation, and commentary on the tractate that stands at the head of the Authentic Memoirs, together with Jackson's studies of the author's other treatises. Jackson's dating argument (pp. 4–5 of that edition) is the one this article's header follows: the tractate's mention of Julius Africanus, who died in 240, and its reference to the Serapeum of Alexandria, destroyed in 391, set the outer limits within which Mertens' floruit of about 300 CE sits. Jackson also bequeathed the field a caution that Section XIV will use: his reading of the Omega tractate's “propitious tinctures” as a doctrine of astrologically favourable times was challenged by Daniel Stolzenberg, who argued that Zōsimos's point runs the other way — the genuine tinctures are precisely those not dependent on times, demons, or fate. The disagreement is recorded here at the point where the edition is introduced, because it is the model of how this article handles its sources: the modern authorities are named, their readings are attributed to them, and where they disagree, the disagreement — not a silent choice between them — is what the reader is given.

// HOW TO CITE ZŌSIMOS //

Modern scholarship cites the Authentic Memoirs by Mertens' memoir numbers (the Visions are Memoirs X–XII) and the older literature by the volume and page of Berthelot and Ruelle's Collection (the Visions stand in its third volume). Jung's essay cites the Berthelot text throughout. A reference that gives neither edition is, in this field, a reference that cannot be checked — the archive treats such references, wherever it meets them, as paraphrase at best.

The newest layer of the textual basis deserves its sentence. The Visions received a full modern text, translation, and commentary in Joshua Werrett's Oxford doctoral thesis of 2024, The Visions of Zosimos of Panopolis, which reads the three dreams in their scientific, religious, and literary contexts together and argues — against both the purely psychological and the purely technical readings — for a real dream worked up with a poet's freedom. This article follows the Oxford commentary's narrative of the visions in Sections VII and VIII, in paraphrase, alongside Mertens and Jackson. The textual tradition of Zōsimos is thus still an open workshop: a corpus assembled in the eighth century, copied in the eleventh, printed in the nineteenth, delimited in the twentieth, and re-edited in the twenty-first. The Greek tradition did not end at Constantinople. It changed its address.

V. The Commentators Read Zōsimos: Synesius, Olympiodorus, Stephanus

Greek alchemy's third age — after the founders of the first centuries and the systematiser of c. 300 — is the age of the commentators, and its shape is the strongest evidence for the claim of Section II: that Zōsimos had become a classic. Cristina Viano's standard periodisation, followed in the handbooks, names the succession plainly. The commentators are a generation tied to the Neoplatonic schools, whose writings were designed primarily to clarify the ideas of the previous generations — Democritus and Zōsimos above all — and whose work represents, in Viano's judgment, the most advanced stage of ancient alchemical theory. They do not bring new recipes. They bring exegesis: the conviction that the old texts contain a coherent philosophy of nature, and that the commentator's task is to draw it out. Zōsimos is both their model for that conviction — he had practised it on Democritus — and its principal object.

Synesius: The Dialogue with Dioscorus

The succession opens in the fourth century with the commentary transmitted under the name Synesius: a dialogue addressed to Dioscorus, a priest of Serapis at Alexandria, expounding the Physika kai mystika of pseudo-Democritus. Two cautions attach to the name, and the scholarship states both. The alchemical Synesius is probably not Synesius of Cyrene, the Neoplatonist bishop of Ptolemais, though the attribution may have been intended to borrow his lustre; and the dialogue's value for this article lies less in its authorship than in its posture. Its author declares his exegetic intention openly — to disclose Democritus's thought, the sequence of his teachings, and his processes and materials — and in doing so he works downstream of Zōsimos in two senses: chronologically, since the relative chronology established by the citations puts Zōsimos first, Synesius after him, and Olympiodorus after both; and methodologically, since the commentary he writes is the genre Zōsimos founded for the art. He also preserves, in quotation, fragments of the older literature — pseudo-Democritus among them — that would otherwise be lost, which is the commentator's second service to the tradition: he is an ark as well as an interpreter. The hub's Greek alchemy origins article treats the Democritean books themselves; here the point is the relay — Democritus read by Zōsimos, Zōsimos's method inherited by Synesius, Synesius read verbatim by Olympiodorus, and all three read by Stephanus.

Olympiodorus: Commentary as Doxography

In the sixth century, Olympiodorus produced the tradition's most ambitious surviving commentary: an exegesis of a lost treatise of Zōsimos, the Kat' energeian — On Action, or According to Action — together with sayings of Hermes and the other philosophers, transmitted under the title On the Sacred Art. Whether the alchemical Olympiodorus is identical with Olympiodorus the Younger, the Alexandrian Neoplatonist who commented on Plato and Aristotle, is a genuinely open question — Cristina Viano's study of his Aristotle commentary sets out the case for identity, and the handbooks record it as possible rather than proven — but the commentary's character is not in doubt. Its centrepiece is a doxography: a systematic comparison in which the first principles (archai) of nine early Greek physicists — Thales' water, Heraclitus' fire, the air of Diogenes of Apollonia, and their fellows — are set beside the principles of the alchemical art, and Hermes, Chymes, Agathodaimon, and Zōsimos himself are enrolled among the art's founding physicists. The gesture deserves a moment's attention. A doxography is how Greek philosophy wrote its own history: to give a school a doxography was to declare it a philosophy with a past. Olympiodorus, commenting on Zōsimos, writes alchemy into the succession of Greek natural philosophy at exactly the point where the Neoplatonic schools were writing that succession for themselves. Olivier Dufault's study of transmutation theory in the corpus adds the necessary cold water: of the three authors he examines — Zōsimos, Synesius, Olympiodorus — only Olympiodorus's commentary articulates something approaching a general theory of transmutation, and the tidy “theory of the art” that later centuries attributed to the Greek alchemists wholesale is largely the commentators' construction, projected backward onto founders who worked, as Zōsimos did, by analogy, aphorism, and dream. The construction is not a fraud. It is what commentary is — and Zōsimos, who had done the same service for Democritus, could hardly have complained at receiving it.

Stephanus of Alexandria: The Nine Lectures

The chain reaches its Byzantine culmination in Stephanus of Alexandria, the universal philosopher and public professor who lectured at Constantinople under the emperor Heraclius (r. 610–641) on Plato, Aristotle, geometry, arithmetic, astronomy, and music — and on alchemy. His alchemical work consists of nine Praxeis, lectures on the making of gold, together with a letter to Theodore interpolated among them; F. Sherwood Taylor's translation and commentary (published in Ambix in the 1930s) remains the standard English point of entry, and Taylor's introduction records both the attribution debate — some scholars held the alchemical lectures pseudepigraphal, or as late as the ninth century — and the evidence that settles the lectures' place in the chain: Stephanus quotes Olympiodorus and Synesius verbatim and comments on pseudo-Democritus, so that the whole succession stands inside his text in order. In Stephanus the commentary tradition becomes a curriculum. The lectures are addressed, ultimately, to an emperor; they open with prayer; they move through the authorities in sequence; and they treat the art's theory as a branch of the same philosophy the professor taught in the morning on Aristotle. Zōsimos's memoirs, written three centuries earlier as letters to a patroness in Upper Egypt, have become — by the ordinary Greek machinery of citation, commentary, and lecture — a set text of the imperial capital. That transformation, and not any single doctrine, is the Greek tradition's deepest fact about him.

THE ANONYMOUS SUCCESSORS

After Stephanus the chain continues anonymously — the Christian philosopher, the Anonymous Philosopher, and the Byzantine excerptors who cite Stephanus as already ancient — until the compilers fold the whole succession into the corpus the Marcianus preserves. Citation has become custody.

A last observation closes the section, because it disciplines the ones that follow. The commentators cite Zōsimos constantly and quote him selectively: what they needed from him was authority for doctrine — for the unity of matter, for the reading of Democritus, for the dignity of the art — and not the dream treatises, which they largely leave alone. The Visions survived not because the commentary tradition cherished them but because the corpus compilers copied whole books rather than excerpts alone. Their modern fame is therefore doubly accidental: preserved by the anthologists of the eighth century, completed only in the codex of 1478, printed by Berthelot, and made famous in 1937 by a psychologist. The next four sections read them as what they are in the Greek text — the visionary core of the Authentic Memoirs — and keep their strange afterlife, Jung included, in its labelled place.

VI. The Visions as Greek Text: On Virtue and the Dream Treatises

The Visions are three short dream narratives transmitted as Memoirs X, XI, and XII of the Authentic Memoirs in Mertens' numbering, under the title Peri aretēs — On Excellence, or On Virtue — and known in the older literature also as On the Composition of the Waters, from the technical discourse that frames them. Their position in the memoir sequence is itself an editorial fact worth pausing on: as the text reaches us, the three sections of On Excellence are interrupted by shorter chapters on chemical procedures that are clearly not part of the work, so that the Byzantine transmission delivers the dream treatise in pieces, separated by recipes. Whether that dislocation happened in the corpus's assembly or in later copying, its effect on readers has been real: for most of their history the Visions were encountered as fragments embedded in a technical anthology, and the modern editions earn their keep partly by reassembling them into the single, carefully structured work — discourse, dream, waking interpretation, dream again — that their internal cross-references show them to be.

Their textual condition is the second fact. The Visions survive in more than forty manuscripts — a wide tradition by the standards of Greek technical literature — but the tradition is lopsided: the oldest witness, Marcianus graecus 299, preserves only the first lesson, and the first complete copy is Parisinus graecus 2327, copied in Crete in 1478. The text of the second and third dreams therefore rests on witnesses a full millennium younger than their author, and every narrative detail in Sections VII and VIII is given as the standard accounts — Mertens, Jackson's studies, the Oxford commentary, and the translation tradition behind Jung's essay — agree in reporting it. Where the accounts diverge in detail, this article follows the majority report and says nothing it cannot ground. That is the philological price of reading these texts at all, and it is cheaper than the alternative, which is not reading them.

// DREAM OR LITERATURE? //

Whether Zōsimos actually dreamed the Visions has been debated since Taylor noted their strongly oneiric character in 1937. Jung held that the vision might well have been an actual happening. The Oxford commentary's position — a real dream, worked up with a poet's freedom — is the current scholarly resting point. The question matters for biography; for the reading of the text, what matters is that Zōsimos presents the dreams as dreams, interprets them inside the narrative, and builds doctrine on the interpretation.

The frame matters as much as the dreams. On Excellence does not begin with the altar. It begins with a discourse — the most quoted sentences Zōsimos ever wrote, given here in paraphrase — on the composition of the waters: the movement and growth of things, their embodying and disembodying, the drawing of spirit out of body and the bonding of spirit within body, all of it expressly said to proceed not from foreign natures imported from outside, but from a single nature acting upon itself, one kind containing both the hard shells of minerals and the moist pulp of plants. Only then, in the treatise's famous transition — in paraphrase: as I was saying these things, I fell asleep — does the dream begin. The structure is the doctrine. The discourse states the theory of pneuma in the abstract; the dream then shows it, in persons; and the dreamer's own waking gloss, which closes the first lesson, identifies the vision with the discourse — he asks himself, in paraphrase, whether what he has seen is not precisely the composition of the waters, and concludes that he has understood rightly. Greek allegory rarely announces its own key so openly. Zōsimos hands the reader the key, turns it himself, and then dreams again — as if to say that the understanding, once won, must be tested against further visions. Sections VII and VIII follow that sequence exactly.

A word on the title's key term before the narrative begins. Aretē — excellence, virtue — is in this text a technical as well as a moral word: it names the perfected state toward which both the metal and the dreamer are travelling, the condition of a thing that has become fully what its nature fits it to be. The men in the visions are repeatedly described, in paraphrase, as seeking to attain excellence and becoming spirits in the attempt. Keeping the double sense in view — the excellence of a tincture that has taken perfectly, and the excellence of a soul that has been perfected — is the whole art of reading these three short texts, and the sections that follow will not separate the senses, because Zōsimos does not.

VII. The First Vision: Ion on the Bowl-Shaped Altar

What follows is paraphrase throughout, following the standard accounts named in Section I. No sentence in this section or the next is a quotation.

Zōsimos falls asleep over his own discourse and sees a sacrificer standing high before him on an altar shaped like a bowl — a phialē, the shallow libation bowl of Greek cult, enlarged to the scale of a sanctuary. Fifteen steps lead up to the altar. A voice comes from the figure on it, announcing, in paraphrase, that he has accomplished the descent of the fifteen steps into darkness and the ascent of the fifteen steps into light; that the one who renews him is the sacrificer, who casts away the grossness of the body; and that, sanctified as a priest by compelling necessity, he now stands perfected as a spirit. Zōsimos, holding the figure to conversation, asks who he is. The answer, in a thin or fine voice, names the first of the Visions' immortal persons: he is Ion, priest of the inner sanctuaries, and he submits himself to an unendurable torment.

The torment is then narrated, and its narration is the most famous passage in Greek alchemy. In paraphrase: one came in haste at early morning, overpowered Ion, pierced him through with a sword, and dismembered him in accordance with the rule of harmony; drew off the skin of his head with the sword; mingled the bones with the pieces of flesh; and burned the mingled remains on the fire of the art — until Ion, through the transformation of the body, perceived that he had become spirit. Every element of the sequence is load-bearing. The dismemberment is ordered — performed according to a rule of harmony, not in frenzy — as the analysis of a substance is ordered: a taking-apart into constituents that preserves what the whole contained. The mingling of bone and flesh reverses the separation even as it completes it, as the separated fractions of a work are recombined. The fire is expressly the fire of the art — the furnace's fire, named in the dream's own register. And the outcome is stated in the doctrine's exact vocabulary: not resurrection of the body, but transformation into spirit — the pneuma drawn out of the sōma and standing, perfected, on the altar. Ion is sacrificer and sacrifice in one person: the voice that announces the renewal is the voice of the one renewed, and the priest who performs the rite is the material the rite is performed on. Section IX returns to what readers have made of that identity; here it is enough to see that the text insists on it.

The vision does not end in triumph. As Ion speaks, and as Zōsimos holds him by force to keep him talking, the figure's eyes become as blood; he spews forth all his own flesh; and before the dreamer's eyes he changes into the opposite of himself — into a mutilated anthrōparion, a little man, a manikin — tearing at his own flesh with his own teeth. The sequence refuses any clean allegorical settling. The perfected spirit of the altar's top step is also this: a diminished, self-consuming remnant, produced by the same process that produced the priest. Readers who know the workshop will recognise the double outcome without any prompting from the text — a work that yields both the tincture and the dross, the volatile spirit that rises and the mutilated residue that remains in the vessel, gnawing at itself. Zōsimos offers no gloss on the anthrōparion within the first lesson, and the scholarly tradition has respected the omission more often than the popular one has: Jung's famous identification of the figure as the first homunculus of alchemical literature is presented in Section IX as what it is — Jung's interpretation, of great influence and no textual compulsion.

Zōsimos wakes. The waking is part of the text, and its content is the interpretation: he asks himself, in paraphrase, whether this is not the composition of the waters — the bubbling water, white and yellow, the divine water of Section XI — and concludes that he has understood rightly, and falls asleep again. Two features of that gloss deserve the reader's full attention. First, the dreamer interprets the dream technically: his first thought is not of gods or omens but of the treatise he was writing when he slept — the vision is filed, by its own author, under the composition of the waters. Second, the interpretation does not close the matter. He sleeps again, and the altar is still there. The Visions' structure — discourse, dream, gloss, dream, gloss — is the rhythm of the work itself as Zōsimos conceives it: operation, result, reading of the result, further operation. The altar, like the vessel, is returned to until the colour changes.

THE BOWL ALTAR (PHIALĒ)

A libation bowl become a sanctuary: the vessel-shape of Greek offering, scaled up until a man can stand on it and men can be boiled in it. The altar is the kratēr of the Hermetic tradition (Section XIV) and the crucible of the workshop at once — the text sustains both registers without choosing.

THE FIFTEEN STEPS

Descended into darkness, ascended into light — by the same figure, in the same speech. Mertens read in the descent and ascent the rising and falling of vapours in the reflux apparatus; the religious reading, an initiatory descent and return, is set beside it in Section IX. The text gives the number and the directions, and no key.

ION

Priest of the inner sanctuaries; sacrificer and sacrifice in one person; dismembered according to the rule of harmony and burned on the fire of the art until he became spirit. His name has invited speculation since antiquity; the text explains the torment, not the name, and this article follows the text.

THE ANTHRŌPARION

The little man into whom Ion changes — mutilated, self-tearing, the perfected spirit's opposite and product. Jung's homunculus reading is treated, labelled as Jung's, in Section IX; the text itself leaves the figure unglossed.

VIII. The Later Visions: Boiling Water, Metal Men, the Place of Punishment

Paraphrase throughout, as in Section VII.

Zōsimos sleeps again and returns to the same place — the visions insist on the identity of the place, as the work insists on the identity of the vessel — and finds the altar transformed in its use. The bowl is now full of boiling water, and in the water are men: many people, suffering in the bubbling water and yet alive in it. One of them — a man being boiled, yet still living — speaks to the dreamer, and his speech, in paraphrase, is the second lesson's doctrine: the sight Zōsimos sees is the entrance, and the exit, and the transformation; those who seek to obtain the art — or, in the title's word, to attain excellence — enter here, and become spirits by escaping from the body. The image repays slow reading. The water that boils men without killing them is the divine water of the discourse, the sulphurous liquor in which bodies are macerated until their spirits loosen; the men in it are at once the metals in the bath and the candidates at the rite, and the speaking sufferer's gloss collapses the two registers into a single sentence — entrance, exit, transformation — that could stand as the motto of the whole treatise. Zōsimos wakes, interprets, and sleeps a third time.

The later visions populate the place with the metal men. A man of copper appears, submitting to the same unendurable violence that Ion underwent; and a man of lead likewise — the leaden figure associated in the tradition's reading with Agathodaimon, the good spirit of the canon (Section XIII). The sequence is a scale, and the Greek reader was expected to feel it as one: copper and lead are two of the four fixed bodies of the art, the substrates of Section X, and their persons undergo in the dream exactly what their metals undergo in the vessel — dismemberment, burning, boiling, the loosening of spirit from body. Where the first vision gave the process a priest, the later visions give it the periodic table of its century: the bodies themselves, standing up in the altar-place and speaking their transformations. A further figure completes the population of the place in the standard account: the punisher, set over a lead altar in the place of punishment, where all who enter burst immediately into flame and submit themselves to the unendurable torment. The lead altar is the dark twin of the bowl altar of the first vision — lead, the black prime matter of the art, the heaviest and basest of the bodies, furnishing the furniture of the place where transformation is suffered as penalty rather than undergone as rite. The distinction between the two altars is the distinction the whole corpus draws between the work done in knowledge and the same fire met without it.

The Serpent at the Temple

A later sequence in the visionary material gives the teaching a second emblem, recorded here as the standard accounts report it: a serpent guarding a temple, which the operator is instructed, in paraphrase, to take, sacrifice, and flay; to separate the bones from the flesh; and to recompose the remains into the first step of the stair by which the temple is entered. The emblem compresses the whole visionary doctrine into a single portable image — the guardian of the holy place is itself the material of the first step toward it; nothing is wasted, not even the obstacle — and its afterlife in the tradition, where serpent, temple, and stair recur for a thousand years, needs no emphasis. The archive notes the sequence's position honestly: it belongs to the later visionary strata of the memoirs as the editions present them, and its narrative is reported here, like everything in these two sections, in paraphrase from the standard accounts rather than from any single verified rendering.

Read as a sequence, the three lessons describe a single itinerary three times, at three depths. The first vision gives the person: Ion, the priest who is his own offering, ascending and descending the fifteen steps. The second gives the place: the altar become a bath, the entrance that is also the exit, the crowd of the boiling who do not die. The third gives the population and the penalty: the metal men in their order, the lead altar, the punisher, the place where the fire is torment without gloss. Discourse, person, place, penalty — and over the whole sequence, the dreamer's repeated waking voice, identifying each new horror with the composition of the waters. It is that voice, finally, that makes the Visions a Greek text rather than a mere marvel: the allegory carries its exegesis inside itself, and the exegesis is technical. The next section sets the three great modern ways of taking the sequence — the apparatus reading, the religious reading, and Jung's — side by side, with their labels on.

IX. Reading the Visions: Apparatus, Allegory — and Jung, Labelled as Jung

Three readings of the Visions dominate the modern literature, and this article presents them as readings — attributed, dated, and weighed — because the archive's rule for this text is that no interpretation, however famous, may be presented as the text's plain meaning.

1. The Apparatus Reading

The oldest modern reading is the workshop's own. On this account — developed from Taylor's 1937 study through Mertens' commentary — the bowl-shaped altar is the apparatus: the vessel in which bodies are dismembered (analysed, taken apart), burned, macerated, and recomposed as spirits is the reflux chamber and the still of the memoirs translated into a sanctuary. Mertens read Ion's fifteen steps down and up as the rising and falling of the vapours in the kerotakis — the spirit descending as condensate, ascending as vapour, in the sealed circulation of the device. The blackening of the metal in the work, melanōsis, is the death the priest undergoes; the boiling water is the divine water at work; the metal men are the bodies in their order of treatment. The reading's strength is that it is the dreamer's own: Zōsimos wakes and names the composition of the waters. Its limit is that it does not exhaust the text — the discourse's gloss explains the vision's subject, not its terror — and the best modern scholarship, the Oxford commentary included, holds the apparatus reading as necessary and insufficient at once.

2. The Religious Reading

The second reading sets the same sequence in the religious literature of Zōsimos's own centuries, and finds the parallels dense. A priest who sacrifices himself and is dismembered, ascending steps from darkness to light and becoming spirit, belongs to the world of late-antique ascent and initiation: the Hermetic rebirth, in which the candidate is stripped of the body's grossness and reconstituted as a being of light; the Gnostic ascent past the powers, in which the pneumatic self escapes the body that fate built around it; the baptismal immersion, in which the old person is drowned and a new one rises — a parallel the scholarship on Zōsimos's doctrine of conversion has pursued in detail, reading his divine water and the baptismal language of the Gospel of Philip against each other, with each text used to gloss the other. These are presented here as parallels — the archive's word, and the scholarship's careful one. A parallel establishes a shared imaginative world; it does not establish borrowing, membership, or identity of doctrine, and Sections XIV and XV will keep that discipline when they treat theurgy and the demons. What the religious reading adds to the apparatus reading is the observation that Zōsimos's contemporaries would have heard the Visions in both registers at once, because in their world the registers were not yet separate departments.

3. Jung's Reading — Jung's, and Labelled as Such

The third reading is the famous one, and it is introduced with its full bibliographical identity so that no reader can mistake its provenance. C. G. Jung delivered his lecture on the Visions at the Eranos conference at Ascona in August 1937; it was published in the Eranos-Jahrbuch for 1937 (Zurich, 1938), revised and considerably expanded in 1954, and collected in English as “The Visions of Zosimos” in Alchemical Studies, volume 13 of the Collected Works. Jung worked from the Berthelot and Ruelle text. His reading, in paraphrase, is this: the visions are an allegory of the alchemical process understood as a drama of the psyche; the tormented homunculi — the anthrōparion above all, which Jung took as the first appearance of the homunculus in alchemical literature — personify the transformations of the work, burning and boiling themselves into something else; the sacrificial act, in which priest and victim are one, figures the operator's identification with his material; and the whole sequence records, in Jung's judgment, a genuine visionary experience — he held that the vision of Zōsimos might well have been an actual happening — whose images are archetypal, the earliest recorded evidence, in his famous estimation, of the individuation process in Western literature.

// THE ARCHIVE'S CAUTION ON JUNG //

Jung's essay is a work of twentieth-century psychology that uses Zōsimos; it is not a commentary that recovers him. Jung selected from the corpus what his psychology required, read the dream figures as projections of the unconscious, and valued the text precisely where the philologists find it most resistant. None of that diminishes the essay's historical importance — it made the Visions famous and sent two generations back to the Greek — but a reader who meets Ion first in Jung will meet Jung's Ion: the homunculus, the archetype, the individuation drama. The Greek text's Ion is a priest on an altar in a treatise on the composition of the waters, and the distance between those two figures is the distance this article exists to keep visible. Jung's reading is therefore cited in this archive only with his name attached, never as the text's plain meaning.

The scholarly caution that balances Jung is not a dismissal but a relocation. Modern Zōsimos scholarship — Mertens, Jackson, Dufault, the Oxford commentary — reads the Visions as a literary-theological construction built on a dream's foundation, in dialogue with the Hermetic and Gnostic texts of its own century and with the apparatus of its own workshop. On that reading the visions' power does not depend on their being a transcript of anyone's unconscious, ancient or modern: it depends on their being the most accomplished surviving example of the thing Zōsimos invented — an allegory that interprets itself, in which the exegesis of a technical process and the drama of a soul's transformation are a single Greek text, refusing to be pried apart. The doctrine that the dream discourses state in prose is the subject of the next three sections.

X. Sōma and Pneuma: Doctrine in the Greek Philosophical Register

Zōsimos's doctrine of matter is stated in two words, and this section's business is to keep those words in their Greek register — to say what sōma and pneuma do in his sentences before any later system tidies them. A body (sōma) is what is fixed, weighable, and resistant: in the metal work, pre-eminently the four fixed bodies — lead, tin, copper, and iron — which serve as the substrate (hypostasis, in the analysis the scholarship draws from the texts) that receives change. A spirit (pneuma) is what is volatile: the vapour that rises from a heated substance, escapes the body it was shut in, and — the operative point on which the whole art turns — carries a quality with it. The sublimed vapours of sulphur, of the arsenic compounds, of mercury, are pneumata; and Zōsimos's texts, in the passages the scholarship on his doctrine of conversion collects, say the equation outright, in paraphrase: every sublimed vapour is pneuma, and so are the tingeing qualities themselves. Spirit is not a metaphor laid over the chemistry. In the Greek of these texts it is the chemistry's name for its active principle — the Stoics' all-pervading breath, which held the cosmos together by its tension, domesticated to the scale of a still.

The work of the art is then definable in two coined words that the discourse at the head of the Visions displays, and that occur, as Mertens noted, only there in his corpus: aposōmatōsis, disembodiment — the extraction of pneuma from a body — and episōmatōsis, embodiment — the tying or bonding of pneuma to a body. Between those two operations the whole of Zōsimos's practice and the whole of his allegory move. The still performs disembodiment; the tincture performs embodiment; Ion undergoes both on the altar. And the discourse's governing claim binds the operations to a metaphysics: they proceed, in paraphrase, not from foreign natures brought in from outside, but from the one nature acting on itself — the monoeidēs nature, the single-kind nature, which possesses in itself both the hard shells of the minerals and the moist pulp of the plants. Olivier Dufault's cold-water caution from Section V applies here with precision: this is not yet a systematic theory of transmutation of the kind Olympiodorus would later construct. It is something more characteristic of Zōsimos and, for the historian, more interesting — a working monism, stated as an aphorism at the head of a dream, in which the unity of matter is less a theorem than a licence: because the nature is one, the spirit drawn from any body may in principle be bonded into any other, and the art is the knowledge of how.

One identification in the doctrine deserves its own paragraph because it anchors the monism in a substance. Zōsimos does not conceive the prime matter abstractly: he identifies it, in the texts the scholarship cites, with black lead — molybdos melas. The heaviest, darkest, most Saturnian of the bodies is the undifferentiated ground to which the work first reduces its materials and from which their re-qualification proceeds. The lead altar of the third vision (Section VIII) is this doctrine furnishing its own dream: the place of punishment is built of prime matter, because prime matter unworked — body from which no spirit has been drawn, into which none has been bonded — is precisely the condition the visions figure as torment. The converse operation has its own image in the corpus, noted by Dufault: transformation as a turning inside out (ekstrophē), the hidden interior of a body brought outward and made manifest as its new surface and colour — said of metals, and said, in the anthropology of the Omega tractate (Section XIV), of the inner human being as well. One word, two benches: the metal turned inside out shows its tincture; the person turned inside out shows the light that was shut within. The doctrine section of the companion article surveys the same material from the workshop side; this article's point is the register. In Greek, these are not two doctrines with a resemblance. They are one vocabulary declining itself through two nouns.

// A NOTE ON “SOUL” //

Later alchemy systematised a triad — body, soul, spirit — and assigned each term a fixed technical sense. In Zōsimos's own pages the working pair is sōma and pneuma; psychē, soul, does its work chiefly in the human register — the soul to be perfected, the divine spark shut in the elements. Importing the later triad into his text tidies him at the cost of falsifying him, and this article follows the scholarship in declining to do it.

The human register is not an appendix to the doctrine; it is its second half, and the scholarship on conversion states the join exactly. The pneuma that tinges a metal and the pneuma whose liberation perfects a person are, for Zōsimos, the same word doing the same work at two scales: in the vessel, a quality-bearing vapour drawn out and re-fixed; in the person, the luminous, divine element — the spark of the Gnostic anthropology his texts breathe — extracted from its mixture with the flesh and restored to its own kind. That is why the discourse on the waters can open a treatise called On Excellence without changing the subject, and why the dreamer, waking from a flaying, thinks first of his recipe. The next two sections follow the doctrine into its two operative media: the water that carries the spirit, and the tincture that fixes it.

XI. The Divine Water: Theion Hydōr and the Sulphur–Divine Pun

The central agent of Zōsimos's art is a liquid with a pun for a name, and the pun must be handled carefully, because it is exact, deliberate, and easy to get wrong. The Greek phrase is theion hydōr (θεῖον ὕδωρ). Hydōr is water. Theion is the crux: Greek possesses two words of identical form — theion (θεῖον), the neuter of the adjective theios, “divine”; and theion (θεῖον), the noun meaning “sulphur” — and in the genitive, theiou, the two are indistinguishable even in principle. The phrase therefore says, simultaneously and undecidably, “divine water” and “sulphur water”: the volatile, sulphur-bearing liquor of the stills, and the sacred medium in which bodies are immersed and perfected. Matteo Martelli's study of the divine water in the pseudo-Democritean writings — the tradition Zōsimos inherits — establishes the philology: the ambiguity is grammatical before it is mystical, it sharpens precisely in the inflected forms, and the Greek alchemists play on the polysemy knowingly, building a deliberately complex and at times misleading nomenclature on it. Cristina Viano's work, on which the conversion scholarship draws, adds the observation that Zōsimos is conscious of the double sense and theorises it: the word “divine”, in his usage, is applied to things that move from below upward — to what rises. The sulphur water is divine, in his Greek, because rising is what it does.

What the water is, operationally, the texts and their modern readers describe consistently: a prepared, volatile, sulphurous liquid — in the workshop register, the product of the stills, in which sulphur and its companions have been made liquid and active — that is then applied to bodies. Metals are immersed in it, macerated in it, dyed by it; it is the medium in which disembodiment and embodiment (Section X) are transacted, the carrier that loosens a body's own spirit and delivers another's. Zōsimos wrote whole memoirs on its preparation and behaviour — on the evaporation of the divine water that fixes mercury, in one transmitted title — and the Visions' dreamer, waking from Ion's flaying, identifies the vision with its composition before he thinks of anything else. The bubbling water of the second vision, in which men suffer and do not die, is the divine water in its allegorical person: the bath in which bodies are held at the boil until their spirits come loose. Nor is the baptismal resonance a modern imposition. The scholarship on Zōsimos's doctrine of conversion reads the immersion of metals in the divine water and the baptismal immersion of the imperfect human as deliberate analogues within his system — the metal immersed and rising with an indelible new colour, the person immersed in the kratēr of the Hermetic treatise and rising rectified (Section XIV) — and uses each register, cautiously, to gloss the other. The pun is the doctrine in miniature: one liquid, two names, and the refusal of the Greek to let its reader decide which name is the literal one.

THEION (θεῖον) — “DIVINE”

The neuter of theios, divine, sacred. In this sense the water is the holy medium of the work — the bath of transformation, continuous with the baptismal and Hermetic kratēr imagery of Sections VIII and XIV.

THEION (θεῖον) — “SULPHUR”

The noun for brimstone. In this sense the water is a sulphur-bearing preparation of the stills — the tingeing liquor whose vapours whiten, yellow, and fix. Sulphur's older ritual life as a purifying fumigant stands behind both senses.

Two disciplines govern the term's use in this archive, and both are stated here so the reader can hold the article to them. First, the divine water is not a single recipe. The tradition knows more than one preparation under the name — the pseudo-Democritean fragments preserved by Moses and by Synesius already distinguish two kinds, their composition determining the form of the name — and any account that prints one formula as the divine water has mistaken a family for an individual. Second, the pun is Greek, and it does not survive translation: every English rendering must choose “divine” or “sulphur” and footnote the other, which is why this article keeps the transliterated phrase in view. Zōsimos's most important substance is a homonym. A tradition that took words as seriously as deeds could hardly have arranged a better emblem for itself.

XII. Baphē: Tincture as the Spirit's Action on Bodies

If the divine water is the art's medium, the tincture is its act. Baphē (βαφή) is, in ordinary Greek, a dyeing: the immersion of a prepared fibre in a dye-bath until the colour goes all the way through and cannot be washed out. The volatile agents of Section X — the quality-bearing pneumata — are called tinctures in the texts for exactly the dyer's reason: as the scholarship's analysis (Peter Fraser's, as the conversion literature reports it) puts it, the sublimed vapours responsible for endowing metals with qualities were themselves named baphai, dyeings. A tincture is a spirit considered under the aspect of its result: pneuma in transit, baphē at the moment of fixation, when the vapour's quality passes into the body and becomes the body's own colour — permanently, through and through, as dye passes into wool. The distinction between a coating and a tincture is the whole doctrine in one contrast: a gilded surface wears; a tinctured body is its new colour, because the spirit that coloured it has been bonded within it — episōmatōsis, embodiment, achieved.

The dyer's metaphor also carries the doctrine of preparation, which Zōsimos's texts make the hinge of success and failure alike. Cloth that has not been scoured and mordanted sheds its dye; a body that has not been prepared — cleansed, opened, reduced toward its prime matter — sheds its tincture, and the work produces a semblance: a colour that sits on the surface and deceives the eye, the standing emblem in his letters for every false success, in metal and in souls. The genuine tincture takes because the body was ready to receive it. Section XV will meet the same structure in the religious register, where the unprepared operator's work is the demons' opportunity; here it is enough to register that in Zōsimos's Greek the moral and the technical vocabularies share a single causal grammar — preparation, reception, fixation — because they were never two vocabularies to begin with.

“One Nature Delights in Another”: An Attribution, Carefully Made

No sentence in Greek alchemy is quoted more often, or attributed more carelessly, than the aphorism of the natures — rendered in its familiar English form as the declaration that nature delights in nature, nature conquers nature, nature masters nature. This article attributes it with the care the scholarship requires. The aphorism belongs to the Democritean tradition: it stands as the refrain of the pseudo-Democritean Physika kai mystika, the first-century books transmitted under Democritus's name, where it is placed in the mouth of Democritus's legendary Persian master, Ostanes, and it closes recipe after recipe as the tradition's motto of affinity — like acting upon like, one nature seizing and transforming another because they are kin. Zōsimos inherits the aphorism, cites the tradition that carries it, and builds his own cognate doctrine — the single nature, monoeidēs, acting on itself, of Sections VI and X — in its neighbourhood. But the sentence is not his coinage, and the archive does not give it to him. The distinction is a small model of this article's whole method: a Greek alchemist's originality lies not in the mottos he repeats but in the commentary he builds around them, and Zōsimos's commentary on the one-nature doctrine — the discourse on the waters, the dream of Ion, the identification of prime matter with black lead — is originality enough. Readers who meet the aphorism elsewhere attributed flatly to Zōsimos are meeting the tradition's habit of crediting the commentator with his author's text: the very habit Mertens' edition exists to correct.

The tincture doctrine has one more register, and it leads directly to the eschatology of the next sections. Zōsimos distinguishes, in the Omega tractate and the Final Account, between tinctures that depend on times, places, and the favour of the local spirits — the kairikai baphai, the “timely” or “propitious” tinctures of the older translations — and the natural tinctures that the philosopher obtains by understanding, independent of the calendar of fate. Jackson's edition read the phrase as commending astrologically opportune timing; Stolzenberg's rejoinder, that Zōsimos's argument runs in precisely the opposite direction — the timely tinctures are the demons' counterfeit, the natural tinctures the philosopher's freedom from fate — has reshaped the discussion, and this article follows the debate rather than either party. What is not in dispute is the distinction itself, or its weight: the question of which tinctures to seek is, for Zōsimos, the question of whether the art serves fate or escapes it. That is a religious question wearing a technical term, and it is the door into Section XIV.

XIII. The Canon of Authorities: How a Greek Alchemist Builds a Lineage

Every Greek commentary presupposes a canon — a bounded set of authors whose words are worth unfolding — and Zōsimos's memoirs are unusually explicit about his. The canon, as he constructs and deploys it, has five principal names, and each performs a distinct function in the architecture of his authority. The companion article traces the same chain from the biographical side; this section treats it as what it is in the Greek text — a literary construction, the deliberate building of a lineage by a writer who understood that in Greek intellectual life a doctrine is worth what its pedigree is worth.

HERMES

The fountainhead. Hermes Trismegistus — the Egyptian Thoth in Greek dress — stands at the canon's head as its divine author: Zōsimos cites a book of Hermes On Natures and reports Hermes and Zoroaster declaring the race of philosophers superior to fate. Hermes functions as scripture: the authority that does not need arguing from. See the hub's Hermetica article.

DEMOCRITUS

The literary classic. The four books under Democritus's name — gold, silver, stones, purple — are the canon's textus receptus, the work the whole commentary tradition exists to expound. Zōsimos cites “the divine Democritus” on whitening as a court cites precedent. See Greek alchemy origins.

MARIA

The technical authority. Maria the Jewess is the canon's warrant for apparatus and operation — quoted, uniquely among the authorities, by tolerances and specifications. Her function in the lineage is precision: she is the proof that the tradition's knowledge is exact. Her own article in this hub is Maria the Jewess; the companion article treats the attribution problem in full.

AGATHODAIMON

The spectral authority. The “good spirit” surfaces in the citations — on the apparatus, in the dream tradition's leaden man — as pure antiquity: a voice from before the record, whose function is depth. A canon needs a figure at the edge of memory, and Agathodaimon is Zōsimos's.

CHYMES

The eponym. Chymes — also transmitted as Chemes or Chimes — is the shadowy sage whom Olympiodorus's doxography enrols beside Hermes, Agathodaimon, and Zōsimos among the art's founding physicists, and whose name the tradition connected with the art's own (chēmeia). His function is foundation: the canon's claim that the art had a founder, and that the founder can be named.

ZŌSIMOS HIMSELF

The canon's latest member — by his successors' act, not his own claim. Olympiodorus sets him in the doxography beside Hermes and Chymes; Stephanus lectures downstream of him; the compilers shelve him at the corpus's centre. The lineage he built for the ancients was extended, by the tradition's ordinary machinery, to include its builder.

The construction deserves analysis, because it is a Greek intellectual's construction and its joints show the culture's joinery. A lineage of this kind does three kinds of work at once. It legitimates: a practice whose founders include a god, a Presocratic, and a prophetess is not a trade secret but a philosophy with ancestors — the claim Olympiodorus's doxography would later formalise by filing the alchemists beside Thales and Heraclitus. It organises: the canon distributes labour among its members — Hermes for first principles, Democritus for the text, Maria for the bench, Agathodaimon for depth, Chymes for the name — so that a citation is also a classification, telling the reader what kind of authority is being invoked. And it polices: to stand inside the lineage is to read the ancients as the lineage's authorised reader reads them, which is the polemical use the companion article documents in the letters — rivals are refuted not by experiment but by bibliography, convicted of not having read Democritus properly. None of this is cynicism. It is how Greek paideia transmitted everything it valued, from Homer to Galen; Zōsimos's distinction is to have built the machinery for a subject that had previously travelled in recipes and revelations, and thereby to have made alchemy, for the first time, a literature with a history of itself.

Behind the five names stands the canon's mythic charter, which the companion article treats in its place and which this article notes only for its Greek literary function: the story, retailed in the Book of Sophe material, of the fallen angels who taught the arts of nature to the women they desired, and of the first book, called Chema, from which the science took its name. As myth it is cognate with the Enochic watcher legends; as canon-building it is the lineage's foundation charter, the narrative that explains why this knowledge exists in books at all — and why its custody is dangerous, guarded, and worth a commentary. A Greek reader of c. 300 met such charters at the head of many canons. Zōsimos's readers met his, and believed the books that followed it were worth copying for a thousand years. On the evidence of the Marcianus, they were right.

XIV. The Final Account and On the Letter Omega: Eschatological Framing

Two texts frame the corpus at its head and at its close, and both frame it eschatologically — they set the work of the waters inside a doctrine of last things: of fate and freedom from fate, of the inner human's destiny, and of a final accounting in which the operator, not only the metal, is weighed. On the Letter Omega stands first in the Authentic Memoirs as their programmatic introduction — the tractate Jackson edited and translated in the 1978 monograph on which this section rests. Its Greek text is preserved, a transmission detail worth the reader's notice, only in the second recension of Marcianus graecus 299: the corpus's programmatic text hangs by the single thread of the great codex of Section III. The Final Account — also rendered Final Count or Final Quittance — stands at the other end of the oeuvre as its summation, the last word of the Mertens groups' fourth division. Read together, as the scholarship reads them, the two texts state the anthropology within which the recipes and the dreams alike do their work.

The Omega tractate's anthropology, in paraphrase from Jackson's edition and the studies that follow it, divides the human being in two. There is the outer human — Adam, the bodily person, born under heimarmenē, fate, the astral determinism of the age's common cosmology, ruled by the powers that administer the spheres. And there is the inner human — named in the tractate's Gnostic register Phōs, “Light”, the luminous man within — who is not fate's creature and can, by knowledge, be freed from its administration. Hermes and Zoroaster are cited for the charter of that freedom: the race of philosophers, in their reported declaration, stands above fate. The alchemical consequence is immediate and it is the tractate's point. An art that depends on propitious times — on the calendar of the spheres, on the favour of the local spirits who administer places and seasons — is an art still enslaved to the outer human's masters. The philosopher's art, the natural tinctures of Section XII, is the inner human's work: performed in knowledge of the one nature, it succeeds not when the stars permit but when the operator understands. Eschatology here is not a doctrine about the end of the world. It is a doctrine about the end of the practitioner's servitude, stated at the head of a book about furnaces — and it converts every recipe that follows into a small rehearsal of liberation.

The Final Account closes the arc with an image that gathers the whole corpus into one sentence's neighbourhood. Theosebeia is counselled, in paraphrase, to cease wandering from shrine to shrine and from spirit to spirit, to rectify herself — the Greek verb is dieuthynein, to make straight — and, when she recognises that she has been perfected, to spurn matter, hurry to Poimandres, and, baptised in the kratēr, ascend swiftly to her own kind. The allusions are precise, and they are the earliest datable evidence that the philosophical Hermetica were being read, by name, in Egypt around 300 CE: Poimandres is the divine interlocutor of the first treatise of the Corpus Hermeticum, and the kratēr — the mixing bowl of mind in which the willing are baptised — is the vessel of the fourth. Walter Scott, editing the Hermetica in 1924, already drew the conclusion the modern scholarship repeats: Zōsimos had read those two treatises soon after 300. The hub's Hermetica article treats the corpus itself; here the point is the framing. The alchemist's last word to his pupil is a Hermetic ascent, and its central image is a vessel — the bowl of the first vision, the baptismal kratēr, the still's receiver, at last indistinguishable. The eschatological frame does not decorate the technical corpus. It tells the reader what the technical corpus was for.

// OMEGA //

The tractate's title names the last letter of the Greek alphabet — the letter of endings, and, in the Christian idiom of the same centuries, of the End itself. A memoir on furnaces introduced under the sign of Omega announces its author's intention before its first sentence: the apparatus book will be read eschatologically, or it will be misread. Jackson's edition takes its title from the tractate's traditional companion description, On the Virgin, reflecting the tractate's concern with the pure, unworked state from which the work begins.

A caution of attribution closes the section, in the archive's manner. The Final Account and the Book of Sophe material form the most myth-laden of Mertens' four groups, and individual doctrines drawn from them carry a slightly different evidential weight than the Authentic Memoirs themselves — a distinction the companion article flags wherever it matters, and which this article repeats here because the eschatological framing rests substantially on these two texts and on the Omega tractate. The framing is Zōsimos's as the critical tradition delimits him. It is not a Byzantine overlay: the Omega tractate stands in the securest group of all, and its anthropology is the memoirs' own front door.

XV. Between Workshop and Theurgy: Demons, Sacrifice, and the Religious Landscape

Zōsimos worked at a bench, in a century when the bench stood in a crowded religious landscape, and this final doctrinal section places him in it — doctrinally, as the task of the article requires, with the biographical treatment of his circle and his rivals left to the companion article where it belongs. The landscape's coordinates, for a Greek writer of c. 300, can be fixed briefly. The old civic and temple cults still functioned, though the world that would suppress them was a lifetime away. The philosophical schools were in their late Platonic flowering: Plotinus was a generation dead, Porphyry's works circulated — Zōsimos cites him, a citation that helps set his date — and Iamblichus (c. 245–c. 325), his exact contemporary, was composing the defence of theurgy that would define late Platonism's ritual turn: the doctrine that the gods are reached not by thought alone but by rites, symbols, and material tokens (synthēmata) whose efficacy the gods themselves established. The Hermetic circles read their revelations; the Gnostic conventicles their apocalypses; the Christian church was a generation from empire. Zōsimos's texts breathe all of this air. What they do not do — and the discipline of this section is to keep the distinction bright — is enrol their author in any of its parties. Iamblichean theurgy is presented here as contemporary context, the nearest philosophical articulation of the world Zōsimos inhabited. Membership in it is not claimed for him, by this article or by the scholarship it follows. The system itself — Plotinus, Porphyry, Iamblichus and the chains of sympathy — is expounded in this hub's Neoplatonism and Theurgy.

Within that landscape, Zōsimos's most distinctive doctrinal position is a hostility — and it must be stated as doctrine, since its biographical expression in the letters is the companion article's ground. In the Omega tractate and the Final Account, the local spirits — the daimones attached to places, shrines, and seasons — are figured as hungry: hungry, in the texts' paraphrased charge, not only for sacrifices but for the souls of those who feed them. Sacrifice to them, and the arts that depend on their favour — the timely tinctures of Section XII, dispensed at their altars at their hours — are the counterfeit of the art: real effects, perhaps, but purchased by servitude to fate's local administrators. Against that economy Zōsimos sets the philosopher's alternative in the Final Account's counsel, paraphrased in Section XIV: stay home, cease the wandering among shrines, rectify yourself, and the genuine, natural tinctures follow — because the work's true agent is not a spirit to be bribed but a nature to be understood. Kyle Fraser's study of the corpus's Enochic material and Daniel Stolzenberg's of the tinctures converge on the same doctrinal centre: Zōsimos's alchemy is deliberately purged — of astrological timing, of demonic mediation, of temple sacrifice — and the purge is not a modernising scruple but a Gnostic-Hermetic theology of freedom, in which the powers that demand sacrifice are precisely the powers the inner human exists to escape. Lawrence Principe's summary of the corpus makes the operational corollary a headline: daimonic interference produces a deceitful semblance of the art, and the genuine procedure requires its absence. The hostility to demonic sacrifice is thus not a prejudice decorating the doctrine. It is the doctrine's negative pole — the definition, by refusal, of what a natural tincture is.

The Gnostic parallels gather, at the end, around the figure with whom this article's reading began: the priest who is his own sacrifice. Set Ion beside the religious texts of his own centuries — as parallels, labelled as parallels, in the archive's discipline — and the family resemblance is exact without being an identity. In the Hermetic rebirth, the candidate is stripped of the body's grossness and reconstituted; in the Gnostic ascents, the pneumatic self passes the powers by knowledge, leaving the bodily garment behind; in the baptismal imagery that the conversion scholarship reads alongside Zōsimos, immersion drowns the old person and raises the new. Ion undergoes all three grammars at once, in a furnace, on an altar shaped like a vessel, and wakes as a recipe. No single tradition of his century owns that sentence. It is Zōsimos's own synthesis, stated in Greek, in a commentary on the composition of the waters — which is to say, it is the precise point at which the workshop and the sanctuary, the commentator and the visionary, the Greek textual tradition and the Egyptian religious world behind it, meet in one author and decline to be separated. The later tradition separated them anyway, into laboratory alchemy and spiritual alchemy, and argued for fifteen centuries about which was the real one. The Greek text, read in its codices and its commentators, is the standing evidence that the separation was performed on Zōsimos, not by him.
