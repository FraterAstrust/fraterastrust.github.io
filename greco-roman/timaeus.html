I. The Second Foundation: A Dialogue That Became a World

This article belongs to the Foundations cluster of the Alchemy wing, and it treats the wing's second foundation. The first is the four-element scheme itself — fire, air, water, and earth as the roots of all bodies — which the hub treats in its own article on the four elements. That scheme is older than Plato: it is Empedocles' inheritance. What Plato's Timaeus did, around 360 BCE, was to give the four roots a physics — to say what an element is, why there are exactly four of them, how they pass into one another, and what kind of cosmos they compose when a rational maker orders them toward the good. Nearly everything the later tradition meant by “element theory” — in Greek alchemy, in Arabic natural philosophy, in Latin scholasticism, and in the Hermetic literature this wing exists to document — descends from that act of foundation, whether its heirs were developing Plato's construction or, as Aristotle did, rebuilding the house while keeping its address. The companion article on Aristotle's matter theory treats the rebuilding; this article treats the original building.

The Timaeus is also, by a wide margin, the most continuously read work of Greek philosophy in the pre-modern world. Other Platonic dialogues vanished from the Latin West for a thousand years; Aristotle's treatises travelled by Syriac and Arabic roads and arrived in Latin Europe late and in pieces. The Timaeus never went away. It was commented on in Plato's own school, quarrelled over by his immediate successors, translated in part by Cicero, translated again — with a vast commentary — by Calcidius, epitomised by Galen and carried in that form into Arabic, expounded at enormous length by Proclus, glossed by the masters of Chartres, and read by every educated European who read any Plato at all until Ficino's complete Plato of 1484 finally gave the West its other dialogues back. For most of its history, “Plato” effectively meant this one dialogue, and “Plato's cosmology” meant the cosmos it describes: a living, ensouled, spherical world, made by a good craftsman in the likeness of an eternal model, out of elements that are geometrical solids at bottom.

A note on method, in the form this hub's readers will recognise from the wing's other articles. Direct quotation from the Timaeus is given only where it has been verified in a named modern translation, and the translation is named at the quotation. The two translations relied on throughout are F. M. Cornford's, published with his commentary as Plato's Cosmology (1937), and Donald J. Zeyl's (Hackett, 2000). Everything else that reports the dialogue's contents is paraphrase, labelled as such where the wording matters, and keyed to the dialogue's Stephanus pagination — the page-and-letter references (29d, 53c, and so on) by which every edition of Plato has been cited since the sixteenth century. The Timaeus attracts confident misquotation more than almost any ancient text, because so many later systems have wanted its authority for doctrines it does not quite contain. This article's discipline is the opposite one: where the dialogue is strange, disputed, or silent, the article says so, and the disputes are stated as disputes.

THE DIALOGUE

Written c. 360 BCE, among Plato's late works. Dramatically a sequel to a conversation resembling the Republic's, and the first panel of an unfinished trilogy: Timaeus on the cosmos and human nature, Critias on ancient Athens and Atlantis, and a Hermocrates that was never written. Almost the whole of it is one speech by Timaeus of Locri.

THE SPEECH'S THREE MOVEMENTS

Modern editions divide Timaeus' discourse (27d–92c) into three parts: the works of Intellect (29d–47e); the works of Necessity (47e–69a), prefaced by the “second beginning” that introduces the receptacle; and the two causes cooperating in the making of the human being (69a–92c). This article follows that division.

One framing point before the frame itself. The Timaeus is routinely described in popular writing as Plato's “creation myth”, and the description is not wrong, but it conceals the dialogue's double nature. It is at once a story — a god makes the world, in an order, for reasons — and a piece of technical natural philosophy, containing the most detailed physical theory any Greek author produced before Aristotle: a geometry of matter, a mathematical harmonics of soul, a physiology, a pathology, and a theory of sensation. Its author knew the two registers were in tension, and he built the tension into the text: the speaker announces in advance that his account can only be a likely story (Section IV). Holding both facts at once — that this is a myth that knows it is one, and that it is also the ancient world's most serious cosmology — is the whole discipline of reading it, and Sections IV and V return to the tension from the two directions of the oldest scholarly quarrel it provoked.

// WHY THIS ARTICLE SITS IN THE ALCHEMY WING //

The alchemists treated elsewhere in this hub did not inherit their elements from Empedocles directly. They inherited them as Plato had rebuilt them — corpuscular, geometrical, transformable by rule — and as Aristotle, Galen, and the Arabic tradition then reworked the Platonic scheme. A reader who wants to know why Alexandrian alchemy speaks of bodies dissolving, recombining, and ripening toward perfection needs the dialogue in which transformation first became arithmetic. That dialogue is this one.

II. The Frame: One Absent, a Festival, and a City Set in Motion

The dialogue opens with an absence. Socrates counts his companions — one, two, three — and asks, in paraphrase, where the fourth is: a member of yesterday's company is missing, kept away by indisposition (17a). The three present are Timaeus, Critias, and Hermocrates, and the conversation they are about to have is explicitly a repayment. Yesterday, in a discussion the dialogue summarises at 17c–19b — a city ordered much as the Republic's ideal city is ordered, with its classes, its common arrangements, and its guardians — Socrates played host and speaker. Today the guests are to entertain him in return, and Socrates states what he wants from the entertainment: not another portrait of the ideal city at rest, but the city in motion — set down in the world, at war, negotiating, fighting, behaving as the living thing his static description could not show (19b–c, in paraphrase).

The programme the three guests agree on divides the labour in the order of being itself. Timaeus will speak first, beginning with the origin of the cosmos and ending with the nature of human beings; Critias will then take those human beings, set them in the Athens of nine thousand years ago, and show the ideal city in action against Atlantis; Hermocrates was to speak third (27a–b, in paraphrase). Of the three panels, the first exists — it is this dialogue — the second exists unfinished as the Critias, and the third was never written. The setting, on the usual reading of the dramatic clues, is Athens during the Panathenaea, the great festival of Athena — a fitting stage for a story in which Athens herself will be the heroine.

The Speakers

TIMAEUS OF LOCRI

Presented (20a, in paraphrase) as a visitor from Locri in southern Italy, a man of wealth and standing who has held his city's highest offices and reached, in Socrates' introduction, the summit of philosophy — above all of astronomy and the study of the nature of the universe. Whether a historical Timaeus of Locri wrote anything is doubtful; a treatise On the Soul of the World later circulated under his name, but it is a much later summary of this dialogue, not its source. Plato's Timaeus is best read as a constructed authority: the Pythagoreanising astronomer-philosopher the speech requires.

CRITIAS AND HERMOCRATES

Critias — the dialogue leaves it genuinely uncertain whether the notorious Critias of the Thirty Tyrants or his grandfather is meant, and scholarship has argued both — is the keeper of the Atlantis story, which he claims descends from Solon. Hermocrates, the Syracusan general who had defeated Athens in Sicily, is present chiefly as the promised third speaker: a standing reminder that the trilogy's last panel, which might have judged the other two, is missing.

The frame matters to everything that follows, for two reasons. The first is literary: nearly all of what the world knows as “Plato's cosmology” is placed in the mouth of a guest, in a speech Socrates commissions and then does not interrupt. The Timaeus is a monologue wearing a dialogue's frame, and its pronouncements arrive with an authority no Socratic cross-examination is allowed to test. Ancient and modern readers alike have disagreed about how far Plato stands behind his speaker — the question runs underneath the dispute of Section IV — but the form itself is a fact: this is doctrine delivered, not doctrine debated. The second reason is structural. The promised sequence — cosmos first, then the city in history — makes cosmology the foundation of politics in the trilogy's design: you cannot show the good city in motion until you know what kind of world it moves in, and what kind of animal the human being is who moves it. Timaeus' speech honours the commission exactly. It ends, as Section XII shows, with the human body and soul — the point at which Critias was to take over.

// HOW TO READ THE REFERENCES //

References in the form 29d or 53c–55c are to the Stephanus pagination: the page numbers of Henri Estienne's 1578 Geneva edition of Plato, subdivided by the letters a–e, which all modern editions print in their margins. They are the coordinates of Plato scholarship. A reader with any translation — Cornford, Zeyl, or another — can find every passage this article discusses by those numbers alone, whatever the page of the translation itself.

III. Atlantis, in One Section

This article gives Atlantis exactly one section, deliberately. The full treatment — the story's sources, its geography, and its enormous modern afterlife as a supposed lost continent — belongs to the Antiquity division's article on Plato's Atlantis, and the reader who wants Atlantis should go there. What a reader of the Timaeus needs is only the story's function inside this dialogue, which is smaller, stranger, and more interesting than the legend built on it.

Within the frame, the Atlantis story is Critias' contribution, and in the Timaeus he only summarises it (20d–25d); the full telling was reserved for the Critias. In paraphrase of the summary: Critias recounts a tale he heard from his grandfather, who had it from Solon, who had it from Egyptian priests at Saïs. The priests told Solon that the Greeks are children in memory, that cataclysms periodically destroy civilisations and their records, and that nine thousand years before Solon's time there existed an Athens of the very constitution Socrates described yesterday — and an island power, Atlantis, larger (in the text's phrase) than Libya and Asia together, situated beyond the Pillars of Heracles. Atlantis made war on the peoples within the straits; primeval Athens, alone and abandoned by her allies, defeated the invasion and liberated the Mediterranean world; and afterwards, in a single day and night of catastrophe, earthquakes and floods swallowed the Athenian army and sank Atlantis beneath the sea, leaving the Atlantic unnavigable for its mud. Critias insists, repeatedly and in terms, that the story is not a fiction but true (20d, 26e, in paraphrase) — an insistence modern readers have taken both as Plato's wink and as his earnest, and which the Antiquity division's treatment examines at length.

Inside the trilogy's design, the story's job is plain: it is the city in motion Socrates asked for. The ideal constitution, which the Republic could only describe at rest and which critics could dismiss as a pattern laid up in heaven, is here given a past: it once existed, it fought, and it won. Atlantis is its necessary opposite — wealth, sea-power, and imperial appetite grown past measure — and the war between them is the frame's whole point, the exhibit the cosmology is commissioned to support. Timaeus' speech, which follows immediately, is therefore not an interruption of the Atlantis story. It is its foundation: first the world and the human animal must be made, before the city made of those animals can be shown in action. Readers who come to the Timaeus for Atlantis alone invert the dialogue's own priorities; the dialogue spends five Stephanus pages on Atlantis and sixty-five on the cosmos. This article follows the dialogue.

IV. A “Likely Story”: The Status of the Account and the Oldest Dispute in Platonism

Before Timaeus says anything about the world, he says something about his own speech, and the whole later history of the dialogue is contained in the warning. The discourse opens (27d–28a) with a distinction that governs everything: between what always is and never becomes, grasped by understanding with a reasoned account, and what becomes and never fully is, grasped by opinion with unreasoning sense-perception. The cosmos belongs to the second class: it is visible, tangible, bodily, and therefore a thing that has become (28b–c). And from the classification Timaeus draws the methodological consequence (29b–d, in paraphrase): accounts are akin to the things they set forth. An account of what is eternal and unchanging can itself be stable and irrefutable; an account of a likeness — and the cosmos is a likeness, an eikōn, of an eternal model — can only be a likely account, an eikōs logos or eikōs mythos: in the phrase every translation must wrestle with, a “likely story”. The wordplay is untranslatable and deliberate: eikōs, likely, is the adjective of eikōn, image. A discourse about an image can be no more exact than its subject permits.

The warning is not a shrug, and Section IV's first duty is to state its force accurately, because two opposite misreadings of it are endemic. On one side, “likely story” is quoted as if Plato were confessing that the cosmology is mere poetry, to be enjoyed and not believed. The text does not support the shrug: Timaeus undertakes to give an account “no less likely than anyone else's” (29c, in paraphrase), the account is to merit belief (pistis, 29c) even though it cannot command the certainty reserved for knowledge of eternal things, and the speaker repeatedly marks the points at which he is exceeding what human beings can settle (for instance at 53d, where a definitive account of the elementary triangles is reserved to a god and to the man dear to a god). On the other side, the warning is ignored altogether, and the speech is mined as flat doctrine. That reading is equally unsupported: the dialogue itself grades its own epistemic status, and any honest account of its teaching must carry the grade with the teaching. The modern scholarly consensus, represented by the Stanford Encyclopedia's treatment of the dialogue, reads the contrast as one between apodeictic certainty about intelligible matters and warranted plausibility about empirical ones — not as a licence to treat the cosmology as either scripture or decoration.

The Dispute: Did the World Begin?

The second and deeper dispute concerns not the account's certainty but its tense. Timaeus speaks throughout of the cosmos as something that came to be: the craftsman took over a pre-existing disorder and ordered it (30a); time itself came into being together with the heavens (38b); the world soul was compounded, divided, and joined to the body in a narrated sequence of operations. Taken at face value, the dialogue teaches a temporal creation — a before, an act, and an after. But Plato's own school split on the question immediately, and the split is the oldest documented quarrel in Platonism.

THE METAPHORICAL READING: THE OLD ACADEMY

Speusippus, Plato's nephew and successor, and Xenocrates, the Academy's third head — with Crantor, the dialogue's first commentator, often named beside them in the ancient reports — held that the generation of the cosmos is narrated “for the sake of instruction”: as a geometer draws a figure in stages to make its structure intelligible, so the dialogue assembles in time what in reality exists eternally and all at once. On this reading there was no first moment and no pre-cosmic chaos; the “before” of the story is an analytical device, showing what the world would be without the ordering presence of intellect. The reading became the prevailing view among later Platonists, Plotinus and Proclus included.

THE LITERAL READING: ARISTOTLE

Aristotle, who studied in the Academy for twenty years, read the dialogue literally — and attacked it on that reading. In On the Heavens (I.10, 279b–280a) and Physics VIII he argues against a cosmos that began but will never end, and against a beginning of time itself (Physics 251b), treating Plato as the one thinker who made the world generated yet indestructible. Aristotle knew the metaphorical defence and names it — only to reject it: the geometrical analogy, he argues, fails, because a diagram's stages coexist in the finished figure, whereas a generated cosmos's beginning is a real event. Plutarch and Atticus later defended the literal reading within Platonism itself.

Modern scholarship has inherited the quarrel entire, and this article states its modern form rather than pretending it closed. A. E. Taylor's commentary (1928) and, most influentially, Cornford's Plato's Cosmology (1937) defended the metaphorical reading: Cornford held that the creation narrative is a myth in form, that time cannot have begun since “before time” is incoherent, and that the pre-cosmic disorder represents, in story-form, the element of necessity in a world that has always depended on a reason it did not choose. Harold Cherniss pressed the Academy's testimony to the same side. Against them, Gregory Vlastos's defence of the literal reading (from 1939 onward) reopened the case: Vlastos argued that the text's temporal language is too sustained and too specific to be waved away, and that Plato — unlike his successors — was willing to hold that the cosmos genuinely began, its order imposed on a motion that genuinely preceded it. The contemporary literature remains divided along recognisably the same lines: David Sedley's Creationism and Its Critics in Antiquity (2007) defends a robustly literal, creationist Plato, and Sarah Broadie's Nature and Divinity in Plato's Timaeus (2012) likewise takes the craftsman's agency with full seriousness, while other recent interpreters seek positions between the poles — a real dependence of order on intellect, without a datable first morning. The position of this article is the one the evidence supports: the dispute is genuine, it is twenty-four centuries old, it was already a dispute among men who had heard Plato lecture, and no summary that settles it in a sentence can be trusted. What can be said without controversy is what the dispute is about: whether the world's dependence on reason is a fact of its history or a fact of its structure. Both parties agree on the dependence. They disagree about the tense.

// A SECOND DISPUTE HIDING INSIDE THE FIRST //

Entangled with the question of generation is the question of the craftsman himself. Is the demiurge a distinct divine agent, literally deliberating and building? A personification of the world soul's rationality? A figure for the intelligible model's own causal presence in the world? The dialogue never says, and the options map only loosely onto the literal–metaphorical divide: one can hold a literal creation by a figurative craftsman, or an eternal cosmos ordered by a real god. The Stanford Encyclopedia's survey concludes that the craftsman's intellect cannot simply be identified with the world soul (which is itself his product) or with a form (forms are objects of thought, not thinkers), and leaves him, in its phrase, sui generis. Readers should know that the most famous person in the dialogue is also its least identified.

V. The Demiurge and the Model

The argument that produces the craftsman is short and is given at 28a–29b (in paraphrase throughout this section). Everything that becomes, becomes by some cause. The cosmos is visible, tangible, and bodily; therefore it is perceptible; therefore it belongs to the class of things that become; therefore it has a cause — a maker. A maker works by looking to a model. If the model he looks to is eternal and unchanging, his work will be beautiful; if to something generated, it will not. The cosmos is the most beautiful of generated things; therefore its maker looked to the eternal. The maker receives his enduring name at 28a: the demiurge (dēmiourgos) — in ordinary Greek, a craftsman, a skilled public worker, the word for a potter or a smith raised to cosmic scale. Cornford and Zeyl both render him “the craftsman”; older translations' “Demiurge” has become his proper name in the literature, and this article uses both.

His motive is stated at 29e–30a, in the dialogue's most consequential piece of theology (in paraphrase): he was good, and in the good no envy or grudging ever arises; being free of envy, he wanted everything to become as like himself as possible. He found the visible world not at rest but moving in a discordant and disorderly fashion, and he brought it from disorder into order, judging order to be in every way better. Two features of this famous passage deserve emphasis, because later ages repeatedly flattened them. First, the craftsman does not create from nothing. He takes over what is already there — the verb matters — and his work is ordering, not summoning into existence. Whatever the Timaeus teaches, it is not creatio ex nihilo, and the medieval readers who found Genesis in it (Section XIII) were reading with an effort the text itself does not make. Second, his goodness is defined negatively and precisely: it is the absence of phthonos, grudging. The Platonic god is not a jealous god. In a religious landscape full of divine envy, the sentence is a manifesto, and the Hermetic and Neoplatonic traditions both built on it.

The Model: The Living Thing

The model to which the craftsman looks is named at 30c–31b (in paraphrase): the Living Thing (zōon) — the intelligible original that contains within itself all intelligible living things, as the cosmos contains all visible ones. The argument for the cosmos's unity runs off the model's: as the model is one and complete, embracing every kind of living being in the intelligible order, so its image must be one and complete, embracing every kind in the visible order. There cannot be two cosmoses, because there is no second model for a second cosmos to copy, and a cosmos that left some kind of creature outside itself would be, to that extent, an image of something less than the whole. Whether the Living Thing is a single form or an ordered constellation of forms — the form of Animal somehow containing the forms of all species — is a question the dialogue raises by its phrasing and does not resolve, and the scholarly literature (the Stanford Encyclopedia's survey states both options) leaves it open. What is not open is the structural role: the model is the standard by which every subsequent detail of the construction is justified. The cosmos has the features it has because the best possible image of that original, in this medium, must have them.

The medium imposes its first requirement at once (31b–32c, in paraphrase). The image must be bodily: therefore visible and tangible; therefore it must contain fire, by which things are seen, and earth, by which they are solid to the touch. But two extremes cannot be joined without a mean, and because the cosmos is a solid, not a plane, it requires two means — air and water — set in continuous proportion: as fire is to air, so air is to water, and as air is to water, so water is to earth. The four elements enter the dialogue, on its first presentation of them, not as a chemist's list but as a proportion — a bond (desmos) of mathematical friendship holding the world's body together. The point is foundational for this hub's subject, and it is worth pausing on: in the Timaeus, the fourfold scheme of the elements is justified geometrically before it is described physically. The elements of Section X, built from triangles, are the same doctrine at a deeper level — body resolved into number all the way down. What Intellect makes, it makes as a mathematician makes; the works of Necessity, when they come, will be what resists the proof.

// THE CRAFTSMAN'S LIMITS //

The demiurge of the Timaeus is not omnipotent, and the dialogue is careful about it. He works in a medium he did not make, with materials whose properties he does not choose, toward a perfection the medium only partly permits. The results are therefore always of the form: the best possible under the conditions. Later Platonism, embarrassed by a limited god, progressively allegorised the limitation away; later theology, reading the dialogue through Genesis, denied it outright. The text's own position is the craftsmanlike one, and it is the source of the dialogue's characteristic explanatory pattern — purpose achieved through constraint, intellect “persuading” necessity (48a) rather than abolishing it.

VI. The World Soul and Its Harmonic Ratios

A cosmos that is an image of a Living Thing must itself be alive, and a living thing that is to be intelligent must have a soul; intellect, Timaeus argues, cannot be present in anything apart from soul (30b, in paraphrase). The craftsman therefore compounds a world soul before he finishes the world's body, and places it at the centre, stretched throughout the whole and enveloping it from outside (34b, 36e, in paraphrase) — older than the body it animates, and its mistress, though the narrative, for instruction's sake, described the body first. The compounding of the soul (35a–b) is the dialogue's most metaphysically dense passage. In Zeyl's translation — the one verbatim quotation this article permits itself at length, verified in his 2000 edition's text as widely reprinted — the craftsman mixes, for each of the soul's three ingredients, an intermediate between the indivisible and the divisible:

“In between the Being that is indivisible and always changeless, and the one that is divisible and comes to be in the corporeal realm, he mixed a third, intermediate form of being, derived from the other two. Similarly, he made a mixture of the Same, and then one of the Different, in between their indivisible and their corporeal, divisible counterparts. And he took the three mixtures and mixed them together to make a uniform mixture, forcing the Different, which was hard to mix, into conformity with the Same.” — Timaeus 35a–b, translation Donald J. Zeyl (2000).

The mixture is thus made of Being, Same, and Different, each blended across the divide between the intelligible and the bodily — which is precisely what a soul must be, if it is to know both orders: akin to the indivisible in its grasp of what is eternal, akin to the divisible in its commerce with what becomes. The cognition of the world soul is then explained mechanically, and beautifully, by its geometry. The craftsman splits the blended strip lengthwise into two, crosses them like the letter X, and bends each into a circle: the circle of the Same, revolving uniformly in its own plane, and the circle of the Different, set at an angle and divided into seven unequal circles — the orbits of the moon, the sun, and the five planets known to Greek astronomy (36b–d, in paraphrase). When the soul's circles, in their revolutions, make contact with anything of the order of Being, judgements of sameness and difference run through the whole soul and constitute its knowledge; contact with the divisible order yields true opinion (37a–c, in paraphrase). Cosmology and epistemology are one mechanism: the heavens are the world soul thinking, made visible.

The Division: Soul as a Scale

Between the compounding and the bending comes the passage for which the dialogue was studied as a musical text for two thousand years: the harmonic division of the soul-stuff (35b–36b). In paraphrase of its procedure: the craftsman first cuts from the mixture a series of portions in two interleaved progressions — the doubles, 1, 2, 4, 8, and the triples, 1, 3, 9, 27 — the seven numbers the text gives in the order 1, 2, 3, 4, 9, 8, 27. (Ancient commentators, Crantor among them as Plutarch reports, arranged the series in the shape of the letter lambda, Λ, the doubles down one leg and the triples down the other, with 1 at the apex; the arrangement is a commentator's diagram, not Plato's drawing, and it is labelled as such here.) He then fills the intervals: into each double and triple interval he inserts the two means, harmonic and arithmetic, which generate the ratios of the fifth (3:2), the fourth (4:3), and the tone (9:8); and he fills each fourth with tones, leaving in each a residual interval, the leimma — the “leftover” — whose ratio is 256:243, the Pythagorean semitone. The result is a continuous scale spanning four octaves and a major sixth, built into the substance of the soul itself.

Why build a soul out of a scale? The dialogue's answer is its deepest piece of psychology. The soul knows by recognition of ratio: its revolutions are attuned to the ratios that structure the intelligible world, and the visible heavens — whose periods are those same ratios embodied in motion — are given to embodied souls as the instrument by which their own disordered revolutions may be re-attuned (47a–e, 90c–d, in paraphrase; Section XII returns to the therapy). The world soul is therefore not a metaphor laid over the astronomy. It is the claim that one and the same mathematical order runs in the heavens, in the knowing soul, and in the music that moves between them — the claim from which the whole later doctrine of the harmony of the spheres descends, and the reason the Timaeus was read for centuries as the charter text of mathematical harmonics, from the Pythagoreanising commentators through Boethius to Kepler, who took the dialogue's solids and its ratios together as the programme of his Harmonices Mundi (1619).

// A WARNING ABOUT THE NUMBERS //

The seven numbers and the ratios named above are the dialogue's own, and they are not in dispute. Almost everything beyond them is: the exact reconstruction of the scale (which octave species Plato intended, and in which direction it ran), the total number of terms in the fully filled series, and the question whether the division describes a musical scale at all or a more general mathematics of proportion have been argued from Crantor to the present commentary literature — Proclus's discussion of 35b–36b alone fills a volume of his commentary. This article gives the construction the text states, and declines, as the text's best modern translators do, to pretend the reconstruction is settled.

VII. Time, the Heavenly Gods, and the First Human Souls

The first movement of the discourse closes with the furnishing of the cosmos: time, the heavenly gods, and the mortal kinds. Each is treated here briefly, because each matters to the foundations this article documents, and none is its centre.

Time, the Moving Image

The model is eternal, and eternity — abiding in unity — cannot be given whole to a generated thing. So the craftsman makes, in the dialogue's most famous single figure (37d, in paraphrase), a moving image of eternity: time, proceeding according to number, of which days and nights, months and years are the parts, and for whose measurement and guardianship the heavenly bodies are made as instruments (38c, in paraphrase). The fixed stars are divine and eternal living beings, each a sphere of fire turning in place (40a–b); the planets — named in the dialogue's order as the moon, the sun, Venus and Mercury in the sun's train, and the three outer wanderers whose names the text leaves to those who know them (38c–d) — run their courses in the circle of the Different, at speeds and inclinations fitted to their ratios. Time, on this account, is not a container the cosmos sits in. It is a feature the cosmos has, generated with it — which is exactly why the question of Section IV, whether anything can be “before” the cosmos, is so much harder than it looks.

The Younger Gods and the Delegation

The heavenly bodies are gods — the dialogue calls them so without embarrassment (38e, 40d) — and to them the craftsman delegates the making of mortal creatures, in an address reported at 41a–d (in paraphrase): gods of gods, his works, of whom he is maker and father; what he has bound cannot be unbound except by his will, and his will is a greater bond than their composition. He compounds the immortal part of the mortal creatures himself — individual souls, mixed in the same bowl as the world soul but from the remnants of its mixture, second and third in purity (41d) — assigns each soul to a star, teaches them the laws of destiny, and leaves the weaving of mortal bodies around them to the younger gods. The delegation is a piece of theological engineering, and the text states its purpose: the maker of the whole must not be the direct author of the evil and mortality that embodied life involves (42d, in paraphrase). Individual souls that live justly return to their native stars; those that fail are reborn lower — as women, then as beasts, in a descending scale the dialogue sets out at its close (90e–92c) with a zoology of moral failure that modern readers find, variously, systematic, satirical, or repellent. This article reports the doctrine without decorating it: it is integral to the dialogue's ethics of embodiment, and its final Stephanus pages are the hinge on which the whole speech turns back into the frame — the human nature Critias required is now, at last, fully made.

// THE FIRST MOVEMENT'S SHAPE //

Works of Intellect, 29d–47e: the craftsman and the model; the world's body in its fourfold proportion; the world soul compounded, divided, and bent into its circles; the body joined to the soul; time and the heavenly gods; the making of human souls; and, as the movement's crown, the design of the eyes and the gift of sight — given, Timaeus says (47a–b, in paraphrase), so that by beholding the revolutions of intellect in the heavens we might correct the revolutions of thought in ourselves. Philosophy itself is the first movement's final product.

VIII. The Second Beginning: Necessity and the Receptacle

At 47e the discourse stops and starts again. What has been said so far, Timaeus announces (in paraphrase), displays the works of Intellect; but the cosmos is the offspring of a mixture — of Intellect and Necessity (anankē) together — and a complete account must set beside the divine cause the other kind of cause: the wandering cause, the errant, the way things behave of themselves when no purpose guides them (48a). Intellect rules Necessity, where it rules, by persuasion — by inducing her to guide the greatest part of what becomes toward the best (48a). The physics of the dialogue's second movement is the study of what had to be persuaded: the properties of materials, the mechanics of their interaction, everything in the world that is so not because it is good but because it cannot be otherwise. Modern scholarship, following the dialogue's own vocabulary, calls these the contributing causes (sunaitiai): real causes, without which the purposes of Intellect could not be effected at all, but causes that explain by compulsion, not by aim.

The second beginning immediately runs into a difficulty that forces the dialogue's boldest posit. The account so far has worked with two kinds: the intelligible model, and its visible copies. But consider, Timaeus argues (48e–49a, in paraphrase), what we actually say about the so-called elements. We watch the cycle of transformation — this appearing now as fire, now as condensed air, now as water, now as earth and stones, and round again (49b–c) — and the observation ruins the language of stable things: nothing that appears in the cycle keeps its identity long enough to be called “this” rather than “suchlike”. Fire is not a this; it is a recurring character. But the character recurs somewhere — in something that abides while the characters pass, receives them all, and is none of them. The discourse therefore requires, and Timaeus formally introduces, a third kind (triton genos, 48e): beside the model and the copy, the receptacle (hupodochē) of all becoming — “as it were its nurse” (49a).

What the Receptacle Is Said to Be

The characterisation that follows (49a–52d) is the most argued-over stretch of the dialogue, and this article reports its main strokes in paraphrase, marking the images as images. The receptacle is invisible and formless, all-receiving, and it never takes on any character of its own: like gold worked successively into every shape, which remains gold and is truly called by no shape's name (50a–b); like the neutral base-oil the perfumer prepares, which must be as odourless as possible if it is to receive every scent (50e); like soft stuff that takes impressions precisely because it keeps none (50c, 50e–51a). It is the mother, as the model is the father and the generated world the offspring (50d, 51a). And at 52a–b it receives the name under which philosophy has debated it ever since: chōra — space, room, the where — “everlasting, admitting no destruction, providing a seat for all that comes to be” (52b, in paraphrase). It is apprehended, Timaeus says in the dialogue's most quoted epistemological phrase (52b), not by the senses and not by understanding, but by a kind of “bastard reasoning” — an inference we cannot do without and cannot quite legitimate — and it is hardly an object of belief: we dream, as it were, when we say that everything that exists must be somewhere, in some place, occupying some room, and that what is neither on earth nor in heaven is nothing (52b–c, in paraphrase).

THE THREE KINDS (52d)

Timaeus' own summary, in paraphrase: three things, distinguished in three ways, existed even before the heaven came to be — being (the intelligible model), becoming (the generated copy), and chōra (the receptacle in which becoming occurs). The triad is the dialogue's final ontology, and it supersedes the two-kind scheme with which the discourse opened. Every later system that divides reality into form, matter, and their compounds is, knowingly or not, renegotiating this triad.

THE PRE-COSMIC SHAKING

Before the craftsman's ordering — in whatever sense of “before” Section IV permits — the receptacle was not at rest. Filled with powers neither alike nor evenly balanced, it was shaken by them and shook them in return, swaying unevenly like a winnowing basket (52e–53a, in paraphrase), so that the traces (ichnē, 53b) of the four kinds were perpetually sorted, like toward like, into the regions they would later occupy by design. The traces are the elements before geometry: fire-ish, air-ish, water-ish, earth-ish manifestations without stable corpuscles, measure, or number. Order, when it comes, will come as shape and number imposed on a motion that was never stillness.

The interpretive difficulties are real and are stated here rather than smoothed. The receptacle is said both to receive the copies of the forms and to be the place in which bodies are; the things that “enter and leave” it are described in terms that have never stopped generating commentary — forms themselves cannot enter it (52a is explicit that the model neither receives anything into itself nor enters into anything else), so what enters must be copies or characters of forms, and interpreters from Cherniss and Cornford onward have divided over exactly what a “copy in the receptacle” is, and over whether the receptacle is best understood as a material substratum, as space, or as a single posit doing both jobs at once. The Stanford Encyclopedia's survey of the question represents the current state of play: the analogies (gold, base-oil, impression-stuff) pull toward substratum; the name chōra and the function of providing a seat (hedra, 52b) pull toward space; and the text gives no sign of feeling the two as rivals. Plato, on the most defensible reading, did not distinguish matter and space because his physics did not yet need them distinguished. The distinction is the tradition's, and the next section follows what the tradition made of it.

IX. Is the Receptacle Matter? The Afterlife of Chōra

No question about the Timaeus has a longer afterlife than this one, and none requires more care in separating what Plato wrote from what his readers needed him to have written. The stakes are this hub's own: if the receptacle simply is prime matter, then Plato founded the matter-theory that Aristotle systematised and alchemy inherited, and the foundations cluster is a straight line. The evidence supports something more interesting and less straight.

Begin with the negative fact, which is not in dispute. Plato never calls the receptacle hulē — the word that means timber, stuff, and then, in Aristotle's technical coinage, matter. The dialogue's own words for it are receptacle, nurse, mother, impression-bearer, and chōra, space. Nor does the receptacle behave as Aristotelian matter behaves. Aristotle's prime matter is pure potentiality: it has no existence, motion, or character apart from the forms it receives, and it certainly does not do anything. Plato's receptacle, by contrast, is everlasting in its own right, exists before the ordering (in the story's terms), and is an agent in the physics: it shakes, it sorts, it sways its contents like toward like. It has, if the expression may be allowed, a career of its own, independent of the forms whose copies visit it. A posit with its own eternity and its own motion is not a blank potency, and the scholars who insist on the difference — among modern interpreters, those who read chōra primarily as space have pressed it hardest — have the text's behaviour of the thing on their side.

The identification nevertheless begins early, and it begins with the best of witnesses. Aristotle, in Physics IV.2 (209b), reports that Plato in the Timaeus identified matter and space — treating the receptacle, “the participant”, and chōra as one — and in On Generation and Corruption (II.1, 329a) he criticises the receptacle as an inadequate attempt at what his own theory of matter does properly. Aristotle's reading is testimony and appropriation at once: he is the first reader on record to assimilate the receptacle to hulē, and he does it in the act of replacing Plato's physics with his own. The assimilation then becomes the tradition's common sense. The Middle Platonists and Neoplatonists debated whether Plato's receptacle was matter, body, or space, generally in Aristotle's vocabulary; Calcidius's Latin rendered the receptacle's stuff as silva — Latin for wood, the calque of hulē — and through Calcidius the Latin Middle Ages read the Timaeus with a matter-concept already installed in the translation; the scholastic doctrine of materia prima, prime matter, was then available to be read back into the dialogue as its obvious meaning, and usually was.

// THE HONEST VERDICT //

The identification of chōra with prime matter is a later reading — inaugurated by Aristotle, transmitted by Calcidius's silva, and completed by scholasticism — not a doctrine stated in the dialogue. What the dialogue states is stranger: a third kind, neither form nor copy, which is place and recipient at once, characterless yet everlasting, known by a bastard reasoning and furnished with a motion of its own. Later philosophy needed a matter; the Timaeus gave it something it could mistake for one, and the mistake was among the most productive in the history of physics. This article accordingly uses “matter” of the receptacle only inside reports of later readings, never as the dialogue's own term — and the reader will find the same discipline in the companion article on Aristotle's matter theory, where hulē is at home.

The afterlife has a modern chapter as well, and it belongs in a full account. Twentieth-century philosophy rediscovered chōra precisely at the point where the matter-reading fails: in the receptacle's refusal to be any thing with characters, later thinkers found a figure for whatever precedes and conditions the order of forms without joining it — Jacques Derrida's essay Khōra (1993) is the most famous instance, treating the receptacle as that which gives place without itself taking a place in the system it enables. Whether that reading is Plato's is as disputable as any other in this section; that the dialogue's third kind still generates philosophy, in registers its author could not have foreseen, is a fact of its reception — and a measure of how deliberately underdetermined Plato left it. He apologised for its obscurity in advance (48e–49a): a difficult and obscure kind, he calls it, in paraphrase, before he has even named it. Twenty- four centuries of commentary constitute the apology's footnote.

X. The Elements as Regular Solids

The craftsman, coming to order the receptacle's traces, does not merely sort them. He shapes them — “by forms and numbers” (53b, in paraphrase) — and the shaping is the founding act of geometrical element theory. The reasoning, at 53c–55c (in paraphrase), runs as follows. Bodies are solids; solids are bounded by planes; planes are composed of triangles; and among triangles, two right-angled kinds are elementary: the isosceles right triangle (half of a square) and the scalene right triangle whose longer side is, in the modern statement, √3 times its shorter: the half-equilateral, the triangle got by bisecting an equilateral triangle. From these two triangles, and from no others, the faces of the regular solids can be built; and to the four kinds the craftsman assigns the four most beautiful solids that those faces make possible.

Three precisions keep the table honest. First, the assignments are argued, not arbitrary: at 55d–56b (in paraphrase) Timaeus reasons from each solid's geometry to the kind's observed behaviour — fire's sharpness and mobility from the tetrahedron's few faces and acute angles, earth's stability from the cube's square bases — so that the scheme is a genuine, if a priori, physical theory: the sensible properties of the kinds are to be explained by the shapes and sizes of their corpuscles, not merely labelled by them. Second, the corpuscles come in many sizes (57c–58c, in paraphrase): each kind comprises varieties differing in the magnitude of their elementary triangles, and from the varieties and their mixtures the dialogue derives the familiar species of the physical world — the fusible and liquid kinds of water, metals among them; the kinds of earth, stones and ceramics; the kinds of air, mist and aether-bright air; the kinds of fire, flame and light. Plato's “elements” are thus not four stuffs but four families of geometrical particles, and ordinary matter is always a mixture. Third, the solids are far below the threshold of sight: the elementary bodies are too small to be seen individually, and we perceive only their masses in aggregate (56c, in paraphrase). The Timaeus is, in the strictest sense, an atomic theory — with the decisive difference from Democritus that its atoms are not ultimate. They are built, and can be unbuilt, at the level of the triangle.

// THE DODECAHEDRON, WITHOUT THE LEGEND //

The fifth solid's single sentence (55c) — that the god used it for the whole, embroidering or painting it with figures — generated two millennia of superstructure: the dodecahedron as the shape of the heavens, as a fifth element (quintessence), as the key to cosmic architecture. The sentence itself assigns it no element, no triangles, and no physical role inside the cosmos, and Aristotle's aether — the genuinely influential fifth body — is a separate doctrine with its own arguments, treated in the companion article on Aristotle's matter theory. This article records the sentence and the legend separately, as the hub's method requires.

XI. Transformation by Triangle Arithmetic — and Why Earth Stands Outside

The geometrical construction pays its way at once, in the dialogue's explanation of the phenomenon with which the receptacle discussion began: the elements transform into one another. The mechanism (56c–57d, in paraphrase) is mechanical and exact. The corpuscles of the kinds, packed without void in the plenum of the cosmos and perpetually circulating, press upon, cut, and crush one another. A corpuscle broken up does not vanish: its faces come apart into their constituent elementary triangles, and the triangles — which neither the craftsman nor chance can alter in kind — recombine on the spot into new corpuscles, of whatever kind the arithmetic of their number permits. Transformation is thus neither the annihilation of one stuff nor the creation of another. It is reassembly, and its laws are the laws of counting.

The Arithmetic

Because fire, air, and water are all built from the one scalene half-equilateral triangle, their faces are mutually convertible, and the dialogue gives the exchange rates in faces (56d–e, in paraphrase). One water corpuscle, of twenty faces, dissolved, yields two air corpuscles and one of fire — sixteen faces and four, twenty in all. One air corpuscle, of eight faces, yields two of fire. Two fire corpuscles, conversely, combine into one of air; and so the cycle runs, upward by combination and downward by division, wherever the crushing of the plenum supplies the force. The quantities are conserved at the level that matters: not the corpuscle, which perishes, but the triangle, which does not. It is difficult to overstate the importance of this construction for the tradition this hub documents. Here, for the first time in surviving Greek thought, elemental transmutation has a mechanism, a conservation law, and a mathematics — and every later theory of transmutation, alchemical ones included, is a descendant, a rival, or a ruin of this one. The hub's article on the four elements follows the scheme's career; the alchemical articles follow what the workshops made of the licence it granted: that base kinds are not fixed in their nature, because nature herself unbuilds and rebuilds them daily.

// EARTH'S EXCLUSION, STATED EXACTLY //

Earth stands outside the cycle, and the dialogue is explicit about why (54c–d, 56d, in paraphrase): the cube is built from the other elementary triangle, the isosceles right triangle, and isosceles triangles can no more compose an equilateral face than scalene ones can compose a square. Earth corpuscles, broken up, can therefore reassemble only as earth. The exclusion is not an observation — empirically, earthy stuffs plainly do burn, dissolve, and vitrify — and it was already felt as a difficulty in antiquity; Aristotle criticised the whole triangle physics, and the exemption of earth in particular, in On the Heavens III and On Generation and Corruption II. It is a theorem, deduced from the construction, and it shows the construction's character better than any summary: Plato's physics prefers the integrity of its mathematics to the convenience of its phenomena, and says so by letting the deduction stand. The “likely story” has here produced a claim that is precise, false to appearances, and entirely rational — a combination its author, one suspects, understood perfectly well.

The same section of the dialogue (57c–68d, in paraphrase) works the corpuscular theory outward into a complete physics of qualities: the varieties of each kind and their compounds; the mechanics of motion in a plenum without void, where displaced bodies circulate into one another's places; the causes of heaviness and lightness — redefined, against common usage, by motion toward or away from a body's kindred region (62c–63e); and then, at length, the theory of sensation itself, in which colours, sounds, tastes, smells, heat and cold, pleasure and pain are each explained as the action of specific corpuscular shapes and motions on the specific structures of the sense organs (61c–68d). The pattern throughout is the second movement's pattern: given the triangles and their necessities, the appearances follow. Intellect chose the construction; Necessity, in the working out, does the rest — and is “persuaded”, in passage after passage, into results a teleologist can approve.

XII. The Human Frame: Marrow, Humours, and Disease

The third movement (69a–92c) turns the cosmology inward. The younger gods, having received the immortal soul from the craftsman's hands, weave the mortal around it: the mortal parts of the soul — the spirited part housed in the chest, the appetitive in the belly, the immortal itself in the spherical head, the body's acropolis (69c–70a, 90a, in paraphrase) — and the body's fabric entire: marrow, that seed-stuff compounded of the finest triangles in due proportion, from which brain, spine, and generative seed are all formed (73b–d); bone, flesh, sinew, skin, hair, nails; the heart as guardhouse and fountain of the blood's circuit; the lungs as its cushion; the liver, smooth and bright, as the mirror on which the intellect's images are cast for the appetitive soul that cannot understand words (71a–72d). The anatomy is fanciful in detail and revolutionary in method: every organ is explained twice over, once by its purpose and once by its materials — Intellect's design executed under Necessity's constraints — exactly as the cosmology was.

Disease: The Elements Out of Order

The dialogue's last third contains the pathology toward which this whole article has been a preparation, and it is stated here with a pointer, because the hub treats its afterlife in full elsewhere. Diseases of the body are classified at 81e–86a (in paraphrase) in three ascending orders. First, diseases of the primary bodies themselves: any of the four kinds present in excess or deficiency, or displaced from its own region into an alien one, disorders the whole economy, since the body's order is nothing but the kinds in their proportions and places (81e–82b). Second, diseases of the compounded tissues — marrow, bone, flesh, sinew — when their formation runs in reverse and they decay out of their natural sequence (82b–84c). Third, and most elaborately, the diseases wrought by breath, phlegm, and bile (84c–86a): air trapped and forcing its passages; phlegm, acid and briny, loosening the flesh's fastenings; bile, in its several kinds, inflaming and dispersing, its darkened mixtures with blood producing the fevers — and the fevers themselves are timed by the elements, excess of fire giving continuous fever, of air the quotidian, of water the tertian, of earth, slowest to purge, the quartan (86a, in paraphrase).

Diseases of the soul follow, and they are the dialogue's ethical climax (86b–87b, in paraphrase): folly — anoia — in its two kinds, madness and ignorance, arises in large part from the body. Excessive pleasures and pains derange the soul's revolutions; acid and briny phlegm and bitter bilious humours, wandering the body without vent, mingle their vapours with the motions of the soul in its three seats and produce, according to the seat assailed, ill-temper and melancholy, rashness and cowardice, forgetfulness and stupidity. The moral consequence is drawn in the dialogue's most Socratic sentence, paraphrased at every retelling: no one is willingly bad; the bad man becomes bad through a faulty bodily constitution and a defective upbringing, and blame belongs in large part to the planters rather than the plant, the educators rather than the educated (86d–87b). The cure prescribed is proportion: soul and body exercised in due ratio to one another, neither moved without the other (87c–89d), and above all the re-attunement of the soul's revolutions to the revolutions of the cosmos by the study of its harmonies (90c–d) — the world soul's mathematics returning, at the scale of a single life, as medicine.

// THE HUMOURS, WITH A WARNING //

It is tempting — and common in popular accounts — to call the Timaeus the source of the four-humour doctrine. The text does not support the promotion. Plato's pathology works with phlegm and bile (in several kinds) inside a physics of the four elements; the canonical scheme of four humours — blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile — correlated with the elements, the seasons, and the temperaments is the work of the Hippocratic tradition and, above all, of Galen's systematisation of it, five centuries later. The dialogue's genuine bequest to medicine is structural rather than doctrinal: disease as disorder of proportion among the body's constituents, and soul-sickness as body-sickness understood. The full account — Hippocratic humours, Galen's system, and its Arabic and Latin career — is the hub's article on the humours and Galen, to which this section is the pointer.

XIII. Transmission I: Cicero, Calcidius, and the Latin Millennium

The Timaeus was famous in its own school from the start — Crantor, within a generation of Plato's death, wrote the first commentary on it, now lost — and its Latin career begins with Rome's greatest prose writer. Cicero, in the last period of his life (c. 45 BCE), translated a substantial portion of the dialogue into Latin: his version, which survives, covers Timaeus' discourse from its metaphysical opening through the works of Intellect (roughly 27d–47b in the usual reckoning), without the frame and without the entire second half. It is a translator's monument — Cicero recasting Plato's Greek periods in his own — but it was not the channel of the dialogue's medieval life: it circulated thinly, and the Middle Ages knew the Timaeus overwhelmingly through the second Latin version.

That version is Calcidius's. In the early fourth century CE — the dedication is addressed to an Osius, usually identified with Hosius of Córdoba, the bishop who advised Constantine — Calcidius translated the dialogue from 17a to 53c: the frame, Atlantis and all, the demiurge, the world soul, and the account as far as the receptacle and the first construction of the solids — and equipped it with a long commentary, itself a small library of Middle Platonic astronomy, harmonics, psychology, and doctrine on fate and matter. The consequences are difficult to exaggerate. For roughly eight hundred years, Calcidius's partial Timaeus was, as the Stanford Encyclopedia's history of medieval philosophy puts it, for all practical purposes the Latin West's Plato: the one extensive Platonic text continuously available between late antiquity and the twelfth century, when Henricus Aristippus's translations of the Meno and Phaedo (c. 1156) — themselves little read — first kept it company, and Ficino's complete Plato still lay three centuries ahead. The hub's Transmission article follows the broader traffic of texts east and west; the Timaeus's Latin road, treated here, is that traffic's most consequential single lane.

WHAT THE LATIN WEST HAD

Through Calcidius: the frame and the Atlantis summary; the demiurge and the model; the world's body and soul with its harmonic division; time and the heavenly gods; the making of human souls; and, at the very end of the translation's reach (53c), the threshold of the receptacle and the solids. Enough, in short, for a complete Christian-Platonic cosmology of creation — and medieval readers, the masters of Chartres above all (Thierry of Chartres, William of Conches), built exactly that on it, reading the craftsman's work in the light of Genesis.

WHAT IT DID NOT HAVE

Everything from 53c to the end: the full construction of the elements and their transformation, the physics of qualities and sensation, the anatomy, and the pathology of Section XII. The Latin Middle Ages' Plato is therefore a curiously truncated figure — cosmogonist and psychologist of the world soul, but not the triangle physicist or the physician. The geometrical element theory reached Latin readers by other roads: through Aristotle's criticism of it, through Calcidius's commentary paraphrase, and through the Arabic tradition (Section XIV). A history of “Platonism” that ignores the cut at 53c will misunderstand what medieval Platonism was.

Calcidius's commentary deserves its own sentence of justice. It was, in several medieval centuries, read more than the text it served, and it carried into Latin a cargo of Greek science — the astronomy of the planetary periods, the harmonics of the world soul's division, accounts of earlier cosmology — that Latin Europe possessed from no other source. When the twelfth-century schools made the Timaeus the charter of a new natural philosophy, it was Calcidius's Plato they read: a Plato already interpreted, already harmonised with a millennium of commentary, already furnished with silva for matter (Section IX). The dialogue the Renaissance would recover in Greek was, among other things, an escape from its own Latin shadow.

XIV. Transmission II: The Greek East, the Arabic Timaeus, and Proclus

In the Greek East the dialogue never needed recovering. It stood at the summit of the late-antique philosophical curriculum — the Neoplatonic schools read it last or near-last among Plato's works, as the crown of physics and theology together — and it attracted the commentary tradition's full weight. The culmination is Proclus (412–485), whose Commentary on the Timaeus, the largest surviving ancient commentary on any Platonic dialogue, expounds the text lemma by lemma with the whole of earlier Platonism, Orphic theology, and Greek astronomy marshalled in its support; the surviving portion carries the exposition as far as 44d, and even in its truncated state it fills five volumes in the modern Cambridge translation. Proclus reads the creation metaphorically, the demiurge theologically, and the dialogue entire as a sacred text of the Platonic succession — the reading whose dominance in the Greek tradition Section IV recorded. His commentary, and the theurgic Neoplatonism it serves, are treated in the hub's article on Neoplatonism and theurgy; here it is enough to mark that for the Byzantine millennium, to read the Timaeus was to read it through Proclus.

The Arabic Timaeus

The dialogue's Arabic career is real, important, and easily overstated, and this article states it with the care the Stanford Encyclopedia's survey of Greek sources in Arabic philosophy warrants. Arabic readers knew the Timaeus chiefly through two channels. The first is Galen's Compendium of the dialogue — Galen had written both a compendium of the Timaeus and a commentary on its medical passages — and the compendium circulated in Arabic in the translation tradition of Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq (d. 873) and his circle, Ḥunayn having rendered it into Syriac and his associate ʿĪsā ibn Yaḥyā into Arabic; Galen's epitome, not Plato's full text, is the form in which the dialogue's cosmology and physiology chiefly entered Arabic medicine and philosophy. The second channel is translation of the dialogue itself: the bibliographer Ibn al-Nadīm's Fihrist records a translation by Yaḥyā ibn al-Biṭrīq, made in the circle of al-Kindī, which Ḥunayn is said either to have corrected or to have superseded with a version of his own; neither version survives, and the Arabic Timaeus is a documented presence rather than an extant book. A fragment of Proclus's commentary (on 89e–90c, the dialogue's closing exhortation) also circulated in Arabic, its translator unknown. What Arabic philosophy made of the dialogue arrived, therefore, filtered — through a physician's epitome and a lost translation — and it is Galen's Plato, the Plato of the soul's mixtures and the body's proportions, who stands behind the dialogue's substantial Arabic afterlife in medicine and in the cosmology of the falāsifa. The pattern is the transmission pattern of this whole hub: what travels is not always the text, but the text as its carriers needed it.

XV. The Timaeus Among Hermetists and Alchemists

It remains to close the loop this article opened: to say why a dialogue of Plato's stands at the foundation of an alchemy wing, in a hub whose presiding name is Hermes. The connections are not decorative, and they run in both directions along the whole length of the tradition the wing documents.

First, the cosmos. The Timaeus gave the ancient world its standard picture of a living universe: one animal, ensouled throughout, its parts bound by proportion and sympathy, its heavens divine and its elements in perpetual lawful circulation. That picture is the presupposition of the Hermetic literature treated in this hub's article on the Hermetica — the Poimandrēs's cosmos, made by Mind through a craftsman, governed through the spheres, and ascended by the soul that knows itself, is the Timaeus's world read in a devotional register, two centuries on and in Roman Egypt — and it is equally the presupposition of Alexandrian alchemy, whose operations assume a cosmos in which all bodies are kin, compounded of the same kinds, and open to the same transformations. Hermetists and alchemists did not merely respect the dialogue. They lived inside its physics.

Second, the transformation. Alchemy's founding wager — that the kinds of body are not fixed, that base matter can be unmade and remade toward perfection — has many sources, but its first rigorous statement is Section XI's: corpuscles dissolved to triangles and reassembled by number, in a cosmos where such dissolution and reassembly are the ordinary metabolism of nature. Plato himself drew no furnace conclusions from the doctrine, and earth, the alchemists' own starting material, stands excluded from his cycle (a difficulty the tradition worked around, chiefly by following Aristotle's more permissive element theory instead — see the hub's four elements and Aristotle's matter theory articles). But the form of alchemical theory — hidden geometrical or proportional constitutions beneath sensible qualities; change as the rearrangement of constituents; perfection as proportion restored — is the Timaeus's form, and Zosimos and his successors wrote as its heirs even when they cited Hermes.

Third, the authority. In the genealogy of the prisca theologia — the ancient theology that Renaissance Hermetism, as the Hermetica article sets out, traced from Hermes through Orpheus and Pythagoras to Plato — the Timaeus occupied the position of Plato's most Hermetic work: the dialogue in which Plato himself seemed to teach what Hermes had taught, a made and living cosmos, a world soul, a divine craftsman, the soul's descent and return. Ficino, who had translated Hermes before Plato at Cosimo's command, commented on the Timaeus within his Plato commentaries and read it in that succession; the dialogue's demiurge and world soul stand behind the Renaissance natural magic whose theory, as the Hermetica article is careful to note, its practitioners drew chiefly from the Neoplatonists — Proclus's Timaeus commentary among them — rather than from the Corpus Hermeticum itself. The loop closes exactly there: the dialogue that founded element theory also furnished, through its commentators, the cosmology in which Hermetic and alchemical practice understood itself. Foundations are rarely so load-bearing for so long.

// READING THE TIMAEUS TODAY //

Read it in Zeyl (2000) for a lucid modern text with a full analytical introduction, or in Cornford (1937) for the translation bound to the century's most influential commentary — knowing that Cornford's commentary argues the metaphorical reading of Section IV as it goes. Read it in the dialogue's own order, and do not skip the second beginning at 47e: the receptacle and the triangles are the price of admission to everything the later tradition built. And read it with its grade attached — a likely story, offered by a speaker who tells you exactly how much certainty a cosmos can bear.

XVI. Sources

The works below were consulted in the preparation of this article. Zeyl (2000) and Cornford (1937) are the backbone translations; the single verbatim quotation (35a–b, Section VI) is Zeyl's, named at the quotation. All other renderings of the dialogue's contents are paraphrases, labelled as such in the text and keyed to Stephanus pagination. The Stanford Encyclopedia's article on the dialogue, read in full, supplied the modern scholarly framework — the threefold division of the discourse, the status of the “likely account”, the receptacle debate, the triangle physics, and the teleology discussion — against which the translations were checked.
