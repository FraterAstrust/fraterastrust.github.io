I. The Foundation Beneath the Art: Why Aristotle Comes First

This article opens a new cluster in the Alchemy wing of the Greco-Roman hub — the foundations cluster. The articles it stands beside treat the practitioners: the workshop recipes of Bolos and pseudo-Democritus in Greek Alchemy: Origins, the visions and apparatus of Zosimos of Panopolis, the professors of The Commentators. This article treats none of them. It treats the philosophy underneath all of them — the account of what bodies are, how they change into one another, and why a mixture is more than a heap — that Greek alchemy did not invent, but inherited, assumed, quoted, commented on, and eventually argued with for nearly two thousand years. That philosophy is, in its essentials, one man's: Aristotle of Stagira (384–322 BCE).

The claim needs stating precisely, because it is easily overstated in both directions. Aristotle wrote no alchemical work. Nothing in his surviving corpus is a recipe, a furnace manual, or a promise to make gold. The works that travelled under his name promising exactly those things are pseudepigrapha, and Section XIV of this article flags them as such, one by one. What Aristotle wrote was a natural philosophy — a systematic account of change, matter, form, and the composition of bodies — inside which alchemy, when it emerged in Hellenistic and Roman Egypt some centuries later, found its working assumptions already built. When Zosimos speaks of bodies and spirits, when the commentators lecture on the unity of matter, when an Arabic author explains that metals grow in the earth from two opposed principles, the vocabulary, the distinctions, and often the very sentences being glossed are Aristotle's. To read the alchemists without him is to read conclusions without their premises.

WHAT ARISTOTLE SUPPLIED

The analysis of change into matter, form, and privation; the four elementary qualities — hot, cold, wet, dry — as powers that act and are acted on; the four simple bodies built from them; the doctrine of true mixture (mixis), in which ingredients cease to exist actually and persist potentially; the two exhalations and the underground generation of metals; and, in Meteorologica IV, the first systematic account of the homogeneous bodies and their dispositions — fusible, malleable, fissile, combustible, and the rest.

WHAT HE DID NOT SUPPLY

No philosopher's stone. No elixir. No sulphur–mercury theory of metals in those terms — that theory is an Arabic development whose Greek ancestry this article reconstructs carefully, and as a reconstruction, in Section XIII. No doctrine that art can perfect metals into gold stated as his own conclusion: the question of transmutation is one his framework makes discussable, not one he settles in the alchemists' favour. And no alchemical work of any kind under his own hand.

One framing distinction governs everything that follows, and the alchemical tradition's confusion of the two is responsible for half its mythology. There is the historical Aristotle: a fourth-century BCE philosopher, Plato's pupil and critic, founder of the Lyceum, whose lecture texts survive in the corpus edited in antiquity and transmitted through Greek, Syriac, Arabic, and Latin copies. And there is “Aristotle” the authority: the name under which late-antique, Arabic, and medieval writers published works he never wrote — lapidaries, letters to Alexander, secrets of secrets — because his name had become the strongest warrant a natural-philosophical claim could carry. Both figures shaped alchemy. Only the first is the subject of Sections II–XIII. The second gets his own section (XIV), under his correct label: pseudepigraphy.

II. The Corpus in Its Own Order: Physics, Generation and Corruption, Meteorologica

Aristotle's natural philosophy is not a single book but an ordered course of study, and he tells the reader the order himself. The opening chapter of the Meteorologica (I.1, 338a20–339a9, in paraphrase) looks back over the works that precede it and forward to those that follow: first the Physics, on the principles of nature and change in general; then On the Heavens (De Caelo), on the cosmos and its fifth element; then On Generation and Corruption, on the elements, their mutual transformation, and the general processes of coming-to-be and passing-away; then the Meteorologica itself; and after it, the works on animals and plants. H. D. P. Lee's introduction to the Loeb Meteorologica sets out this sequence, and it matters for alchemy for a simple reason: the texts the alchemical tradition used most heavily are the last three members of the physical sequence — the works where general principles descend into particular kinds of body. A reader who begins, as this article does, with the Physics is following Aristotle's own syllabus.

The Man, in the Minimum the Subject Requires

Aristotle was born in 384 BCE at Stagira, a Greek city on the Macedonian coast; his father, Nicomachus, was physician to the Macedonian king Amyntas III. At seventeen or eighteen he came to Athens and joined Plato's Academy, where he remained for some twenty years, until Plato's death in 347 BCE. After years in Asia Minor and Lesbos — the Lesbos years, spent partly on the marine biology that fills the zoological works, are the empirical root of his natural philosophy — and a period as tutor to the young Alexander of Macedon, he returned to Athens in 335 BCE and founded his own school in the precinct of the Lyceum. He left Athens in 323, on Alexander's death, and died at Chalcis in 322 BCE. The works that survive are, on the standard scholarly account, not books he published for a general readership — those “exoteric” works, much admired in antiquity, are lost — but the treatises of the school: lecture texts, working papers, and research dossiers, edited into the corpus we have by later hands, traditionally by Andronicus of Rhodes in the first century BCE. That origin explains their manner — compressed, technical, full of cross-reference — and, in part, their textual problems, of which Meteorologica IV (Section XII) is the most consequential for this hub.

That inverted reading is itself a historical fact with consequences — it is why the Aristotle of the alchemists can look, to a reader of the whole corpus, simultaneously faithful and strangely partial. He is being read through his last chapters.

III. Hylomorphism: Form, Matter, and Privation

The doctrine modern scholarship calls hylomorphism — from hylē, matter, and morphē, form — is Aristotle's answer to a problem he inherited from the earliest Greek philosophy: how is change possible at all? Parmenides had argued, with a rigour Aristotle never ceased to respect, that what-is cannot come from what-is-not: being cannot arise from non-being, nor perish into it. His successors had multiplied elements, atoms, and seeds to evade the conclusion. Aristotle's solution, set out in Physics I.7–9 (in paraphrase throughout this section), is an analysis rather than a substance. Every change, he argues, involves three principles: something that underlies and persists through the change (the substratum, hypokeimenon); a form (eidos, morphē) that is acquired; and a privation (sterēsis) — the absence of that form in the substratum — that is lost. A man becomes musical: the man persists, musicality is acquired, unmusicality is shed. Bronze becomes a statue: the bronze persists, the statue's shape is acquired, shapelessness in that respect is shed. Nothing has come from nothing. The form was not created out of void; it came to be in a matter that was already there, capable of receiving it.

Matter (hylē — the word's ordinary Greek sense is timber, wood as material) is the name for the underlying principle considered as that out of which a thing comes to be and which persists in the result. Form is the name for what the thing becomes: its structure, its defining organisation, in the limiting case its essence — what makes this bronze a statue of Hermes rather than a lump, and this organised body a human being rather than a heap of tissues. Neither principle is, in general, a separate thing. Bronze can exist without being a statue, because bronze is itself already a compound of matter and form at a lower level (Section V follows the regress down); but the form of a living thing — its soul, in Aristotle's technical use — cannot exist apart from a body it organises, and the matter of a living thing, considered precisely as its matter, is nothing but its capacity to be so organised. Matter and form are principles distinguished in analysis, correlative to one another, not two ingredients shaken together in a vessel. Every later misunderstanding of Aristotle in the alchemical tradition — and there are distinguished ones — begins by forgetting that sentence and treating his matter as a stuff among stuffs.

// MATTER IS SAID IN MANY WAYS — AND THE LEVELS MATTER //

“Matter” in Aristotle is relative to a level of analysis. Bronze is the matter of the statue; copper and tin, in turn, are the matter of the bronze; the homogeneous bodies are the matter of the organs; the elements are the matter of the homogeneous bodies. At each level, the matter of that level is itself a formed substance one level down. The question whether the regress terminates — whether there is a first or prime matter that is matter and nothing else — is the question of Section V, and it is a genuinely open one. What is not open is the structure: hylomorphism is a ladder, and most of Aristotle's own uses of “matter” stand on its middle rungs.

The analysis distinguishes, at the same stroke, the kinds of change. In alteration, a substance persists and exchanges one quality for its contrary: the same body grows hot, then cold. In growth and diminution, it exchanges quantity; in locomotion, place. In generation and corruption — coming-to-be and passing-away without qualification — what changes is the substance itself: water becomes air, a living thing dies and its body resolves toward the elements. Here the substratum cannot be the substance that perishes, since ex hypothesi it does not persist as that substance; it is the substance's matter that underlies the exchange of substantial forms. On Generation and Corruption I.4 (319a–320a, in paraphrase) draws the line exactly there: alteration is change in the properties of a perceptible substratum that remains the same, generation is change of the whole, when nothing perceptible remains as the same substratum. The distinction is the hinge of Aristotle's chemistry, if the anachronism may be allowed once and then dropped: alteration rearranges a body's qualities, but generation replaces what the body is — and transmutation, as the alchemists would later conceive it, claims to be generation at the level of the metals. Aristotle's framework is what makes that claim formulable, and also what makes it hard: a true generation requires the old form to be genuinely lost and a new one genuinely acquired, not a surface repainted. The workshop art of colouring metals — the oldest stratum of the tradition, treated in Greek Alchemy: Origins — lives, in Aristotelian terms, on the alteration side of the line, and the whole later theoretical effort of alchemy can be read as an attempt to cross it honestly.

Finally, nature itself is defined inside this analysis. A natural thing, for Aristotle (Physics II.1, in paraphrase), is one that has within itself a principle of motion and rest — of change and of stability — whereas an artefact has its principle outside itself, in the craftsman. A bed does not beget a bed; a vine begets a vine. The definition will matter in Section XI, because Meteorologica IV's central process, concoction, is precisely the working of a thing's own internal heat upon its own matter — nature operating as an internal craftsman — and the analogy between that internal heat and the artisan's external fire becomes one of the deepest channels through which Aristotelian physics flows into laboratory practice. Art imitates nature, in this tradition, not as a slogan but as a piece of physics: the furnace is an attempt to supply, from outside, the mastery of heat over matter that a living body supplies from within.

IV. The Four Causes, and Potentiality and Actuality

To explain a thing, for Aristotle, is to answer the question “why?” — and “why?” has, he argues in Physics II.3 and Metaphysics V.2, four irreducibly different kinds of answer. The Greek word is aitia, conventionally translated “cause”, though “explanatory factor” is closer to its breadth. The four, stated in paraphrase, are these. The material cause: that out of which a thing comes to be and which persists in it — the bronze of the statue, the timbers of the house. The formal cause: the thing's form or essence, what it is to be that thing — the statue's shape, the house's plan, the living thing's soul. The efficient or moving cause: the primary source of the change — the sculptor, the builder, the father. And the final cause: the end, that for the sake of which — the statue as commemoration, the house as shelter, the acorn's oak. Matter and form are intrinsic to the thing explained; mover and end may stand outside it. A complete natural philosophy must be able to give all four, and Aristotle's standing complaint against his predecessors is that each school grasped one or two and mistook them for the whole.

MATERIAL CAUSE

That out of which. Bronze, timber, the elements, the homogeneous bodies. The alchemists' Aristotle is disproportionately the Aristotle of this cause — which is exactly what Sections IX–XIII document.

FORMAL CAUSE

What it is. Essence, structure, the ratio of a mixture. When a medieval author asks whether art can change a metal's species, he is asking about this cause in Aristotle's vocabulary.

EFFICIENT & FINAL CAUSES

That by which, and that for the sake of which. Heat is Aristotle's great efficient instrument in the sublunar world; the final cause is, by his own remark in Meteorologica IV.12, least evident precisely where matter predominates — a sentence the authenticity debate (Section XII) put to hard use.

Bound to the four causes is the pair of concepts that does the deepest work in the matter theory: potentiality (dynamis) and actuality (energeia, entelecheia). Matter, Aristotle says in Metaphysics H.6 and Θ (in paraphrase), is what a thing is potentially; form is what it is actually. The bronze is potentially a statue before the sculptor works, and the statue is that potential actualised. The concepts are not decorative. They are the machinery that will let Aristotle say, in the mixture theory of Section VIII, that the ingredients of a true mixture are present in it potentially but not actually — neither annihilated nor merely hidden — and recoverable from it because they were never actually destroyed. Nearly every sophisticated later theory of metallic composition is a variation on that one modal distinction. When the Latin tradition renders it as in potentia versus in actu, it is translating the hinge on which Aristotelian chemistry turns.

It is worth pausing on what this apparatus excludes, because the exclusions are as influential as the doctrines. Aristotle rejects the void: a place with nothing in it plays no role in his physics, and his arguments against it (Physics IV.6–9) set the terms on which atomism was refused for two millennia. He rejects atoms as the explanation of body: indivisible magnitudes cannot, he argues in On Generation and Corruption I.2 and On the Heavens III, do the work required of them — they cannot alter, cannot genuinely mix, cannot account for the continuity of growth and qualitative change. And he rejects the separate existence of Platonic Forms: form, for Aristotle, exists in the matter it informs, not in a heaven of its own. What remains, when the exclusions are made, is a continuum physics of qualities, powers, and proportions — a physics in which the smallest scale of explanation is not a particle but a ratio. The irony that later alchemy would rebuild a corpuscular theory out of Aristotelian materials — the story William R. Newman tells in Atoms and Alchemy (2006), taken up in Sections VIII and XIII — is one of the great reversals in the history of matter theory, and it begins here, in what Aristotle refused.

V. Prime Matter (Prōtē Hylē): The Doctrine and the Dispute Over Whether It Exists

Follow the ladder of Section III downward. Bronze is matter for the statue and form for its own ingredients; flesh is matter for the organ and a compound of elements; the elements — earth, water, air, fire — are matter for everything sublunar. But the elements themselves change into one another: water becomes air, air becomes fire, in the cycle On Generation and Corruption II.4 describes. Generation requires a substratum (Section III). What, then, underlies elemental change? When water, as a substance, perishes and air comes to be, what persists? There is no lower rung of formed body to point to. The answer given by the long tradition of Aristotle's readers — Greek, Arabic, and Latin — is prime matter: Greek prōtē hylē, “first matter”, a substratum that is the matter of the elements themselves, itself possessed of no form, no quality, no quantity, no determination of any kind — pure potentiality, never existing separately, existing only as actualised in one element or another, and persisting through their transformations as the bare capacity to be first one and then the other. On this reading, prime matter is the bottom of the ladder: matter that is not also a formed something.

The key text is On Generation and Corruption II.1 (329a24–35). In paraphrase — the passage is compressed and its translation disputed at exactly the points that matter, so this article paraphrases rather than quotes it — Aristotle says that there is a matter of the perceptible bodies, out of which the so-called elements come to be, but that it has no separate existence and is always bound up with a contrariety; that the substratum, not either contrary, is the matter for both contraries; and that the principles are therefore three: the potentially perceptible body, the contrarieties, and fire, water, and the like as already-formed bodies. Metaphysics Z.3 (1029a) contributes the famous thought-experiment of stripping away: take a body and remove, in thought, its lengths, breadths, depths, and every affection, and what is left — matter, defined there by a via negativa as that which in itself is neither a particular thing nor a quantity nor any other category — cannot, Aristotle himself concludes in that chapter, be substance in the primary sense either. The traditional reading takes GC II.1's inseparable, contrariety-bound matter, informed successively by the elementary qualities, to be exactly this prime matter, and finds the doctrine already in the Physics' analysis of change, which requires an underlying subject for every generation, elemental generation included.

The Traditional Reading and Its Pedigree

The traditional interpretation is not a medieval invention imposed on an innocent text. It is ancient. The Greek commentators read Aristotle this way — Simplicius, in his commentary on the Physics, treats prime matter as the substratum of elemental change — and the reading passes through the Arabic tradition into Latin scholasticism, where it becomes load-bearing: Aquinas's De Principiis Naturae (§13 in the usual citation, as the modern reference literature notes) states prime matter as pure potentiality in terms the schools would repeat for centuries. For the alchemical tradition the doctrine did specific work. If all metals share one prime matter, differentiated only by the forms and quality-ratios imposed on it, then the difference between lead and gold is, at bottom, a difference of form and proportion in a common substrate — and what differs only in form and proportion is, in principle, reformable. Prime matter is the metaphysical licence under which transmutation theory operated. It is not too much to say that medieval alchemy's theoretical respectability is the traditional reading of GC II.1, put to work.

The Challenge: King, Charlton, and the Revisionists

In the middle of the twentieth century the traditional reading was attacked, twice and independently, and the attack has never been fully answered or fully accepted. H. R. King, in the Journal of the History of Ideas in 1956, argued against attributing a doctrine of prime matter to Aristotle; Friedrich Solmsen replied critically in the same journal in 1958, and A. R. Lacey again in 1965. The more influential assault came from William Charlton, in the appendix on prime matter in his Clarendon edition of Physics Books I–II (1970). Charlton's case, stated here in paraphrase as his scholarly position, is that Aristotle neither needs nor holds a doctrine of a characterless substratum beneath the elements: that the analysis of change in Physics I requires a persisting subject for accidental change — a substance — and a material cause for generation, but that in substantial generation the matter that persists is always some identifiable stuff (as bronze persists through the statue's making and unmaking), and that elemental transformation can be understood without positing a further, quality-less entity beneath the elements themselves. On the revisionist alternative that King and Charlton share — the point is made explicitly in H. M. Robinson's 1974 defence, below — the elements themselves are the most fundamental kind of matter there is; the regress simply stops at them, and “the matter of the elements” in GC II.1 names not a stuff beneath them but their own material aspect, inseparable from the contrarieties that constitute them.

The defence came promptly. H. M. Robinson, “Prime Matter in Aristotle”, Phronesis 19 (1974), pp. 168–188 — read in full for this article — argues, in paraphrase, that the revisionist alternative fails on the texts: that Aristotle's own principles require something to remain through a change if the change is to be a change of anything; that elemental transformation, in which no element and no perceptible body persists, therefore requires a substratum that is not itself an element; and that GC II.1, 329a24–35, read without revisionist presuppositions, posits exactly such a prime matter — a bare potentiality, existing only as actualised in a determinate element, which is what persists when one contrariety replaces another and an element's identity changes. Robinson concedes what the traditional reading always conceded: prime matter is not a body in its own right, not separable from the elements, and not a geometrical or atomistic substrate. His claim is the narrower, stronger one that the revisionists, having rejected it, have nothing coherent to put in its place as the most fundamental form of matter. Later scholarship has multiplied positions rather than converging: Mary Louise Gill's Aristotle on Substance (1989) develops a revisionist account on which the substratum of substantial change does not persist in the manner the tradition supposed; other scholars — S. Marc Cohen and Christopher Byrne among them, as the recent literature summarises — have tried to salvage a prime matter with minimal essential characteristics of its own, such as extension or corporeality; Daniel Graham has argued the notion incoherent, on the ground that what is deprived of all characteristics is simply nothing; and David Ebrey's recent work on the matter of the elements argues, in paraphrase, for a middle position: that the matter for elemental transformation is a genuine common matter, but a specific potentiality — a potentiality to become hot or cold, wet or dry — rather than a pure potentiality to become anything whatsoever.

// THE POSITION OF THIS ARTICLE //

The dispute is live, technical, and unresolved, and this article takes no side in it — because for the history of alchemy, the dispute has a peculiar asymmetry. Whatever Aristotle himself held, the tradition that built alchemy held the traditional reading. Simplicius, the Arabic commentators, and the Latin schools read a prime matter out of — or into — GC II.1, and it is their Aristotle, prime matter included, who stands behind Jabirian and Latin matter theory. The historian of alchemy must therefore report both facts without collapsing either: that prime matter as a characterless stuff may be a construction of Aristotle's readers rather than a doctrine of Aristotle, and that as a construction it is ancient, textually motivated, and historically enormous. Popular accounts that present “Aristotle's prime matter, the formless stuff of all things” as a settled datum of his system are repeating the tradition's Aristotle while believing they are repeating the text's. The text, at 329a, is eleven lines long and has supported two millennia of disagreement. That is the accurate sentence, and it is more interesting than the flattened one.

One distinction, finally, protects the rest of this article from the dispute. Most of what alchemy took from Aristotle does not depend on prime matter at all. The quality square (Section VII), the mixture theory (Section VIII), and the exhalation theory (Sections IX–X) operate at the level of the elements and their qualities — the middle rungs of the ladder, where Aristotle's meaning is comparatively clear and the scholarly quarrels are correspondingly smaller. Prime matter is the foundation stone the tradition laid under the whole edifice. Whether Aristotle cut it himself is the question his modern readers are still, legitimately, fighting over.

VI. The Elementary Qualities: Active and Passive Powers

Descend now from metaphysics to physics proper. Bodies, for Aristotle, act on one another and are acted on — fire heats, water wets, a drug purges, food nourishes — and On Generation and Corruption I.6–9 lays down the conditions: action and passion require contact, and they occur between things that are alike in genus and contrary in species, which can therefore act and be acted on reciprocally until an equilibrium — a ratio — is reached. The question Book II then answers is: which contrarieties are primary for tangible body as such? Aristotle surveys the tangible differentiae — hot and cold, wet and dry, heavy and light, hard and soft, viscous and friable, rough and smooth, coarse and fine — and argues (GC II.2, 329b–330a, in paraphrase) that all the rest are reducible to four: the hot, the cold, the wet (or moist), and the dry. Heavy and light are excluded from the primary list on the principled ground that they are neither active nor passive in the relevant way — they do not, as such, act on or suffer from other bodies. The four that remain are the elementary qualities: the primary contrarieties of tangible body, from which every other tangible character derives.

Within the four, Aristotle draws the distinction on which Meteorologica IV is built. The locus is Meteorologica IV.1 (378b10–13), in E. W. Webster's translation in the Oxford Translation, verified for this article:

“Two of the qualities, the hot and the cold, are active; two, the dry and the moist, passive.” — Aristotle, Meteorologica IV.1, 378b, trans. E. W. Webster (Oxford Translation)

The definitions behind the labels are functional, and GC II.2 (in paraphrase) supplies them. The hot is that which gathers together things of the same kind — and, by the same power, separates the unlike, which is why fire both fuses a metal and refines a mixture. The cold gathers both like and unlike indifferently: it congeals, contracts, and fixes. The moist is that which, being readily adaptable in shape, is not bounded by a boundary of its own but takes the boundary of what contains it, while yet holding together — the behaviour of fluids. The dry is the opposite: readily bounded by its own boundary, hardly adaptable — the behaviour of solids. Hot and cold are called active because they are the workers: they determine, conjoin, separate, congeal, and dissolve. Moist and dry are called passive because they are the worked-upon: the determinable material that heat and cold determine. Webster's rendering of IV.1 continues the thought in terms this article paraphrases rather than quotes: heat and cold determine, conjoin, and change things, moistening, drying, hardening, and softening them, while the dry and the moist, singly and in combination, are the subjects of that determination.

Associates the homogeneous, dissociates the heterogeneous. The great digestive and refining power: concoction, boiling, broiling, and ripening in Meteorologica IV are all its work, in degrees.

ACTIVE: COLD

Associates like and unlike alike. Congeals, condenses, fixes, and — as the privation of heat — presides over putrefaction, the unmaking of formed bodies when their proper heat fails.

PASSIVE: MOIST

Easily bounded by another's boundary, not by its own; cohesive for all that. The determinable: what heat and cold can shape because it yields. Water is its elemental seat.

PASSIVE: DRY

Bounded by its own boundary, not easily by another's. The resistant: what gives a body its fixed outline and its refusal to flow. Earth is its elemental seat.

Two consequences follow, and both run straight into the laboratory tradition. First, the qualities are powers (dynameis), not ingredients. To say a body is hot is not to say it contains a quantity of a stuff called Heat; it is to say it can do what hot things do — gather, refine, concoct — and can be made cold by what overmasters it. Matter theory in this register is a theory of capacities and their ratios, and its mathematics, such as it is, is a mathematics of more and less: mastery, balance, and excess. Second, because action is reciprocal and proceeds until a ratio is reached, every mixture has, built into its physics, the notion of a proportion that can be right or wrong — a mean at which the active powers have mastered the passive matter into a stable form, and deviations from which are defects to be corrected. The Arabic science of the balance (mīzān) in the Jabirian corpus, and the Latin alchemists' endless language of correcting, maturing, and perfecting metals, are elaborations — conscious elaborations, through the commentary tradition — of exactly this Aristotelian structure. The qualities were never merely descriptive. From the beginning they were a programme: master the passive with the active, in the right ratio, and the body is perfected; fail, and it putrefies. Meteorologica IV.1 says so in almost those terms, as Section XI shows.

VII. The Quality Square: The Four Simple Bodies

Four qualities, two contrarieties. The hot cannot combine with the cold, nor the wet with the dry, in the same respect at the same time — contraries refuse to be coupled, as On Generation and Corruption II.3 puts the point (in paraphrase). Of the six possible pairings of four terms, two are therefore impossible, and exactly four couplings remain. Each coupling, embodied in the underlying matter, is one of the four simple bodies — the elements in Aristotle's strict sense. The derivation is given at GC II.3, 330b3–5, and this article quotes it in H. H. Joachim's translation in the Oxford Translation, verified for this article:

“Fire is hot and dry, whereas Air is hot and moist (Air being a sort of aqueous vapour); and Water is cold and moist, while Earth is cold and dry.” — Aristotle, On Generation and Corruption II.3, 330b, trans. H. H. Joachim (Oxford Translation)

Set out as the square the tradition always drew — and in the canonical positions this hub uses throughout, which are Aristotle's own — the system is:

HotCold
Dry FIRE — hot + dry EARTH — cold + dry
Wet (Moist) AIR — hot + wet WATER — cold + wet

The square is not a diagram imposed on the theory; it is the theory, in its most portable form. Read its geometry and the physics follows. Bodies sharing a side share a quality and transform into one another easily, by the exchange of a single quality: fire becomes air when its dryness yields to moisture; water becomes earth when its moisture yields to dryness. Bodies standing at opposite corners — fire and water, air and earth — share no quality, and their transformation requires both contrarieties to be overcome, which is why GC II.4 (in paraphrase) treats diagonal transformation as possible but slower and harder, often proceeding through an intermediate neighbour. The whole cycle of elemental change — the great wheel of sublunar generation and corruption, driven, Aristotle argues in II.10, by the sun's annual motion along the ecliptic, which brings the active powers alternately near and far — is the square, traversed in time. And the square's afterlife is coextensive with learned medicine and natural philosophy themselves: Galen's humours are this square mapped onto the body's fluids, the temperaments are this square mapped onto its constitutions, and the Jabirian analysis of the metals (Section XIII) is this square doubled, into interior and exterior quality-pairs. A reader who has the square has the key that opens every room in the foundations cluster — the elements themselves, their history before and after Aristotle, are treated as a system in The Four Elements.

// “ELEMENT” AND “SIMPLE BODY”: A TERMINOLOGICAL CAUTION //

Aristotle's word is stoicheion — originally a letter of the alphabet, a unit in a series — and his definition (De Caelo III.3, in paraphrase) is functional: an element is that into which other bodies can be analysed and which is present in them potentially or actually, and which is not itself analysable into bodies different in form. The “fire” and “water” of the square are, strictly, the simple bodies — fire-like and water-like bodies, as GC II.3 itself warns (in paraphrase): the fire we see is an excess of heat, as ice is an excess of cold, and the simple body corresponding to fire is “such-as-fire”, not common flame. The elements of the square are limiting cases defined by quality-pairs, of which the familiar fire, air, water, and earth of experience are the nearest, impure approximations. Alchemical writers who treat the four as four shelf-stuffs are already one step of simplification away from their source.

The simple bodies have, further, natural places and natural motions (De Caelo IV; GC II.3, in paraphrase). Fire and air move toward the boundary of the sublunar world, earth and water toward its centre; each pair is contrary to the other, fire to water and earth to air. Above the moon, none of this holds: the celestial region is composed of a fifth body, aether, whose nature is to move eternally in circles and which admits no contrariety, no generation, and no corruption. The boundary is absolute for the matter theory. Alchemy is a sublunar art by physical necessity in this system: its materials are the four corruptible bodies and their mixtures, and the heavens act on them only from outside — through the sun's heat, the seasons, and the celestial motions that drive the cycle of change. Astrological alchemy's characteristic claim, that celestial influence can be timed, caught, and stored in sublunar matter, is an attempt to make that external action as strong as physics allows without breaking the boundary. Aristotle supplies the boundary. The attempt is the tradition's own.

VIII. Mixture (Mixis) Against Mere Juxtaposition

If the quality square is Aristotle's most portable doctrine, the theory of mixture — Greek mixis, also krasis in some contexts — is his most consequential one for the history of alchemy. It is set out in On Generation and Corruption I.10 (327a30–328b), with its conditions prepared in I.6–9, and everything in this section is paraphrase of that chapter and of the modern scholarship on it, except where quotation is expressly marked. The problem mixis solves is the problem of the homogeneous body. A lump of flesh, a bar of bronze, a cup of wine-and-water: divide them as finely as you like, and every part is still flesh, bronze, wine-and-water — the same in kind as the whole. (Aristotle's term is homoiomerēs, “with like parts”, Latinised as homoeomerous; Section XI meets the class in full.) How can such a body be composed of ingredients — elements, or copper and tin — that are nowhere in it separately visible? Two bad answers were on the table, and Aristotle rejects both.

The first bad answer is juxtaposition — composition, in his term synthesis: the ingredients persist, actually and unaltered, as tiny particles laid side by side, and the apparent homogeneity is an illusion of our coarse senses. This is, in paraphrase, the account Aristotle attributes to Empedocles and to the atomists: flesh as a mosaic of element-particles, a wall of bricks and stones at microscopic scale. His refutation (GC I.10, in paraphrase) is brisk. If the ingredients persist actually, there is no mixture but only an aggregate, fine-grained at best; a being with keen enough sight — his example is Lynceus, the Argonaut whose eyes pierced the earth — would see not flesh but a heap. Division could in principle recover the ingredients unaltered, as stones are recovered from a demolished wall. And the body would not be homogeneous in truth, only in appearance: any part of it, examined closely enough, would resolve into this particle of fire, that of earth, and neither would be flesh. What the senses report as one uniform stuff would be a crowd.

The second bad answer is annihilation: the ingredients perish outright, and a wholly new body replaces them, owing them nothing. Aristotle rejects this too, and the rejection is what makes his theory a theory of mixture rather than of replacement. For a mixture can be resolved: wine can be recovered, in some measure, from wine-and-water; metals can be separated from their alloys by the refiner's art; the elements come back out of flesh when flesh rots and burns. If the ingredients had perished without remainder, their recovery would be a creation from nothing, which his physics forbids. Mixis must steer between the wall and the void.

// THE DOCTRINE, IN ONE SENTENCE //

In a true mixture, the ingredients act on one another reciprocally until their powers reach a common ratio; the resulting body is actually one homogeneous substance, in which the ingredients no longer exist actually — but they exist potentially, their powers preserved in the ratio they have jointly established, and for that reason they can be separated out of the mixture again. Mixture is the unification of alterable things that have acted on and been acted on by one another: that, in paraphrase, is the definition GC I.10 arrives at, and two thousand years of commentary is commentary on it.

The conditions Aristotle lays down (in paraphrase) are as instructive as the definition. Mixture requires ingredients that can act and be acted on reciprocally — contraries within a common genus, as Section VI established. It requires them to be readily divisible, so that part can meet part throughout and not merely at a surface: a drop of wine in ten thousand gallons of water does not mix, Aristotle observes, but is converted — quantity is part of the physics, and gross disproportion defeats mixture by overwhelming one power with another. And it requires the action to proceed to a genuine mean: the mixed body has one ratio, one set of dispositions, one nature — not a statistical average of two natures persisting side by side. Theodore Scaltsas's survey “Mixing the Elements”, written for the Blackwell Companion to Aristotle and read for this article, usefully collects the modern interpretative options — from readings on which the ingredients' forms perish and only their powers survive in the mixture's form, to readings on which the ingredients persist in some diminished mode — and the spread of those options is itself the historical point: the text fixes the structure (actual unity, potential presence, recoverability) and leaves the metaphysical mechanics open enough for centuries of schools to build in.

Why this mattered so much to alchemy can now be said exactly. Transmutation theory needs a composition theory in which (a) a metal is a genuine unity, not a heap — else “gold” names a texture, not a nature; (b) its constituents are really in it, potentially — else there is nothing for art to work on; and (c) the constituents' ratio can be changed — else the metal's nature is fixed by its recipe forever. Mixis supplies all three, in Aristotle's own terms, without a single alchemical premise. William R. Newman's work — The Summa Perfectionis of Pseudo-Geber (1991) and Atoms and Alchemy (2006) — has shown, in the account this article follows, how the Latin tradition then performed its great reversal inside this framework: reading Meteorologica IV's talk of pores (poroi) and small masses (onkoi), and developing Aristotle's own scattered doctrine of minima naturalia — the least quantity of a substance that still bears its form, below which division destroys the nature — the pseudo-Geber and his successors rebuilt mixture as a union of enduring corpuscles of sulphur and mercury, combined per minima, in differing purity, size, and proportion. That is juxtaposition, readmitted at the level of the minima and baptised with Aristotelian vocabulary — the very model GC I.10 refuted, returning as the working theory of the most sophisticated Latin alchemy. Newman traces the line from there to Sennert and Boyle: early modern corpuscularianism, on his thesis, descends not from Democritus read directly but from Aristotle read alchemically. The thesis is debated — reviewers have pressed hard on whether onkoi in Meteorologica IV are corpuscles at all, and on Aristotle's explicit rejection of pores as void in GC I.8 — and Section XII returns to that debate where it belongs, at the disputed book itself. What is not debated is the starting point: the debate is about Aristotle's text, conducted in Aristotle's terms, by men who believed the text contained a chemistry. Mixis is why they believed it.

JUXTAPOSITION (SYNTHESIS)

Ingredients actually present, unaltered, side by side. A wall of bricks; a heap of grain and barley. Apparent unity only. Aristotle: not mixture.

TRUE MIXTURE (MIXIS)

Ingredients potentially present, their powers balanced in one ratio; the product actually one homogeneous body, resolvable into its ingredients because they were never annihilated. Flesh, bronze, wine-and-water. Aristotle: mixture proper.

A last distinction, easily lost and worth keeping sharp for Section XIII: mixis is not generation. In generation, one substance perishes and another comes to be, and the product is not resolvable into its predecessor as into an ingredient. In mixture, the product is a new homogeneous body whose ingredients persist potentially as its constituents. Whether a metal is best understood as a mixture of more primitive constituents, or as a generated substance with its own substantial form, is a question Aristotle's texts leave genuinely underdetermined — he treats metals among the homogeneous bodies, themselves products of the exhalations' congelation — and the Arabic and Latin traditions exploited the underdetermination in both directions. The sulphur–mercury theory, when it comes, is precisely a mixis-theory of the metals: two constituents, potentially present, in variable ratio and purity, whose rebalancing would be transmutation. It is wearing Aristotelian clothes. Whether the body underneath them is Aristotle's is Section XIII's question.

IX. The Two Exhalations: The Engine of Books I–III

The Meteorologica proper — Books I–III, whose authenticity has never been seriously doubted — explains the phenomena of the sublunar region between the earth's surface and the moon: rain and rivers, winds and earthquakes, comets, shooting stars, haloes and rainbows, thunder and the sea's salt. Its explanatory engine, introduced at I.4 and developed at II.4 (in paraphrase except where quotation is marked), is a single mechanism of great elegance. The sun's heat, acting on the earth, draws up from it not one evaporation but two exhalations — Greek anathymiaseis. From the moisture on and in the earth rises a moist, vaporous exhalation: potentially water, moist and relatively cold, the source of rain, dew, and hoar-frost when it condenses. From the dry earth itself rises a dry, smoky exhalation: potentially fire, hot and dry, the source and substance of the winds, and — ignited in the upper region — of shooting stars, comets, and the Milky Way as Aristotle conceives it. Neither occurs pure: the moist cannot exist without the dry nor the dry without the moist, and to name either is to name a predominance. In Webster's translation, consulted for this article in the excerpts of Books II–IV printed for the University of California course reader, the distinction is drawn (in the form of a course excerpt, here paraphrased in part) between vapour, moist and cold, and the smoky evaporation, hot and dry. The two exhalations are, in the vocabulary of Section VI, the quality square set in motion vertically: water-and-earth, loosened by solar heat, rising as opposed powers into a region where their recombinations are the weather.

The mechanism's reach is the point. Above the surface, the exhalations' mixtures, condensations, and ignitions generate the whole meteorological repertoire — including, at II.7–8, earthquakes, which Aristotle explains (in paraphrase) as the dry exhalation, as wind, trapped within the earth and struggling to escape: the smoky exhalation is as much an underground agent as an aerial one. That double theatre — the same two powers at work above the earth and within it — is the bridge to the metals. For if exhalations are shut up inside the earth, in its cavities and in the body of its stones, and there compressed, congealed, and cooked by the earth's own heat over time, then the underground should have its own products, corresponding in kind to the two powers that make them. Books I–III prepare that conclusion and, at their close, promise it. The final chapter of Book III ends (in paraphrase of 378a15 ff.) by announcing a treatment of the bodies formed within the earth — the metals and the so-called fossils — as the exhalations' subterranean effects, parallel to their effects in the air. Lee's Loeb introduction notes the sequel honestly: the promise is never fulfilled in the extant works in the form announced — a full treatise on metals and minerals, kind by kind, does not survive, and may never have been written. What survives is one dense, magnificent chapter: III.6. It is enough. The whole of later metallogenesis is a commentary on it.

Meteor. I.4

THE TWO EXHALATIONS INTRODUCED

The moist exhalation from water and the dry, smoky exhalation from earth, posited as the material basis of the phenomena of the upper sublunar region.

Meteor. II.4

VAPOUR AND SMOKE DEFINED

The vaporous exhalation moist and cold, source of rain; the smoky hot and dry, source and substance of winds. Neither exists without the other; predominance names the kind.

Meteor. II.7–8

THE EXHALATION UNDERGROUND: EARTHQUAKES

Wind — the dry exhalation in motion — imprisoned in the earth's hollows shakes it. The subterranean theatre is established before the metals are mentioned.

Meteor. III.6

FOSSILS AND METALS

The two exhalations, shut up in the earth, produce two corresponding kinds of body: the dry makes the fossils, the vaporous makes the metals. Section X.

Meteor. IV

THE HOMOGENEOUS BODIES IN GENERAL

Not the promised kind-by-kind treatise on metals and minerals, but a general physics of homogeneous bodies — attached to the Meteorologica in transmission, disputed in authorship. Sections XI–XII.

X. Underground: Fossils and Metals in Meteorologica III.6

Meteorologica III.6 (378a15–b6) is a short text with an outsized posterity, and this article quotes its core in Webster's translation, verified in the published excerpt consulted for this article. Aristotle turns from the exhalations' effects above the earth to their operations below, when shut up in its parts:

“We maintain that there are two exhalations, one vaporous the other smoky, and there correspond two kinds of bodies that originate in the earth, ‘fossiles’ and metals.” — Aristotle, Meteorologica III.6, 378a, trans. E. W. Webster (Oxford Translation)

“Fossils” — Greek orykta, things dug up — is the older and broader sense of the word: not petrified organisms, a meaning the word would not acquire for two thousand years, but the whole class of dug-up, non-metallic mineral bodies. Webster's text continues, in the passage consulted, with the dry exhalation's products (here paraphrased in part and quoted in part): the heat of the dry exhalation is the cause of all the fossils — the kinds of stones that cannot be melted, and realgar, ochre, ruddle (red ochre), and sulphur, and the other things of that kind, most fossils being either a coloured earthy powder or, like cinnabar, a stone compounded of it. The detail deserves a pause. Sulphur appears in Aristotle's own list — as a product of the dry, smoky exhalation, among the dug-up bodies. It appears as a product, not as a constituent principle of metals. Remember the placement; Section XIII will need it.

The metals are the vaporous exhalation's work:

“The vaporous exhalation is the cause of all metals, those bodies which are either fusible or malleable such as iron, copper, gold.” — Aristotle, Meteorologica III.6, 378a–b, trans. E. W. Webster (Oxford Translation)

The mechanism, in paraphrase of the same passage in Webster's translation, runs as follows. The vaporous exhalation, imprisoned in the earth and especially in stones, is compressed by the dryness of its surroundings and congeals — as dew or hoar-frost congeals — except that in the metals' case the congelation occurs before the exhalation has separated off into actual water. Metals are therefore, in Aristotle's deliberately paradoxical formula, water in a sense and in a sense not: their matter was what might have become water, but can no longer do so. Hence their defining dispositions fall out of their genesis. They are fusible and malleable because their matter is congealed vapour — a moisture mastered and fixed, but moisture still. And they all, gold excepted, are affected by fire and contain an admixture of earth, because they still contain the dry exhalation within them: the two exhalations never occur pure (Section IX), and a metal is the moist one's congelation with the dry one shut up inside it. Gold's exception — unaffected by fire — is stated, in the passage, as a fact of its composition, the limiting case of a metal in which the earthy, combustible admixture is at its least. Later ages would build an entire axiology of metallic perfection on that clause. Aristotle states it as mineralogy.

THE DRY EXHALATION → THE FOSSILS (ORYKTA)

Infusible stones; realgar; ochre and ruddle; sulphur; cinnabar and the coloured earths. Hot and dry in predominance: burnt, powdery, earthy bodies, made by the dry exhalation's heat.

THE VAPOROUS EXHALATION → THE METALS

Iron, copper, gold, and their kind: fusible or malleable bodies, made by the moist exhalation congealed under compression within the earth, especially in stones, before it could become water.

Three features of the chapter must be held firmly, because the genealogy of Section XIII depends on holding them and on nothing else. First, Aristotle's metals have one material cause, not two: the vaporous exhalation, with the dry present as an admixture and contaminant, not as a co-principle. There is no pair of metallic parents in this text. Second, the exhalations are processes and predominances, not substances with names on a shelf: “the vaporous” is moisture rising under heat, not mercury; “the smoky” is dry earth exhaling, not sulphur — sulphur is one of its downstream products. Third, the account is continuous with the weather. The same powers that make rain and wind make, underground and under compression, dew's sterner cousins the metals. Metallogenesis, for Aristotle, is meteorology conducted in the dark, at geological patience. John A. Norris's study, “The Mineral Exhalation Theory of Metallogenesis in Pre-Modern Mineral Science”, Ambix 53 (2006), pp. 43–65 — consulted for this article — traces exactly this theory's long career and makes the point this section has been building toward: that the vapour stage mattered, in Aristotle and in his successors, because vapour is the state in which elemental mixing can occur, allowing composite yet homogeneous metallic matter to form. The exhalation is mixis (Section VIII) in transit: qualities meeting in a fluid medium, reaching a ratio, and then being frozen into a body by congelation. Whoever later wanted metals to have ingredients in proportion found the machinery here, already assembled.

XI. Meteorologica IV: The Homogeneous Bodies

The fourth book transmitted under the Meteorologica's name is a different kind of text from the three before it, and the difference announces itself in its first lines. It is not about weather at all. It is, in H. B. Gottschalk's summary — from his 1961 article on the book's authorship, quoted in the modern reference literature and paraphrased in part here — a description of the formation, out of the four elements, of the homoeomerous substances: minerals such as stones and metals, and organic substances like flesh, skin, and hair, and of the changes they can undergo under the influence of heat, cold, and moisture. Where Books I–III explain phenomena, Book IV explains materials. It is the closest thing in the Greek corpus to a systematic treatise on what a later age would call the chemistry of common substances, and the alchemical tradition treated it accordingly: as the master's own handbook of matter. This section expounds it as it stands, under its transmitted attribution; Section XII then gives the authorship problem its own hearing.

Concoction: Nature's Cooking

The book opens (IV.1–3, in paraphrase except where quotation is marked) by installing the active qualities of Section VI as the agents of all natural becoming and destruction in plants, animals, and their parts. Natural becoming is the change the active powers introduce into a thing's underlying matter — the passive qualities — when the powers stand in a certain ratio to that matter. When hot and cold master the matter, they generate the thing; when they fail, wholly or partly, the product is imperfect — imperfectly boiled, unconcocted. The master process is concoction (pepsis — the word also means digestion, and cooking, and the ripening of fruit; Aristotle exploits the whole range deliberately). Webster's translation, verified for this article, defines it:

“[C]oncoction is a process in which the natural and proper heat of an object perfects the corresponding passive qualities, which are the proper matter of any given object.” — Aristotle, Meteorologica IV.2, 379b, trans. E. W. Webster (Oxford Translation)

Every term in the definition works. The heat must be the thing's own — natural and proper to it — though external heat may assist, as a bath assists digestion without digesting. The passive qualities perfected are the thing's proper matter: concoction does not impose an alien form but brings the matter to the form that is the thing's nature — in some cases, Webster's text continues in paraphrase, the end of the process is the nature of the thing itself, its formal cause and essence. Concoction's species are ripening, boiling, and broiling; its failure is inconcoction, from lack of proper heat — that is, from cold — whose species are rawness and imperfect boiling and broiling. And the system's dark pole is putrefaction (sēpsis): the strict opposite of true becoming, the end toward which all natural destruction tends, when a body's proper heat fails and alien heat — the heat of its surroundings — masters its moisture instead. Things that putrefy begin moist and end dry; the formed body un-forms. A matter theory that contains, as paired technical terms, a word for a body's perfecting by its own heat and a word for its rotting when that heat is lost did not need to wait long for readers with furnaces. The laboratory programme of maturation — of holding a material at the right heat until its own nature perfects — is concoction, externalised.

Solidification, Melting, and the Catalogue of Dispositions

Chapters IV.4–7 (in paraphrase) work through drying, moistening, solidification, and melting, assigning each its agents: bodies are solidified by heat, which dries out their moisture, or by cold, which drives out their heat; what cold solidifies, heat melts, and what dry heat solidifies, water dissolves — with iron, melted by heat and solidified by cold, and wood, earth-and-air and therefore combustible but unmeltable, among the passage's working examples. Chapter IV.8 then states the book's constitution for homogeneous bodies, in the passage Webster renders (here paraphrased in part): bodies are formed by heat and cold, operating by thickening and solidifying; the active qualities fashion them, the moist and dry are present as passive; and so water and earth are the constituents of the homogeneous bodies — in plants and animals, and in metals such as gold and silver — water and earth and their respective exhalations, shut up in the compound bodies. The reader of Section X should hear the click: Book IV's constitution of metals presupposes Book III's genesis of them. Whatever its authorship, the book was composed — or placed — to continue the exhalation theory into a general physics of materials.

The same chapter (IV.8, 385a, in paraphrase following Webster's list) enumerates the passive dispositions by which homogeneous bodies are differentiated from one another — the book's most quoted and most “chemical” passage. A homogeneous body may be apt or inapt: to solidify; to melt; to be softened by heat; to be softened by water; to bend; to break; to be comminuted; to receive an impression; to be moulded; to be squeezed; to be tractile or non-tractile; malleable or non-malleable; fissile or non-fissile; apt or inapt to be cut; viscous or friable; compressible or incompressible; combustible or incombustible; apt or inapt to give off fumes. Eighteen paired dispositions — a periodic table's distant ancestor in form, if not in content: a finite grid of material behaviours, generated from the interplay of two active and two passive powers, against which any stuff can be profiled. Chapters IV.9–11 apply the grid: solidification and melting are analysed kind by kind, and the homogeneous bodies are sorted by their predominant constituent — gold, silver, copper, tin, lead, glass, and many stones are of water, because they melt by heat; iron, horn, bone, wood, bark, and the earthy “tears” such as myrrh and frankincense are bodies in which earth preponderates, softening by heat at most, or giving off fumes and forming by refrigeration; salt and natron stand apart as earthy bodies formed by neither route. Chapter IV.12 closes the book by ascending the ladder of composition — elements, homogeneous bodies, non-homogeneous organs, whole organisms — and assigning each level its causes, with the remark (in paraphrase) that the final cause is least obvious where matter predominates. It is the remark of a philosopher who knows exactly what kind of book he has written: a book about the level at which form is thinnest and matter does most of the talking. The alchemists, who worked at precisely that level, may be forgiven for feeling it was written for them.

CONCOCTION (PEPSIS)

Perfection of a body's proper matter by its own proper heat. Species: ripening, boiling, broiling. Failure: inconcoction — rawness — from cold, the want of heat.

PUTREFACTION (SĒPSIS)

Concoction's opposite: a body's moisture mastered by alien heat when its own heat fails. The natural end of every formed body not destroyed by violence.

THE EIGHTEEN DISPOSITIONS

IV.8's catalogue of aptitudes — fusible, malleable, fissile, combustible, and the rest — by which homogeneous bodies are told apart. Antiquity's systematic materials science.

One further doctrine of Book IV proved, in the event, to be its most fertile: its casual, working use of pores (poroi) and small masses (onkoi) in explaining how heat, moisture, and vapours penetrate, fill, and transform bodies (IV.3, IV.8–9, in paraphrase). In a continuum physics that officially rejects void and atoms, the language sits uneasily — uneasily enough to become, in modern scholarship, the chief internal evidence against the book's authenticity (Section XII), and, in the medieval tradition, the chief textual seed of alchemy's corpuscular turn (Section VIII). The same sentences were, for one age, proof the book was not Aristotle's, and, for another, proof that Aristotle had anticipated the laboratory's particles. Both ages were reading the same Greek. The episode is the purest case in this article of a general law of the hub: in the history of matter theory, a text's difficulties are its fertility.

XII. Meteorologica IV: The Authenticity Debate

The debate is as old as the commentary tradition, and its terms were set by the commentators themselves. Alexander of Aphrodisias (fl. c. 200 CE), in the opening of his commentary on the book — the passage is quoted in James G. Lennox's review of the modern literature, consulted for this article — maintains, in paraphrase, that the book entitled “the fourth” of Aristotle's Meteorology does belong to Aristotle, but not to the treatise on meteorology, for the matters discussed in it are not proper to meteorology; Alexander accordingly placed it with On Generation and Corruption. Olympiodorus (sixth century CE; Section XV) placed it between On the Heavens and On Generation and Corruption. Only Ammonius, on the report Lee's introduction preserves, held it to be both genuine and in the right place. Three distinct questions are entangled in that ancient disagreement, and the modern debate is unintelligible until they are separated: (1) Is Book IV by Aristotle? (2) If so, is it in its right place — is it a part of the Meteorologica at all? (3) If not by Aristotle, whose is it? Antiquity already answered (2) mostly in the negative while answering (1) mostly in the affirmative. The twentieth century reversed the emphasis.

The Case Against: Hammer-Jensen and the Strato Attribution

The only sustained attempt to argue the book out of the corpus is Ingeborg Hammer-Jensen's, in Hermes 50 (1915) — her article “Das sogenannte IV. Buch der Meteorologie des Aristoteles” — as summarised in Lee's Loeb introduction, read in full for this article. Her argument, in paraphrase, turned on the natural philosophy behind the book: its explanations struck her as mechanical and atomistic in character — above all its use of poroi (pores) and onkoi (small masses) in chapters 3, 8, and 9, which she took to be un-Aristotelian and indicative of a connection with atomism — and the characteristic Aristotelian insistence on the final cause struck her as absent. On those grounds she ascribed the work to Strato of Lampsacus, Theophrastus's successor as head of the Lyceum, on the ground, as Lee reports it, that Strato was the most atomistic of the Peripatetics. Léon Robin's Aristote (1944) likewise argued for Strato, and the attribution had a long currency: W. D. Ross called the book, in Lee's report, “pretty certainly not genuine”, and Werner Jaeger referred to it as spurious. H. B. Gottschalk's “The Authorship of Meteorologica, Book IV”, Classical Quarterly (1961), while documenting the book's utter discontinuity with Books I–III, concluded — in the abstract preserved in the modern reference literature — that on the whole Theophrastus is the most likely candidate for its authorship. The sceptical case, at its strongest, is thus not one case but a family: the book is too mechanical, too corpuscular, too little teleological, and too discontinuous to be Aristotle's — and the school supplied plausible alternative hands.

The Case For: Joachim, Lee, and the Modern Restoration

The defence is older than the attack's fame suggests. H. H. Joachim — the translator of On Generation and Corruption quoted in Section VII — treated the book as genuine in his edition of that work and in his 1903 article on Aristotle's conception of chemical combination. V. C. B. Coutant's Columbia dissertation on Alexander's commentary (1936) criticised Hammer-Jensen's case directly, and Lee, in the Loeb introduction read for this article, concurs with Coutant, in paraphrase, that Hammer-Jensen failed to make a convincing case — adding that arguments from a book's ideas and their consistency with a philosopher's main tenets are bound to be, to some extent, subjective. Lee's answers to the two main objections, in paraphrase, deserve stating because they remain the defence's core. To the missing final cause: Aristotle himself supplies the answer in IV.12, overlooked by Hammer-Jensen — the final cause is least obvious where matter predominates, and a treatise on the material level of nature will necessarily speak mostly of material and efficient causes. To the pores and masses: the language, on the defence's reading, does not commit its user to Democritean atomism or to void — a pore need not be an empty space, and an onkos need not be an atom; Carmela Baffioni's later study (1981), as summarised in the modern literature, pressed the same point, noting that the atomists' word for void is kenon, that onkoi can hardly be translated “atoms”, and that Meteorologica IV identifies poros with kenon only once. Craig Martin's study of the Renaissance reception, “Renaissance Medicine and the Reinterpretation of Aristotle's Meteorologica”, consulted for this article, states the current position in a sentence this article adopts: currently accepted opinion has restored Aristotle as the true author, although the book's placement within the corpus remains a subject for discussion — with the added historical note that no one explicitly questioned the authorship before the twentieth century, while Renaissance commentators disputed the placement freely, Pomponazzi proposing that the book's real title should be De mixtione, On Mixture, and Agostino Nifo entitling it, in Greek, On Mixed Bodies.

AGAINST ARISTOTLE'S AUTHORSHIP

Discontinuity with Books I–III; a mechanical, apparently corpuscular physics of pores and masses; teleology recessive; the programme of Meteorologica I.1 completed by the end of Book III, and III.6's promised treatise on metals and minerals not delivered by Book IV. Holders, in their several forms: Hammer-Jensen (Strato), Robin (Strato), Ross, Jaeger, Gottschalk (Theophrastus the likeliest alternative).

FOR ARISTOTLE'S AUTHORSHIP

Ancient attribution unbroken until 1915; IV.12's own explanation of teleology's recession; pores and masses readable without atomism or void; doctrinal continuity with Generation and Corruption on qualities, mixture, and the homogeneous bodies. Holders: Joachim, Coutant, Lee; the volume edited by Cristina Viano, Aristoteles chemicus (2002), and the current consensus Martin reports.

Two further positions keep the map honest. The first is placement without impugning authorship — Alexander's ancient position, and the one most modern editors in practice adopt: the book is Aristotle's, and it is a separate treatise, belonging after On Generation and Corruption, which editors and copyists attached to the Meteorologica because its subject — the products of the elements' mutual action — adjoined meteorology and because III.6's unfulfilled promise left a vacancy it seemed to fill. The second is the position of the 2002 Venice seminar volume edited by Cristina Viano, Aristoteles chemicus: Il IV libro dei Meteorologica nella tradizione antica e medievale — reviewed by Lennox in Aestimatio (2004), the review consulted for this article — whose collective work treats Book IV as a critical text for Aristotle's investigation of matter, the matter–form relation, and unqualified generation, and traces its ancient and medieval tradition through Alexander's and Olympiodorus's commentaries and into the Arabic Hermetic and alchemical reception. On that volume's showing, it is precisely through the Islamic tradition of interpretation, relying heavily on Olympiodorus, that Book IV becomes inextricably bound up with the alchemical and Hermetic traditions. The authenticity question, that is, has a curious property: its historical effects are independent of its answer. For the eighteen centuries in which the book did its work — in the lecture halls of Alexandria, the translation houses of Baghdad, and the universities of Paris and Padua — it was Aristotle's, and was read with the full weight of his name. The modern doubt, even where it is strongest, is a doubt about a fourth-century BCE fact. It is not, and cannot be, a doubt about the book's career. This hub therefore cites the book as Meteorologica IV, under Aristotle's name, with this section as its standing caveat — the same treatment the hub gives every transmitted attribution whose antiquity is itself a historical force.

// WHY THE DEBATE MATTERS BEYOND PEDANTRY //

If Book IV is Strato's or Theophrastus's, then “Aristotle's chemistry” is his school's chemistry, a generation on — and the alchemical tradition's founding text is Peripatetic rather than Aristotelian in the strict sense. If it is Aristotle's, then the master himself wrote the ancient world's most systematic materials physics, pores and all, and the corpuscular reading of him has a foothold in his own hand. Either way, the book is early Peripatetic, continuous with the genuine corpus, and the direct ancestor of the commentary and alchemical traditions. The genealogy of Section XIII does not stand or fall with the attribution — a fact worth stating, because popular alchemy writing routinely makes it stand and fall with far less.

XIII. From Exhalations to Sulphur–Mercury: A Genealogy, Honestly Stated

The sulphur–mercury theory holds that the metals are composed, in differing proportion, purity, and degree of concoction, of two principles: a hot, dry, combustible principle, sulphur, and a cold, moist, fusible and volatile principle, mercury — not simply the shelf substances brimstone and quicksilver, though the theory's language never fully separates the principles from their namesakes, but sulphur and mercury as the metals' proximate constituents, their philosophical parents. Gold is the two in perfect balance and purity, fully concocted; the base metals are the two in imbalance, impurity, or immaturity; and transmutation is, in principle, the correction of the ratio. The theory dominated Islamic and Latin alchemy for the better part of a millennium, until the eighteenth century. Its earliest known systematic statement is in the Arabic Jabirian corpus — the vast body of works ascribed to Jābir ibn Ḥayyān, tentatively dated by modern scholarship to c. 850–950 CE and generally regarded not as one eighth-century man's work but as the product of a school writing under his name across that period. William R. Newman's Britannica account of Jābir, consulted for this article, states the corpus's signature doctrine in a sentence: its works posit that all metals are composed of mercury and sulphur. The question of this section is where that doctrine came from — and the question must be answered with a care the tradition itself rarely used, because the answer is a genealogy reconstructed by scholars, not a doctrine any ancient author states.

What Aristotle Actually Supplied

Set the two accounts side by side and the kinship is real but partial. Aristotle (Sections IX–X) gives: metals generated inside the earth, over time, from an exhaled vapour congealed under compression; two exhalations in play, the moist predominating in metals, the dry present within them as admixture; the moist exhalation characterised as moist and cold, the dry as hot and dry — the exact quality signatures the sulphur–mercury theory assigns to mercury (cold and moist) and sulphur (hot and dry); fusibility and malleability explained by the moist constituent, combustibility and earthiness by the dry; and one metal, gold, exempt from fire's action as the limiting case of purity. Add Meteorologica IV's framework — metals as homogeneous bodies, water and earth their constituents, water and earth and their exhalations shut up in them; mixture as ratio; concoction as perfection by heat — and every structural element of the later theory is present in Aristotle except the theory itself. William H. Brock's Fontana History of Chemistry, in the summary consulted for this article, states the received derivation in its usual form, in paraphrase: that according to the Jabirian corpus and the Latin Summa, metals were generated inside the earth by the admixture of a fiery, smoky principle, sulphur, to a watery principle, mercury — an idea Brock's text explicitly describes as based upon Aristotle's explanation in the Meteorologica. Norris's Ambix study (Section X) documents the mechanism of the descent with more precision: the mineral exhalation theory was variously incorporated with the sulphur–mercury theory by medieval Arab authors — incorporated with, that is, as a distinguishable doctrine combined with another, not simply renamed.

What the Arabic Development Added

The transformation, as modern scholarship reconstructs it, proceeded by three moves, none of them Aristotle's. First, hypostatisation: the two exhalations — processes, predominances of quality in rising vapour — hardened into two constituent substances, present in every metal as its ingredients. Aristotle's metals have one material cause with an admixture (Section X); the theory's metals have two parents. Second, identification: the hardened principles took the names, and much of the behaviour, of the two most theoretically suggestive substances in the workshop — sulphur, the combustible stone that burns with a blue flame and is itself one of Aristotle's dry-exhalation products, and mercury, the liquid metal that amalgamates, volatilises, and seems to be metal in its fluid state. The identification is a workshop insight, not a textual one: nothing in Meteorologica III.6 equates the vaporous exhalation with quicksilver. Third, systematisation: the Jabirian corpus doubled the quality square, analysing each metal (in the account given in the modern reference literature, consulted for this article) by exterior and interior quality-pairs — lead externally cold and dry but internally hot and moist, gold the reverse — and built on the two principles a quantitative science of the balance, in which a metal's nature is its principles' proportions, weighed in qualities and letters. Aristotle's ratio-language made this thinkable. The balance itself, with its numerology, is the Jabirian school's own creation, inside a Shiʿi intellectual world Aristotle could not have imagined.

// THE HONEST SENTENCE //

Aristotle did not teach the sulphur–mercury theory. No passage of his states that metals are composed of sulphur and mercury, and the passage the theory descends from — Meteorologica III.6 — lists sulphur among the dry exhalation's products and never mentions mercury at all. What Aristotle taught is the theory's grammar: subterranean generation from opposed exhalations bearing the same quality signatures, mixture as proportion, concoction as perfection, gold as the fire-proof limit. The Arabic alchemists, reading him through the commentators (Section XV) and through their own laboratory experience of brimstone and quicksilver, converted the grammar into a doctrine. Scholars — Norris on the exhalation theory's career, Newman and Lawrence M. Principe on the Latin corpuscular development, Brock in synthesis — reconstruct that conversion as a genealogy. A genealogy is a claim about descent with modification, argued from texts and dates. It is not a quotation, and this article does not present it as one. Alchemical tradition, by contrast, routinely presented the finished theory as Aristotle's — the first and most successful of the attributions Section XIV examines.

The Latin sequel completes the arc. The Jabirian works, translated under the Latinised name Geber, and then the Summa perfectionis of the Latin pseudo-Geber — shown by Newman's 1991 study to be the work of a thirteenth-century Italian author, Paul of Taranto, writing under Jābir's name — gave the sulphur–mercury theory its corpuscular form: unified particles of the two principles, differing in purity, size, and proportion, combining per minima to form the metals (Section VIII). Craig Martin's Ambix study of the Renaissance commentaries on Meteorologica IV, consulted for this article, adds the necessary counterweight from the universities: that the mercury–sulphur theory championed by the alchemists clearly did not correspond to Aristotle's text, which held metals to be formed of earth, water, and the exhalations — and that Renaissance Aristotelians, unlike their medieval predecessors, largely said so, and were uniformly sceptical of transmutation in their commentaries even as the alchemical reading flourished outside them. The genealogy, in short, forks: one line runs from Aristotle through commentary into alchemy and out the far side into early modern corpuscularianism; the other runs from Aristotle through commentary into the universities, where the same texts were used to refuse the alchemical conclusion. Both lines are Aristotelian receptions. Neither is simply Aristotle.

XIV. The Other Aristotle: The Pseudo-Aristotelian Alchemical Corpus

Everything in this section is pseudepigraphy: works Aristotle did not write, ascribed to him by writers who knew exactly what his name was worth. The flag is not a formality. The pseudo-Aristotelian corpus is large, influential, and — unlike the disputed Book IV of Section XII, an early Peripatetic text with an ancient attribution — in its major items demonstrably medieval and demonstrably not his: composed in Arabic centuries after his death, in worlds he never knew, and fathered on him through fictitious translation histories. Modern scholarship files the whole shelf under “pseudo-Aristotle”, and this article follows it without exception. No doctrine drawn from these works is Aristotle's doctrine, whatever their manuscripts claim.

The Secret of Secrets (Sirr al-Asrār / Secretum Secretorum)

The most widely diffused pseudo-Aristotelian work of the Middle Ages purports to be Aristotle's letter of counsel to his pupil Alexander the Great: an encyclopaedic treatise covering, in the modern reference account consulted for this article, statecraft, ethics, physiognomy, astrology, alchemy, magic, and medicine. Its own preface claims a pedigree — Greek original, Syriac translation, Arabic translation by the ninth-century scholar Yaḥyā ibn al-Biṭrīq — for which, as the scholarship states plainly, no Greek original exists: modern scholarship finds the work to have been composed in Arabic, most likely in the tenth century, after the Encyclopaedia of the Brethren of Purity and before the late tenth century. It survives in a long version of ten books and a short version of seven or eight. Its Latin career was immense: a partial translation by John of Seville (c. 1120, some 150 surviving copies) and a full translation by Philip of Tripoli at Antioch (c. 1232, more than 350 copies) made the Secretum secretorum one of the most copied books in Europe, and enlarged thirteenth-century versions incorporated alchemical material and an early form of the Emerald Tablet — on which see the hub's Emerald Tablet: Roots. For alchemy's history the Secretum matters as infrastructure: it taught Latin Europe to expect occult natural knowledge — the hidden properties of stones, plants, and numbers, and the making of gold among them — from “Aristotle” writing privately to a king. The expectation outlived the book.

The Book of Stones (Kitāb al-Aḥjār) and the Lapidary Aristotle

The Arabic Kitāb al-Aḥjār, the Book of Stones ascribed to Aristotle — in one strand of its transmission presented as translated by Lūqā ibn Isrāfīūn (Luke son of Serapion), in another as an abridgement of an Aristotelian original said to have treated seven hundred stones — is a lapidary: an entry-by-entry catalogue of precious stones, magnets of every fanciful kind, animal stones, stones that melt, and stones of dusty consistency such as sulphur, arsenic, and vitriol, each entry giving the stone's varieties and colours, its mines and places, and its properties — medical and “peculiar” (the khawāṣṣ, occult properties) alike. The scholarly presentation of the text consulted for this article notes its double address: to the experts of the alchemical craft, and to the physicians who use mineral drugs. Its Latin descendant, the Liber de mineralibus Aristotelis known to the medieval West, carried the lapidary Aristotle into the same libraries that held Albertus Magnus's genuine De mineralibus — which cites Aristotle constantly and pseudo-Aristotle oftener than its author knew. The genuine Aristotle wrote no lapidary; his successor Theophrastus wrote a real one, On Stones, and the contrast is instructive: the Peripatetic school's actual mineralogy is sober, observational, and short. The pseudo-Aristotelian lapidary is none of those things, and was read for a millennium longer.

The Pseudo-Aristotelian Hermetica and the Remaining Shelf

A third cluster, studied in Liana Saif's “A Preliminary Study of the Pseudo-Aristotelian Hermetica” (Al-ʿUṣūr al-Wusṭā 29, 2021), consulted for this article, survives in Arabic as a group of treatises — al-Isṭimākhīs, al-Isṭimāṭīs, al-Hādhīṭūs, al-Ustuwaṭṭās, and their companions, together forming a work on the causes of the spiritual forces — which claim to record conversations between Aristotle and Alexander, in which the philosopher instructs his royal pupil on the cosmos, the genesis of everything in it, and astral magic: talismanry, rituals for attracting planetary and spiritual forces, amulets, and extensive astrological rules. Saif's study stresses the cluster's double allegiance — Aristotelian in its frame, Hermetic in its substance — and its resonance with Zoroastrian astro-cosmogenic doctrines. Around these three clusters the shelf extends: letters of Aristotle to Alexander on the regimen of health and on the wonders of India; a physiognomy ascribed to Aristotle and said to be translated by Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq; mineralogical and alchemical opuscula that attached themselves to the Secretum in its printed editions, including a tract De mineralibus that appears in no incunable edition and surfaces in print only in 1501; and, in Latin, short alchemical pieces under Aristotle's name that seem to be original Latin compositions which acquired the attribution in transmission. The pattern is uniform enough to state as a rule: where a text promises secrets — of rule, of stones, of the stars, of gold — and signs itself Aristotle to Alexander, it is medieval, and its Aristotle is a character in it, not its author.

// HOW TO TELL THE TWO ARISTOTLES APART IN THE WILD //

Three tests, in descending order of certainty. Language and world: the genuine corpus is Greek, fourth-century BCE, innocent of Islam, of Latin scholasticism, and of any king's secret archive; a work that knows those worlds is not his. Form: the genuine works are school treatises — analytical, cross-referring, addressed to no one; letters to Alexander, dialogues of instruction to a king, and books of secrets are the pseudepigrapha's native forms. Doctrine: the genuine Aristotle promises nothing; a text that offers a recipe, a talisman, or a method for gold under his name has failed the test its author hoped it would pass. Book IV of the Meteorologica passes all three tests in form and fails none demonstrably in doctrine — which is why its case (Section XII) is an argument, and these are not.

It remains to say why the pseudepigraphy worked, because the answer returns the article to its subject. Aristotle's name could authorise alchemy only because Aristotle's genuine philosophy had already made him the supreme authority on the sublunar world — on elements, mixture, and the generation of metals. The forgers were not attaching their secrets to a random sage; they were attaching them to the one author whose real work discussed how metals form in the earth. Pseudepigraphy is, among other things, a bibliography: it tells the historian exactly which genuine texts a later age found authoritative, by showing which author's name it stole. The pseudo-Aristotelian shelf is thus, read correctly, evidence for the thesis of this article — that Aristotle's matter theory is alchemy's foundation — even though not one book on it is by him.

XV. What the Alexandrians Actually Took: Aristotle in the Lecture Hall

The transmission from Aristotle to the alchemists was not a rumour; it was a curriculum. Its clearest surviving figure is Olympiodorus of Alexandria (c. 495/505–after 565 CE), and he embodies the whole argument of this article in one career — which is why the hub's Commentators article, to which this section is the foundation-side companion, gives him its central chapters. Olympiodorus, pupil of Ammonius Hermiae and his successor as head of the Alexandrian school, lectured on Plato and on Aristotle from the professor's chair, and his surviving commentaries preserve the format of live teaching. Among them is a commentary on Aristotle's Meteorology — datable with unusual precision because it mentions a comet of 565 — in which the master text of Sections IX–X is expounded, lemma by lemma, to students in a Christian empire by the last pagan head of the school. And ascribed to the same name is an alchemical commentary: a treatise on Zosimos's work On the Activity (the Kat' energeian), expounding the sacred art's authorities in the same exegetical manner. Whether the philosopher and the alchemical commentator are one man or two is a genuine scholarly question — the Commentators article states the identity debate fairly, and this article does not settle it — but the question's very existence is the datum: in sixth-century Alexandria, the distance between lecturing on the Meteorologica and lecturing on Zosimos was small enough that posterity could not keep the lecturers apart.

What, concretely, did the lecture hall take from Aristotle? Cristina Viano's survey “Byzantine Alchemy, or the Era of Systematization” (in the Oxford Handbook of Science and Medicine in the Classical World, 2018), consulted for this article, characterises the commentator generation — Synesius in the fourth century, Olympiodorus in the sixth, Stephanus of Alexandria in the seventh — as a generation tied to the Neoplatonic milieu whose writings were designed primarily to clarify the ideas of the previous generations, Democritus and Zosimos above all, and which represent, in her judgement, the most advanced stage of ancient alchemical theory. The clarification was performed with Aristotle's tools, and the tools can be enumerated. From Aristotle the commentators took the unity of matter: the doxography Olympiodorus constructs for the art's founders reads the early authorities as successive witnesses to a single material substrate of transformation — hylomorphism, turned into a history of philosophy. They took the quality square as the grid on which every operation could be plotted: dissolution, volatilisation, fixation, and tingeing as manipulations of hot, cold, wet, and dry. They took mixis as the model of what an operation achieves — not a coating but a new homogeneous body — and concoction as the model of how time and heat achieve it. And they took, from the school tradition Aristotle founded, the form of their own work: the commentary itself, lemma and exposition, theōria and lexis, by which a riddling ancient sentence is unfolded term by term before students. The Commentators article anatomises that form; its point here is genealogical. Alchemy entered the lecture hall, and the lecture hall's furniture was Aristotle's.

Stephanus of Alexandria, the cluster's last figure, completes the trajectory: a philosopher lecturing On the Great and Sacred Art in nine lectures at Constantinople, under imperial patronage, in the early seventh century — alchemy expounded from a chair of philosophy, in the commentary manner, with Aristotle's physics as its unquestioned frame. Within a generation of his death Alexandria itself had fallen to the Arab conquest, and the frame travelled — in Syriac and Arabic translation, with the commentaries riding on the texts — to Baghdad, where the Jabirian school would rebuild it into the sulphur–mercury theory of Section XIII. The Alexandrians' Aristotle is thus not one influence among others. He is the medium in which Greek alchemy was systematised, the language in which it was translated, and the authority under whose name its boldest later claims were issued. What they took, itemised, is this article's whole contents: form and matter, the four qualities and their square, mixture and concoction, the two exhalations and the metals they make underground — and a book, the fourth of the Meteorologica, whose authorship they never thought to doubt.

XVI. Reading Aristotle Today: What the Alchemists Owed, and What They Added

A modern reader who comes to Aristotle from the alchemical tradition should be warned about the temperature of the prose. The Physics and On Generation and Corruption are lecture texts: compressed, allusive, written for students who had heard the arguments aloud. The rewarding order is the tradition's own inverted one, now knowingly adopted: begin with Meteorologica III.6 and IV entire — a dozen pages that contain the metals, the homogeneous bodies, and the catalogue of dispositions — in Webster's Oxford translation or Lee's Loeb; proceed to On Generation and Corruption I.10 and II.1–5, for mixture and the square; and only then to Physics I–II, where the analysis of change and the four causes will read, after the concrete texts, as the general theory of a world the reader has already visited. Reeve's Hackett translations offer a modern alternative for the Physics and other works, with the Greek's technical vocabulary handled consistently. Barnes's Complete Works remains the standard one-stop English Aristotle. For the two live disputes, Robinson (1974) against Charlton (1970) on prime matter, and Lee's Loeb introduction against Hammer-Jensen (1915) on Book IV, are short, primary, and still the best doors into their respective literatures.

The assessment, finally, in the double form this hub uses for every founder. What the alchemists owed Aristotle is the possibility of their theory: a world in which bodies are form-and-matter compounds of four quality-defined elements, in which true mixture makes homogeneous substances with changeable ratios, in which metals grow underground from opposed exhalations and ripen under heat, is a world in which transmutation is a research programme rather than a magician's boast — false, as it turned out, in its central hope, but structured, criticisable, and fertile for fifteen hundred years, which is more than most research programmes achieve. The programme's by-products were not negligible: the commentary tradition's glossary of material dispositions, the Arabic science of proportion, the Latin corpuscular theory that Newman traces into the Scientific Revolution, and the very idea — Aristotelian to its root — that a material's behaviour is the lawful expression of its composition. Chemistry did not descend from alchemy by repudiating this inheritance but by cashing it: Lavoisier's elements are not Aristotle's, but the question they answer — into what can bodies be analysed, and what is present in them potentially? — is GC's question, asked with better instruments.

What the alchemists added, and must not be allowed to backdate, is equally clear after Sections XIII–XIV: the two principles, sulphur and mercury, as constituents; the identification of the exhalations with workshop substances; the balance's quantitative mysticism; the corpuscular reinterpretation of mixture; the stone, the elixir, and the whole soteriology of perfected matter; and the shelf of secret books under Aristotle's stolen name. Additions are not disgraces — the sulphur–mercury theory organised real laboratory knowledge for a millennium, and the corpuscular turn helped to found modern matter theory — but they are additions, and the discipline of this hub is to date them. Aristotle's matter theory ends, as it began, with a distinction its inheritors kept blurring and its historians must keep sharp: between the philosopher who explained how metals are born in the earth from vapour and smoke, and the legend who was made to promise that men could unmake and remake them at will. The first is the foundation. The second is the building's most famous ghost. This article has kept to the foundation — the house of qualities, ratios, and concoctions, on which everything else in the wing was built.
