A handful of the living, in the Greek and Roman telling, went down into the house of Hades and returned: to ask a question, to fetch a hound, to steal a queen, to sing a wife back, to found an empire, or — once, unforgettably — to fetch a dead tragedian. This article maps the place they went to, tells each descent in full, and keeps the stories, the rites behind them, and the philosophy later built on them apart.

I. Katabasis, Nekyia, Anabasis: The Words and the Distinctions

The Greek word at the centre of this article is katabasis — from kata, “down”, and bainō, “I go” — a going-down. In its specialised modern use, which follows the ancient stories’ own shape, a katabasis is a journey by a living person down into the realm of the dead, and back. The return has its own word, anabasis, a going-up, and the pair belong together: a descent without a return is not a katabasis but a death. The Sibyl of Cumae states the asymmetry in the most quoted lines of the whole tradition, warning Aeneas before she will guide him (Virgil, Aeneid 6.126–129, trans. Robert Fagles, 2006):

“The descent to the Underworld is easy. Night and day the gates of shadowy Death stand open wide, but to retrace your steps, to climb back to the upper air — there the struggle, there the labor lies.” — VIRGIL, AENEID 6.126–129, TRANS. ROBERT FAGLES (2006)

Against katabasis the scholarship sets a second word, and the distinction is load-bearing for everything that follows. A nekyia (from nekys, a corpse, a dead person) is a rite for summoning and questioning the dead — an act of necromancy — in which the dead come up, or out, to the living, rather than the living going down to them. Odyssey Book 11, the oldest and most influential underworld episode in Greek literature, bears the traditional title Nekyia, and it earns it: Odysseus does not enter the house of Hades. He sails to the edge of the world, digs a pit, pours blood into it, and the dead come to him (Section VIII). Radcliffe Edmonds, in Myths of the Underworld Journey (2004), builds his analysis of the whole family of stories on the recognition that each telling is a fresh piece of work with traditional materials — his term, following Lévi-Strauss, is bricolage — and that the pattern (obstacles at the boundary, means of overcoming them, and a result that marks off one group of people from another) is constant while the meaning is remade by every teller. This article follows that discipline. Homer, Hesiod, Pindar, Aristophanes, Plato, Apollodorus, and Virgil are not chapters of one doctrine. They are successive, competing maps of the same dark country, drawn for different audiences and different purposes, and they disagree — about the rivers, about the judges, about whether anyone down there is happy — in ways this article reports rather than reconciles.

Three registers must therefore be kept apart throughout, and the article’s sections are arranged to keep them so. First, the tales as told: the geography and the descents as the poems and mythographers present them (Sections II–XIII). Second, the ritual footing: the oracles of the dead, the burial customs, and the hero cults that gave the stories a footing in practice (Sections IV and XV). Third, the philosophical remaking: what Plato, and after him the whole later tradition, did with a geography they no longer simply believed (Sections XIV and XVII). A sentence drawn from one register is never allowed to do duty in another without the transfer being named.

KATABASIS

A living person’s descent into the realm of the dead and return: Heracles, Orpheus, Theseus (in part), Aeneas, and — in comedy — Dionysus. The return, the anabasis, is the feat; the descent itself, as the Sibyl says, is easy.

NEKYIA

A summoning of the dead for questioning, performed at the boundary — a pit of blood at the edge of Ocean, a tomb, a lakeside oracle. Odysseus’s rite in Odyssey 11 is the type case: the dead come to the living man, who never crosses into their country.

THE TRAVELLERS

Each descent in this article has a stated errand: knowledge (Odysseus), a labour imposed by a master (Heracles), theft (Theseus and Pirithous), love (Orpheus), a father and a destiny (Aeneas), a poet (Dionysus). The errand, not the scenery, is what each story is about.

II. Hades the God, Hades the Place

Greek uses one word, Haidēs — in the older form Aïdēs, traditionally explained as “the Unseen” — for both the god who rules the dead and the realm he rules, and the ambiguity is ancient and productive. When Homer’s heroes go down “to Hades”, they go to a house and to its master at once: the Odyssey speaks throughout of “the house of Hades”, a dwelling with gates, a threshold, and a household, ruled by the god and his queen. Keeping the two senses apart is the first act of map-making, because the god has a biography and the place has a geography, and they are not the same story.

The God

Hades the god is a son of Kronos and Rhea, brother of Zeus and Poseidon, and in the division of the cosmos that the Iliad puts into Poseidon’s mouth (15.187–193, paraphrased) the three brothers cast lots for their domains: Zeus drew the sky, Poseidon the sea, and Hades the murky darkness below — the dead and the underworld — while earth and Olympus remained common to all. He is, in the Theogony’s family tree treated in this hub’s Theogony, “strong Hades, pitiless in heart, who dwells under the earth” (Hesiod, Theogony 455, Evelyn-White’s rendering, paraphrased in context here). His epithets mark his double nature. As receiver of the dead he is polydegmōn and polyxenos, “host of many” — the one host no guest refuses. As keeper of the earth’s buried wealth he is Ploutōn, Pluto, “the Wealthy One”, the name under which Rome received him as Dis; the euphemism is transparent and was already felt as one in antiquity — wealth is the polite name for the power nobody wishes to name plainly. His queen is Persephone, daughter of Demeter, whose abduction and divided year are the charter myth of Eleusis told in this hub’s Mysteries; in the underworld she is no victim but a sovereign, dread Persephone, whose permission every traveller in this article must in the end obtain.

Two features of the god matter for the descents. First, he is not a devil. Greek tradition gives Hades no programme of tormenting the dead for its own sake, no rebellion against Zeus, and almost no myths of his own initiative at all beyond the abduction of Persephone: he is a gaoler-king administering an allotment, bound by the same order of things as his brothers, and — crucially — capable of being petitioned, bargained with, and on one famous occasion moved by a song (Section XI). The punishments of Tartarus (Section VI) fall on those who offended gods, not on ordinary human sinners. Second, he is nearly invisible in cult. Where Zeus and Poseidon had temples in every city, Hades had almost none — the famous exception, the sanctuary at Elis where a temple of Hades was opened once a year and entered only by its priest, is reported by Pausanias (6.25, paraphrased) precisely as a curiosity. Greeks sacrificed to him, when they did, with averted faces and offerings struck into the ground. The god of the dead was honoured by being left alone, and his realm was imagined in the same spirit: not worshipped, not explored, and — except by the travellers of this article — not visited.

The Place

Hades the place, in the earliest texts, is less a country than a condition. Homer’s dead are psychai, breath-souls, strengthless heads (the Odyssey’s recurring phrase, in Murray’s translation) who flit like shadows or dreams, witless until they drink blood, and housed in a murk — Erebos is Homer’s word for the darkness in and under which they gather — that has a gate, a meadow, and almost no interior architecture. Its location is correspondingly vague, and the vagueness is geographical in two directions at once. It is below: heroes go down to it, it lies under the earth at a depth Hesiod will presently measure (Section VI). And it is beyond: Odysseus reaches its threshold by sailing across Ocean to the land of the Cimmerians, where the sun never shines (Section VIII), so that the realm of the dead sits at the edge of the world’s map as well as under its floor. Later tradition multiplied entrances and stitched the two directions together — caves and lakes in the known world, at Tainaron, at Heraclea, at Avernus, where the upper world was held to open downward (Section XV) — but the Homeric picture never lost its authority: a house at the world’s edge and under its foundations, whose inhabitants are what is left of people when strength, memory, and voice have gone out of them.

// NOT HELL //

The single most important correction a modern reader needs is negative. Homeric Hades is not a place of moral judgement for ordinary people: there is no heaven for the good and no hell for the bad in Odyssey 11, only the same grey twilight for kings and hirelings, which is why Achilles’ verdict on it (Section VIII) is so bleak. Judgement, reward, and punishment enter the map slowly — Sections V–VII and XIV trace the entry — and are never, in the pagan tradition, universalised into the two-destination scheme later ages take for granted.

The population of the place, as the tradition assembles it, divides into kinds, and the descents meet them in a fairly stable order that Virgil will eventually codify (Section XII): the unburied, who cannot properly enter; the untimely dead — infants, the unmarried, those cut off early; the great company of ordinary shades in the meadow; the heroes, who keep something of their earthly pursuits and grievances; the exceptional sufferers, punished for offences against gods; and, at the brightening margin, the exceptional blessed. The staff of the place is equally stable: Charon at the crossing (Section IV), Cerberus at the gate (Sections IV and IX), the judges in their court (Section V), Hermes in his one underworld office — psychopompos, conductor of souls, who in Odyssey 24 (paraphrased) leads the suitors’ ghosts down with his golden wand, squeaking like bats — and Hecate at the crossroads and thresholds, honoured already in Hesiod above all the Titan-born (see Theogony, Section VII). What the tradition never supplies is a survey. Every traveller sees a part, from a path, in the dark, and the maps disagree because the journeys did.

III. The Rivers of the Dead

An underworld with a geography needs water, and the tradition supplied it river by river, over centuries. The finished system — five named rivers, each with a character and an office — is so familiar from later literature that it is easy to mistake it for a Homeric given. It is not. The rivers enter the texts one or two at a time, and the full fivefold scheme is a late systematisation, completed by Plato and canonised by Virgil. Tracing the entries in order is the cleanest demonstration in this article of how the geography of the dead was built.

Styx: The First River, and an Oath Before It Is a Crossing

The oldest underworld river is the Styx, and in Hesiod — its fullest early treatment, Theogony 775–806, paraphrased from Evelyn-White’s translation — it is not yet primarily a river of the dead at all. Styx is a goddess, eldest daughter of Ocean, who lives apart from the gods in a glorious house vaulted with great rocks and propped to heaven with silver pillars, far under the earth, where a branch of Ocean flows through the dark night: a tenth part of Ocean’s water is allotted to her. Her office is the gods’ oath. When strife arises on Olympus, Zeus sends Iris with a golden jug to fetch the famous cold water of Styx, and a god who pours a libation of it and swears falsely lies breathless for a full year, cut off from nectar and ambrosia, and exiled from the gods’ councils for nine more. The river of the dead begins, in other words, as the cosmos’s instrument of truth — the water by which even gods are bound — and its dreadfulness is juridical before it is topographical. Homer knows Styx in the same register: gods swear by “the down-flowing water of Styx”, and the Iliad (8.369, paraphrased) can use “the gates of Hades and dread Persephone” and Styx together as the limit of Heracles’ labours. Only later does Styx settle into the role the handbooks give it, as the river Charon ferries — and even then the texts disagree about which river the ferry crosses (Section IV).

Acheron and Cocytus: The Homeric Pair

Homer’s own underworld rivers, named where Circe gives Odysseus his sailing directions (Odyssey 10.513–515, paraphrased from Murray’s translation), are Acheron, the river of woe, and its two tributaries: Pyriphlegethon, the river blazing with fire, and Cocytus, the river of wailing, which Circe calls a branch of the water of Styx; all three flow together into Acheron at a rock where the traveller is to dig his pit. That is the whole Homeric river-system: a confluence at the edge of Ocean, described in four lines, as a landmark for a rite. Acheron had a double life the other rivers never quite matched, because there was a real Acheron — a river of Thesprotia in north-western Greece, flowing through a gloomy gorge into an Acherusian lake near the coast — and the real river anchored the mythic one to a visitable place, with an oracle of the dead on its banks (Section XV). Cocytus, “Wailing”, stays mostly a name and a sound: the river whose name is what the dead do beside it.

Pyriphlegethon and Lethe: Fire and Forgetting

Pyriphlegethon — Phlegethon in its shortened Latin form — is Homer’s fire-river, and Plato will give it the fullest ancient description: a stream that issues into a vast region of fire, forms a boiling lake of water and mud larger than the sea, coils many times around the earth, and plunges at last into Tartarus, throwing up jets of fire — the lava streams of the upper world are its offshoots — wherever it nears the surface (Phaedo 113a–b, paraphrased; Section XIV). Lethe, Forgetfulness, is the latest arrival and the most consequential. As a personified power she is old — Hesiod makes Lethe a daughter of Strife (Theogony 227, paraphrased) — but as an underworld river or plain whose water the dead drink and forget, she belongs to the developed eschatologies: to the gold tablets’ geography, where the forbidden spring is hers by implication and the chosen water is Memory’s (the tablets’ own treatment is in The Mysteries, Section IX, and is not duplicated here); to Plato’s Myth of Er, where the souls cross the plain of Lethe and drink from the river of Unmindfulness before rebirth (Section XIV); and to Virgil, whose Anchises points out the souls at Lethe’s stream who must forget everything before they can return to bodies (Section XII). A river that erases the traveller is the tradition’s most elegant invention: the underworld’s final defence is not a wall but a drink.

The systematisation, when it comes, is Plato’s. In the Phaedo’s closing myth (111c–114c, paraphrased throughout; Section XIV) the four great rivers — Ocean, Acheron, Pyriphlegethon, and Cocytus, the last there called the Stygian river, whose lake is Styx — are set into a single hydraulic machine: all flow into and out of Tartarus, which pierces the whole earth, on a great oscillation, like breathing, and each takes the character of the soil it passes through. Virgil inherits the Platonic completeness and gives it a traveller’s itinerary: Acheron’s flood, the Stygian marsh, Cocytus winding round the grove before the descent, Lethe in Elysium. The reader should hold the chronology firmly. Homer gives a confluence of three streams at the world’s edge; Hesiod gives an oath-river in a silver-pillared house; Plato gives a system; Virgil gives a route. The five-river underworld of the handbooks is the sediment of eight centuries, and this article cites each river, wherever it can, from the stratum in which it actually appears.

IV. The Crossing: Charon, the Obol, and Cerberus

Between the living and the dead the tradition sets water, and on the water a boatman. Charon — his name has no agreed etymology, and the flashing-eyed Charon of Etruscan demonology, Charun, is a cousin whose exact relation to the Greek ferryman is debated — is absent from Homer and Hesiod altogether. He enters the record in the archaic epic tradition and in art: the lost epic Minyas knew him, the great fifth-century painting of the underworld by Polygnotus in the Lesche of the Cnidians at Delphi — described in detail by Pausanias (10.28, paraphrased) — showed him poling his boat on Acheron with the dead aboard, and Attic white-ground lekythoi of the fifth century BCE, the oil-flasks placed in graves, show him again and again: an old, rough, unshaven working man in a short tunic and cap, standing in his skiff with the pole in his hands, receiving a shade at the reed-fringed shore, sometimes from Hermes’ hand. The iconography is the theology. Charon is not a judge and not a tormentor; he is a ferryman doing a job, and his one condition is the condition of his trade: the fare.

The Obol: A Literary Fare with an Archaeological Footing

The fare was an obol — a small coin, a sixth of a drachma — and the custom of placing it with the dead is the point at which this article’s geography touches the ground most firmly. Greek and Latin literary sources name the coin, call it Charon’s naulon, his boat-fare, and specify its placement in or on the mouth of the corpse; Aristophanes makes the fare a joke his whole audience is expected to get (Section XIII), and later writers from Virgil’s commentators to Lucian treat it as common knowledge. Archaeology confirms that the custom behind the joke was real, and also corrects its tidy form. Coins are found in burials across the Greek and Roman world from the fifth century BCE onward — in the mouth, yes, but also on the eyes, in the hand, and beside the head; of various denominations, not always the single obol of the texts; sometimes several, sometimes purpose-made thin gold pieces, the danakai, that were never currency at all; and in only a minority of burials, never universally. The honest summary, which this article adopts, is the one the brief for it prescribes: the obol is a practice with archaeological footing, stated as such — a real and widespread funerary custom that the literature simplified into a single coin in the mouth for a single ferryman. The simplification itself is evidence: a culture does not joke about a fare, or mint imitation coins for the dead, unless the crossing and its cost are part of its furniture of thought. What the archaeology will not support is the reverse inference, that every coin in a grave is a ferry fare and every Greek expected to pay one; grave coins did other work too, as offerings and as wealth, and the specialists who study them (the modern literature runs from Kurtz and Boardman’s Greek Burial Customs through the studies gathered under the heading of “Charon’s obol” as an archaeological term of art) warn against letting the ferryman claim them all.

The crossing had a second condition, stricter than the coin: burial. In Virgil’s systematisation (Section XII) the unburied crowd the near bank and may not cross for a hundred years, and the rule is already the emotional engine of Odyssey 11, where the first shade Odysseus meets is Elpenor — unburied on Circe’s roof, begging for his mound and his oar (Section VIII). The dead who had received their rites belonged below; the unburied belonged nowhere. Greek horror at the unburied corpse — the premise of Sophocles’ Antigone — is the same belief in its civic form: burial is the passport, and the obol is small change beside it.

Cerberus at the Gate

Past the crossing stands the gate, and at the gate the dog. Cerberus — Greek Kerberos; Hesiod (Theogony 310–312, paraphrased) makes him a child of Typhaon and Echidna, brother to the Hydra and the Nemean lion — is in Hesiod “the brazen-voiced hound of Hades, shameless, fifty-headed, eater of raw flesh”. Homer knows him without the name, as simply “the hound” whom Heracles was sent to fetch (Iliad 8.368; Odyssey 11.623, paraphrased). Later tradition regularised the heads to three — Apollodorus (2.5.12, paraphrased) gives him three dogs’ heads, a dragon’s tail, and serpents’ heads along his back — and gave him his famous double office, which the Latin tradition states as a maxim and the Greek stories everywhere imply: he fawns on those who enter and devours those who try to leave. Cerberus is the anabasis problem with teeth. The descent is easy; the gate-dog faces the other way. How the travellers get past him is therefore a diagnostic of each story: Heracles wrestles him, bare-handed, by his master’s permission (Section IX); Orpheus sings him to sleep in the later tellings; the Sibyl throws him a honey-cake drugged with soporific herbs, and Virgil’s three throats gape, take it, and sink (Aeneid 6.417–425, paraphrased); and Aristophanes, characteristically, leaves him offstage and gives the door to a doorman with a grudge (Section XIII).

V. Judges, Meadows, and the Ordinary Dead: The Asphodel Meadow

Most of the dead, in the early tradition, go nowhere in particular, and the name of nowhere is the asphodel meadow. Asphodel — a grey-white lily of poor Greek soils, food of the poor in life — carpets the Homeric underworld: Achilles strides away over it rejoicing at his son’s fame (Odyssey 11.538–539, in Murray’s translation), Orion drives the wild beasts he slew across it (11.572–575), and in Odyssey 24 (paraphrased) the suitors’ ghosts are led down past Ocean, the White Rock, the gates of the Sun, and the land of dreams to the meadow where the spirits dwell. It is not a punishment and it is not a reward. It is a pasture for shades: flat, dim, and endless, the visual form of a belief that death levels everyone into the same thin continuation. The meadow’s population keeps its earthly shape and, in the heroes’ case, its earthly preoccupations — Orion hunts, Minos judges, Heracles’ phantom carries its bow — but without body, strength, or, until blood is drunk, full wits. That is the baseline of Greek eschatology, and every brighter or darker region in this article is an exception carved out of it.

The Judges

Judgement enters the meadow early but narrowly. In Odyssey 11 (567–571) Odysseus sees Minos, the glorious son of Zeus, golden sceptre in hand, seated and giving judgement to the dead, who sit and stand about him through the wide-gated house and ask for his decisions — in Murray’s translation, paraphrased here only in arrangement. The scene is a king holding court, not a tribunal of souls: Minos settles the dead’s disputes as he settled the living’s in Crete, and Homer gives no hint that he assigns anyone’s eternal lot. The developed bench of three — Minos, Rhadamanthys, and Aeacus — is a later construction, assembled from figures each of whom begins elsewhere. Rhadamanthys, Minos’s brother, is in Homer already exceptionally placed: the Odyssey (4.563–565, paraphrased) sets him in Elysium, and Pindar will make him the assessor of the judge of the dead and counsellor in the Isles of the Blest (Section VII). Aeacus, grandfather of Achilles, famed in life for justice and for praying Athens out of a drought, becomes in the developed tradition the underworld’s doorkeeper and judge of Europeans — Aristophanes demotes him, unforgettably, to Pluto’s porter with a score to settle (Section XIII). Plato completes the bench and gives it jurisdiction: in the Gorgias myth (523a–524a, paraphrased) Zeus appoints his three sons — Minos, Rhadamanthys, and Aeacus — as judges of the dead at the parting of the roads, because judgements in the old days, delivered on clothed and living defendants, were corrupted by fine appearances; henceforth the dead are judged naked, soul by soul, by dead judges, and sent right to the Isles or left to Tartarus. The Phaedo (113d, paraphrased) has the dead conducted to a place of judgement and sorted — the middling to Acheron and the lake, the incurable to Tartarus, the curably wicked to Tartarus for a year and out again by the rivers, the exceptionally holy to the upper earth. Between Homer’s court scene and Plato’s tribunal lies the whole moralisation of the Greek afterlife, and it happens, characteristically, not in cult but in philosophy (Section XIV).

MINOS

Cretan king and lawgiver, son of Zeus. In Homer he holds court among the dead with a golden sceptre; in the developed tradition he presides over the bench of three, and Virgil gives him a court of the falsely condemned, shaking the urn and summoning the silent council (Section XII).

RHADAMANTHYS

Minos’s brother, the just man of Crete. Placed by Homer in Elysium; by Pindar at Kronos’s right hand in the Isles of the Blest; by Plato on the bench, judging the dead of Asia. Virgil sets him in Tartarus itself, hearing and punishing confessed frauds (Section XII).

AEACUS

King of Aegina, grandfather of Achilles and Ajax, proverbial for justice. Keeper of the keys and judge in the developed scheme; in Aristophanes’ Frogs, Pluto’s doorman, still furious at Heracles for stealing the dog.

THE ORDINARY DEAD

No verdict at all, in the early map: the asphodel meadow, strengthless and dim, for farmer and king alike. The ordinariness is the point — and the pressure that slowly produced every exception in Sections VI, VII, and XIV.

One further class of the dead stands between the meadow’s anonymity and the exceptions, and the descents keep meeting them: the untimely and the unquiet — the aōroi who died before their season (infants, the unmarried, the slain), the ataphoi who died unburied, and the biaiothanatoi who died by violence. Greek belief, documented across the necromancy evidence assembled by Daniel Ogden in Greek and Roman Necromancy (2001), held such dead to be restless, near the surface, and available: they are the ghosts necromancers could most readily raise, the shades who crowd Odysseus’s pit and Charon’s bank, and the population Virgil will presently arrange in its own border regions (Section XII). The geography of the dead, in short, has a sociology, and it is the sociology of the living: those whom life left unfinished are imagined as unfinished in death, lingering at the boundary where the travellers pass.

VI. Tartarus Below Hades

Below the house of Hades lies somewhere worse, and the tradition is precise about the depth. Tartarus — in Hesiod’s Theogony one of the first things to come into being (116–120), a pit in the depth of the wide-pathed earth — receives its measure at the close of the Titanomachy, when the defeated Titans are hurled down and bound there (720–726). In Evelyn-White’s Loeb translation (1914):

“For a brazen anvil falling down from heaven nine nights and days would reach the earth upon the tenth: and again, a brazen anvil falling from earth nine nights and days would reach Tartarus upon the tenth.” — HESIOD, THEOGONY 722–725, TRANS. H. G. EVELYN-WHITE (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY, 1914)

The image is a surveyor’s: the cosmos is symmetrical, heaven-to-earth as earth-to-Tartarus, nine days’ fall each way, and Tartarus is as far below the underworld as the underworld is below the sky. Around it, Hesiod continues (726–735, paraphrased), runs a fence of bronze, and night spreads in a triple line about it like a neck-circlet; above it grow the roots of the earth and the unfruitful sea; Poseidon fixed gates of bronze upon it, and the Hundred-Handers — Gyes, Cottus, and great-souled Obriareus — live there as trusty warders of Zeus. A little further on (736–745, paraphrased) the sources and ends of earth, Tartarus, sea, and heaven stand all in order in a dank gulf which even the gods abhor, with gates of marble and a threshold of bronze that no one who enters crosses again within the year. Tartarus in Hesiod is a prison, built for defeated gods, and its inmates are the Titans: beings who cannot die, held in the only custody that can hold them. That origin governs its whole later history. Tartarus is never, in the early tradition, where bad people go. It is where dangerous powers are kept.

The human inmates arrive by a specific door: offences against gods in person. The three sufferers Odysseus glimpses in Odyssey 11 (576–600, Murray’s translation, paraphrased here) are the type cases, and each is a cosmic, not a moral, offender. Tityos, the giant son of Earth, lies stretched over nine roods while two vultures tear his liver, because he laid violent hands on Leto, Zeus’s wife, as she passed through Panopeus toward Delphi. Tantalus stands in a pool that shrinks from his chin whenever he stoops to drink, under fruit trees whose branches the wind tosses to the clouds whenever he reaches — Homer, notably, does not say what his crime was; the later tradition supplies the banquet of his son Pelops and the theft of the gods’ nectar and ambrosia. Sisyphus strains to thrust his monstrous stone to the crest of the hill, and as often as he is about to heave it over, the weight turns it back and the ruthless stone rolls down to the plain, and he braces and thrusts again, sweat streaming, dust rising from his head — Homer again gives no charge sheet; the trickster who chained Death and talked his way back from the dead belongs to the later accretions. The pattern is exact: these are not sinners in a general hell but particular, named transgressors against the divine order, punished in person, forever, in a place Odysseus can see but — significantly — does not enter and is not said to be beneath: in the Odyssey the sufferers are simply there, in the same visual field as the meadow, and the hard stratification of Hades above and Tartarus below is Hesiod’s and Plato’s work, not Homer’s.

// IXION, AND THE WHEEL THAT IS NOT IN HOMER //

Later lists of the great sufferers add Ixion, bound to his fiery wheel for attempting Hera, and the Danaids with their leaking jars. Neither is in Odyssey 11: Ixion’s wheel is Pindaric (Pythian 2, paraphrased) and the Danaids’ water-carrying belongs to the developed tradition Plato already plays with in the Gorgias. The catalogue of the damned, like the river-system, grew by accretion — a standing warning against citing “the Greek Tartarus” as if it were a single text.

Plato takes Tartarus over and rebuilds it as the engine of his eschatology: the chasm that pierces the whole earth, into which all rivers flow and out of which they surge (Phaedo 112a–c, paraphrased), the pit into which the incurable are cast by their fitting destiny and never emerge, while the curably wicked are carried out again after a year on the currents of Cocytus and Pyriphlegethon to beg their victims’ forgiveness at the Acherusian lake (113e–114c, paraphrased; Section XIV). Virgil takes it over in turn as the fortress-prison on the left-hand road, with its triple wall, its river of fire, its gate of adamant, and Tisiphone on watch night and day — and, notably, has the Sibyl describe it from outside: Aeneas, a living man on a sanctioned errand, does not go in (Section XII). The descent tradition’s deepest place is thus the one place its heroes are shown but never taken. Even katabasis has a floor it does not cross, and the floor is Tartarus.

VII. The Bright Exception: Elysium and the Isles of the Blest

Against the meadow’s grey and Tartarus’s bronze, the tradition slowly grows a bright exception — slowly, and at first only for a favoured few, on grounds that have nothing to do with moral desert. The earliest Elysium in Greek literature is a favour, not a judgement. In Odyssey 4 (561–569, paraphrased from Murray’s translation) the sea-god Proteus tells Menelaus that he, personally, will not die in Argos: the immortals will send him to the Elysian plain at the ends of the earth, where fair-haired Rhadamanthys is, and where life is easiest for men — no snow, no storm, no rain, but Ocean always sending the breezes of the west wind to refresh mankind. The reason given is not virtue. It is that Menelaus has Helen for a wife and is therefore Zeus’s son-in-law. Elysium begins as nepotism at the end of the world: a pleasant climate reserved by family connection, located, like Homer’s Hades threshold, at the earth’s edge rather than below it.

Hesiod gives the exception an address and a population. In the Works and Days (167–173, Evelyn-White’s translation, paraphrased in part), the fourth race of men — the god-like race of heroes, the demigods who fell at Thebes and Troy — are not all covered by the earth: to some of them Zeus grants a life apart, at the ends of the earth, where they live untouched by sorrow in the Islands of the Blessed along the shore of deep-swirling Ocean, happy heroes for whom the grain-giving earth bears honey-sweet fruit flourishing three times a year. (A line found in some papyrus copies adds that Kronos rules over them, released from his bonds; most editors treat the Kronos lines as a later interpolation, and this article flags them as such wherever they matter — Pindar, below, has the motif in his own right.) Two things have changed from Homer. The blessed place now belongs to a class — the heroes as a generation — and it has detached itself from the underworld altogether: the Isles lie beyond Ocean, in the far west, in the light. Greek blessedness, at its first appearance, is not a better room in Hades. It is an exemption from Hades.

Pindar, two and a half centuries later, moralises the exception — and the change is one of the most consequential in Greek religion. In Olympian 2 (476 BCE), composed for Theron of Acragas, the afterlife passage (56–80, paraphrased except where quoted) runs: below the earth a judge passes sentence on the sins committed in this realm of Zeus, with hateful necessity; the good receive a life without toil, in sunlight by night as by day, neither vexing earth nor sea for a livelihood; the oath-keeping go to live with the honoured gods; the wicked endure pain no eye can bear to look on. And for those who have kept their souls utterly from wrong through three lives on either side, there is a road out. In Diane Arnson Svarlien’s translation (1990):

“Those who have persevered three times, on either side, to keep their souls free from all wrongdoing, follow Zeus’s road to the end, to the tower of Kronos, where ocean breezes blow around the island of the blessed, and flowers of gold are blazing…” — PINDAR, OLYMPIAN 2.69–75, TRANS. DIANE ARNSON SVARLIEN (1990)

The ode continues (paraphrased): Rhadamanthys sits at Kronos’s right hand as his counsellor, and among the island’s inhabitants are Peleus and Cadmus — and Achilles, brought there by his mother Thetis’s prayers to Zeus, Achilles who laid low Hector and Memnon. Three novelties stand in this passage, and scholarship has worried at all three for a century. First, judgement by conduct: the sorting below is by how a life was lived, not by family or fame. Second, reincarnation: the three-times test presupposes repeated lives, a doctrine the tradition associates with Pythagoras and with the Orphic milieu — how far Pindar is reporting Theron’s Sicilian beliefs, how far preaching his own, is the standing debate, and it is reported here as a debate. Third, the relocation of the blessed: Pindar’s island is reached by Zeus’s road from the judgement below, so that the bright exception has begun to be annexed to the underworld system it began by exempting its holders from. Virgil completes the annexation: his Elysium is a region inside the underworld, on the right-hand road, with its own sun and stars (Section XII). The full arc — Homer’s favour at the world’s edge, Hesiod’s heroic exemption beyond Ocean, Pindar’s moralised island, Virgil’s department of the dead — is the slowly brightening exception of this article’s brief, and it brightens by being moralised, systematised, and finally filed.

// ELYSIUM IS NOT HEAVEN //

Even at its brightest, the pagan exception is not a universal offer. Homer’s Elysium is for Zeus’s in-laws; Hesiod’s Isles are for the hero generation as a whole; Pindar’s island is for souls that pass a three-lives test almost nobody could pass; the gold tablets’ blessed road (see The Mysteries) is for initiates carrying the right words. Ordinary good people, in every early scheme, go to the meadow with everyone else. The tradition’s hope grows — but it grows as privilege, initiation, and philosophy, not as a general promise, and this article keeps that proportion in view.

VIII. Odysseus at the Pit of Blood: Odyssey 11

The oldest descent in this article is not a descent. That is the point on which the scholarship insists, and the text bears it out line by line. In Odyssey 10 (paraphrased) Circe tells Odysseus that before he can sail home he must first complete another journey — to the house of Hades and dread Persephone, to consult the spirit of Theban Tiresias, the blind prophet whose wits Persephone has left intact alone among the dead — and she gives him not a route underground but sailing directions: across Ocean, to the shore of the Cimmerians, to the rock where Pyriphlegethon and Cocytus flow into Acheron, there to dig a pit and pour the offerings. Book 11, the Nekyia, is Odysseus’s first-person narration of that rite, told at the Phaeacian court — which is to say, the tradition’s foundational underworld text is a traveller’s tale told over wine, by the most accomplished liar in Greek literature, to hosts he is trying to impress. The archive does not discount it for that. It files it correctly: this is the map as the archaic Greek imagination drew it when a hero went to ask the dead a question.

The Rite at the Pit

The procedure is given with a ritualist’s exactness (11.24–37). In A. T. Murray’s Loeb translation (1919):

“I drew my sharp sword from beside my thigh, and dug a pit of a cubit’s length this way and that, and around it poured a libation to all the dead, first with milk and honey, thereafter with sweet wine, and in the third place with water, and I sprinkled thereon white barley meal.” — HOMER, ODYSSEY 11.24–27, TRANS. A. T. MURRAY (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY, 1919)

Odysseus then vows the future sacrifices — a barren heifer at home in Ithaca, and for Tiresias alone a wholly black ram, the best of the flock — takes the sheep his comrades Perimedes and Eurylochus hold, and cuts their throats over the pit so that the dark blood runs into it (11.27–37, paraphrased). The effect is immediate and terrible: the spirits gather out of Erebus, brides and unwedded youths, toil-worn old men, tender maidens new to sorrow, and many men slain in war still wearing their blood-stained armour, thronging about the pit with a wondrous cry, until pale fear seizes the living man. His comrades flay and burn the carcasses and pray to Hades and Persephone; Odysseus himself sits with drawn sword and will not suffer the strengthless heads of the dead to approach the blood until he has enquired of Tiresias (11.37–50, paraphrased). Every element here recurs in the later necromancy evidence of Section XV — the pit (bothros), the triple libation, the black victim, the blood as the medium that temporarily restores speech and memory to the witless dead, the sword as crowd control at the boundary — and the direction of influence runs, as Ogden’s study shows, between rite and story in both directions: Homer describes something his audience would recognise as an evocation, and every later evocation is staged, consciously or not, in Homer’s scenery.

Elpenor, Tiresias, Anticleia

The first shade is not the prophet but a casualty: Elpenor, the youngest of the crew, who had fallen drunk from Circe’s roof the night before and broken his neck, and whose corpse lies unburied in her hall (11.51–83, paraphrased). He speaks without drinking — the freshly dead, in the logic later necromancy makes explicit, still have voice — and his business is the rite’s first lesson: bury me, burn me with my armour, heap my mound on the shore of the grey sea, and plant on it the oar I rowed with, lest I become a cause of the gods’ wrath against you. Before prophecy, before knowledge, the dead ask for burial. The traveller’s first debt below is to the unburied at his feet.

Tiresias comes bearing his golden staff, knows Odysseus, and asks the question every shade will echo — why have you left the light of the sun to behold the dead and this region where there is no joy? (11.90–97, paraphrased). He drinks the dark blood and delivers the book’s prophecy, the errand’s whole yield (11.100–137, paraphrased): the return will be grievous because Poseidon nurses his wrath for the blinding of his son; yet home may still be reached, in evil plight, if Odysseus curbs himself and his comrades and leaves the cattle of Helios on Thrinacia unharmed; if they are harmed, ship and crew are lost and Odysseus comes home late, alone, on another’s ship, to find woes in his house. Then, beyond the return, the seer adds the strange coda: when the suitors are slain, Odysseus must take a shapely oar and walk inland until he reaches men who know nothing of the sea, who will mistake the oar for a winnowing-fan; there he must plant it and sacrifice to Poseidon, and thereafter death will come to him from the sea, gently, in sleek old age, with his people prosperous around him. It is the only prophecy in the poem that reaches past its own ending, and it is bought at the pit: the descent tradition’s first lesson is that the dead know what the living cannot — the shape of the whole road, including the part after home.

Then, released at last to the blood, Anticleia — Odysseus’s mother, alive when he sailed for Troy (11.84–89, 150–224, paraphrased except where noted). She tells him what no oracle could: that Penelope abides in his halls in sorrow, that Telemachus holds his lands unharassed, that his father Laertes wastes in the fields in grief and age, and that she herself died of neither disease nor Artemis’s gentle arrows but of longing for him — for his counsels and his tender-heartedness. Three times Odysseus springs to clasp her, and three times she flits from his arms like a shadow or a dream, and the pain grows sharper at his heart; and when he asks if Persephone has sent him a phantom to make him grieve the more, she explains the physics of the place (11.216–222, paraphrased): this is the appointed way with mortals when one dies — the sinews no longer hold flesh and bones together, the fire’s might destroys them once the life leaves the white bones, and the spirit, like a dream, flits away and hovers. Virgil will take the triple failed embrace and give it to Aeneas and Anchises (Section XII); Dante will take the explanation. The scene is the nekyia’s heart, and its knowledge is not prophecy at all. It is news from home, carried by the one messenger who cannot be embraced.

Heroines, Heroes, and the Glimpsed Sinners

What follows is arranged as two catalogues and a coda. First the heroines (11.225–332, paraphrased): Persephone sends forth the wives and daughters of chieftains, and Odysseus, holding them back with his sword so that each drinks and speaks in turn, questions them one by one — Tyro, Antiope, Alcmene the mother of Heracles, Megara, Epicaste the mother of Oedipus, Chloris, Leda, Iphimedeia, Phaedra, Procris, Ariadne, Maera, Clymene, and hateful Eriphyle, who took gold for her husband’s life. The catalogue is a genealogy of the heroic age told through its women, and its function in the poem is the function of all Homeric catalogue: to set the teller’s small story inside the whole known world of story. The Phaeacian interlude that breaks the book (11.333–384) — the queen’s praise, Alcinous begging him to continue — is the poem’s own admission that the underworld has become an entertainment of wonders, and the second half answers the request in kind.

The heroes (11.385–567, paraphrased except where quoted) come sorrowing, each asking after those dear to him. Agamemnon, drinking, tells his murder as a feast-day slaughter — slain like an ox at the stall by Aegisthus and Clytemnestra — and draws the poisoned moral he will not let go: return in secret, trust no one wholly, though he concedes Penelope is prudent and true. Achilles comes with Patroclus, Antilochus, and Ajax, and asks how Odysseus dared come down to Hades, where dwell the unheeding dead, the phantoms of men outworn; and when Odysseus comforts him — you were honoured as a god in life, and now you rule mightily among the dead, so grieve not — Achilles gives the tradition its most famous verdict, in Murray’s translation (11.488–491):

“I should choose, so I might live on earth, to serve as the hireling of another, of some portionless man whose livelihood was but small, rather than to be lord over all the dead that have perished.” — HOMER, ODYSSEY 11.489–491, TRANS. A. T. MURRAY (LOEB, 1919)

The line is the heroic age’s own epitaph, spoken by its greatest name: the glory that was worth a short life at Troy is, from below, worth less than the meanest living hireling’s day. And then — the poem’s fairness — Achilles asks after his son, hears of Neoptolemus’s prowess in council and battle and in the Wooden Horse, and departs with long strides over the asphodel meadow, rejoicing. Ajax, by contrast, stands apart in his wrath over the contest for Achilles’ arms, hears Odysseus’s soothing words, and answers not a word, but goes his way to Erebus to join the other spirits (11.543–567, paraphrased): the underworld preserves earthly grievance exactly as it preserves earthly love, and silence, in Homer, is a shade’s last dignity. The coda (11.568–627, paraphrased) turns from conversation to sight: Minos judging (Section V), Orion hunting, the three great sufferers Tityos, Tantalus, and Sisyphus in their torments (Section VI), and at last the phantom of Heracles — his eidōlon only, the poem insists, for he himself feasts among the immortal gods, wedded to Hebe — who recognises Odysseus and tells him, as one labourer to another, that Eurystheus once sent him hither to fetch the hound of Hades, the mightiest task of all, and that he carried the hound off with Hermes and Athena as his guides. Odysseus waits, hoping to see Theseus and Pirithous, glorious children of the gods — the poem’s own pointer to the next two descents of this article — but the myriad tribes of the dead come thronging with a wondrous cry, pale fear seizes him lest Persephone send up the Gorgon’s head, and he flees to his ship (11.627–640, paraphrased). The nekyia ends as it began, at the boundary, with the living man back on the water and the dead receding into Ocean. He brought away knowledge, and nothing else. That is what a nekyia is for.

// WHY THE DISTINCTION MATTERS //

Calling Odyssey 11 a “descent to Hades”, as handbooks loosely do, misreads its ritual logic and its geography alike. Odysseus never crosses Acheron, never pays Charon (who is not in Homer), never passes Cerberus, and never enters the house whose inmates he glimpses as sights at the end. He performs an evocation at the world’s edge and the dead attend him. The later tradition knew the difference: Aeschylus’ lost Psychagogoi staged Odysseus’s rite at a lakeside oracle of the dead, and the nekyomanteia of Section XV are its institutional form. Heracles, Orpheus, and Aeneas go down. Odysseus summons. The two acts have different rules, different risks, and different yields, and this article keeps their names separate throughout.

IX. Heracles for Cerberus: The Twelfth Labour as Descent

The first true katabasis in the tradition belongs to Heracles, and its form is set by his whole career: it is a labour. The full Heracles of this hub is treated in Heracles and the Labours; here the twelfth and last labour is told as what it is, the descent that all the others in this article imitate, parody, or fail at. The standard prose account is Apollodorus, Library 2.5.12, in J. G. Frazer’s Loeb translation (1921), paraphrased throughout this section except where noted. Eurystheus, the master Heracles serves, imposes as his final task the fetching of Cerberus from Hades — a labour whose point, as the shade of Heracles himself tells Odysseus, is that no mightier task could be devised (Section VIII). It differs from every other labour in kind. The lion, the hydra, the boar are animals in the world. Cerberus is the border of the world, and to fetch him the labourer must cross into a jurisdiction where strength, the only currency Heracles has ever spent, may not be legal tender.

The Preparation: Eleusis

The tradition therefore gives him a preparation, and it is ritual, not athletic. Apollodorus (2.5.12, paraphrased) reports that before descending Heracles went to Eumolpus at Eleusis to be initiated into the mysteries — and that there was a difficulty: a foreigner could not then be initiated, and Heracles was moreover polluted by the slaughter of the centaurs, so that he was first purified of the bloodshed by Eumolpus, and a device was found — in the fuller tradition, adoption into an Athenian house — to qualify him. The Lesser Mysteries themselves were explained in Athenian tradition as instituted for his sake (see The Mysteries, Section III). The meaning of the aetiology is plain and is stated here as the tradition’s own logic, not as history: the man of strength may not go down raw. He must first be purified, sponsored, and initiated — admitted, that is, to the one institution in Greece that promised its members a better lot below (see The Mysteries, Section V) — and only then does he take the road, in Apollodorus’s geography, down through the entrance at Tainaron, the southern cape of the Peloponnese, one of the attested gates of the dead (Section XV). The descent tradition’s first rule is thus established at its head: preparation is part of the feat.

Below: The Shades, the Rescue, the Wrestling

Below, Apollodorus’s account (2.5.12, paraphrased) is a tour in the Homeric manner. When the souls saw Heracles they fled, except Meleager and the Gorgon Medusa; Heracles drew his sword on the Gorgon as if she were alive, and learned from Hermes that she was an empty phantom — the living man’s weapons, and his reflexes, corrected by the guide. Meleager tells his story and, in the fuller telling that Bacchylides’ fifth ode (paraphrased) already knows, moves Heracles to tears — the only tears of the labours — and wins from him the promise to marry his sister Deianira, the marriage that will in the end kill him: the descent plants the seed of the descender’s own death, a pattern the tradition repeats (Section XVI). Near the gates Heracles finds Theseus and Pirithous bound fast for Pirithous’s wooing of Persephone; stretching out their hands to him as if he could raise them from the dead by his might, they are dealt with in Section X — Theseus raised, Pirithous abandoned when the earth quakes. He rolls away the stone of Ascalaphus, the informer pinned for betraying Persephone’s eating of the pomegranate. Wishing to provide the souls with blood, he slaughters one of the cattle of Hades; Menoetes, their herdsman, challenges him to wrestle, has his ribs broken, and is let off at Persephone’s request. The cattle episode is the nekyia folded inside the katabasis: even Heracles, mid-descent, performs Odysseus’s rite.

The climax is a negotiation followed by a wrestling match, in that order, and the order is the story’s teaching. Heracles asks Pluto for Cerberus, and Pluto grants him — on a condition: he may take the dog if he masters him without the weapons he carries (2.5.12, paraphrased). The labour is thereby redefined as the one feat of strength in the cycle that must be performed unarmed, in the house of the dead, under its king’s licence. Heracles finds the hound at the gates of Acheron, and, wearing his cuirass and covered by the lion’s skin, flings his arms around the beast’s head; though the dragon in its tail bites him, he never relaxes his grip until the dog yields. He carries Cerberus up, ascending — in Apollodorus’s geography — through Troezen, shows him to Eurystheus, and then carries him back down to Hades. The return journey matters as much as the fetching: the dog is borrowed, not stolen, exhibited, not kept, and restored to his gate. Where Pirithous came to steal a person and stayed forever (Section X), Heracles comes with permission, takes the guardian himself as a credential, and puts him back. Katabasis, in its founding instance, is a licensed visit.

// THE HOUND IN ART AND CULT //

Heracles dragging Cerberus, the dog rearing and the serpents of his back and tail writhing, is among the most painted scenes of archaic Greek vase-painting, and the labour had a cultic address: Heraclea Pontica, on the Black Sea, claimed the very cave of the ascent, showed the aconite said to have sprung from Cerberus’s foam, and housed an oracle of the dead in the same complex of traditions (Section XV). The geography of Section XV and the story of this section are, at Heraclea, the same place described twice.

One sequel completes the pattern. Heracles is the only figure in this article who descends twice in effect: in Euripides’ Alcestis (paraphrased) he wrestles Death himself at a tomb in the upper world and brings Alcestis back veiled to her husband, and in the lost and later traditions he is credited with raising others. But his own shade-scene in Odyssey 11 states the tradition’s final settlement of his case: the phantom is below, the man himself is on Olympus, married to Hebe. Heracles is the one descender whose anabasis never ends — he does not merely come back from the dead, he is promoted out of mortality altogether. Every later traveller is measured against that exception, and none repeats it.

X. Theseus and Pirithous: The Descent That Failed

Every tradition of licensed visiting needs its cautionary counter-case, and the Greek tradition’s is perfect in its symmetry: two friends descend together to steal the queen of the dead, and the underworld keeps them. The full Theseus of this hub is treated in Theseus; the descent is told here from its two standard prose sources, Apollodorus, Library 2.5.12 (where it is an episode of Heracles’ labour) and the Epitome 1.23–24, both in Frazer’s translation and paraphrased except where quoted.

The compact that sends them down is a bride-theft escalated past all reason (Epitome 1.23, paraphrased). Theseus, king of Athens, and Pirithous, king of the Lapiths, had sworn to help each other marry daughters of Zeus. For Theseus they carried off the child Helen from Sparta — the abduction that will in time bring her brothers, the Dioscuri, down on Athens. For Pirithous the lot and the oath demanded a bride of the same rank, and Pirithous named Persephone herself, the wife of Hades. The madness is precise: not a shade, not a mortal woman, but the queen of the realm they must enter, in her husband’s own house. They descend — the tradition gives the road variously, by Tainaron among them — and are received with the underworld’s own black courtesy. In Frazer’s translation of the Epitome (1.24):

“But when Theseus arrived with Pirithous in Hades, he was beguiled; for, on the pretence that they were about to partake of good cheer, Hades bade them first be seated on the Chair of Forgetfulness, to which they grew and were held fast by coils of serpents.” — APOLLODORUS, EPITOME 1.24, TRANS. SIR JAMES GEORGE FRAZER (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY, 1921)

The punishment is exquisitely fitted, and the fitting deserves attention. The would-be thieves are not chained by a gaoler; they are seated as guests. Hospitality — the institution Pirithous’s whole career of bride-theft abused — is the trap; the chair is Lethe’s own furniture, forgetfulness made solid, and the guests grow to it, flesh to rock in the variant Pausanias reports from the old poet Panyasis (10.29.9, paraphrased), so that the boundary between the visitor and the place he violated is abolished: they become part of the underworld’s fixtures. Pausanias’s description of Polygnotus’s great underworld painting at Delphi (10.29.9, paraphrased) shows them so, seated together in Hades, Theseus holding both his own sword and Pirithous’s, while Pirithous gazes at the now useless blades in anger that swords are of no service for their present venture — the heroic age’s equipment, carried down and voided. It is the exact negative of Heracles’ condition in Section IX: there, weapons are laid aside by agreement and strength, under licence, prevails; here, weapons are kept, no licence is sought, and strength is simply furniture.

The rescue, when Heracles arrives on his own labour (Section IX), divides the pair for ever, and the division is the tradition’s verdict. From Apollodorus (Library 2.5.12; Epitome 1.24, paraphrased): the two stretch out their hands to Heracles as if his might could raise them from the dead. He takes Theseus by the hand and raises him up — in the anecdotal embroidery the sources themselves flag, leaving a portion of Theseus’s flesh adhering to the rock, which Athenians were said to blame for their countrymen’s spareness in that part ever after — but when he would have raised Pirithous, the earth quaked, and he let go. Pirithous, the Epitome concludes, remained bound for ever; Theseus was brought up and sent to Athens. The earthquake is the underworld’s own ruling, delivered in its own language: one prisoner is released on the licensed visitor’s surety, the other is not negotiable. Scholarship and the tradition alike read the difference the same way. Theseus was an accomplice, guilty of the oath and the entry; Pirithous was the author — the desire was his, the bride was his choice, the offence against Persephone was his suit. The underworld’s court, like Plato’s judges in Section V, distinguishes the curable from the incurable, and it delivers its distinction not in a speech but in a tremor.

// A FAILED DESCENT IS STILL A DESCENT STORY //

Theseus and Pirithous contribute no knowledge, fetch no object, and free no one; the story’s yield is purely negative, and that is its function in the set. Set beside Sections VIII, IX, XI, and XII, it isolates the variable the others share: every successful traveller goes down on an errand the underworld can recognise — a question, a labour under orders, a plea in song, a father’s summons under Apollo’s sanction — and every one of them treats the realm’s king and queen as sovereigns to be petitioned. Theseus and Pirithous alone treat the realm as a house to be robbed. The chair of forgetfulness is what the geography of the dead does to tourism of that kind, and the tradition keeps the story, prominently, as the map’s warning label.

XI. Orpheus for Eurydice: Song, the Condition, and the Backward Look

Orpheus’s descent is the most retold story in this article, and the one where the division of labour within this hub must be stated before the story is told. Orpheus the figure — the Thracian singer, his Argonaut voyage, the books and rites that carried his name, the Derveni papyrus, and the gold tablets buried with initiates — belongs to The Mysteries, Sections VII–X, where the tablets’ texts and the debate over “Orphic religion” are treated in full. This section tells only the descent narrative: the journey down for Eurydice, as the two great Roman tellings give it. Cross-refer, for everything doctrinal, to The Mysteries; what follows is the story, kept as a story.

A caution on dates first, because the famous story is younger than its fame suggests. That Orpheus descended and returned is old: Euripides (Alcestis 357–360, paraphrased) already wishes for Orpheus’s voice to charm Demeter’s daughter and her husband and bring Alcestis up, and the singer’s power over the powers below is a commonplace by the late fifth century BCE. But the fully elaborated Eurydice narrative — the wedding, the snake, the descent, the condition, the backward look, the second death — survives complete only in the two Roman accounts: Virgil, Georgics 4.453–527, and Ovid, Metamorphoses 10.1–85, both paraphrased in this section except where quoted. Plato, earlier, knows a pointedly different version: in the Symposium (179d, paraphrased) Phaedrus says the gods showed Orpheus only a phantom of his wife, because he was soft, a mere lyre-player, and had not the courage to die for love as Alcestis did, but contrived to enter Hades alive. The variant is precious evidence that the story was still fluid in the classical period — and that antiquity itself already read Orpheus’s living descent, against Alcestis’s dying one, as a kind of cheating. The Roman telling answers the charge by making the cheat impossible: he may enter alive, but he may not verify.

The Story as Virgil Tells It

Virgil sets the tale inside another: the beekeeper Aristaeus, whose pursuit caused Eurydice’s death, learns it from Proteus as the explanation of his own losses (Georgics 4.453–485, paraphrased). Eurydice, fleeing Aristaeus along the riverbank, did not see the great water-snake in the deep grass, and died of its bite on her wedding day’s aftermath. Orpheus’s mourning fills the poem’s most famous music: he sang her on the lonely shore at sunrise and at sunset, and then he went down — through the Tainarian gate, in Virgil’s geography — into the deep groves of dread, and stood before the king and queen of the dead and sang. The effect of the song is the underworld’s one recorded change of heart (4.471–484, paraphrased): the thin shades came flocking like birds to a shelter at evening; the Furies’ snake-hair stilled; Cerberus held his three throats agape and forgot to bark; Ixion’s wheel stopped in the wind. Song does below what it does above in the Orpheus legend — it suspends the nature of things — and the suspended nature includes the law that the dead do not return. Eurydice is granted to him, on the condition Proserpina imposes: she follows behind, and he must not turn his eyes back until both have reached the upper air.

The ascent, and the failure, occupy a dozen lines (4.485–503). In A. S. Kline’s translation of the Georgics, the moment runs, in excerpt:

“…when a sudden madness seized the incautious lover, one to be forgiven, if the spirits knew how to forgive: he stopped, and forgetful, alas, on the edge of light, his will conquered, he looked back, now, at his Eurydice.” — VIRGIL, GEORGICS 4.485–491, TRANS. A. S. KLINE (POETRY IN TRANSLATION)

In that instant, the poem says, all his labour was poured out and the pact with the pitiless king was broken, and three times a crash was heard on the waters of Avernus. Eurydice speaks her only lines — what madness, she cries, has destroyed her, wretched, and him; see, the cruel Fates call her back, and sleep closes her swimming eyes; farewell; she is borne away wrapped in vast night, stretching out hands that are no longer his — and she fled from his eyes suddenly, like smoke mingled with thin air (4.494–500, paraphrased from Kline’s translation). Orpheus grasps at shadows in vain, and Charon, the ferryman of Orcus, will not let him cross the barrier of the marsh again: the second descent, the one that might repair the first, is the one thing the geography refuses everyone, even him. Twice robbed of his wife, he mourns seven months under a high cliff by deserted Strymon, singing in the cold caves, charming tigers and moving oaks; refuses all love and marriage thereafter; and is at last torn apart by Thracian women in the rites of Bacchus, his head and lyre thrown into the Hebrus — and the head, floating seaward, the lifeless tongue still murmuring, calls her name, and the banks give it back the whole length of the stream: Eurydice, ah, poor Eurydice (4.504–527, paraphrased). Ovid’s telling (Metamorphoses 10, paraphrased) differs in the motives it assigns — Orpheus turns, in Ovid, fearing she was not there and eager to see her — and in its sequel, the singer’s death and his shade’s descent to find her below at last, where he may look at her freely and walk with her, sometimes following, sometimes going before and glancing back in safety. The Ovidian ending is the tradition’s one act of mercy toward its singer, and it is granted only when he arrives the ordinary way, dead.

// THE CONDITION, READ PLAINLY //

The backward look is usually moralised — doubt, impatience, disobedience — and the texts support several of those readings at once: Virgil calls it a dementia, a sudden madness, and in the same breath one to be forgiven if the dead knew how to forgive. The plainer reading is structural. The condition converts the ascent into the exact discipline of the nekyia and the mysteries alike: to proceed without verification, trusting a presence that may not be checked. Orpheus, whose art had verified everything — stones moved, beasts gathered, hell itself stilled to listen — is undone by the one act his art cannot perform, believing without seeing. The story is the descent tradition’s treatise on faith, written as a failure.

What the descent taught, in this case, is stated by its outcome with unusual completeness. Orpheus wins from the underworld the one concession no other traveller asks: not knowledge, not a credential, not a destiny, but a person, back. The concession is real — the poem insists the song deserved it — and it is annulled not by the gods’ treachery but by the traveller’s own hand at the threshold of success. Among the descents of this article, his is the only one whose failure is located neither in the errand (Theseus’s theft) nor in the realm’s refusal (Pirithous’s earthquake) but in the final ten paces of the return. The anabasis, the Sibyl’s hard part, is where he falls; and the tradition has ever since used his name for the general truth that getting down is easy, getting back is the labour, and the labour is longest at the light. For the Orpheus who matters to doctrine — the supposed author of the tablets’ road-map, the patron of initiates who hoped to make the journey once, dead, and make it well — the reader is referred, as this section promised, to The Mysteries, Sections VII–X.

XII. Aeneas: Aeneid VI as the Roman Summa

Virgil’s sixth book is the descent toward which the whole tradition had been travelling, and the one through which the whole later West received it. Composed as part of the Aeneid in the years 29–19 BCE, Book VI takes every element assembled in Sections II–VII — the rivers, Charon, the unburied, Cerberus, the judges, the meadow, the sufferers, Tartarus, Elysium, Lethe — and walks a single traveller through them in a single night, in order, with a guide who explains the system as she goes. It is, in the strict sense, a summa: Homer’s nekyia, Hesiod’s Tartarus, Pindar’s moralised island, Plato’s judgement and rebirth, the mysteries’ language of initiation, and the Italian geography of Avernus are all present, arranged, and made to serve a purpose none of them had served before — the legitimation of Rome. This section follows the book’s own order, in paraphrase throughout except for the Sibyl’s warning already quoted in Section I (Fagles’s translation). The reader who wants the poem whole should go to the poem: Fagles (2006), Robert Fitzgerald (1983), and Frederick Ahl (2007) are the modern English versions this hub recommends, and the summary below is a map of their common text, not a substitute for it.

Cumae, the Sibyl, and the Golden Bough

Aeneas lands at Cumae, on the Bay of Naples, and climbs to Apollo’s temple, whose doors Daedalus carved, and to the cave of the Sibyl Deiphobe, priestess of Apollo and of Trivia — Hecate in her Italian seat (6.1–97, paraphrased). Possessed by the god, she prophesies the Italian war to come, and Aeneas asks his boon: to go down to his father Anchises in the underworld. Her answer is the warning of Section I — the descent is easy, the return is the labour, and few, of the race of gods and loved by Jupiter, have done it — followed by two requirements, and the requirements are the tradition’s rules stated as a checklist (6.98–155, paraphrased). First, the golden bough: deep in the dark grove grows a tree bearing a bough of gold, leaf and stem alike, sacred to Juno of the underworld — Proserpina — which no one may pluck and enter the earth’s hidden places without carrying as her gift; when one is torn away, a second grows in its place, and the bough yields of itself, easily, if fate calls the man — if not, no force can master it. Second, a burial: a comrade of Aeneas lies dead and unburied, polluting the fleet, and must receive his rites before any descent. Aeneas finds the body — Misenus, his trumpeter, drowned by Triton for challenging the god with his conch — builds the pyre, and, praying in the wood, is led by his mother Venus’s two doves to the tree, where the gold gleams through the ilex like mistletoe in winter; he breaks off the lingering bough and carries it to the Sibyl’s cave (6.156–263, paraphrased). The bough is the tradition’s most durable single object — passport, offering, and proof of election in one — and its later career, from Servius’s commentary to Frazer’s The Golden Bough, which took its title and its starting problem from this passage, belongs to the reception history of Section XVII.

The Descent and the Crossing

The entry is a real place, and Virgil insists on its reality (6.264–294, paraphrased). Before the cave at Avernus — the birdless lake in the volcanic country behind Cumae, whose mephitic air antiquity already explained by its name, a-ornos, “no birds” — sacrifices are made at night to Hecate, Earth, Proserpina, and the king of the Stygian pit: black cattle, oil on the entrails, whole bullocks burned, and at dawn the ground trembles, the woods shake, and the dogs of Hecate are heard coming through the gloom. “Keep away, you who are uninitiated, keep away” the Sibyl cries — the mysteries’ own proclamation, transplanted to the crater’s edge — and Aeneas goes in with drawn sword under her guidance. The vestibule of Orcus holds the personified evils: Griefs and avenging Cares, pale Diseases, sad Old Age, Fear, ill-counselling Hunger, shameful Want, Death and Toil, Death’s brother Sleep, the mind’s evil joys, warlike Death on the threshold opposite, the Furies’ iron chambers, and Discord with her snaky hair bound in bloody ribbons; in the courtyard a vast shadowy elm spreads the branches under which empty Dreams are said to cling, leaf by leaf, and beyond it the monsters — Centaurs, Scyllas, hundred-handed Briareus, the Hydra hissing, the Chimaera breathing fire, Gorgons, Harpies, and three-bodied Geryon — at which Aeneas, terrified, snatches at his sword, and the Sibyl instructs him: these are flitting phantoms, bodiless lives under a hollow semblance of form, and steel would strike at air (6.269–294, paraphrased). The lesson of Heracles and the Gorgon (Section IX) has become a guided tour’s first exhibit.

At the river — Virgil’s crossing-water is Acheron, welling into the Stygian marsh and Cocytus — Charon keeps his station (6.295–416, paraphrased): a squalid ferryman of terrible aspect, his chin covered with unkempt white, his eyes standing in flame, his soiled cloak knotted from his shoulders, poling his iron-dark boat and ferrying the throng that presses on the banks like autumn leaves, like birds driven inland by winter cold — mothers and men, the bodies of great-hearted heroes bereft of life, boys and unmarried girls, and young men laid on the pyre before their parents’ eyes. Aeneas, marvelling at the crowd that is turned away, is taught the system’s rule (Section IV): those Charon takes are the buried; the unburied host flits about the shore and may not cross for a hundred years of wandering. Among them is Palinurus, Aeneas’s own helmsman, lost overboard off Italy and murdered on an unknown shore, who begs to be taken across or buried where he lies; the Sibyl rebukes the unlawful wish and consoles him with the future: the people of that coast, driven by portents, will bury him, build his mound, and name the cape for him for ever — Cape Palinuro keeps the promise in the modern atlas. Charon, who once repented taking Theseus and Pirithous and Heracles across, challenges the armed living man, and is answered by the bough: the Sibyl draws it from her breast, and the ferryman’s anger subsides; he turns his boat, takes the huge hero aboard — the skiff groans under a weight it was not built for, and leaks — and lands them on the far shore of formless mud and blue-grey sedge, where the Sibyl throws Cerberus his honey-cake steeped in drowsy herbs, and the three throats gape and take it, and the great back relaxes along the ground and fills the cave (6.385–425, paraphrased).

The Regions of the Dead, and Dido

Beyond the gate Virgil arranges the dead in districts, and the arrangement is his systematisation’s core (6.426–547, paraphrased). First, at the very threshold, the wailing of infants’ souls, torn from the breast and taken by the black day before life’s sweetness; beside them, those condemned to death on a false charge; and Minos is here, in his judicial office, shaking the urn, summoning the silent council, and learning the lives and the charges of the dead. Next, the sorrowful fields’ first company: those who, guiltless, procured their own deaths by their own hand and threw away their souls, hating the light — who would now, in the upper air, gladly endure poverty and hardest toil, but fate forbids, and the hateful marsh binds them with its sad water and the ninefold Styx confines them. Then the Fields of Mourning, lugentes campi, where those whom hard love consumed with cruel wasting wander in myrtle woods on hidden paths: Phaedra, Procris, Eriphyle, Evadne, Pasiphae, Laodamia, Caeneus — and among them, fresh in her wound, Dido. The encounter (6.450–476, paraphrased) is the book’s most famous silence and a deliberate reversal of Odysseus and Ajax. Aeneas, weeping, swears he left her unwilling, by the gods’ commands, and that he did not believe his going would cause her death; he begs her to stay, to say whom she flees — his last sight of her in life, he tells her, this pain. She does not answer. She turns her eyes away, no more moved by his begun speech than if she were hard flint or Marpesian rock, and at last hurries into the shadowy grove where Sychaeus, her first husband, answers her sorrows and equals her love. Aeneas follows her a while with tears and pity — and goes on, as Odysseus went on from Ajax, but with the roles of silence and pleading exchanged: in the Roman summa, it is the traveller who is refused. Beyond lies the district of the war-famous, where the Trojan dead throng him weeping for joy and the Greek captains shrink from his arms and glittering weapons as they did in life, and where Deiphobus, Helen’s last husband, mutilated in face and hands, tells him the truth of Troy’s last night (6.477–547, paraphrased).

The Fork: Tartarus Described, Elysium Entered

The road forks, and the Sibyl expounds the left branch from outside its wall (6.548–627, paraphrased). To the left, under Pluto’s steep place, lies the way to Tartarus: a fortress with a triple wall, a torrent of fire — Phlegethon, with its rolling rocks — a gate of solid adamant that no force of men or gods can upheave, an iron tower, and Tisiphone, girt in her bloody robe, sleepless night and day, while from within come groans, savage lashes, the dragging of iron chains. Rhadamanthys of Cnossus holds that sternest court, hearing and punishing frauds and compelling confession of sins deferred, in life, to a vain late repentance. The catalogue of the punished runs from the Titans and the Aloidae and Salmoneus, who mimicked Jupiter’s thunder with his chariot and torches, through Tityos — whose body covers nine acres and whose liver the vulture renews by tearing — Ixion and Pirithous on their wheel and under their ever-falling rock, Tantalus at his feast that recedes, to the unnamed modern damned: those who hated their brothers, struck a parent, defrauded a client, brooded alone over newfound wealth, were taken in adultery, or broke their oaths and sold their country — the Sibyl breaks off: do not ask to learn their fates; not if she had a hundred tongues, a hundred mouths, and a voice of iron could she run through all the forms of crime and names of punishment. The right branch, by contrast, they take: the road to Elysium, entered by fixing the golden bough on Proserpina’s threshold as her due offering (6.628–636, paraphrased). Virgil’s Tartarus is Plato’s and Hesiod’s rebuilt as a Roman prison-court, and his decision that the travellers shall not enter it is the tradition’s own rule (Section VI) observed at its most systematic: the summa describes the pit in full, from the gate.

Elysium, Anchises, and the Parade of the Unborn

Elysium (6.637–702, paraphrased) is the bright exception domesticated: joyful places, green and pleasant, groves and lawns where a more generous air clothes the fields in purple light, and the inhabitants know their own sun and their own stars; some exercise on the grass and wrestle on the yellow sand, some dance and sing, and Orpheus himself is there in his long robe, striking the seven notes with thumb and with the plectrum of ivory — the singer of Section XI, filed at last among the blessed musicians. Here are the race of Teucer, the heroes born in better years, and all those who kept memory alive by deserving well: those who suffered wounds fighting for their country, priests who were pure in life, poets worthy of Apollo, those who bettered life by discovering the arts, and those who made others remember them by kindness — all with snow-white fillets round their brows. The Sibyl asks Musaeus, tallest in the throng, where Anchises dwells, and they are directed down into a green valley, past the stream of Lethe and the nations and peoples of the air, to where Anchises is reviewing the souls shut in the vale — his descendants, the souls that must return to the upper world (6.679–702, paraphrased).

The reunion repeats Homer’s physics exactly, and means the opposite (6.703–751, paraphrased). Anchises, who has been expecting him, weeps for joy; three times Aeneas tries to throw his arms around his father’s neck, and three times the phantom, grasped in vain, escapes his hands, like light winds, most like a winged dream — Anticleia’s lesson, relearned at the centre of the Roman world. Then Anchises teaches. His discourse (6.724–751, paraphrased) is the book’s philosophical core and a deliberate collage of the tradition’s late materials: a single spirit nourishes heaven and earth, sea and moon and sun, mind moving the mass; from it come the kinds of men and beasts and the life of birds; souls have a fiery vigour and a heavenly origin, clogged by their noxious bodies, whence their fear and desire, grief and joy; death does not purge the long-grown taint, so punishments are suffered below — some hung to the winds, some washed in the vast flood, some burned in fire — until, the wheel of time fulfilled, the stain is worn away and pure ethereal sense remains. Then, after a thousand years, the god calls them in a great company to the river Lethe, that they may drink forgetfulness and begin again to wish to return into bodies. Plato’s Phaedo punishments, the Republic’s millennial cycle, and the tablets’ Memory inverted into a blessed Forgetting (Section XIV): all of it is here, put into a dead father’s mouth as the constitution of the cosmos.

And then the parade (6.752–886, paraphrased). Anchises leads Aeneas and the Sibyl up a mound and points out the unborn Romans filing past in the vale of Lethe, naming them one by one: Silvius and the Alban kings; Romulus, who will found the city; the kings of Rome and the early heroes; and, turned to with the discourse’s most famous apostrophe, the line of Julius — Augustus Caesar, offspring of a god, who will found the golden age again in Latium, in the fields once ruled by Saturn. The catalogue runs through the conquerors of Greece and the destroyers of Carthage, pauses in grief at Marcellus, the heir who will die young, over whose shade Anchises scatters lilies with full hands in the book’s most mourned passage, and closes with the charge that defines the imperial philosophy the descent exists to deliver (6.851–853, paraphrased): others will beat out breathing bronze more softly, draw living features from marble, plead cases better, and chart the heavens; the Roman’s arts are these — to rule the peoples with his power, to impose the habit of peace, to spare the subjected, and to war down the proud. Aeneas is fired with love of the fame to come, asks after the Italian war, is warned and instructed, and is dismissed at last with the Sibyl through the gates of Sleep — there are two, the gate of horn for true shades and the gate of ivory for false dreams, and Anchises sends his son out, notoriously, by the ivory gate (6.893–899, paraphrased). Two millennia of commentary have worried at that exit — a false-dream gate for the truest vision in the poem — and this article records the puzzle without resolving it: Virgil ends the summa by questioning, in the tradition’s own imagery, the status of everything the summa has shown. Aeneas rejoins his fleet and sails for Caieta. The descent’s yield is not knowledge of death. It is Rome.

// KATABASIS BECOME IMPERIAL PHILOSOPHY //

Set the errands side by side and the Roman transformation is exact. Odysseus goes down for sailing directions and news of home; Heracles, for a credential; Orpheus, for a person. Aeneas goes down for a future — a parade of men not yet born, a doctrine of the soul that makes history cyclical and Rome its present turn, and a charge addressed not to him alone but to every Roman who will read the poem. Virgil has taken the oldest device in Greek story, the visit to the dead, and pointed it forward: the underworld becomes the one place where the unborn can be inspected, and katabasis becomes the empire’s philosophy of history, delivered by a father, in Elysium, beside Lethe. Nothing in the later Western tradition — not Dante’s guided tour, not the alchemists’ journeys of the soul (Section XVII) — escapes the shape Virgil gives it here.

XIII. The Tradition Laughing at Itself: Aristophanes’ Frogs

In January 405 BCE, with Athens in the last year of the Peloponnesian War, Aristophanes produced the Frogs at the Lenaea and took first prize. It is the tradition’s comic descent, and it is evidence of a peculiarly valuable kind: a parody is a census of what an audience can be relied on to recognise. Everything the Frogs jokes about — the road, the lake, the fare, the doorman, the blessed initiates — is thereby certified as common Athenian property in 405 BCE. The play is paraphrased throughout this section from the Frogs as a whole; its text survives entire, and the older English version of the Athenian Society (1912) and Jeffrey Henderson’s Loeb translation are the versions consulted. No line is quoted verbatim except the frogs’ refrain, which is a transliteration of the Greek.

The premise inverts Orpheus. Dionysus, god of the theatre, despairs of the state of tragedy — Euripides and Sophocles have both just died, and the living tragedians are, in his judgement, pygmies — and resolves to go down to Hades and bring Euripides back. Being a god of no particular courage, he equips himself as the one traveller whose route is proven: he puts on Heracles’ lion-skin and club over his own saffron robe and tragic buskins, takes his slave Xanthias and the baggage, and calls first at Heracles’ own house for directions (paraphrased). The scene is the descent tradition’s first travel consultation, and its comedy is the comedy of expertise: Heracles, roaring with laughter at the costume, suppresses it long enough to give a proper itinerary — the shortcuts and the long way round, the harbour, the bakeries, the resting-places, the great lake where the ferryman will take him across for two obols — and Dionysus, who has been asking eagerly after the fastest route (a rope and a stool, poison, a high tower: the jokes are suicide jokes, and Heracles bats each away), sets off down the respectable road. Every element of Sections III and IV is present, priced, and signposted. By 405 the geography of the dead had a tourist literature, and Aristophanes is it.

The crossing is the play’s title scene (paraphrased). Charon — an old man who will take no slave aboard as freight, so Xanthias must walk round the lake — receives Dionysus, collects the fare, and, in the ferryman’s one recorded act of labour relations, makes his passenger take an oar and row. The frogs of the marsh strike up their chorus — brekekekex koax koax, the refrain transliterated — Dionysus rows worse and complains louder, competes with the chorus in volume, wins by main shouting, and arrives blistered and furious on the far shore, where a monster of the Empusa kind gives him a properly heroic fright to recover from. Then the play’s second chorus enters, and the tone changes in a way scholarship has always marked: the initiates, the blessed dead of the Eleusinian mysteries, processing with Iacchus in the meadows of the underworld, singing of the sun that shines for them alone below, of the myrtle groves and the dancing — the bright exception of Section VII staged, in a comedy, as a torchlit procession (see The Mysteries, Section III). Aristophanes can laugh at everything in his underworld except this. The initiates’ chorus is beautiful, and meant to be.

At Pluto’s door the Heracles costume collects its debt (paraphrased). Aeacus, the judge of Section V demoted to porter, recognises — or thinks he recognises — the dog-thief who once stole Cerberus, and threatens the supposed Heracles with the underworld’s whole penal apparatus: the hounds of Cocytus, the hundred-headed Echidna, the Tartesian lamprey fastening on his lungs, Tithrasian Gorgons tearing his entrails — a comic catalogue that is also a fair inventory of Tartarus’s furniture as a 405 audience knew it. Dionysus and Xanthias trade costumes, and identities, through the door-scenes’ escalating beatings — each claiming, as the whip falls, to be the god, since a god feels no pain, a test both pass by flinching in unison — until Pluto and Persephone settle the matter and the play’s second half begins: the contest of the tragedians, Aeschylus against Euripides for the throne of tragedy beside Pluto’s chair, judged by Dionysus with scales, prologues, lyrics, and a final political question about Athens, and decided — against his own announced errand — for Aeschylus, whom he leads back up to save the city with good counsel. Pluto speeds them off with gifts and a rope for the living politicians he disapproves of. The descent that began as a fan’s errand ends as a civic one.

// EDMONDS ON THE FROGS //

Radcliffe Edmonds’s reading of the Frogs (in Myths of the Underworld Journey, 2004, paraphrased) takes the play as the tradition’s own act of boundary-drawing: the comedy sorts the dead — and, through them, the living audience — into the blessed initiates and the uninitiated crowd of murderers, oath-breakers, and bad poets who wallow in the mud and dung of the lake’s near shore, a sorting the parabasis then turns, without breaking stride, into political advice about which Athenians to trust. The joke and the eschatology are the same instrument. A descent story, on Edmonds’s showing, is always a way of drawing a line between groups of people; comedy simply draws it in the open.

What the Frogs proves, for this article’s purposes, is saturation. A century and more after Pindar, eight centuries’ worth of map-making still to come in Virgil, an Athenian comic poet could build a prize-winning evening out of nothing but the geography of the dead and the audience’s fluency in it: the fare, the lake, the doorman’s grudge, the initiates’ sunshine, the great sinners’ apparatus, the throne beside Pluto. The tradition laughing at itself is the tradition at its most confident — and the laughter is affectionate, because the map being mocked is the map the mockers expected, in earnest, to travel.

XIV. Philosophy Remakes the Map: Plato — Er and the Phaedo

Plato stands to the geography of the dead as Virgil does, but from the other direction: not a poet systematising the tradition into an epic, but a philosopher taking the tradition’s furniture apart and rebuilding it as an argument. Two of his constructions concern this article, and both are treated briefly here, as the brief prescribes — the Phaedo’s eschatology, already drawn on for the rivers and the judges (Sections III, V, VI), and the Myth of Er that closes the Republic. Both are introduced by Plato himself with the same disclaimer, which this article takes as its instruction for reading them: they are mythoi, stories told at the point where argument runs out, whose details are not to be insisted on and whose moral shape is the truth they carry.

The Phaedo: The Earth Hollowed, the Rivers Set Flowing

The Phaedo is set on the day of Socrates’ death, and its closing myth (107c–115a, paraphrased throughout) answers the dialogue’s own question — what happens to the soul — with a geography. The true earth, Socrates says, is not the surface we live on, which is a hollow full of sediment; we live like frogs round a pond, and the real surface, far above, is pure and bright, the home of the souls of the exceptionally holy. Within the earth, chasms and channels connect everything, and the greatest chasm, Tartarus, pierces the whole globe; into it and out of it all rivers surge on a vast oscillation, as if the earth breathed (Section III). The dead are led by their guardian spirit to the place of judgement and sorted: the middling cross Acheron in vessels to the Acherusian lake, dwell there, are purified, pay penalties, receive rewards, and are in time sent back to birth; the incurable — great sacrilege, many murders — are cast into Tartarus and never emerge; the curably wicked are cast in for a year and then vomited out by Cocytus and Pyriphlegethon to the lake’s edge, where they cry out to those they wronged and beg to be let out, and if they persuade them, their sufferings end, and if not, they are carried round again, until they do. It is the first fully moralised hydraulics in Western literature: the river-system of Section III has become a penal transport network, forgiveness by one’s victims is its parole board, and the whole machine exists to make a single ethical point with a map. Socrates’ own verdict on it (114d, paraphrased) is the philosopher’s charter for every eschatology since: no sensible man would insist these things are exactly as described, but that something like them is true of our souls and their dwellings is a fitting belief, and a risk worth taking — the myth is, in his word, a noble risk, and a charm to repeat to oneself.

Er: The Traveller Who Came Back on Purpose

The Myth of Er (Republic 10.614b–621d, paraphrased except where quoted) is Plato’s katabasis, and it is built, pointedly, as an anti-Odyssey. Socrates announces it as not the tale Odysseus told to Alcinous — the nekyia of Section VIII, named and set aside — but the tale of Er, son of Armenius, a Pamphylian soldier slain in battle, whose body was found undecayed on the tenth day and who revived on his funeral pyre on the twelfth and reported what he had seen. Er is the tradition’s one commissioned traveller: at the place of judgement the judges tell him he must be a messenger to mankind of what is there, and he is forbidden to drink the water of forgetfulness that the other souls must drink, so that the report survives. What he reports (paraphrased) is a meadow between two pairs of openings, in earth and in heaven, with judges seated between, sending the just up to the right with their sentences bound before them and the unjust down to the left with theirs behind; souls returning, clean and bright from heaven, dusty and travel-worn from below, embracing in the meadow and exchanging news like festival-goers; penalties and rewards tenfold, reckoned over thousand-year cycles; and then the machinery of rebirth — the spindle of Necessity turning on its axis, the Sirens singing on the circles, the Fates, Lachesis, Clotho, and Atropos, enthroned about it — and before Lachesis the lots and the patterns of lives laid out, far more numerous than the souls, of every animal and every human condition, tyrannies and obscurities, wealth and poverty, beauty and strength, lives of animals and of men.

The prophet’s proclamation over the lots, in Benjamin Jowett’s translation, is the philosophical remaking in a single sentence:

“Virtue is free, and as a man honours or dishonours her he will have more or less of her; the responsibility is with the chooser — God is justified.” — PLATO, REPUBLIC 10.617e, TRANS. BENJAMIN JOWETT

The souls choose, in the order of their lots, and the choices are the myth’s tragedy and comedy (paraphrased): the first chooser, a man come down from heaven who had lived virtuously by habit in a well-ordered city, without philosophy, snatches the greatest tyranny and discovers, too late, that it contains the eating of his own children, and beats his breast and blames fortune and the gods — everything but himself; Odysseus himself, the nekyia’s own traveller, cured by suffering of ambition, is found choosing last and contentedly, hunting for the life of a private man who minds his own business, which he finds lying disregarded in a corner and says he would have chosen even with the first lot; Ajax’s soul, remembering the judgement of the arms, chooses a lion’s life; Agamemnon’s, an eagle’s; Thersites becomes an ape. Then each soul receives the genius of its chosen life, drinks — all but Er — from the river of Unmindfulness in the plain of Lethe (some, unwisely, more than the measure), and at midnight, with thunder and earthquake, they are swept upward to birth like shooting stars. The geography of Sections II–VII is all here — meadow, judges, rivers, forgetting — and every piece of it has been re-purposed: the map no longer describes where the dead go. It describes how the living choose, and it sends its traveller back, as no Greek traveller was ever sent before, with a doctrine for export and instructions to remember it. Philosophy’s katabasis is a fact-finding mission, and its anabasis is publication.

// THE GOLD TABLETS, BY CROSS-REFERENCE //

Plato’s remaking had a popular contemporary, and the two are constantly confused. The gold tablets buried with initiates from the late fifth century BCE onward — edited by Fritz Graf and Sarah Iles Johnston in Ritual Texts for the Afterlife (2007; 2nd ed. 2013) — give the dead traveller a road-map and a script: avoid the first spring, drink from Memory’s pool, name yourself a child of Earth and starry Heaven. They are this hub’s best evidence that ordinary initiates carried the geography in their graves. Their texts, their findspots, and Edmonds’s argument that they mark their bearers as extraordinary rather than orthodox are treated in full in The Mysteries, Section IX, and are deliberately not re-treated here; this article cites them only as the ritual counterpart of Plato’s literary map.

XV. The Ritual Footing: Nekyomanteia, Hero Cult, and Necromancy

The stories had addresses. Greek practice knew places where the dead could actually be consulted, rites by which they could actually be raised, and tombs at which they were actually fed and questioned — and the modern study that organises the whole dossier is Daniel Ogden’s Greek and Roman Necromancy (Princeton, 2001), the first comprehensive survey of the subject, on which this section rests throughout. Ogden’s central distinctions govern what follows. Necromancy, in the Greek evidence, is divination by the dead: the ghost is consulted because the dead are held to know — the future, hidden things, the whereabouts of the lost — and its characteristic settings are the tomb, the battlefield, and the institutional oracle of the dead, the nekyomanteion. It is not, in the classical evidence, the lurid reanimation-trade of later Roman literature, though Ogden traces that development too. And its patron text, everywhere, is Odyssey 11: the pit, the blood, the black victim, and the sword are the rite’s charter, quoted by practitioners and parodied by Lucian alike.

The Four Nekyomanteia

Four oracles of the dead stand out in the sources, and Ogden’s treatment of them (paraphrased throughout) is a model of the archive’s discipline: two are cave sites, two are lakeside precincts, and the archaeology supports far less than the tourist literature claims.

ACHERON IN THESPROTIA

The most famous nekyomanteion, on the real river Acheron near its Acherusian lake in north-western Greece. Herodotus (5.92, paraphrased) already knows it as a working oracle: Periander of Corinth sends envoys there to question the ghost of his dead wife Melissa, who refuses to answer until her burial wrong is righted — a necromantic consultation reported as sober history. The Hellenistic complex excavated at Mesopotamos was identified by its excavator Sotirios Dakaris as the oracle; Ogden argues, on the building’s plan and finds, that it is better read as a fortified farmstead, and the identification remains contested — reported here as contested.

TAINARON

The southernmost cape of the Peloponnese, a cave by the sanctuary of Poseidon at the tip of the Mani, where the tradition set one of the entrances to Hades: Heracles was said to have dragged Cerberus up here, and the Spartan regent Pausanias, starved to death in sanctuary, was the object of a famous consultation — the Spartans summoned his ghost with psychagōgoi, ghost-evokers, to appease it (the episode is Thucydidean and Plutarchan, paraphrased). Cape, cave, sanctuary, and oracle form one complex: the gate of the dead at the land’s end.

HERACLEA PONTICA

The Greek colony on the Black Sea whose very name claims Heracles, and whose cave — the Acherusian headland’s cavern, described by Xenophon’s Anabasis (6.2, paraphrased) as the place where Heracles descended for the dog, its marks still shown — housed an oracle of the dead in the same tradition. Story, cult-place, and show-cave coincide: the descent’s souvenir, institutionalised.

AVERNUS IN CAMPANIA

The crater lake behind Cumae where Virgil sets Aeneas’s descent (Section XII). The literary tradition — Ephorus, and Strabo reporting him (5.4, paraphrased) — knew a lakeside oracle of the dead here, served by men who dwelt in underground passages and received consultants, and identified its people with Homer’s Cimmerians, who never see the sun. The tufa tunnel-complexes at Baiae were proposed in the last century as the oracle’s remains; the identification is, as with Acheron, argued rather than established, and is flagged as such.

Around the four great addresses stood the rite’s ordinary practice, and Ogden’s dossier for it is wide (paraphrased). Consultations happened at tombs, above all the tombs of those who had died violently or before their time, whose ghosts were held to linger near the body and to be both knowledgeable and aggrieved; on battlefields, where the untimely dead were thickest; and through professional psychagōgoi, evocators, whose techniques the sources describe with a consistency that suggests a real trade — the pit and the blood of the Homeric model, offerings of honey, milk, wine, and oil, the black victim, nocturnal timing, dream-incubation at the oracle, in which the consultant slept in the precinct and met the dead in sleep, and, in the later and more theatrical stratum, ventriloquism, concealed tubes, and boy mediums, all of which Ogden reports from the sources that allege them, with their credibility assessed case by case. The Greek Magical Papyri of Roman Egypt — this archive’s Egyptology division treats them at The Greek Magical Papyri — preserve the trade’s late working papers: spells to summon a corpse-spirit as an assistant, to question the dead in dreams, to restrain the restless dead from troubling the living. Between Herodotus’s Periander sending to Acheron and the papyri’s recipes lies a millennium of continuous practice, and its continuity is the ritual footing of every story in this article.

Hero Cult: The Dead Who Stayed Useful

The nekyomanteia’s civic counterpart is hero cult. The heroes — the powerful dead of the epic age and of local legend — received cult at their tombs (hērōa) in every Greek city: sacrifices of the chthonic type, poured into the ground or a pit, offered at night, the victim black and wholly burned, in pointed contrast to the shared, daylight, meat-dividing sacrifice of the Olympians. In return the hero protected his place: healing heroes at their shrines, oracular heroes who answered in dreams, battle-heroes whose presence was fetched to fights — Athens brought Theseus’s bones home from Scyros with full honours in the 470s BCE and built him a sanctuary, an episode of hero cult as state policy (Plutarch, Theseus and Cimon, paraphrased; see Theseus). Hero cult is the institutional proof that the Homeric picture of the dead — witless, strengthless, gone — was never the whole Greek belief. The ordinary dead went to the meadow. The exceptional dead, like the exceptional places, stayed near the surface, hungry, knowledgeable, and available for consultation and for help. The descent stories live exactly in the gap between those two beliefs: they are about the journeys that the gap made imaginable, between a world where the dead are nothing and a practice where some of the dead are nearly gods.

// THE EGYPTIAN MIRROR //

Greece was not the only archive culture to consult its dead in writing. Egypt’s letters to the dead — petitions and complaints inscribed on bowls and papyri and deposited in tombs from the Old Kingdom onward — and its great underworld geographies, the Amduat and the Book of Gates, which map the night journey of the sun through a divided and judged realm of the dead, are treated in this archive’s Egyptology division at The Netherworld Books and Book of the Dead Passages. The comparison is instructive and is left there: Egypt mapped the dead’s country for the dead to travel; Greece, characteristically, sent living tourists down to report on it.

XVI. What a Descent Taught

The descents can now be read as a set, and the set has a curriculum. Each traveller brings back a different kind of yield, and the tradition is remarkably consistent about which errands pay and which do not.

Three lessons recur across the whole set, and they are the closest thing the tradition has to a doctrine of the descent. First: the dead know. Every sanctioned journey is, at bottom, an information-gathering expedition — Tiresias’s prophecy, Anchises’s parade, Melissa’s complaint at Acheron, Er’s report — and the knowledge is always of the same kind: not secrets of the underworld for their own sake, but the shape of the traveller’s own road, seen whole. The dead are worth consulting because they stand where the road ends and can describe it. Second: the visit is licensed or it is fatal. Purification and initiation (Heracles), a patron god’s sanction and a guide (Aeneas), a plea that the sovereigns grant (Orpheus), a rite correctly performed at the boundary (Odysseus), a commission from the judges themselves (Er): every success carries a permit, and the one expedition without one ends in the Chair. Third: the return is the feat, and it is priced at the threshold. The Sibyl’s warning (Section I) is borne out by the casualty list, which is a list of failures of return: Pirithous never returns; Eurydice is lost in the last paces of returning; Aeneas exits under the sign of the false-dream gate; Odysseus flees the pit in fear of the Gorgon; even Heracles’ return imports his death in the form of a marriage arranged below. Visiting and escaping are different acts, and the tradition’s whole wisdom about its darkest geography is compressed into the difference.

A fourth lesson belongs to the audience rather than the travellers, and Edmonds’s analysis (Section I) names it: a descent story sorts people. Who may go down, who is met there, who is blessed and who is bound, who comes back and who is left — every telling draws the line between the initiated and the uninitiated, the licensed and the thieves, the choosers who choose well and the choosers who snatch, in a new place. Aristophanes draws it between the sunlit initiates and the sinners in the mud, and then turns the same line on the Athenian electorate. Plato draws it between those who have philosophy and those who have only habit. Virgil draws it between Rome and the rest of the world, with the unborn on one side of it. The geography of the dead is, in every age, a map of the living drawn in the dark — which is why every age redraws it.

XVII. Later Readings: The Soul’s Journey and the Alchemists

Later ages read the descents as the soul’s journey, and the reading was available early: Plato himself, in the Phaedo’s analogy between dying and initiation and in Er’s commissioned return, had already turned the traveller’s road into an itinerary of the self, and the Neoplatonic commentators completed the turn, allegorising Odysseus’s voyage, Heracles’ labours, and Orpheus’s song as the soul’s descent into body and its return to its source. The alchemists of the Alexandrian and later traditions were among the heirs of that reading: in the mytho-hermetic interpretation whose classic statement is Antoine-Joseph Pernety’s, the labours, voyages, and descents of the heroes are the Great Work told in fable — the matter’s dissolution in the dark vessel, its nigredo, its death and its return to light — and Orpheus, Heracles, Theseus, and Aeneas become, in that key, so many figures of the art and its artist. That reading is a large subject with its own evidence and its own rules, and it is given one paragraph here by design: its full treatment in this hub is The Alchemical Allegory: Myth as the Tradition’s Teaching Veil, to which the reader is referred for the Pernety method, its ancient warrants, and its limits. What belongs to this article is only the observation that closes its own argument: the descents could bear the alchemical reading because they were already, in their first tellings, stories about going down into a dark, governed, transformative place under strict conditions, and coming back — if one came back — changed and carrying something. The alchemists did not invent that shape. They inherited it, recognised it, and, in the tradition’s own manner, drew their line with it.
