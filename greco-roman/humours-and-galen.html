I. A Physics Shared by Body and World: What This Article Is

This article belongs to the Foundations cluster of the Alchemy wing, and it is — like its companion on the Hermetica — not, strictly speaking, an article about alchemy. It is an article about the physics alchemy inherited. Before a Greek, Arabic, or Latin alchemist could say what a furnace does to a metal, he needed a language for what a body is: what it is mixed from, why one mixture is healthy and another diseased, why one substance heats and another cools, and by how much. That language was built by physicians. Its elements are four qualities — the hot, the cold, the wet, and the dry — its bodily bearers are the four humours, and its grammar is mixture: Greek krasis, the word that gives us, through Latin temperamentum, the temperament of a patient, of a drug, and eventually of a metal.

The story has two great stations, six centuries apart. At the first, an anonymous treatise of the Hippocratic Corpus, On the Nature of Man (late fifth or early fourth century BCE), states the four humours — blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile — as the constituents of the human body, bound to the four qualities and to the turning seasons. At the second, Galen of Pergamum (129–c. 216 CE), physician to gladiators and to emperors, takes that treatise as canonical, comments on it line by line, and builds around it the most durable medical system of the pre-modern world: a classification of temperaments, a theory of disease as imbalance, and — the part this hub most needs — a pharmacology in which every simple drug and food is graded, in degrees from the first to the fourth, by its power to heat, cool, dry, or moisten. When the Arabic and Latin alchemists later speak of correcting a metal's temperament, of a substance too hot in the third degree or a body suffering a cold and wet dyskrasia, they are speaking Galen's quantified language, whether they know it or not.

THE QUALITY SQUARE

The frame everything in this article hangs on. Fire is hot and dry; Air is hot and wet; Water is cold and wet; Earth is cold and dry. Two pairs of opposites — hot/cold, wet/dry — generate exactly four combinations, and the four elements, the four humours, the four seasons, and the four temperaments are all read off the same square. See The Four Elements.

THE CANONICAL MAPPING

Blood answers to Air: hot and wet. Yellow bile answers to Fire: hot and dry. Black bile answers to Earth: cold and dry. Phlegm answers to Water: cold and wet. This mapping — completed by Galen and the tradition after him, as Sections VI–VII and X explain in detail — is used consistently throughout this hub, and throughout this article.

A note on method, because this subject, like the Hermetica, attracts confident invention. No direct quotation appears in this article unless it is verbatim in a named translation, and the translation is named at the quotation. The Hippocratic quotations are from W. H. S. Jones's Loeb translation of On the Nature of Man (Hippocrates, vol. IV, Loeb Classical Library, 1931), checked against the Greek text's standard chapter divisions. Everything else — Galen's arguments, the Hippocratic treatises' contents, scholars' positions — is paraphrase, and is labelled as paraphrase or attributed by name where a position is one scholar's rather than the field's. The scholarly frame follows Vivian Nutton's Ancient Medicine (2nd ed., 2013); the Galen scholarship follows R. J. Hankinson (ed.), The Cambridge Companion to Galen (2008), and P. N. Singer's translations in Galen: Selected Works (Oxford World's Classics, 1997) and, with Philip van der Eijk, Galen: Works on Human Nature, vol. I: Mixtures (Cambridge, 2018).

// WHERE THIS ARTICLE SITS //

The Foundations cluster supplies the theory the workshop texts assume: Aristotle on matter and the qualities for the physics, The Four Elements for the square itself, this article for the body's mixtures and the degrees of drugs, and the Technical Hermetica for the Hermetic medicine — iatromathematics, sympathies, and the healing art under Hermes' name — that grew on the same soil.

One warning before the detail. The four-humour system is usually met today as a diagram — four coloured boxes, four personality types, a curiosity. The diagram is the end of the story, not its beginning, and it flattens two things this article works to keep separate. First, the treatise that states the humours does not itself contain the whole diagram: On the Nature of Man gives the humours, their qualities, and their seasons, but the explicit binding of each humour to an element, and the full scheme of the ages of life, are completions due to Galen and the tradition after him (Sections VII and X state exactly which parts are whose). Second, the system's real historical power was never the diagram. It was the degrees: the claim that qualitative action could be measured on a scale, matched against a measured imbalance, and corrected by contraries of equal strength. That claim — medicine as applied, quantified mixture-theory — is what alchemy took over whole. Section XII is therefore the centre of this article, and it is written to be used, not merely read.

II. Before the Humours: Alcmaeon and the Equality of Powers

The humours were not the beginning. Behind On the Nature of Man stands a century of Greek thinking in which health had already been defined — not as the presence of a healthy substance, nor as the absence of a disease-thing, but as a relation among powers. The earliest surviving statement of that definition is also the most beautiful, and it comes from the Greek west, from Croton in southern Italy, a city famous for its physicians and its Pythagorean associations.

Alcmaeon of Croton (fl. c. 500 BCE)

Alcmaeon, son of Peirithous, wrote — probably in the first half of the fifth century BCE, possibly earlier — a book of which only fragments and reports survive. He is credited in the ancient testimony with identifying the brain, not the heart, as the seat of understanding, and with tracing channels (poroi) from the sense-organs to it. But the fragment that matters here is his definition of health, preserved by the doxographer Aëtius and conventionally numbered DK 24 B4. In the standard modern rendering (as quoted in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's Alcmaeon article): the equality — isonomia, the term for equal political rights in a well-ordered city — of the opposing powers that make up the body, “the wet, the dry, the hot, the cold, the sweet, the bitter etc.”, preserves health, whereas the monarchy of any one of them produces disease.

Three features of that sentence deserve to be dwelt on, because all three run through everything that follows. First, the powers are plural and open-ended: Alcmaeon's list ends with an “etc.” — hot and cold, wet and dry, but also bitter and sweet and, by implication, whatever other opposed powers the body contains. Nothing in the fragment fixes their number at four, and the later fourfold schemes are a narrowing of Alcmaeon's open field, not a quotation of it. Second, health is a constitution, described in the political vocabulary of the Greek city: equality of powers against the tyranny of one. Disease is not an invader but a coup — one power ruling alone. Third, and most consequentially, the account is dynamic: the body is a standing balance of opposed tendencies, continually re-struck, and the physician's art is the art of restoring a balance that nature is always in the process of losing. Every later definition in this article — the Hippocratic “duly proportioned”, Galen's eukrasia — is Alcmaeon's political metaphor with the politics worn smooth.

// WHAT ALCMAEON DOES NOT YET HAVE //

No humours: his powers are qualities and flavours, not fluids. No fixed tetrad: the list of opposites is illustrative, not closed. No elements: nothing is said about fire, air, water, and earth. What he contributes is the form of the theory — health as proportioned equality among opposed powers, disease as one power's excess — into which the humours, the elements, and the degrees will later be poured.

Alcmaeon also stands at the head of a second line that matters to this hub: the line of medical philosophy written as natural philosophy. His book addressed physiology, psychology, embryology, sleep, and death inside a single account of nature, and Aristotle thought him worth a treatise of refutation. The Hippocratic writers inherit that ambition — a medicine that is also a physics — and Galen completes it. When the alchemists, centuries later, write treatises in which the furnace and the body obey one set of principles, they are working in a genre Alcmaeon's generation invented.

III. Empedocles and the Four Roots

If Alcmaeon supplied the form, Empedocles of Acragas (c. 494– c. 434 BCE) supplied the number. His poem On Nature — fragments of which survive, quoted by Aristotle, Plutarch, and the later commentators — taught that all things are mixtures of four “roots” (rhizōmata): fire, air, water, and earth, eternal and unchanging in themselves, combined and separated in endless proportions by two cosmic powers, Love, which unites, and Strife, which divides. The full treatment of the four elements — their history before and after Empedocles, their Aristotelian transformation, and their alchemical career — belongs to the companion article, The Four Elements, and is not repeated here. What this article needs is only the medical consequence, and it is large.

Empedocles was remembered in antiquity as a physician as well as a philosopher and wonder-worker, and his Sicilian milieu produced a medical tradition of its own: Philistion of Locri, a generation or two later, is reported to have built the human body explicitly from the four elements and to have explained health as their proportion and disease as the excess of one — Alcmaeon's isonomia, now with Empedocles' four roots as the powers whose equality is at stake. Plato, who knew Philistion's Sicilian medicine, took a version of the scheme into the Timaeus, where the body is compounded of the four elements and its diseases are traced to their excess, deficiency, and displacement. The channel matters more than the detail: by the time the Hippocratic writers were at work, a fourfold physics of the body was already in circulation, with a Sicilian and a Platonic branch, and any new medical system had to position itself against it.

That positioning is exactly what On the Nature of Man does, and it does it by refusal as much as by adoption — a point the secondary literature, following Jacques Jouanna's study of the treatise, is emphatic about, and which Section VI states in full. The treatise's author will not say that man is fire, air, water, or earth. His constituents are not the cosmic roots but the body's own fluids. The fourfold pattern is retained; the Empedoclean content is declined. The binding of humour to element, one-to-one, which the later diagram treats as the system's foundation, is therefore a later synthesis — Galen's and his successors' — of two originally distinct fourfolds: Empedocles' roots of all things, and the Hippocratic humours of the body. The synthesis was so successful that its seams vanished. This article keeps them visible, because the hub's alchemical texts live precisely on the seam: it is only once humour and element are identified that a medicine of the body and a physics of metals become one science.

// A SECOND ROOT: BREATH AND BLOOD //

Empedocles also gave the tradition two durable images: respiration through the pores of the skin as well as the lungs, demonstrated by his famous clepsydra analogy, and blood — especially the blood around the heart — as the seat of thought. Neither survives into the Galenic system in its Empedoclean form, but both show how early the questions were set that the humoral theory would answer in its own terms: how the inside of the body trades with the outside, and where in the mixture the person resides.

IV. The Quality Physics Beneath: Aristotle's Four Qualities

Between Empedocles and Galen stands the thinker who turned the four roots into a physics of qualities, and without whom the degrees of Section XII are unintelligible: Aristotle (384–322 BCE). The full account — prime matter, form, the four causes, and the sublunary theory of mixture — is the subject of the companion article, Aristotle on Matter, Form, and the Qualities. Here, only the load-bearing pieces.

In On Generation and Corruption Aristotle argues, in paraphrase, that the tangible qualities reduce to two pairs of opposites: hot and cold, which he treats as active powers — they act on other things, combining and separating — and wet and dry, which he treats as passive — they are acted on, the wet being readily shaped and the dry retaining its shape. The four simple bodies are then defined by one quality from each pair: fire is hot and dry, air hot and wet, water cold and wet, earth cold and dry — the quality square of Section I, now with a physical rationale. Because each element shares a quality with its neighbours (fire and air share heat; air and water share wetness), the elements can transform into one another by the replacement of a single quality, and because qualities admit of more and less, mixtures are not all-or-nothing: a body is hotter or colder, wetter or drier, by degree. Every later use of the word “degree” in medicine and alchemy lives in that Aristotelian space — qualities as intensities on a continuum, not as labels pasted on things.

ACTIVE: HOT AND COLD

The pair that does things. Heat concocts, ripens, thins, and separates the unlike; cold condenses, thickens, and fixes. In the body, innate heat is the engine of digestion — food is literally “cooked” (Greek pepsis, concoction) into blood. In the furnace, the same pair does the same work on metals and minerals.

PASSIVE: WET AND DRY

The pair that is worked on. The wet yields, flows, and takes form; the dry resists, holds form, and crumbles rather than flows. A body's wet–dry balance is its texture: the softness of phlegm, the friability of black bile's earthy dregs, the fusibility of a metal, the dryness of a calcined powder.

Aristotle also supplies the second indispensable notion: mixture proper (mixis), as distinct from mere juxtaposition. In a true mixture, on his account, the ingredients do not survive intact side by side, nor is one simply destroyed: they blend into a single homogeneous body whose qualities are a new, intermediate disposition of theirs, recoverable in principle because the ingredients persist potentially. Galen's whole doctrine of krasis — and the alchemists' conviction that a mixture's qualities can be adjusted rather than merely averaged — presupposes this analysis. A temperament, for the tradition that follows, is not a recipe of parts but a state of a blended whole, and it can therefore be shifted, by regimen, by drugs, or by fire, along the continua of the qualities. Hold that sentence; Sections VIII, XII, and XV are its unfolding.

V. The Hippocratic Corpus: A Library, Not a Man

Everything in Sections VI–VIII is drawn from writings that travel under one man's name, and the first duty of an honest account is to say what that name does and does not mean. Hippocrates of Cos (c. 460–c. 370 BCE) was a real physician, famous in his lifetime — Plato names him, with respect, as a byword for the medical art, and Aristotle mentions him as an example of a man great not in stature but in his science. Of writings securely by his own hand, we possess none that can be proved. What we possess is the Hippocratic Corpus: some sixty treatises in the Ionic dialect of Greek — textbooks, lectures, case-books, surgical manuals, polemics, speeches, aphorism collections — composed by many authors, in many places, over more than a century, and gathered under Hippocrates' name in the Hellenistic age, most plausibly in connection with the great library at Alexandria in the third century BCE. Vivian Nutton's Ancient Medicine, the frame this article follows, treats the Corpus throughout as what it demonstrably is: not a book but a library, and a quarrelsome one.

The treatises disagree with each other, openly and on fundamentals. On Ancient Medicine attacks the importation of philosophical postulates — the hot, the cold, the wet, the dry, wielded as explanations — into medicine. On Regimen builds health on two constituents, fire and water. On the Sacred Disease argues that epilepsy is no more divine than any other disease and traces it to phlegm obstructing the vessels of the brain. The Epidemics record case after case with a clinician's reticence about theory altogether; the surgical treatises are content with hands and instruments; Airs, Waters, Places founds an environmental medicine on winds, waters, and the seasons of a city. The number of humours, where humours are counted at all, is not everywhere four. Any sentence that begins “Hippocrates believed…” and ends with a doctrine should therefore be read as shorthand — for “a Hippocratic author believed”, or, very often, for “Galen needed Hippocrates to have believed” (Section IX). The shorthand is unavoidable; this article uses it only where the text flags it.

// THE “HIPPOCRATIC QUESTION”, STATED PLAINLY //

Scholars have argued since antiquity over which, if any, Corpus treatises are by Hippocrates himself; Galen already had his canon of the genuine, the interpolated, and the spurious, and modern scholarship has not converged on a list. The position taken here, following Nutton and the standard reference literature, is the minimal one the evidence compels: the Corpus is multi-author; no treatise in it is proved to be Hippocrates' own; and the one treatise whose authorship antiquity lets us discuss with a name attached is the subject of the next section — where the name attached is not Hippocrates'.

VI. On the Nature of Man: The Four Humours Stated

On the Nature of Man (Greek: Peri physios anthrōpou) is a short treatise — fifteen chapters in the usual division, with the appended Regimen in Health sometimes counted as its continuation — and it carries more subsequent history per page than any other medical text of antiquity. It is here, and, in this explicit form, only here in the Corpus, that the four humours are stated as a complete system. Jacques Jouanna, the treatise's modern editor, calls it in his study of the four-humour theory's legacy the only treatise of the Corpus to which an author's name can be attached — and the name is Polybus.

The Authorship Dispute

The attribution rests on Aristotle. In the History of Animals (III.3), discussing the description of the blood-vessels, Aristotle cites an account he ascribes to Polybus — and the account he describes corresponds to the vessel-description embedded in On the Nature of Man. Later biographical tradition made Polybus Hippocrates' pupil and son-in-law, and on that basis the treatise entered the Corpus as the work of the master's own household: close enough to Hippocrates to carry his authority, distinct enough to explain why the master himself never quite says this elsewhere. Galen, for his part, held the treatise's core to be genuinely Hippocrates' own, and his commentary on it is one of the foundation documents of the whole later system (Section X). Modern scholarship has been less comfortable at every joint of the story. The treatise as transmitted is probably composite — the anatomical chapters sit awkwardly with the humoral ones, and Jones, in his Loeb introduction, already treated its parts as of different dates, placing the opening section somewhere between about 440 and 400 BCE. The Polybus attribution itself is widely accepted — it is the best ancient testimony we have — while the son-in-law relationship has recently been challenged as a Hellenistic biographical invention, of the kind ancient lives of famous men manufacture to reconcile competing attributions: on that challenge, Polybus the author is real, but his marriage into the house of Hippocrates, and with it any “Coan school” succession built on the marriage, is not evidence but embroidery. This article's usage follows the evidence as it stands: the treatise is attributed to Polybus on Aristotle's testimony; it is a Hippocratic treatise by collection, not a proved work of Hippocrates; and Galen's contrary conviction is reported as Galen's.

The Argument of the Treatise (chs. 1–6, in paraphrase)

The treatise opens as a polemic, and its first target is monism — the doctrine that man is one thing. Its author declines, in paraphrase, to discuss the nature of man beyond its bearing on medicine: those who say man is air, or fire, or water, or earth — some one element and nothing else — share, he argues, a single idea under different names, and their disagreement about the name betrays the idea. Physicians who say man is blood only, or bile only, or phlegm only, commit the same error in medical dress. Against both he sets a simple, devastating test: if man were a unity, he could never feel pain, for there would be nothing in him for the one thing to suffer from; and the cures for his diseases would be one, whereas in fact they are many. Plurality is therefore not a theory but a clinical observation. The body contains many constituents, which heat, cool, dry, and wet one another beyond nature and so engender diseases — many constituents, many forms of disease, many healings.

Chapter 4 then names the constituents, in the sentence on which two millennia of medicine are a commentary. Jones's Loeb translation, verbatim:

“The body of man has in itself blood, phlegm, yellow bile and black bile; these make up the nature of his body, and through these he feels pain or enjoys health.” — ON THE NATURE OF MAN, CH. 4, TRANS. W. H. S. JONES (LOEB, 1931)

The chapter continues, in paraphrase: he enjoys the most perfect health when these constituents are duly proportioned to one another in compounding, in power, and in bulk, and are perfectly mingled; pain is felt when one of them is in defect or in excess, or is isolated in the body and not compounded with the others — for the place it has left is diseased by the emptying, and the place where it floods is diseased by the excess. Alcmaeon's isonomia (Section II) has acquired fluids, proportions, and a pathology of separation: disease is not only too much or too little, but a humour standing apart, unmixed — a formulation Galen will never improve on, only annotate. Chapter 5 adds the empirical proofs, in paraphrase: the four are visibly distinct in colour and to the touch — not equally warm, cold, dry, or moist — and each can be drawn off separately, by a drug that purges phlegm, by a drug that purges bile, by the black bile a purge brings away, and by the blood that flows from a wound, on any day of the year. The humours are not postulates. They are what comes out of the patient.

// WHAT CHAPTER 4 DOES NOT SAY //

It does not name the elements. Fire, air, water, and earth are the treatise's opponents' candidates in chapter 1, not its own constituents in chapter 4; the humours are bound, in the treatise itself, to the qualities and the seasons, not one-to-one to the cosmic roots. Jouanna's study is the standard modern statement of this point, and Galen's commentary is where the element-binding is made explicit (Section X). The canonical mapping of Section I is therefore historically layered, and this article dates each layer rather than projecting the finished diagram back into 400 BCE.

VII. Seasons, Ages, and the Turning Year

Chapter 7 of On the Nature of Man is the treatise's most original movement, and the one that turns a list of fluids into a medicine. Its claim, in paraphrase: the four constituents are always all present in the body, but each waxes and wanes with the year, increasing in the season akin to its own nature and decreasing in the season opposed to it — as the year itself participates in all the qualities, hot, cold, dry, and moist, none of which could persist for a moment without the others. The body is a small year. Its weather changes on schedule, and the physician who knows the schedule can see a disease coming with the season that feeds its humour.

The Seasons in the Treatise (ch. 7, in paraphrase)

Phlegm increases in winter, the author argues, because phlegm is the coldest constituent of the body — you may test it by touch against bile and blood — and winter is its kin: in winter men's sputum and nasal discharge are fullest of phlegm, swellings turn white, and diseases run phlegmatic. In spring, blood increases: the cold relaxes, the rains come, and the season is moist and warm, most akin to blood's nature — spring and summer bring dysenteries and nosebleeds, and men are then hottest and red. In summer, bile rises and holds its strength into autumn: men vomit bile spontaneously, purges bring away the most bilious matter, and fevers and complexions declare it, while phlegm, opposed by the dry warmth, is at its weakest. In autumn, black bile is greatest and strongest, and blood is at its least, autumn being dry and already chilling, contrary to blood's nature; then, as winter returns, the chilled bile shrinks and phlegm grows again with the rains and the long nights. The proof offered is a purge experiment, in paraphrase: give the same man the same drug four times in the year, and he will vomit the most phlegmatic matter in winter, the moistest in spring, the most bilious in summer, and the blackest in autumn. The qualities assigned are the canonical ones, and they are the treatise's own: blood hot and wet, yellow bile hot and dry, black bile cold and dry, phlegm cold and wet.

The temperament column (‡) is the latest layer of all, and Section X returns to it: the four character-names are the medieval and early-modern crystallisation of hints in Galen, not a table Galen himself ever drew in this form.

What the treatise itself builds on the seasons is practical, and it is the germ of all later regimen literature. If each humour waxes in its season, then diet and way of life must be counter-seasonal: the regimen must be adjusted through the year so as to oppose the rising humour before it rules — the doctrine the appended Regimen in Health works out as a seasonal calendar of foods, exercise, baths, and evacuations. The physician of this tradition does not wait for the coup Alcmaeon feared. He governs against it, quarterly, in advance. In that preventive, calendar-bound, quality-counting practice — medicine as the management of a mixture through time — both Galenic dietetics and, at one remove, the alchemist's timed regimen of the furnace (gentle heat in its spring, fierce heat in its summer) have their common ancestor.

VIII. Krasis: Mixture as the Body's Individual Nature

The Hippocratic treatise speaks of the constituents being “duly proportioned” and “perfectly mingled”. The word the tradition will use for that state — and for the individual constitution that results — is krasis: literally a mixing, as of wine with water in the mixing-bowl (kratēr) from which the word derives. Latin renders it complexio and temperamentum — a due tempering — and the double translation survives in our own double vocabulary: a person's complexion was originally their mixture, not their skin, and their temperament was the mixture itself, not the moods it was later blamed for. Krasis is the hinge concept of this article, because it operates at every scale the system touches: a humour has a krasis of qualities, a body has a krasis of humours, an organ has its own krasis within the body's, a drug has a krasis that will act on all of them — and, in the alchemical transfer of Section XV, so does a metal.

EUKRASIA — THE GOOD MIXTURE

Health as a state, not a substance: the constituents proportioned in power and bulk and perfectly mingled (On the Nature of Man, ch. 4). Crucially, eukrasia is individual: each body has its own due proportion, set by its nature, age, sex, place, and season — which is why Galen can speak of a latitude of health rather than a single point (Section XI).

DYSKRASIA — THE BAD MIXTURE

Disease as disproportion: a constituent in defect or excess, or separated and unmixed. Dyskrasia names both the humoral imbalance and — in Galen's wider usage — any departure of a part's quality-mixture from its due state, even where no humour is at fault: a part can simply be too hot, too cold, too wet, or too dry. That wider usage is what makes the degree pharmacology of Section XII a complete therapeutics.

Two Hippocratic consequences follow immediately, and both are stated in the treatise's ninth chapter, the locus of the whole tradition of cure by contraries. In paraphrase of its argument: diseases arising from repletion are cured by evacuation, and those from evacuation by repletion; diseases from exercise by rest, and from idleness by exercise; and the physician must set himself against the established character of the disease, the constitution, the season, and the age — relaxing what is tense and tensing what is relaxed — for in this way the diseased part will best come to rest, “and this, in my opinion, constitutes treatment”. The Jones translation's shortest sentence in the chapter is the one the next two thousand years quoted: diseases due to repletion, it says verbatim, “are cured by evacuation” — and its pair, by repletion. Contraria contrariis — opposites are cured by opposites — is the Latin tag the Middle Ages put on this chapter, and it is a fair tag. But note what the chapter adds that the tag drops: the contrary must be chosen against four variables at once — disease, constitution, season, age. Cure is contrariety calibrated to a mixture. The entire Galenic development of this insight consists in giving that calibration a scale.

Regimen — diaita, a word covering food, drink, exercise, bathing, sleep, and the whole ordered conduct of life — is the calibration applied daily, and in the Hippocratic world it is the senior partner of drug therapy, not its handmaid. The reasoning is internal to the theory: food is the body's daily intake of qualities, concocted into blood and so into the humours themselves; a food's krasis therefore feeds the mixture continuously and gently, where a drug strikes it occasionally and hard. Galen will formalise exactly this relation — foods alter the body little and gradually, drugs alter it much and at once, and the same substance can cross the line by dose and preparation — in On the Properties of Foodstuffs and the opening books of the Simples (Section XII). The Hippocratic physician is already, in embryo, what the Galenic physician will be in full: a manager of mixtures, whose pharmacy begins in the kitchen and the gymnasium and only ends at the purge and the lancet.

// BLOODLETTING, IN ITS THEORETICAL PLACE //

Venesection, the era's most famous intervention, is a straightforward consequence of chapter 4: where blood — the hot and wet humour, and the only one that can be drawn off directly and in bulk — is in excess, or where the mixture as a whole is plethoric, evacuation by the vein restores proportion at a stroke. Its Hippocratic uses are specific and argued case by case; its Galenic systematisation, with indications, quantities, and the choice of vein reasoned from the mixture's state, is a literature of its own. The practice outlived the theory by a century and a half, which is a fact about institutions, not about the theory's coherence.

IX. Galen of Pergamum: The Systematiser

Six centuries after Polybus, the humours found the reader who would make them immortal — and who would remake them in the process. Galen was born in September 129 CE at Pergamum in Asia Minor, a city of temples, libraries, and the great healing sanctuary of Asclepius, to a wealthy architect father who, on Galen's own telling, was directed by dreams to turn his son from philosophy to medicine. The education was the best the Greek world offered: philosophy under teachers of the Stoic, Platonist, Aristotelian, and Epicurean schools — whose contradictions, Galen says, nearly drove him to a sceptical despair from which the demonstrative certainty of geometry rescued him — then medicine at Pergamum, Smyrna, Corinth, and Alexandria, under teachers of the rival medical sects (Section XIV). At twenty-eight he was surgeon to the gladiators of Pergamum, an appointment that gave him, in his own phrase's implication, a window into the body no peacetime practice could match: wounds as anatomy lessons, and a record of recoveries he never tired of citing. In 162 he came to Rome, quarrelled with its medical establishment, dazzled it with public anatomical demonstration and impossible prognoses, withdrew, returned at imperial summons, and became physician to Marcus Aurelius and his house. He died, on the now-standard dating, around 216 — the older date of c. 199, drawn from a garbled biographical tradition, is still met in reference works and should be treated as superseded.

The scale of the literary remains is the first fact of Galenism. His surviving works constitute, in Hankinson's summary in the Cambridge Companion, roughly a tenth of all Greek literature surviving from before 350 CE — more than Plato and Aristotle together — and they are the residue of an output perhaps twice as large, much of it lost, some in a single fire that destroyed his library and drug stores near the Temple of Peace in 192. He wrote his own bibliography twice over — On My Own Books and The Order of My Own Books — partly to fix the canon of the genuine against the forgeries already circulating under his name: the Hippocratic problem (Section V), repeating itself around a living author. Anatomy, physiology, pulse-lore, diagnosis, prognosis, pharmacology, therapeutics, logic, ethics, psychology, and commentary — there is no department of ancient medicine he did not occupy, and few in which his occupation did not become the territory's permanent map.

Constantine the African at Monte Cassino and Gerard of Cremona at Toledo carry the system into Latin; the universities make it a curriculum. The alchemists receive medicine's physics with it (Section XV).

Galen's self-presentation — and it is a presentation, crafted across hundreds of pages of autobiography — is that of Hippocrates' heir and completer: the physician-philosopher who recovers the master's true doctrine from the sects that fragmented it, proves it by anatomy and logic, and extends it into a complete science. Modern scholarship, from Nutton's Ancient Medicine to the Cambridge Companion, reads the claim with a double vision that this article adopts. The Hippocrates Galen expounds is substantially Galen's construction: selected from a discordant Corpus, harmonised with Plato and Aristotle, and silent where the texts resist. And the construction was, as constructions go, magnificent — coherent where the Corpus is miscellaneous, quantitative where it is aphoristic, and teachable where it is gnomic. Galenism, the system the next fifteen centuries practised, is not Hippocratic medicine preserved. It is Hippocratic medicine completed by being rewritten, and the humours are its clearest case: stated by Polybus, canonised by Galen, and systematised — elements, ages, temperaments, degrees — by Galen and the tradition that learned to read Polybus through him.

X. Canonising the Humours: The Completed Square

Galen's canonisation of the humours was performed in three texts, and the hub's reader should know their names, because the later tradition cites them as one voice. On the Elements According to Hippocrates argues the physical foundation: the body, like everything below the moon, is compounded of the four elements, known through their qualities. The Commentary on Hippocrates' On the Nature of Man performs the canonisation proper, expounding Polybus's treatise chapter by chapter as the master's own doctrine and drawing out of it the correspondences its text implies but does not tabulate. And On the Doctrines of Hippocrates and Plato — his vast proof that the two authorities agree — extends the correspondences to the soul itself, arguing that the character of a person's thought and feeling follows the mixtures of the body: sharpness and intelligence from yellow bile, perseverance and steadiness from the melancholic humour, simplicity from blood, in the commentary's much-quoted summary of the doctrine.

The completed square, as the tradition received it from these texts, binds the four series together at every point. Blood, hot and wet, answers to air and to spring and to childhood: it is formed, in Galen's physiology, in the liver from the concocted nutriment brought by the veins, and it is the body's proper nourishment, the humour the others are measured against. Yellow bile, hot and dry, answers to fire, summer, and youth: sharp, bitter, and fiery, separated in the gall-bladder, useful in small measure for cleansing and stimulating the gut, inflammatory in excess — the humour of fevers and of choler. Black bile, cold and dry, answers to earth, autumn, and maturity: the heavy, sediment-like residue of the blood, drawn off, in the standard account, by the spleen; necessary in due measure, and in excess the most feared of the four — the humour of quartan fevers, of hard swellings, and of the settled, fearful, delusional state that keeps its name to this day, melancholia. Phlegm, cold and wet, answers to water, winter, and old age: the raw, half-concocted residue of nutriment, associated with the brain and the lungs, the humour of catarrhs, dropsies, and lethargies — useful, Galen allows, for moistening the joints and the gut, burdensome whenever concoction fails, as in the cold stomachs of the old it chronically does.

// THE FOUR TEMPERAMENTS: A DATING WARNING //

The familiar quartet — sanguine, choleric, melancholic, phlegmatic — as a typology of characters, four kinds of person each ruled by a humour, is not a table in Galen. Galen supplies its materials: the humour–soul correspondences above, the mixture-typology of Section XI, and a rich clinical vocabulary of predominance. But the tidy four-personality scheme is a later, chiefly medieval, crystallisation — elaborated in the Arabic and Latin tradition and fixed in the form the modern world inherits by twelfth-century writers such as Honorius of Autun. Galen's own typology of persons runs not by humour but by mixture, and it has nine members, not four. The next section gives it, because it is the typology the degree system actually serves.

One further Galenic doctrine completes the physiology, and the alchemists will need it as much as the physicians: the natural faculties. In On the Natural Faculties Galen argues, in paraphrase, that every part of the body exercises powers that can be watched at work: an attractive faculty, drawing to itself what is proper to it; a retentive faculty, holding it while it is worked on; an alterative faculty, concocting and transforming it — this is the faculty by which food becomes blood, and blood becomes flesh; and an expulsive faculty, casting out the residue. Nutrition, growth, generation, and drug action are all parsed in this grammar: a purge works because a part's expulsive faculty is provoked, a poison kills because alteration is overwhelmed, a food nourishes because alteration succeeds. When Zosimos and his successors describe metals in the furnace attracting, retaining, concocting, and expelling — bodies digesting spirits as stomachs digest food — they are borrowing, consciously or not, Galen's faculty language. The loan is one of the load-bearing walls between the two wings of this hub, and Section XV returns to it.

XI. The Nine Mixtures: Galen's On Temperaments

Galen's treatise Mixtures (Peri kraseōn, Latin De temperamentis) — available in Singer and van der Eijk's Cambridge translation as the centrepiece of Galen: Works on Human Nature — is, as its modern editors put it, a work of natural philosophy as much as of medicine, and it is the theoretical heart of the whole system. Its question is deceptively simple: given that every living body is a mixture of hot, cold, wet, and dry, how many kinds of mixture are there, and how does the physician know which one is in front of him? Galen's answer is a small masterpiece of combinatorics disciplined by the clinic.

One Good Mixture, Eight Departures

In paraphrase of the treatise's first book: the mixtures (kraseis) are nine in all. One is the well-mixed state, eukrasia, in which neither member of either opposite pair predominates. The remaining eight are departures (dyskraseis), and they divide by the logic of the square itself. Four are simple: one quality predominates while the other pair stands balanced — the hot mixture (hot predominating, wet and dry in equilibrium), the cold, the wet, and the dry. Four are compound: one member of each pair predominates together — hot-and-wet, hot-and-dry, cold-and-wet, cold-and-dry, the four corners of the quality square, which are also, the reader will note, the quality-signatures of the four humours and the four elements. Nine mixtures: one centre, four edges, four corners. Every body — and every part of a body, for Galen insists the analysis repeats at the level of each homogeneous part, bone, flesh, nerve, and the rest — can be located on that map, and its location is its temperament.

MixtureKindQualities predominatingSquare position
Eukrasia The good mixture None — both pairs balanced Centre
Hot Simple dyskrasia Hot (wet/dry balanced) Edge
Cold Simple dyskrasia Cold (wet/dry balanced) Edge
Wet Simple dyskrasia Wet (hot/cold balanced) Edge
Dry Simple dyskrasia Dry (hot/cold balanced) Edge

Hot and dry Compound dyskrasia Hot + dry Corner — yellow bile / Fire
Cold and wet Compound dyskrasia Cold + wet Corner — phlegm / Water
Cold and dry Compound dyskrasia Cold + dry Corner — black bile / Earth

Three refinements keep the scheme from the rigidity its later reputation suggests, and all three are Galen's own, emphasised by his modern editors. First, the eukratic centre is not a mathematical point but a latitude: health occupies a band, wider in some constitutions and narrower in others, and within the band a body may be hotter or wetter than its neighbour and be no less healthy — each nature has its own due mixture, relative to its species, age, sex, and habits. Second, mixture is known chiefly by touch, and Mixtures is in large part a training manual for the physician's hand: the standard of comparison is the well-mixed skin — Galen locates the most eukratic part of the human body in the skin of the palm and the fingertips — against which the hand, itself first calibrated, judges hotter and colder, softer and harder, in the patient. Third, mixture is known by function: the well-mixed part performs its activity best, and excellence of performance — in digestion, in sensation, in the whole person's vigour and even temper of soul — is the outward sign of the inward balance. The “well-tempered” person of the treatise is thus not a personality type but a standard of functioning, physical and psychological at once.

// KRASIS BEYOND THE BODY //

The treatise's analysis is deliberately general: mixtures belong to animals, plants, and indeed to all sublunary bodies, and Galen argues the scheme from the physics of the qualities, not from the clinic alone. That generality is the licence the later tradition uses. If a herb, a mineral, and a man are all kraseis of the same four qualities, then one science of mixtures can grade them all — which is precisely the programme of the Simples, and, after it, of alchemy.

XII. The Pharmacology of Simples: Qualities Into Numbers

We come to the doctrine for which this Foundations cluster most needs Galen, and on which the Arabic and Latin medical-alchemical tradition most completely depends: the grading of simples. A simple (Greek haploon pharmakon) is a medicinal substance taken singly — one plant, one earth, one mineral, one animal part — as distinct from a compound, a drug made of several simples by art. Galen's treatise on them, On the Mixtures and Powers of Simple Drugs (conventionally On Simple Drugs; Latin De simplicium medicamentorum temperamentis ac facultatibus), runs to eleven books: the first five lay down the theory of how drugs act and how their powers are to be tested, and the remaining six catalogue the simples in order — plants in Books VI–VIII, earths, stones, and metals in Book IX, animal substances in Books X–XI — assigning to each its mixture and its powers. Sabine Vogt's chapter on Galen's pharmacology in the Cambridge Companion is the modern account this section follows.

First Principle: Qualities Are Judged Relative to Man

A simple is called hot, in Galen's scheme, not because heat is a label in its essence but because it heats us: the powers of drugs are defined by their action on the human body, measured against the well-mixed human standard of Section XI. A substance that produces no perceptible change in a well-tempered body is temperate with respect to that quality. One that changes the body is graded by how much it changes it. This relativity is not a defect in the system; it is the system. Medicine does not need to know what a drug is in the eyes of God. It needs to know what the drug will do to this mixture — and a scale anchored on the human eukratic standard is exactly the instrument required. (The alchemical tradition will quietly re-anchor the same scale on other standards — the temperament of gold, of lead, of the perfect body — without changing its arithmetic.)

The Four Degrees

The change a simple produces admits of more and less (Section IV), and Galen divides that continuum, for each of the four qualities separately, into four degrees (taxeis). In paraphrase of the scheme as Galen states it in the theoretical books of the Simples and as the tradition uniformly received it:

GradeAction on the bodyStanding
Temperate No perceptible heating, cooling, drying, or moistening of a well-mixed body The zero of the scale; preserves the mixture as it is
First degree Acts, but imperceptibly or barely perceptibly: the body is warmed (or cooled, dried, moistened) so slightly that the effect is inferred rather than felt, and no harm can follow Gentle correction; the grade of most foods and of daily regimen
Second degree Acts manifestly: the change is plain to sense and to function, but it does not damage the part or disturb its activity Clear medicinal action, safely borne; the working grade of ordinary simples
Third degree Acts strongly: the change is violent enough that it can harm — it strains or injures the part if misjudged — though it does not of itself destroy it Powerful drugs; to be matched carefully against powerful dyskrasias
Fourth degree Acts destructively: it corrupts and destroys the substance of the part — the grade of caustics, corrosives, and poisons in that quality Outside ordinary therapy in that quality; the scale's warning edge

Every simple therefore carries, in principle, a two-coordinate address on the quality square together with a magnitude: it is, say, hot in the second degree and dry in the first, or cold in the third and wet in the second — one grade for the hot/cold pair (a substance cannot be both hot and cold in the same respect) and one for the wet/dry pair. Pepper, to take the example Galen himself returns to, is praised in the Simples for a powerful conjunction of warming and drying — the profile of a drug for cold and wet dyskrasias, phlegmatic obstructions above all. Rose, by contrast, stands in the tradition as mildly cooling and astringent — gently cold and dry — a first-degree correction for hot fluxes. The profiles, not the plant names, are what the system transmits: a physician who knows that a dyskrasia is cold in the second degree and wet in the second can prescribe from any materia medica whose simples are graded — Greek, Arabic, Indian, or local — because the prescription is written in qualities and degrees, and only the filling of the formulary is regional. That portability is the degree system's world-historical trick, and Section XV shows alchemy performing the same trick with metals.

// WORKED EXAMPLES, AS THE TRADITION TRANSMITS THEM //

Individual gradings were disputed between authorities — Galen himself corrects predecessors, and the Arabic and Latin lists do not everywhere agree — so the following are given as the early-modern Galenic herbals, summarising the received lists, grade them (the examples are those of Culpeper's Key to Galen's Method of Physic, the English tradition's standard exposition): marigold hot in the first degree; saffron hot in the second; ginger hot in the third. Cucumber and barley cold in the first degree; plantain cold in the second; lettuce cold in the third; the poppy — opium — cold in the fourth, stupefying the senses, the textbook inhabitant of the scale's destructive edge. The pattern to carry away is the scale's shape: from food-like gentleness, through manifest and then hazardous strength, to destruction.

How a Grading Is Earned: Galen's Testing Rules

A degree, in Galen's hands, is not an intuition; the opening books of the Simples lay down conditions for establishing a drug's powers that Vogt and the Companion's methodology chapters rightly present as a research programme. In paraphrase, the tester must use the substance pure and unmixed, free of accidental qualities from storage or admixture; must try it on a body that is itself well-mixed, so that the change observed is the drug's and not the disease's; must try it in a simple dyskrasia, not a compound one, so that heating is not confounded with drying; must attend to the whole course of the action, since some substances heat on first contact and cool in their aftermath, or the reverse; and must distinguish what a drug does by its elementary qualities from what it does incidentally — by its texture, for instance, a finely divided substance penetrating where a coarse one cannot. Taste is admitted as evidence, under rules: the bitter, the acrid, the sour, the sweet, and the rest are treated as signs of a substance's mixture, to be confirmed by action, never substituted for it. Galen is under no illusion that the resulting gradings are exact in the modern sense — there was, as Vogt notes, no instrument to measure even temperature — and the tradition's lists disagree at the margins, as the practical note above concedes. What the rules secure is not precision but discipline: a grading is a claim about observed action on a standard body, open to correction by the same method that produced it.

Foods, Drugs, Poisons: One Continuum

The Simples draws its famous boundary inside the continuum, not at its edge. In paraphrase of Galen's definitions: a food is what the body masters — it is altered by the body, concocted, and assimilated, and it changes the body's mixture little and gradually. A drug (pharmakon) is what masters the body — it is not assimilated but alters the mixture, beneficially if its contrariety matches the dyskrasia. A poison is a drug whose alteration is destruction. The three are kinds of action, not kinds of stuff: lettuce, which cools, sits on the food–drug border and is treated in both the food treatise and the drug lists; wine heats, nourishes, and intoxicates at three points of one career; and dose and preparation move a given simple along the scale. Dietetics and pharmacology are therefore one science at two intensities — which is why the Arabic and Latin traditions could write regimens of health and books of simples in the same vocabulary of qualities and degrees, and why a spagyric or alchemical pharmacy, grading its preparations by their heat and their subtlety, is Galen's heir even when it denounces him.

The Exception That Opens a Door: Action by Whole Substance

Galen himself marks the limit of the quality scheme, and the limit matters to this hub out of proportion to its size in his text. Some substances, he holds, do not act by their elementary qualities at all, but by their whole substance (kath' holēn tēn ousian) — by what the Latin tradition will call their specific form. The standard examples are the purgatives that attract one humour specifically — a drug that draws yellow bile does not heat or cool its way to that selectivity — together with many poisons and their antidotes, and the magnet's attraction of iron, which Galen cites as nature's own proof that such unanalysable attractions exist. John Wilkins's study of the whole-substance doctrine in the Simples shows Galen placing foods and poisons, no less than purgatives, under this heading at the theory's deepest level: assimilation itself, the body's recognition of the like, is a whole-substance event. For the history this hub traces, the doctrine is the sanctioned crack in the qualitative edifice through which the occult properties of the later tradition — sympathies, signatures, astral virtues, the specific arcana of Paracelsian pharmacy — will all legitimately pass, each claiming Galen's own warrant that not everything a substance does can be read off its position on the quality square.

XIII. The Therapeutic Method: Cure by Opposites, Measured

The degree system is a pharmacy; the Therapeutic Method (Greek Methodos therapeutikē, Latin Methodus medendi — fourteen books, translated by R. J. Hankinson as Galen: On the Therapeutic Method, Oxford, 1991) is the clinical reasoning that wields it. Philip van der Eijk's chapter on therapeutics in the Cambridge Companion — which observes that Galen's therapeutics, for all its influence, long lacked a comprehensive modern study — presents its core as a logic of indication (endeixis): the nature of the diseased state itself indicates its remedy, and the physician's art is to read the indication exactly. A dyskrasia indicates its contrary; the amount of the dyskrasia indicates the amount of the contrariety; and the strength of the patient, the part affected, the season, and the stage of the disease indicate how much of that remedy the case will bear, and when.

The matching rule that follows is the degree system's reason for existing, and the tradition states it with an arithmetical plainness that deserves emphasis. In paraphrase: a part heated beyond its due mixture by a given amount is to be cooled by that amount and no more — a simple cold in the same degree as the dyskrasia's heat. Under-correct, and the disease remains; over-correct, and the remedy installs the opposite dyskrasia in the disease's place — a cold distemper, in the tag the later herbals repeat from the Simples' third book, twice as bad as the heat removed. Therapy is thus a titration of qualities: diagnosis fixes the coordinates and the magnitude, pharmacology supplies agents of known coordinates and magnitude, and the method is the equation between them. Nothing in ancient science outside astronomy and harmonics comes as close to a quantitative clinical practice — and nothing in the later alchemical literature is more recognisably descended from a single ancestor than the adept's matching of a medicine's degrees to a metal's distemper, in Section XV.

THE THREE ORDERS OF REMEDY

In the Method's ascending order of force: regimen first — food, drink, exercise, bathing, sleep, the management of the daily intake of qualities; drugs second, simples and compounds graded as in Section XII; surgery and evacuation last — the lancet, the cautery, the purge — where mixture must be corrected by subtraction or by hand. The order is a risk calculus, and Galen enforces it polemically against practitioners who reach for the strongest instrument first.

THE NON-NATURALS: A LATER LABEL, A GALENIC THING

The medieval curriculum organises regimen under six headings — air; food and drink; sleep and waking; motion and rest; retention and evacuation; the passions of the mind — the famous six non-naturals. The sixfold list in that form is a product of the Arabic and Latin school tradition (it is systematised in the wake of Johannitius' Isagoge), not a chapter of Galen; the contents, however, are Galen's regimen literature entire, above all On Matters of Health (De sanitate tuenda). The label is dated here so that the thing is not misdated with it.

A word on compounds completes the pharmacy. Where no single simple carries the required profile — hot in the second, dry in the first, say, with a penetrating subtlety and a flavour a patient can keep down — the physician composes one: Galen's two great compound treatises, On the Composition of Drugs According to Places and According to Kinds, give the theory and the formulary, down to the famous inherited compounds such as theriac, the many-ingredient antidote whose manufacture was a civic ceremony in Venice a thousand years later. Composition is mixture-theory at the bench: the compound's krasis is argued from its ingredients' graded qualities, corrected by experience of the whole — and by the whole-substance exception of Section XII, under which a compound, like a purgative, may do what no sum of its parts' qualities predicts. The alchemical compositio of the elixir, argued in exactly these terms by the Latin tradition, is this doctrine with the patient changed.

XIV. Teleology and the Rival Schools

Galen's system did not win because it was the only system on offer. It won against named rivals, in a medical marketplace he describes with undisguised contempt, and two features of his position — its teleology and its middle way between the sects — belong in any account that wants to explain why the humours, of all ancient doctrines, were the ones still standing in 1600.

Nature the Craftsman

Galen's teleology — stated most fully in the seventeen books of On the Usefulness of the Parts, his anatomical masterwork — holds, in paraphrase, that Nature is a craftsman of consummate skill and foresight: every part of the animal body is made for the sake of its activity, nothing is superfluous, nothing is wanting, and nothing could be better disposed than it is. The hand's opposed thumb, the eyelashes' arrested growth, the valves and tunics and doublings of the vessels — anatomy, pursued far enough, becomes for Galen a demonstration of providential workmanship, and he closes the treatise by calling it, in paraphrase, a sacred hymn to the maker: true piety, he argues, lies not in hecatombs but in learning, and teaching others, how great are the maker's wisdom, power, and goodness. His philosophical debts are worn openly: the craftsman is Plato's Demiurge of the Timaeus, naturalised; the principle that nature does nothing in vain is Aristotle's, pressed further than Aristotle pressed it; the Hippocrates who “is continually singing the praises of Nature's foresight” is, as the Cambridge Companion's philosophy-of-nature chapter concedes, a Hippocrates partly of Galen's own composition. Against the atomists — Epicurus above all, for whom the eye was not made in order to see — Galen's teleology is a fighting doctrine: function explains structure, purpose is a cause, and a medicine built on purposive faculties (Section X) is rational in a way no mechanics of particles can match. The doctrine's afterlife is double. It made Galenic anatomy a natural theology that Christian, Muslim, and Jewish readers could each baptise in their own way — one deep reason for the system's fifteen-century run — and it taught the alchemical tradition to read the interior of metals, too, as workmanship to be understood before it is corrected.

The Sects: Rationalists, Empiricists, Methodists

Galen's little treatise On the Sects for Beginners (in Singer's Selected Works) is the standard ancient map of the competition, and its three parties are best understood as three answers to one question: how is medical knowledge possible?

THE RATIONALISTS (DOGMATISTS)

Knowledge by reason: hidden causes — elements, qualities, faculties — may legitimately be inferred from evident signs, and anatomy and natural philosophy are therefore foundations of the art. Hippocrates, as Galen reads him, is their founder; Galen claims their banner, on his own terms.

THE EMPIRICISTS

Knowledge by experience alone: reasoning about hidden causes is illegitimate, and the art consists in remembered observations — one's own, others' reported, and the transition to the similar case. Founded, in the sect's own history, by Philinus of Cos and Serapion of Alexandria in the third century BCE; Sextus Empiricus is their philosophical kin.

THE METHODISTS

Knowledge by the Method: all diseases reduce to manifest common conditions — the constricted, the lax, and the mixed — discernible without theory of hidden causes, and treatable by their contraries in set cycles. Descended from Asclepiades of Bithynia's corpuscles and pores; founded as a sect by Themison, and made notorious — to Galen — by Thessalus of Tralles, who promised to teach the whole art in six months.

Galen's own position, argued across the Therapeutic Method and the sects treatise, is a deliberate synthesis, and Hankinson's Companion chapters on methodology and epistemology are its standard modern analysis. In paraphrase: reason without experience is empty — a deduction about a drug or a dyskrasia must answer to what the trained senses find at the bedside — and experience without reason is blind, a heap of cases that cannot extend itself to the next, dissimilar patient. The true physician commands both, adds anatomy as the map of the theatre in which faculties act, and despises the Methodists above all precisely because their system is the most teachable: a medicine that can be learned in six months, without philosophy, anatomy, or the long apprenticeship of the hand, threatens not only Galen's doctrines but the profession's claim that medicine is a rational art. The polemic is social as well as scientific — Thessalus, in Galen's telling, is an uneducated upstart degrading the art to the level of shoemaking — and modern historians, Nutton included, discount the caricature accordingly: Methodism was a serious, successful medicine, Soranus its great surviving voice, and its common conditions are not a foolish theory but a rival wager on how much theory a practice needs. The wager Galen placed — on more theory, systematised, quantified, and philosophically armed — is the wager the subsequent tradition collected on. It is also the wager alchemy placed on itself.

// WHY THE SECTS MATTER TO AN ALCHEMY HUB //

The sectarian map is the template of every later controversy in the art: bookish systematists against experience-only practitioners, six-month methods against lifelong apprenticeships, hidden causes against manifest signs. The Greek alchemical corpus stages the same quarrels in workshop dress — Zosimos against the mere recipe-followers, the commentators against the unlettered dyers — and the reader who has Galen's map can find the parties again in every century, including the Paracelsian assault on Galenism itself, which replays the Empiricist and Methodist charges with Galen cast, at last, as the dogmatist.

XV. Closing the Loop: The Shared Physics of Medicine and Alchemy

It remains to collect what the hub exists to show: that the system expounded in this article is not background to alchemy but one of its working parts. The transfer can be stated in a single sentence. Temperament theory is a general physics of mixtures, and it does not care what is mixed. Bodies, foods, drugs, minerals, and metals are all kraseis of the same four qualities; health, ripeness, perfection — and disease, crudity, baseness — are all names for the state of a mixture; and every art that works on matter by heating, cooling, drying, and moistening, in graded degrees and at chosen times, is practising the one science in different materials.

The alchemical texts say so themselves, from the beginning. The workshop literature treated in Greek Alchemy's Workshop Root already works by qualities — its dyeings, whitenings, and yellowing are hot and cold, wet and dry operations on metallic bodies — and the tradition's mature theory makes the medical frame explicit. A metal, in the doctrine the Arabic and Latin alchemists inherit and refine, is a body with a temperament: lead cold and crude, its mixture ill-proportioned and its concoction unfinished; gold the eukratic metal, hot and wet in due measure, perfectly concocted, incorruptible because its mixture has no excess to feed corruption. Transmutation, stated in this language, is neither magic nor fraud but therapy: the alchemist diagnoses the base metal's dyskrasia, compounds a medicine — the word is the tradition's own, for the elixir is a medicina graded, in the Latin texts, in orders of strength exactly as Galen grades his simples — and applies it by regimen and by fire until the mixture is corrected and the patient stands eukratic: gold. The philosopher's stone is a drug for metals, prescribed by indication, matched in degree, and administered by a physician whose surgery is the furnace. That this programme failed as metallurgy does not diminish its pedigree as reasoning: it is the Therapeutic Method, faithfully applied to the wrong patients.

THE SAME SQUARE

The quality square of The Four Elements governs the metal as it governs the man: sulphur hot and dry, mercury cold and wet in the great Arabic theory of the metals' principles — a two-principle analysis stated, still, in the vocabulary of qualities and their mixture, and unintelligible without it.

THE SAME WORDS

The cover-names collected in the Decknamen Glossary are thick with the body's vocabulary — bodies, spirits, bloods, menstrua, digestions, putrefactions, medicines, and diseases of metals — because the art that named them thought of the work as a physiology. A deckname is often a diagnosis in disguise.

The Hermetic branch of the same soil deserves its sentence here. The Technical Hermetica — the astrological, medical, and lapidary writings under Hermes' name — practise an iatromathematics: a medicine of cosmic sympathies, in which the zodiacal signs rule the body's members, the planets govern humours and hours, and the physician times his evacuations and his drugs by the heavens. That doctrine is not Galen's — Galen is pointedly cool toward astrological medicine, allowing the moon a critical-days influence and no more — but it grows in Galen's world, speaks his vocabulary of qualities, mixtures, and critical balances, and fuses with Galenism in the Arabic and Latin centuries into the astro-medical regimen literature that every later practitioner, alchemists emphatically included, took for granted. The hub's Foundations cluster exists because these fusions cannot be understood from any one of their ingredients: the elements without the humours are a diagram, the humours without the degrees are a proverb, and the degrees without the furnace are a pharmacy with no fire in it.

A last assessment, in the manner this archive applies to every system it treats. Humoral medicine was wrong about the fluids — there is no black bile as the ancients imagined it, and no humour leaves the body by a purge in the proportions chapter 7 predicts. It was not wrong about everything it built on them. That the body is a regulated system whose states are matters of degree; that constitution is individual and changes with age, season, and place; that food is the gentlest drug and drugs are foods that have crossed a line of force; that a remedy must be matched in strength to the disorder it corrects, and that over-correction is a second disease; that some substances act by specific powers no elemental analysis predicts — these are Galenic sentences that survived the humours, and several of them are now the commonplaces of a medicine that has forgotten their author. The alchemists, who forgot nothing, preserved the whole system — square, humours, mixtures, degrees — inside their art long after the physicians let it go, which is why an alchemy archive must teach it entire. The furnace was the last Galenic institution. This article has been its syllabus.
