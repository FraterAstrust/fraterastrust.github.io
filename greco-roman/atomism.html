I. Two Democrituses, One Physics

This article belongs to the Foundations cluster of the hub's Alexandrian Alchemy wing, and like the wing's article on the Hermetica it is, strictly speaking, a philosophical sibling of the alchemical articles rather than one of them. Atomism — the doctrine that reality consists of indivisible bodies, atoms, moving in void, and that everything else is their temporary arrangement — is not an alchemical theory. It is older than alchemy by some four centuries, it was developed with no reference to furnaces, dyes, or the making of gold, and in its classical form it explains the qualities of things precisely by denying that those qualities are real. It stands in this wing for one reason: the founding book of Greek alchemy, the Physika kai Mystika, travelled under the name of atomism's most famous founder, Democritus of Abdera, while teaching a physics of qualities, sympathies, and transformations that atomism cannot underwrite. That borrowed name is this article's destination (Section XIV). The road to it runs through the real atomism — what it claimed, how we know it, what Epicurus changed, how Lucretius preserved it, and why, for all that, it lost the ancient argument.

First, the discipline of naming on which this hub insists, because three different figures answer to “Democritus” in the sources and confusing them corrupts everything downstream. The full treatment of the second and third figures is in the hub's article on the Origins of Greek Alchemy, whose adjudications this article follows exactly; what follows is the minimum a reader of the atomism needs in hand.

1. DEMOCRITUS OF ABDERA

The historical atomist (c. 460–c. 370 BCE): co-founder, with Leucippus, of the doctrine of atoms and void. He wrote on physics, mathematics, ethics, and much else; nothing he wrote survives whole. He wrote nothing about making gold. He is this article's subject.

2. BOLOS OF MENDES

An Egyptian-Greek compiler of the Hellenistic period, flourishing c. 200 BCE, author of works on sympathies and antipathies, on things made by hand, and on farming — the fountainhead of the later “Democritean” tradition of natural secrets, much of which circulated under Democritus's name rather than his own.

3. THE ALCHEMICAL PSEUDO-DEMOCRITUS

The persona of the alchemical Four Books — on gold, on silver, on stones, on purple — whose surviving form, the Physika kai Mystika, Matteo Martelli's edition places in the second half of the first century CE. Not the atomist, and not simply Bolos either: an anonymous later writer working a tradition Bolos had helped to found.

The three are related as source, tributary, and mask. Bolos inherited the atomist's name and reputation and attached them to a literature of sympathies the atomist never wrote; the alchemical author, centuries later again, inherited the name from that tradition and wrote in it. Keeping the figures distinct is not pedantry. It is the difference between a history of Greek philosophy and a history of Greek alchemy — two histories that share a name and almost nothing else, and whose entanglement is itself one of the most instructive episodes in the transmission this hub exists to document.

A note on method, because atomism attracts confident invention as few ancient doctrines do. Democritus survives in testimony, not in texts (Section II), and this article's quotations are disciplined accordingly. A sentence is given as Democritus's own words only where it is transmitted as a verbatim fragment in the standard collections and is quoted here in a named modern translation, named at the quotation. Everything else — and it is most of the doctrine — is labelled paraphrase or report, with the reporting author named: Aristotle, Theophrastus, Diogenes Laertius, Sextus Empiricus, and the rest of the witness stand. For Epicurus the situation is better and worse at once: three of his letters survive entire, quoted by Diogenes Laertius in Book 10 of the Lives, and they are quoted and paraphrased here from the named translations listed in Section XVI. For Lucretius we have the poem itself, and its few quotations here follow W. H. D. Rouse's Loeb translation, named at each quotation. The backbone modern accounts are A. A. Long and D. N. Sedley's The Hellenistic Philosophers (1987) for the Epicurean material and C. C. W. Taylor's The Atomists: Leucippus and Democritus (1999) — the fragments and testimonia in translation, with commentary — for the founders.

// WHY A PHYSICS ARTICLE STANDS AMONG FOUNDATIONS //

Every other system in this cluster — the four elements, Aristotle's matter theory, Stoic physics — is a physics of qualities: hot and cold, wet and dry, sympathy and mixture, the vocabulary alchemy actually speaks. Atomism is the cluster's control case: the one great ancient physics that refuses qualities altogether. Placing it beside the others shows, by contrast, what alchemy's working assumptions were — and makes the puzzle of its borrowed Democritean name visible in full.

II. The Evidence Problem: Testimony, Not Texts

Begin with the fact that governs everything else in this article: not one work of Leucippus or Democritus survives. Democritus was among the most prolific authors of the fifth century BCE. Diogenes Laertius, writing some six centuries later, preserves a catalogue of his writings — arranged by Thrasyllus, the first-century CE editor of Plato, into tetralogies on the Platonic model — running to works on ethics, physics, mathematics, music, language, agriculture, painting, and tactics: more than sixty titles, among them the Little World System (Mikros diakosmos), his most famous physical treatise. The Great World System listed beside it is reported by Theophrastus to have been Leucippus's work, an attribution modern scholarship generally accepts — an early sign of how thoroughly the two founders' writings were already merged in transmission. Of all of it, nothing remains but quotations, paraphrases, and reports in later authors, collected in the modern era as the fragments and testimonia of Diels and Kranz's Die Fragmente der Vorsokratiker — cited throughout this article, as everywhere, by their DK numbers (67 for Leucippus, 68 for Democritus).

The witness stand, in rough order of value, is this. Aristotle (384–322 BCE) is the earliest substantial witness and by far the most important: he discusses the atomists throughout his physical works, wrote a monograph On Democritus — itself lost, surviving only in passages quoted by the late commentator Simplicius — and, uniquely among our sources, had read the atomists' books entire. He is also an opponent, arguing for his own physics against theirs, and his reports must be read as a rival's evidence: generally careful, never neutral. His successor Theophrastus devoted a treatise, On the Senses, to a detailed exposition and point-by-point critique of Democritus's theory of perception, and his doxographical work stands behind much of the later tradition. Diogenes Laertius (probably third century CE) gives the life, the catalogue of works, and the travel traditions in Book 9 of the Lives — invaluable, and written half a millennium after its subject. Sextus Empiricus (second century CE) preserves, in the course of sceptical argument, some of the most nearly verbatim fragments on knowledge and the senses. Around these stand the doxographers and excerptors — Aëtius, Stobaeus, Galen, Plutarch, Cicero — each quoting for purposes of his own.

Two cautions follow, and both are standing rules of this archive applied to a new case. First, the ethical fragments — the hundreds of sayings on cheerfulness, moderation, and conduct that fill most of the Democritus of popular quotation — are the least secure part of the corpus. Stobaeus transmits two collections, one under Democritus's name and one under that of an otherwise unknown “Democrates”; Diels-Kranz printed both as Democritus, but the authenticity of the sayings, and their relation to the atomism, remain matters of scholarly dispute. This article accordingly builds nothing on them: where the ethical Democritus appears (Section VII), he appears as the tradition's figure, labelled as such. Second, the physical testimony is entangled with its reporters' agendas. Aristotle reports atomism in order to replace it; Sextus quotes Democritus in order to enlist him for scepticism; the doxographers compress him into placita — one-line positions in a table of opinions. The developed system reconstructed in Sections IV–VI is therefore a reconstruction: the modern scholarly consensus, represented here by Kirk, Raven, and Schofield's The Presocratic Philosophers and by Taylor's edition of the fragments and testimonia, built by weighing witnesses against each other. Where the witnesses conflict — on whether Democritean atoms have weight, on whether they are indivisible in thought or only in fact — this article says so rather than smoothing the conflict away.

// THE PLATO SILENCE //

One absence in the record deserves naming, because it is so strange. Plato — Democritus's younger contemporary, whose dialogues name or engage nearly every significant predecessor — never mentions Democritus at all. A late and unreliable anecdote has Plato wishing to burn Democritus's books and being talked out of it. The anecdote is not evidence; the silence is. Whatever its cause, it meant that the atomists entered the later tradition without a Platonic reading to mediate them — one reason, among several in Section XIII, that Aristotle's hostile account faced so little competition.

III. Leucippus: The Founder Who Vanishes

The Greek tradition is consistent on one point: atomism was founded by Leucippus. Aristotle — our earliest witness, and the one with the books in front of him — names Leucippus as the theory's originator, and Theophrastus follows him. Almost everything else about the man dissolves on inspection. The sources variously give his birthplace as Elea, Abdera, or Miletus (DK 67A1) — three cities that between them would make him a pupil of the Eleatics, a fellow-citizen of Democritus, or an heir of the Milesian natural philosophers, and the disagreement is itself the information: already in antiquity, Leucippus had become a position in an argument about influences rather than a recoverable person. He is sometimes said to have studied with Zeno of Elea, which would make atomism a direct answer to the Eleatic paradoxes from inside the school; the report is plausible, ancient, and unverifiable. His one attributed book is the Great World System; a work On Mind is also named. Of his writing, a single sentence is transmitted as his own (DK 67B2, from On Mind), rendered in the standard translations: “Nothing happens at random; everything happens for a reason and by necessity.” It is the founding charter of physical determinism, and it is very nearly all of Leucippus that survives in his own voice.

The eclipse was total and early. Most reports of the developed doctrine name Democritus alone, or the pair together with Democritus in the foreground; the theory came to be regarded, already in antiquity, as essentially the younger man's work — he systematised it, extended it to psychology, epistemology, and the history of human society, and wrote the books the tradition actually read. Epicurus, two centuries on, is reported to have gone further and denied that a philosopher Leucippus had ever existed (DK 67A2) — a denial modern scholarship does not follow, since Aristotle's testimony is too early and too specific to be invention, but which shows how completely the founder had faded behind his successor within the atomist tradition itself. Nineteenth-century scholarship briefly revived the doubt; the current position, represented by Taylor's chapter in The Cambridge Companion to Early Greek Philosophy and by the Stanford Encyclopedia's entries on both men, is that Leucippus founded the theory, that collaboration or close succession linked the two, and that the division of their individual contributions is in principle unrecoverable. This article therefore speaks, as the sources mostly do, of the doctrine of “the atomists” where attribution cannot be separated, and of Democritus where the testimony names him.

// A PATTERN THIS ARCHIVE KNOWS //

A founder eclipsed by the successor whose name the tradition keeps; a corpus absorbed into the successor's catalogue; a later age doubting the founder existed. The reader of the hub's alchemical articles has met the pattern's mirror image in Bolos and pseudo-Democritus (Section I), where the name survived and the man dissolved. In atomism the reverse occurred: the doctrine survived and the founder dissolved. Both are what transmission does to authorship when books are copied for their use rather than their authors.

IV. Atoms and Void: The System Stated

Atomism was built to solve a problem set by someone else. Parmenides of Elea, in the first half of the fifth century BCE, had argued — in verse, with a rigour Greek thought had not seen before — that what is, is one, unchanging, and indivisible: coming-to-be would require something to arise from nothing, which is impossible; motion would require empty space, “what is not”, which cannot exist; plurality and change are therefore illusions of mortal opinion. His followers sharpened the argument: Melissus pressed the impossibility of void, and Zeno's paradoxes attacked plurality and motion directly. The pluralist systems of the mid-fifth century — Empedocles' four elements, Anaxagoras's infinite seeds — are all, in the standard modern reading that Aristotle's reports support, attempts to concede Parmenides his logic while saving the phenomena: to keep what truly exists eternal and unchanging, and to explain apparent change as the rearrangement of things that do not themselves change. Atomism is the boldest of these attempts, because it concedes the most and denies the most at once.

The atomist answer, as Aristotle reports its reasoning in On Generation and Corruption (I.8, DK 67A7), runs in paraphrase as follows. Leucippus sought a theory that would agree with the evidence of the senses — that motion, plurality, and change are real — without abandoning the Eleatic ban on generation from nothing. His solution divides Parmenides' “what is” into an infinity of pieces and grants “what is not” a carefully limited existence:

THE FULL: ATOMS

Infinite in number; each one a miniature Parmenidean being — ungenerated, indestructible, unchanging, perfectly solid, with no void inside it, and indivisible: atomos, uncuttable. Atoms differ from one another only in shape, size, and (in Epicurus's revision) weight. They are too small to be perceived.

THE EMPTY: VOID

Infinite in extent; not a body, not a stuff, but the unoccupied place in which atoms move. The atomists embraced the paradox head-on: void is “what is not”, and it exists. Democritus is reported to have coined a word for the atom by subtraction — den, “thing”, made by cutting the mē- (“not”) off mēden, “nothing” — and to have held that the thing exists no more than the nothing: body and void stand on exactly the same ontological footing (DK 67A6).

Everything else follows. Coming-to-be is atoms gathering into a compound; passing-away is the compound's dissolution; qualitative change is rearrangement within it. Nothing is ever created or destroyed at the level of what is real, so Parmenides is satisfied; everything the senses report happens at the level of compounds, so the phenomena are saved. Motion needs no first mover and no purpose: atoms have always moved, colliding, rebounding — their mutual resistance on impact, antitypia, is the basic mechanical fact of the system — and interlocking where their shapes allow, and every motion is explained by a prior collision in a chain with no beginning. Aristotle objected that this explains each motion and never motion itself (Section XIII); the atomists, on the testimony, did not regard the demand for a first beginning as legitimate. Leucippus's one sentence (Section III) states the resulting order of explanation: nothing at random, everything for a reason and by necessity — “chance”, where the reports use the word of Democritus, meaning absence of purpose, not absence of cause.

Worlds Without Number

The cosmology is the physics at large scale, and it is reported consistently. In the infinite void, atoms in motion gather — by the sorting tendency the reports call like to like, illustrated in a fragment (DK 68B164) by the sieve that sorts grains and the tide that sorts pebbles on a beach — into a great whirl or vortex, in which atoms are separated by size and shape as in a winnowing basket. A world (kosmos) forms: a membrane of interwoven atoms at the whirl's rim, earth at its centre, the heavenly bodies kindled by the speed of their motion. The world grows, holds, and in time decays as its atoms disperse. And because atoms and void are both infinite, this has happened and is happening infinite times: worlds without number, some growing, some declining, some — the doxography adds — without sun or moon, some without living things. Our world is one local weather-system in an infinite material universe, neither unique, nor central, nor made for anyone. No doctrine of the fifth century travels further from the cosmos of myth, and the atomists drew the consequence for the soul as calmly as for the stars: the soul itself is a compound, of especially fine, smooth, round atoms — the atoms of fire — spread through the body and held in by respiration; at death the compound breaks, the soul-atoms scatter, and there is nothing further to fear or to hope. Epicurus and Lucretius would make therapy of that consequence (Sections VIII and XI); in the founders it is simply the physics, stated without emphasis.

// WHAT “INDIVISIBLE” MEANT — AN OPEN QUESTION, KEPT OPEN //

Were Democritus's atoms indivisible in fact — too hard and solid ever to be split — or indivisible in principle, magnitudes with no conceptual parts? The testimony pulls both ways: atoms have shapes, surfaces, and (on some reports) hooks, all of which imply distinguishable parts, yet the theory's answer to Zeno seems to require a true lower limit to division. Modern scholarship — Furley's Two Studies in the Greek Atomists (1967) is the classic discussion — remains divided, and Epicurus's later doctrine of minimal parts (Section IX) is best read as the school's own attempt to repair exactly this joint. This article records the difficulty rather than resolving it.

V. Shape, Order, Position: How Atoms Differ

If atoms are all made of the same undifferentiated solidity, what makes the compounds they form so various? Aristotle's report in the Metaphysics (A.4, DK 67A6) gives the atomists' answer in the most famous analogy of early Greek physics — the letters of the alphabet. Atoms differ, the report says, in exactly three ways, and the letters show all three:

DifferenceGreek termThe letter example (Aristotle's report)What it explains
Shape schēma (Democritus's own word may have been rhysmos, “form”) A differs from N The intrinsic geometry of the individual atom: smooth or rough, round or angular, hooked or concave — the basis of texture, taste, and cohesion.
Order taxis (diathigē, “inter-arrangement”) AN differs from NA The sequence and arrangement of atoms within a compound — the same atoms, differently ordered, make a different body.
Position thesis (tropē, “turning”) N differs from Z (the letter turned) The orientation of each atom in place — the same shape, turned, presents a different face to its neighbours.

Aristotle adds the corollary in Democritus's spirit: a tragedy and a comedy are written with the same letters. Add size — which the letter analogy omits but the other testimony is clear about — and the system's whole vocabulary of difference is complete. There are infinitely many shapes, the reports say, because there is no reason — the atomists' characteristic “no more this than that” argument — why an atom should be one shape rather than another; shapes run from the smooth spheres of fire and soul to rough, angular, and hooked forms whose projections let compounds hold together. The interlocking of irregular atoms, rather than any glue or force, is the system's account of why solid bodies cohere — though the picturesque hooks and barbs of the later summaries should be read as the tradition's illustration of irregular shape, not as standard fittings on every atom.

The explanatory reach of so spare a vocabulary is best seen where Theophrastus preserves its working detail: the theory of tastes in On the Senses. In paraphrase of his report: the sweet is produced by atoms round and fairly large; the astringent by atoms large, rough, polygonal, and not rounded; the sharp by atoms small, angular, and bent; the salty by atoms angular, good-sized, and crooked; the bitter by atoms round, smooth, crooked, and small; the oily by atoms fine, round, and small. The list matters less for its particular assignments — Theophrastus himself criticised them point by point, finding inconsistencies — than for its method. A quality that every other Greek system treated as a basic datum of nature, an irreducible kind of stuff or power, is here treated as a geometrical transaction between the shapes on a tongue and the shapes arriving at it. Nothing in the atom is sweet. Sweetness is what a certain configuration does to a certain organ. That sentence, generalised, is the whole of the next section.

VI. The Senses by Convention: Knowledge and Its Limits

The most quoted sentence of Democritus — one of the very few transmitted as his own words, preserved by Sextus Empiricus and by Galen — is DK 68B9, given here in C. C. W. Taylor's translation in The Atomists (1999):

“By convention sweet and by convention bitter, by convention hot, by convention cold, by convention colour; but in reality atoms and void.”

The sentence needs its standard gloss, because “convention” misleads modern ears. Taylor's rendering of the Greek nomōi does not mean that sweetness is arbitrary, a matter of social agreement like driving on the left. The same atomic configuration regularly produces the same perception in a perceiver built as we are; nothing about the process is capricious. The contrast is between what exists in reality — atoms and void, possessing shape, size, and arrangement and nothing else — and what exists only in relation to a perceiver: colour, taste, heat, and cold are not properties of atoms, nor even, strictly, of the compounds, but events at the meeting of compound and organ. Democritus is drawing, two millennia early, the distinction later philosophy would formalise as primary and secondary qualities — with the atomist's characteristic extra step: even the compound, the table or the honey, is in reality only its atoms, so the demotion of the sensible world goes all the way down to everything we have ever touched.

The theory of perception that supports the sentence is mechanical throughout, and Theophrastus's On the Senses is its fullest surviving exposition. Perception is contact: films or effluences of atoms — for sight, the eidōla, image-films continually shed by the surfaces of bodies — travel through the air, enter the organ, and are registered by the soul-atoms within. Thinking itself is a motion of those same fine atoms, which is why its condition depends on the body's balance: a soul-compound too hot or too cold, Theophrastus reports Democritus holding, thinks badly. Knowledge therefore comes in two kinds, and the fragment that names them (DK 68B11, preserved by Sextus) is paraphrased here rather than quoted, as this article's rule requires where a translation is not being followed verbatim: there is a genuine kind of knowledge and a bastard kind. Sight, hearing, smell, taste, and touch belong to the bastard kind — they are the senses' commerce with compounds. When the bastard kind can go no further, being unable to perceive anything smaller, the genuine kind — reason working on what the senses supply — takes over, and its objects are the atoms and the void themselves.

Democritus saw the trap inside his own epistemology and, uniquely among the early philosophers, dramatised it. In the fragment numbered DK 68B125, also preserved by Galen, the senses are made to answer the mind that has just demoted them — in paraphrase: wretched mind, do you take your evidence from us and then overthrow us? Our overthrow is your own downfall. The protest is exact. Everything reason knows about atoms it inferred from the senses' reports; a theory that declares those reports unreal saws off its own branch. Whether Democritus meant the fragment as a genuine self-criticism, a sceptical concession, or a dialectical exercise remains disputed — Sextus, who quotes the surrounding material, was enlisting him for scepticism, and modern readers divide between a sceptical Democritus and a rationalist one who trusted the genuine knowledge to correct the bastard. What is not disputed is the sophistication of the problem he had posed: it is, in embryo, the standing problem of every reductive physics since — how a world-picture built entirely from appearances can coherently declare appearances unreal. The founders of atomism did not solve it. They were the first to state it, and they stated it against themselves.

// WHY THIS SECTION IS THE HINGE OF THE ARTICLE //

Hold B9 in mind when Section XIV arrives. A physics in which colour, heat, taste, and quality are conventions of perception — real only as events in a perceiver — has no room for the working furniture of alchemy: no real hot and cold to balance, no sympathies between natures, no tincture that is goldenness. Whatever the alchemical Democritus teaches, it cannot be this. The distance between the sentence above and the recipe book that bears its author's name is the measure of the borrowing.

VII. Democritus the Traveller: The Doxographical Sage

The historical Democritus of Sections IV–VI is a reconstruction from testimony. The Democritus of the ancient imagination — the figure later centuries actually inherited, and the only one the alchemical tradition could have borrowed — is a different construction, assembled by the doxography, and this section treats him as what he is: a biographical tradition, whose details are evidence for the reputation, not for the life. Diogenes Laertius (Book 9) reports that Democritus was the son of a wealthy man of Abdera — wealthy enough, the story goes, to have entertained Xerxes on the Persian march through Thrace — and that the son spent his inheritance, a hundred talents by the usual telling, on travel undertaken purely for learning. The itinerary grew in the telling: Egypt, where Diodorus Siculus (I.98) says he lived five years among the priests and learned astronomy and geometry; Persia and the Chaldaeans of Babylon; in some accounts India and Ethiopia as well. Clement of Alexandria preserves what purports to be Democritus's own boast — in paraphrase: that no man of his time had travelled more, seen more lands, or heard more learned men, and that even the Egyptian rope-stretchers, the priestly geometers, did not surpass him in the construction of lines with proof. Theophrastus, a witness of another order, independently called him a man who had seen many countries. Beneath the accretions there is probably a real traveller: the works catalogue includes writings on Babylon and on Meroë, and the range of the fragments' knowledge is wide. But the shape of the tradition — the philosopher formed by Egyptian priests and Persian magi — is a Hellenistic pattern, applied in the same centuries to Pythagoras, to Plato, and to nearly every sage whose authority a later text needed to deepen. Travel, in this literature, is not biography. It is a credential.

The rest of the traditional portrait fills out the same figure. He is the laughing philosopher — the anecdotes, gathered by Diogenes and elaborated for a millennium, show him laughing at the follies of mankind, where Heraclitus wept at them; the pairing became a fixture of Renaissance art and of the pseudo-Hippocratic letters, a small novel in epistolary form in which the Abderites summon Hippocrates to cure Democritus's supposed madness and the physician finds the sanest man in Greece, dissecting animals to find the seat of the bile that men call folly. His ethics, as the tradition transmits it, centres on euthymia — cheerfulness, well-being of soul: the state, in the paraphrase of the reports, in which the soul lives calmly and steadily, untroubled by fear, superstition, or passion, achieved by moderation and by measuring pleasures against their consequences. The ethical sayings through which this teaching mostly reaches us are, as Section II warned, the least securely attributed part of the corpus. The stance itself, however, is well attested, and it is recognisably the ethics an atomist physics would generate: if the soul is a compound of atoms and the gods do not intervene, then tranquillity is an engineering problem of one's own arrangement, and philosophy is the art of arranging it.

// WHAT THE LEGEND WAS FOR //

Read functionally, the traveller legend does for Democritus exactly what the Memphis initiation does for his alchemical namesake in the hub's Origins of Greek Alchemy: it manufactures credentials. A philosopher who has sat with Egyptian priests and Chaldaean astronomers can plausibly know things no Greek school teaches — temple geometry, oriental astronomy, the properties of stones and plants, the secrets of dyers and goldsmiths. The legend is older than the alchemical texts and independent of them; but it built, in advance, the shelf on which those texts would later be placed. Section XIV returns to this.

VIII. Epicurus: The System Rebuilt in the Garden

Atomism's second founder refounded it as a way of life. Epicurus (341–270 BCE), born on Samos to Athenian parents, taught briefly at Mytilene and Lampsacus and in 307 BCE bought a house and garden in Athens — the Garden — and taught there for the remaining thirty-six years of his life. The school he gathered was unusual in the ancient world in ways the sources stress: it admitted women and slaves as full members, it lived communally on simple fare, it withdrew from politics, and it organised itself around friendship and around the memory of the founder to a degree that later critics called cultic — Epicurus's will, preserved by Diogenes Laertius, provides for monthly commemorations of himself and annual ones of his brothers and his friend Metrodorus. He wrote, by the ancient count, some three hundred rolls, including a thirty-seven-book On Nature. Nearly all of it is lost. What survives is a fraction so small and so specific that its survival itself needs explaining: Diogenes Laertius, closing his Lives with Epicurus in Book 10, chose to transcribe three letters entire — the Letter to Herodotus on physics, the Letter to Pythocles on celestial phenomena, and the Letter to Menoeceus on ethics — together with the forty Principal Doctrines (Kyriai Doxai), the authorised catechism of the school. Add the Vatican Sayings, a collection recovered from a fourteenth-century manuscript, the fragments of On Nature carbonised at Herculaneum and unrolled from the papyri of the Villa of the Pisos, and the great inscription of Diogenes of Oinoanda (Section XIII), and the inventory is complete: an epitome tradition, by design. Epicurus wrote summaries of his own system for memorisation, and it is the summaries — built to be carried in the memory of travelling adherents — that outlasted the library.

The Letter to Herodotus is this article's primary Epicurean text, and its physics is quoted and paraphrased here from R. D. Hicks's Loeb translation of Diogenes Laertius, Book 10, checked against Cyril Bailey's translation in Epicurus: The Extant Remains (1926). Its opening doctrines are the founders' doctrines, restated as therapeutic axioms — in paraphrase, following Hicks: nothing comes into being out of what does not exist, for otherwise everything would come from everything with no seeds needed; nothing perishes into nothing, for otherwise all things would long since have vanished; the universe was always as it is now and will always be so, there being nothing outside it into which it could change. The whole of being consists of bodies and space — bodies, whose existence sensation itself attests, and the void, place, intangible existence without which bodies would have nowhere to be and nothing to move through. Of bodies, some are compounds and others the atoms of which compounds are formed: indivisible and unchangeable, completely solid, incapable of dissolution — for if dissolution could go all the way down, everything would already have been destroyed into the non-existent. The universe is boundless, in body and in void alike; the atoms are infinite in number; worlds form within it as the founders taught. A reader of Sections IV–VI will find nothing here to surprise — and that is the point that Cicero, the school's most quotable enemy, pressed with contempt: that Epicurus's physics is Democritus copied. The changes are real, however, and they are concentrated exactly where Democritus's system had been left exposed — by Aristotle's criticisms, which Epicurus, who had studied with a Democritean (Nausiphanes) and read Aristotle closely, knew in detail. Section IX takes them one by one.

// THE PURPOSE CLAUSE //

Epicurus states the aim of the physics at the letter's close, and the school never let it be forgotten: if we were not troubled by our suspicions about the heavens and about death, and by our failure to know the limits of pain and desire, we should have no need of natural philosophy at all. Atomism in the Garden is not research for its own sake. It is medicine — the physics exists to remove the fears (of gods, of death, of fate) that disturb the soul's arrangement, and its doctrines are valued exactly as far as they perform that cure. No alchemical text could be written from inside this purpose, either: the Garden's physics is a therapy, not a technology.

IX. Weight, Minima, and the Rest of Epicurus's Changes

Epicurus's revisions of the founders' system are few, deliberate, and — with one famous exception, reserved for Section X — conservative: each closes a gap that Aristotle or the intervening century had opened. Long and Sedley's The Hellenistic Philosophers is the standard modern presentation of the evidence, and this section follows its arrangement.

PointLeucippus and Democritus (as reported)Epicurus's revision
Weight Disputed in the testimony: some reports give Democritean atoms weight, others make their motion a directionless drift and rebound, with heaviness arising only inside a world's whirl. Epicurus's school later claimed Democritus had no weight at all. Weight is a primary, intrinsic property of every atom, alongside shape and size (Letter to Herodotus §§54, 68–69 in the usual numbering). Atoms therefore have a natural direction of motion: downward, through the void.
Speed Not systematised in the surviving testimony. All atoms move at equal speed through the void, which offers no resistance — “as fast as thought”, in the letter's phrase (§61, paraphrased) — differences of speed in compounds arising only from collisions and entanglements.
Shapes and sizes Infinitely many shapes; sizes unbounded in principle, with one report allowing atoms, somewhere, as large as a world. Shapes are not infinite but incomprehensibly many (§55): for each shape, infinitely many atoms of it, but the repertoire itself is limited — otherwise some atoms would reach perceptible size. Sizes vary, but within a range phenomena permit (§§55–56).
Divisibility Atoms indivisible; whether in thought or only in fact, unresolved (Section IV). The distinction is drawn explicitly. Atoms are physically indivisible because they are solid and voidless; but they are conceived as having minimal parts (elachista) — smallest conceivable units, themselves without parts, which measure the atom as points measure a line without composing it by addition (§§58–59).
Motion's origin Eternal collision and rebound; no first motion, and none felt to be needed. Weight supplies a standing natural motion (the downward fall), impact a second, and the swerve — Section X — a third: the system's most famous and least documented addition.

Two of these revisions deserve a sentence of comment. The doctrine of minimal parts is Epicurus's answer to the difficulty left open in Section IV: an atom must have shape, and shape implies distinguishable extremities, yet if those parts could be separated the atom would not be an atom. The minima solve it by existing only as limits of conception — the atom is measured by its minima as a line is measured by points, but it is not built out of them, and no process can detach one. It is the most technically accomplished move in Epicurean physics, and Lucretius gives it a full exposition (Book 1, in paraphrase) that our sources for Democritus have nothing to match. The doctrine of weight, by contrast, created the problem that defines the next section. Give every atom a natural downward fall at equal speed, and the atoms rain through the void in parallel lines forever, never meeting: no collisions, no compounds, no worlds, no us. Democritus's directionless drift had no such difficulty. Epicurus's tidier system did — and the patch he applied to it became the most debated single doctrine in the whole atomist tradition.

X. The Swerve: The Doctrine and Its Evidence Problem

The doctrine, as our fullest source states it: atoms falling straight down through the void by their own weight, at no fixed time and at no fixed place, swerve — by the least possible amount, no more than a minimum — from the straight line. Lucretius states it at De Rerum Natura 2.216–293, and gives it two jobs to do. The first is cosmological: without the swerve, the parallel rain of atoms would never collide, and nothing would ever have been formed — the swerve is the seed of every encounter in the system. The second is ethical, and it is the one Lucretius lingers on (2.251–293). In Rouse's Loeb translation:

“If all motion is always one long chain, and new motion arises out of the old in order invariable, and if the first-beginnings do not make by swerving a beginning of motion such as to break the decrees of fate, that cause may not follow cause from infinity, whence comes this free will in living creatures all over the earth?” — Lucretius, De Rerum Natura 2.251–255, trans. W. H. D. Rouse (Loeb)

The Latin word is clinamen; the Greek term the sources use is parenklisis, a bending-aside. Cicero — hostile, early, and indispensable — attacks the doctrine in On Fate and On the Nature of the Gods as, in paraphrase, an arbitrary fiction: a motion without a cause, invented to escape necessity, unworthy (he says) of a natural philosopher, since nothing is more shameful for a physicist than to say something happens without a cause. The attack is itself evidence: by the first century BCE the swerve was known, notorious, and attributed to Epicurus personally.

// THE EVIDENCE PROBLEM, STATED PLAINLY //

The swerve does not appear in any surviving writing of Epicurus. It is not in the Letter to Herodotus, which treats atomic motion at length; it is not in the other letters or the Principal Doctrines. Our evidence is Lucretius's exposition, Cicero's attacks, and later doxography — all attributing the doctrine to Epicurus, none quoting him for it. The fragments of On Nature recovered from Herculaneum do show Epicurus arguing, in his own hand, that our practice of praising, blaming, and exhorting one another presupposes a causal principle in ourselves and not only in our original constitution and surroundings — the ethical need the swerve is usually said to serve — without, in the surviving fragments, naming the mechanism. The scholarly positions, represented by Long and Sedley and by the Stanford Encyclopedia's Epicurus entry, divide accordingly: that the swerve is genuinely Epicurus's, lost with the bulk of On Nature, is the majority view, supported by the unanimity and earliness of the attributions; that it was elaborated within the school after his death has been argued and cannot be excluded on present evidence; and what the swerve was supposed to explain — collisions, volition, or both, and how a random atomic event could constitute rather than merely permit responsible action — remains contested in detail. This article presents the doctrine as the tradition's doctrine, attributed to Epicurus by our sources, and flags the attribution as exactly that. Any account that quotes Epicurus stating the swerve is quoting a text that does not survive, and should not be trusted.

One further caution belongs here, because the swerve attracts modern over-claim as reliably as it attracted ancient ridicule. The swerve is not a prophecy of quantum indeterminacy, and Epicurus was not a physicist before his time in any sense that survives inspection: the doctrine is a targeted repair inside a specific ancient system, made to protect two things that system needed — the formation of compounds from a parallel fall, and the reality of voluntary action against Leucippus's charter of necessity (Section III). Read at its own scale, it is remarkable enough: the first attempt in Western thought to build indeterminacy into the foundations of nature for the sake of freedom. It does not need to be dressed as anything else.

XI. Lucretius: The System's Great Poem

Everything the ancient world knew of atomism after Epicurus, it might have known from summaries. That it knows it instead as a poem — seven thousand four hundred lines of Latin hexameter, the longest sustained philosophical argument ever set to verse — is the achievement of Titus Lucretius Carus (c. 99–c. 55 BCE), of whom we know almost nothing the poem does not tell us. The one contemporary notice is Cicero's, in a letter to his brother Quintus of February 54 BCE (in paraphrase): Lucretius's poems are as you write — full of natural genius, and of art too. The biographical tradition that later attached itself to him — that he was driven mad by a love potion, wrote in lucid intervals, and died by his own hand, with Cicero editing the poem posthumously — comes from Jerome, four centuries later, and is treated by modern scholarship as hostile legend, the more suspect for serving so neatly as a Christian moral on the wages of godlessness. The poem is addressed to a Memmius, a Roman politician of the poet's generation, and it states its method in its most famous image (Book 1, in paraphrase): as doctors smear the rim of a cup of bitter wormwood with honey, so that a child will drink the medicine unknowing, so the poet coats Epicurus's austere doctrine in verse — the honey on the rim, the physics the cure.

BookSubjectContents in brief
I Matter and void Nothing from nothing; nothing into nothing; atoms solid, indivisible, eternal; the minima; void; against the rival element theories of Heraclitus, Empedocles, and Anaxagoras; the infinity of the universe.
II Motion and combination The motes in the sunbeam; atomic motion; the swerve (216–293); shapes and their limits; the emergence of sensible qualities from insensible atoms; worlds without number; the decay of our world.
III The soul The mind and spirit as bodily compounds of the finest atoms; twenty-nine proofs, by the usual count, of the soul's mortality; death is nothing to us.
IV Perception and desire The eidōla — Lucretius's simulacra — and the mechanics of sight, hearing, taste, and thought; dreams; the physiology and the wound of love.
V The world and mankind The world's mortality and its formation without design; the history of life; the rise of human society, language, fire, metals, and religion — a Democritean anthropology in Epicurean dress.
VI The heavens and the earth Thunder, lightning, waterspouts, earthquakes, the Nile, magnets, and plague — every terrifying phenomenon given a material cause; the poem ends, without consolation, on the plague of Athens.

The poem's place in the atomist tradition is double, and both halves matter to this archive. Doctrinally, it is our fullest surviving exposition of Epicurean physics — fuller by far than Epicurus's own surviving letters, and the source from which the swerve, the minima in action, and the detailed mechanics of the simulacra are principally known. Long and Sedley print Lucretius as a primary witness alongside the letters, and Sections IX and X of this article rest on him accordingly. But the poem is not a transparent window onto its master. Lucretius writes two and a half centuries after Epicurus, in another language — he complains, famously and in paraphrase, of the poverty of Latin and the labour of coining a philosophical vocabulary for it — and with a poet's licences and a missionary's fire that the Garden's surviving prose does not share: his hymn to Epicurus as a god-like liberator, his savage attacks on religion (religio), and the poem's unresolved ending on the plague have no equivalents in the letters. The honest formulation is the one this article has used throughout: De Rerum Natura is the Epicurean system's great surviving poem, a primary source for the school's physics and a work of genius in its own right — and, like every witness in this article, to be read as a witness, with its date, language, and purposes in view.

XII. The Latin Transmission: How the Poem Survived

The poem very nearly did not survive at all, and the shape of its survival is the Latin counterpart of Section II's Greek evidence problem. De Rerum Natura was read in the first centuries BCE and CE — Virgil, Ovid, and the Augustan poets know it — and then, as Epicureanism faded and a materialist epic became first unfashionable and then suspect, copying dwindled to a thread. Modern textual criticism, founded as a discipline on this very poem by Karl Lachmann (edition of 1850), has shown that all surviving copies descend from a single lost late-antique archetype. Two ninth-century Carolingian manuscripts, both now at Leiden — the Codex Oblongus, copied around 800 and corrected in the circle of the Irish scholar Dungal, and the Codex Quadratus, of the later ninth century, with related fragments (the Schedae) at Copenhagen and Vienna — carry the text through the Middle Ages almost alone. From the archetype's own errors and displacements Lachmann could reconstruct even its physical form: a codex whose leaves, at one point in Book 4, had been bound in the wrong order, an accident faithfully reproduced by every descendant. The poem the modern world reads is, in the strictest sense, one damaged book, copied twice in Charlemagne's centuries and then, effectively, left alone.

Poggio Bracciolini, papal secretary and the age's most systematic hunter of lost Latin books, finds a copy of the poem in a German monastery — never named by him; Fulda is the usual conjecture — and has a transcript made and sent to Niccolò Niccoli in Florence. Poggio's own exemplar is lost; Niccoli's transcription becomes the parent of the fifty-odd Italian humanist copies that follow.

The editio princeps is printed at Brescia. Within two generations the poem is being copied by hand by Machiavelli, argued with by theologians, and mined by natural philosophers; within two centuries, through readers such as Gassendi — who rebuilt Epicurus for a Christian century — its atoms are on the road to Boyle, Newton, and Dalton.

A caution on the famous rediscovery, of the same kind this hub applies to every dramatic survival story. Poggio's find was real and consequential, but the poem had not been suppressed in any documented campaign, nor was his the only surviving copy: the two Leiden codices sat in the north throughout, unknown to the Italian tradition, and the Middle Ages' neglect of Lucretius is adequately explained by the ordinary economics of copying — a long, difficult, doctrinally unrewarding poem is copied rarely — without recourse to bonfires. The modern bestseller narrative that makes 1417 the hinge on which the modern world turned (Stephen Greenblatt's The Swerve, 2011) is a fine story built on a true event and a contestable thesis; the transmission facts above are what the manuscripts support, and they are dramatic enough without the hinge.

XIII. Why Atomism Lost the Ancient Argument

Atomism did not lose for lack of a hearing. Aristotle read Democritus with a respect he grants few predecessors — he is, the On Generation and Corruption remarks in paraphrase, nearly alone among the early thinkers in defining everything consistently and arguing from the nature of the subject — and then dismantled the system so thoroughly that his dismantling became the curriculum. The Epicurean school survived as an institution into the third century CE — the colossal inscription of Diogenes of Oinoanda, a retired citizen who around the second century CE had the Epicurean physics and ethics carved on a colonnade wall in his Lycian city for the salvation, as he says in paraphrase, of strangers and passers-by, is the tradition's last great monument — but the argument, in the schools where arguments were kept, had been settled against the atoms for a thousand years before Poggio. The settlement had three authors: Aristotle, the Stoics, and the theological consensus that followed both.

Aristotle: The Continuum Against the Atoms

Aristotle's case, developed across the Physics, On the Heavens, and On Generation and Corruption, and treated in full in this cluster's article on Aristotle's matter theory, rests on interlocking arguments, of which the decisive ones are these, in paraphrase. Against the void (Physics IV): a void — place with no body in it — is incoherent as a physical posit; in a void there could be no reason for a moving body to stop here rather than there, no natural places and no natural directions, and motion would be either impossible or absurdly unbounded, since speeds vary with the resistance of the medium and a medium of no resistance yields no finite ratios of speed. Against indivisibles (Physics VI): a continuum cannot be composed of indivisible parts. A line is not made of points — points are its limits, not its parts — and what holds for the line holds for magnitude, time, and motion: between any two moments there is a period, within any magnitude a further division. Indivisible atoms in contact raise the same difficulty in physical dress: if two atoms touch part to part, they have parts; if whole to whole, they coincide and no magnitude results. Against rearrangement as change (On Generation and Corruption I): the atomists reduce all change to association, dissociation, and rearrangement, and so cannot distinguish alteration — a thing genuinely becoming different — from mere reshuffling; nor can contact between partless solids explain mixture, growth, or rarefaction without remainder. And behind the technical arguments stands the systematic one: atomism explains by shape and impact alone, and therefore explains no purposes — the regular, end-directed structure of living things, which for Aristotle is the most obvious fact of nature, becomes in the atomist account an accident of the whirl. A physics that cannot say why the eye is for seeing had, by the standards of the school that defined physics for the next two millennia, failed at the subject's centre. The atomists had answers in embryo to several of these arguments — Epicurus's minima (Section IX) address the contact problem directly — but the answers lived in books that perished, while the arguments lived in Aristotle's, which did not.

The Stoics: A Plenum of Quality

The Stoics, atomism's Hellenistic contemporaries and rivals for the same students, built the anti-atomist physics into a complete alternative — the subject of this cluster's article on Stoic physics, to which the reader is referred for the full account. Its relevant features here are three. The cosmos is a plenum: there is no void within the world at all (void subsists, in Stoic doctrine, only outside the cosmos, as the incorporeal emptiness into which it breathes), and the world's continuity is guaranteed by pneuma — breath, a body of fire and air in a state of tension, pervading all matter and holding each thing together by its tensional state. Division is infinite: Diogenes Laertius (7.150–151) reports the doctrine in terms directly opposed to the atom — division proceeds to infinity, not because an infinitely small body is ever reached, but because the cutting never comes to rest. And mixture is total: in krasis, bodies blend through and through, each preserving its qualities while occupying the same place entire — a drop of wine, the Stoics' example, coextended with the whole sea. Every one of these doctrines is a pointed negation of an atomist one, and together they supply what atomism conspicuously lacked: a physics in which qualities are real — the hot and the cold, tension and sympathy, the pervasive rational order (logos) that makes the cosmos a single living animal and binds its parts in sympatheia. For a Mediterranean world that wanted its physics to support providence, divination, and a moralised nature, the Stoic plenum was simply more useful than the atomist void.

WHAT THE WINNERS OFFERED

Aristotle: form and purpose, a finite ordered cosmos, qualities as real principles, and a curriculum. The Stoics: providence, sympathy, a living cosmos, qualities as tensions of a real continuum. Platonism, which absorbed both: a craftsman god and an immortal soul. Each system let nature mean something.

WHAT ATOMISM OFFERED

Infinite worlds by accident; gods who do not intervene (Epicurus kept them, perfectly blessed and perfectly irrelevant, in the spaces between worlds); a mortal soul; no purposes anywhere; and, as its ethics, the management of one's own pleasures and fears in a universe that had not noticed one's existence. Magnificent, consistent — and, for institutions built on meaning, unusable.

The theological settlement completed what the schools began. To the Platonising consensus of late antiquity, pagan and then Christian, atomism's denials were not errors within physics but impieties: no creation, no providence, no judgement, no soul to judge. Epicurus's name became a byword — in rabbinic usage apikoros, from his name, is simply the word for a heretic — and the atomists' books, unprotected by any curriculum, underwent the fate Section XII traced for their greatest: they stopped being copied. It is worth stating the outcome without romance. Atomism lost the ancient argument on arguments — Aristotle's continuum and teleology were, within ancient assumptions, genuinely strong — and it lost the peace to institutions, which is a different contest. Its modern vindication, when it came, came on evidence no ancient disputant possessed: the kinetic theory of gases, the chemical atom of Dalton, the Brownian motion that made atoms visible in their effects in 1905. The ancients who rejected atoms were not fools; they were judges in a case where the decisive exhibits had not yet been found. But the case file, when the exhibits arrived, was found to have been kept — by Aristotle's refutations, by Diogenes's letters, and by one Latin poem — in remarkably good order.

XIV. The Productive Tension: The Name Alchemy Borrowed

Now the loop can be closed. Sometime in the Hellenistic centuries — the hub's Origins of Greek Alchemy gives the full account, whose positions this article follows — the name of Democritus began to gather to itself a literature he never wrote: works on the sympathies and antipathies of natural things, on things made by hand, on agriculture and stones, the compilation-tradition of Bolos of Mendes (fl. c. 200 BCE). Out of that tradition, in the form Matteo Martelli's edition dates to the second half of the first century CE, came the founding text of Greek alchemy: the Four Books of the alchemical pseudo-Democritus — on the dyeing of gold, of silver, of stones, of purple — surviving in epitome as the Physika kai Mystika, with its frame story of the master initiated by the Persian mage Ostanes in the temple at Memphis, its dead teacher, its books hidden in a temple column, and its whole doctrine gathered into the aphorism of the natures: nature delights in nature, nature conquers nature, nature masters nature. Every element of that sentence is a repudiation of the atomist's physics. And it is signed with the atomist's name.

Why the Name Was Worth Borrowing

The borrowing was not random, and the hub's origins article identifies three mutually reinforcing reasons, which this article endorses and extends from the atomist side. First, the traveller legend (Section VII): by the time the workshop literature needed an author, the doxography had already made Democritus the Greek who learned from Egyptian priests, Chaldaean astronomers, and Persian magi — the one classical philosopher whose biography, as antiquity told it, licensed temple recipes. The Memphis initiation of the Physika kai Mystika is not an invention imposed on a stranger; it is the legend's own scene, restaged with Ostanes — himself a stock figure of the same literature — in the teacher's role. Second, the economics of authority: in a manuscript culture of trade secrets, an anonymous recipe is a claim and a recipe by Democritus is an inheritance; the name protected, priced, and transmitted the text, and every later commentator — Zosimos, Synesius, the anonymous scholiasts — had his own authority mortgaged to it. Third, a real affinity, honestly noted: atomism makes material transformation thinkable as few ancient systems do. If copper and gold differ only in the arrangement of their constituents, then an art that rearranges is, in principle, imitating nature's own method — and later theorists who presented their doctrines as interpretations of Democritus were exploiting a genuine structural resemblance between the name they borrowed and the work they did. The resemblance is real. It is also, as the next paragraphs show, exactly where the resemblance ends.

The Real Distance

Set the two physics side by side, and the alchemical Democritus is not a development of the Abderite's doctrine but its inversion at every load-bearing point. The atomist's world contains no qualities: sweet, bitter, hot, cold, and colour exist by convention only, as events in a perceiver (Section VI, DK 68B9) — while the alchemical text works entirely in qualities: natures that delight in, conquer, and master one another, tinctures that carry goldenness as a real power from body to body, the whole vocabulary catalogued in this hub's Decknamen glossary. The atomist's causation is contact and necessity: rebound, interlocking, the whirl, nothing at random — while the Bolos tradition's causation is sympathy and antipathy, action between natures at a distance by kinship and enmity, the framework the atomist system has no place to put. The atomist's elements are infinite in kind and eternally themselves: an atom of one shape never becomes another, and no process transmutes the constituents of things — compounds form and dissolve, that is all — while the alchemical art exists to do precisely what atomism forbids at the fundamental level: to change one nature into another, to conquer copper's nature with gold's. Even the epistemology inverts. Democritus's genuine knowledge distrusts the senses and corrects them by reason; the recipe book's knowledge is initiatory and manual, validated at the bench and guarded as a secret — mystika, things to be kept hidden, in the title itself. The alchemical Democritus is not Democritus with additions. He is a different philosopher wearing the first one's biography.

// THE TENSION, STATED AS THE ARCHIVE HOLDS IT //

Alchemy's founding book travelled under the name of the one Greek physicist whose system cannot underwrite it. This is not an embarrassment to be explained away, in either direction: the alchemists were not failed atomists, and the atomist was not a proto-alchemist. What the borrowed name documents is how authority actually moved in the ancient book world — by reputation, by legend, by the needs of a craft for a pedigree — and it left the two traditions permanently entangled in the sources, so that disentangling them, figure by figure and claim by claim, is the founding discipline of this hub. The reader who wants the alchemical side of the entanglement in full should now go to the Origins of Greek Alchemy, where Bolos, the Physika kai Mystika, and the Ostanes frame are treated at the length they require; the atomist side is the article just read. Between them, the name is the whole story — and neither half is intelligible without the other.

XV. Reading Atomism Today — and the Loop Closed

The modern reader is better placed than any reader since Aristotle, and the reading order this article recommends follows the evidence's own hierarchy. Begin with the founders in the edition built for the purpose: Taylor's The Atomists (1999) gives the fragments and testimonia of Leucippus and Democritus in translation with the commentary that lets a reader weigh the witnesses — Aristotle against the doxographers, fragment against report — rather than merely receive a system. Kirk, Raven, and Schofield's The Presocratic Philosophers (2nd edition, 1983) supplies the wider frame: Parmenides and the Eleatics, against whom the whole doctrine is a reply. For Epicurus, read the letters themselves in Diogenes Laertius Book 10 — Hicks's Loeb translation or Bailey's Extant Remains — with Long and Sedley's The Hellenistic Philosophers (1987) at hand for the testimonia, the Herculaneum fragments, and the school's later development; their arrangement of the evidence for the swerve (Section X) is the model of how an honest dossier on a contested doctrine should look. For Lucretius, the poem entire, in Rouse's Loeb translation or in A. E. Stallings's verse translation (Penguin, 2007) for the poem as poem; the reader who reads only the swerve passage has read the doctrine and missed the work. And for the whole tradition's afterlife in the craft that borrowed its founder's name, return to this hub.

The loop, then, in one paragraph. Atomism began as an answer to Parmenides: being, multiplied to infinity and set moving in a void it should not have had, explaining a world of quality by a reality without any. It was rebuilt by Epicurus into a therapy, given weight, minima, and — by attribution at least — the swerve; sung entire by Lucretius into the one great poem of materialism; refuted by Aristotle, displaced by the Stoics' plenum of qualities, and filed by the schools under impiety, until a single damaged archetype and a papal secretary's sharp eyes carried its poem into the Renaissance and, eventually, into the ancestry of the modern atom. Its founder, meanwhile, led the livelier afterlife of the two: the traveller of the doxography, initiated in every temple of the East, became the authority under whose name the sympathies of Bolos and the dye-books of Roman Egypt founded an art his own physics — by convention sweet, by convention bitter; in reality atoms and void — cannot license and would not recognise. This cluster's other articles take up the physics alchemy actually used: the four elements as a system of real qualities, Aristotle's matter theory, which gave those qualities their philosophy, and Stoic physics, which gave them their cosmos of sympathy. Atomism stands among them as the road antiquity did not take — and as the name on the door of the road it did.

Cambridge: Cambridge University Press The backbone for the Epicurean material: the letters and testimonia in translation with commentary — weight, equal speed, limited shapes, minimal parts, and the dossier on the swerve's evidence, on which Sections IX–X rest.
Diogenes Laertius, Lives of Eminent Philosophers, Book 9 (Democritus) and Book 10 (Epicurus), trans. R. D. Hicks (Loeb Classical Library, 1925) The primary documentary source: Thrasyllus's catalogue of Democritus's works and the travel traditions (Book 9); the three letters of Epicurus and the Principal Doctrines, transcribed entire (Book 10). The Letter to Herodotus is paraphrased from Hicks throughout Sections VIII–IX.
Bailey, C. (1926) Epicurus: The Extant Remains. Oxford: Clarendon Press The letters, Principal Doctrines, and Vatican Sayings in translation with the Greek: the check on Hicks for the Letter to Herodotus, including the minima passages (§§56–59) used in Section IX.
Lucretius, De Rerum Natura, trans. W. H. D. Rouse, rev. M. F. Smith (Loeb Classical Library; Harvard University Press) The poem in the translation quoted at Section X (2.251–255, on the swerve and the decrees of fate) and followed for the book-by-book account in Section XI; the Rouse introduction for the poem's ancient reception.
Lucretius, On the Nature of Things, trans. A. E. Stallings (Penguin Classics, 2007) A modern verse translation consulted for the poem's movement and recommended in Section XV; not quoted in this article.
Aristotle, Physics IV and VI; On Generation and Corruption I; On the Heavens; Metaphysics A.4 (standard texts and translations) The earliest and fullest witness to the founders' system — the letter analogy (DK 67A6), the reasoning from motion to void (DK 67A7) — and, in the same works, the continuum, anti-void, and anti-indivisible arguments of Section XIII.
Theophrastus, On the Senses (De sensibus), in the standard editions and as excerpted in Taylor (1999) The detailed exposition of Democritus's perception theory and the taste-by-shape catalogue of Section V, with Theophrastus's own criticisms noted.
Sextus Empiricus, Against the Mathematicians VII; Galen, as excerpted in Taylor (1999) and Kirk–Raven–Schofield (1983) The transmitting witnesses for the verbatim fragments: DK 68B9 (the senses by convention), DK 68B11 (genuine and bastard knowledge), and DK 68B125 (the senses' reply to the mind), Sections II and VI.
Cicero, On Fate; On the Nature of the Gods I; Letters to Quintus 2.10 (standard translations) The hostile early testimony for the swerve (Section X) and the sole contemporary notice of Lucretius's poems (Section XI).
Taylor, C. C. W. (1999) “The Atomists”, in A. A. Long (ed.), The Cambridge Companion to Early Greek Philosophy. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press The modern synthesis followed for the Leucippus–Democritus relation, the Eleatic motivation, and the properties assigned to the atoms by theory (Sections III–IV).
Berryman, S., “Democritus”; Graham, D. W., “Leucippus”; Konstan, D., “Epicurus”; Sedley, D., “Lucretius”, in The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (consulted in full) The reference checks throughout: the DK 68B9 wording and its gloss, the weight controversy, the whirl and like-to-like, the swerve's absence from Epicurus's surviving writings, Jerome's Lucretius legend, and the book-by-book map of the poem.
Furley, D. J. (1967) Two Studies in the Greek Atomists. Princeton: Princeton University Press The classic discussion of the indivisibility problem — physical versus conceptual — flagged as open in Sections IV and IX.
Martelli, M. (2013) The Four Books of Pseudo-Democritus. Leiden: Brill (Sources and Studies in the History of Chemistry) The hub's authority, followed via the hub's greek-alchemy-origins article, for the three-figure distinction of Section I and the first-century CE dating of the alchemical Four Books in Section XIV.

Dates given without qualification in this article — Democritus c. 460–c. 370 BCE; Epicurus 341–270 BCE and the Garden founded 307 BCE; Lucretius c. 99–c. 55 BCE; Bolos flourishing c. 200 BCE; the alchemical Four Books in their surviving form in the second half of the first century CE (Martelli) — are the dates of the modern scholarly consensus as represented in the works above, and were checked against at least two of the listed sources each, except the pseudo-Democritus dating, which follows the hub's adjudicated position as stated in Section I.
