THE HOMERIC HYMNS

Delos · Delphi · Eleusis · Olympus · c. 700–400 BCE and after

// THIRTY-THREE HYMNS :: ONE NAME :: NO AUTHOR //

Thirty-three poems in epic hexameters, addressed to the gods, transmitted under the name of Homer and written by nobody we can name. Between the great epics and the lyric age they stand as the Greek world's other archaic scripture: the place where a god, rather than a hero, gets a birth, a character, and a life-story. This article treats the corpus as a corpus — its making, its narrow escape from loss, its four great narrative hymns and its twenty-nine shorter ones, and the editions in which a modern reader can hold it.

I. The Corpus: Thirty-Three Hymns Under One Name

The Homeric Hymns are a collection of thirty-three poems in dactylic hexameter, each addressed to a god or goddess, preserved together in the medieval manuscripts under the title Homērou hymnoi — “Homer's hymns.” They range in length from three lines to five hundred and eighty. Four of them — to Demeter, to Apollo, to Hermes, to Aphrodite — are substantial narrative poems, among the most important mythological texts in Greek. One, to Dionysos, survives only as the tail of a poem whose body is lost. The remaining twenty-eight are short pieces, most of them under sixty lines, several under ten: invocations, portraits, miniature myths, festival pieces. Taken together the thirty-three run to some 2,345 lines in the standard numeration — roughly a fifth of the Odyssey — and they were composed, on the arguments set out in Section X, across several centuries, by many hands, in many places. The corpus is an anthology, not a work.

The first discipline this article observes is the one its title already enforces. “Homeric” is an ancient classification, not an authorship claim. The hymns are Homeric in the sense A. W. Verrall put in 1894, in the remark Nicholas Richardson's standard commentary still quotes: the collection is Homeric in the only useful sense the word can bear — it has come down labelled “Homer” from the earliest times of Greek book-literature. The poems share with the Iliad and Odyssey their metre, their artificial epic dialect, and much of their formulaic stock; on that kinship see The Homeric Epics, Sections IV–V. Antiquity, which attributed to Homer everything written in that manner — the Epic Cycle, the comic Margites, the mock-heroic Battle of Frogs and Mice — attributed the hymns to him too, and the earliest surviving reference to any of them, Thucydides in the fifth century BCE, quotes one as Homer's without a flicker of doubt (Section II). No modern scholar defends the attribution. The hymns are anonymous, and their anonymity is not a gap in our knowledge to be apologised for: it is the normal condition of archaic Greek song, in which the tradition, not the individual, was the author of record.

What kind of thing is a “hymn” here? The Greek word hymnos in its archaic use means a song of praise addressed to a god — as distinct from the epic narrative whose subject is heroes, and from the later literary hymns of Callimachus, which are book-poems written for readers. The Homeric hymns stand at the head of the Greek hymn tradition and are built, in their developed form, on a three-part frame that the handbooks standardly describe. First an invocation: the singer calls on the Muse or names the god, often with the god's epithets, birthplace, and parentage — the god's identifying dossier, compressed into the opening lines. Then a central section of praise, which in the long hymns expands into full narrative: the god's birth, deeds, acquisitions, and the winning of his or her province and honours. Then a close: a farewell to the god — chaire, “hail, rejoice” — a prayer for favour or victory in the contest, and, characteristically, a promise to pass on to another song. That closing promise is the corpus's signature, and Section II takes it up: it is the fossil of the hymns' original function.

THE LONG HYMNS

Four narrative poems: to Hermes (580 lines), Apollo (546), Demeter (495), and Aphrodite (293) — preceded in the collection by the fragment of a fifth, to Dionysos, which must once have been of comparable scale. These are treated one by one in Sections IV–VII.

THE SHORT HYMNS

Twenty-eight pieces from 3 to 59 lines: second and third hymns to gods already honoured at length (Dionysos three times in all, Aphrodite three times, Hermes, Artemis, Athena, Hestia, the Dioscuri twice each), and single short hymns to gods the long poems pass by — Ares, Pan, Helios, Selene, Earth. Surveyed, with the full table of the thirty-three, in Section VIII.

WHAT THE CORPUS IS NOT

Not a liturgy. The hymns were not, so far as the evidence shows, the service-book of any cult: they are festival and contest pieces, sung by professional singers, and their relation to actual worship is the indirect one examined in Section IX. Literary myth and cult practice are kept distinct throughout this article, as throughout this hub.

THE THIRTY-FOURTH PIECE

The manuscripts append to the hymns a short piece To Hosts (Eis xenous), four lines reminding the hearer that hospitality is a sacred duty watched by Zeus. It is not a hymn and is not counted among the thirty-three; editors print it as an epigram of the collection. Its presence is itself evidence — a professional singer's pointed parting word to the household that was about to feed him.

The corpus's position in this hub should be stated plainly. Elsewhere the hymns appear as witnesses raided for a story: the Mysteries article draws on the Hymn to Demeter for the charter myth of Eleusis, the Olympians article on several hymns for the gods' portraits, and Descents on the Demeter hymn's geography of the underworld. This article is the corpus's own home: the hymns treated, as the epics are treated in their article, as texts — objects with a manufacture, a transmission that nearly failed, and an editorial history. Where a hymn's cult meaning is the point, the article says so and sends the reader to the article that treats it; the textual treatment stays here.

II. Prooimia: Preludes Sung Before Epic

The single most important ancient statement about the hymns is also the earliest reference to any of them. In the winter of 426/5 BCE the Athenians purified Delos and refounded its festival, and Thucydides, describing the old Ionian gathering on the island, proves its antiquity from Homer (History 3.104). He quotes two passages of what we call the Hymn to Apollo — the Ionians in their long robes assembling for boxing, dance, and song (Hymn 3.146–150), and the praise of the Delian maidens' choir ending in the singer's self-portrait as the blind man of Chios (3.165–172) — and he introduces the first quotation with a generic label: the verses, he says, are “from the prooimion to Apollo” (ek prooimiou Apollōnos). Prooimion means a prelude: an opening piece sung before the main performance. Thucydides, our first witness, thus tells us three things at once — that the hymn circulated under Homer's name, that it was already old and authoritative in the fifth century, and that its kind was understood to be the prelude kind.

“The blind old man of Chios' rocky isle.” — THE SINGER'S SELF-PORTRAIT, HYMN TO APOLLO 172, AS QUOTED BY THUCYDIDES 3.104, TRANS. BENJAMIN JOWETT

The prelude function explains the corpus's most distinctive formal feature. Hymn after hymn ends with the same gesture: the singer bids the god farewell, asks for favour, and announces the transition — in paraphrase, “and I will remember you, and another song too.” The closing line of the Hymn to Demeter (495), of the Hymn to Apollo (546), and of the Hymn to Hermes (580) is that formula, and the Hymn to Aphrodite closes more explicitly still: having begun from the goddess, the singer will pass on to another song. Read as literature these endings are abrupt. Read as the working parts of a performance they are exact: the hymn was the singer's opening number, sung to the god of the festival or of the singer's choosing, after which the rhapsode passed to the main business of the day — the recitation of epic, his own or the tradition's, the Iliad and Odyssey and the other poems treated in The Homeric Epics. The god is honoured first, the audience warmed, the singer's voice and credentials displayed — the blind man of Chios passage is precisely a credential, a request to be remembered — and then the epic begins.

Two cautions belong here, and the scholarship insists on both. First, the prelude theory fits the short hymns best: a five-line invocation to Athena is a plausible curtain-raiser, while a 580-line comic epic about Hermes is a strange warm-up for anything. The long hymns are better understood as festival and contest pieces in their own right — the main event of a rhapsode's appearance at a god's festival, competing for a prize — which grew out of the prelude form and kept its frame. Some scholars have accordingly proposed that parts of the short-hymn stock are excerpts: the invocation and close of longer hymns, cut down to prelude length by singers who needed the frame without the narrative. That is a hypothesis, labelled as such; it cannot be proved for any individual piece, but it explains the corpus's odd shape — many frames, few full bodies — better than any alternative. Second, the word prooimion in Thucydides is applied to one hymn; extending it to the whole collection, as Martin West does in the introduction to his Loeb edition — “the so-called Homeric Hymns are a collection of thirty-three such prooimia”, in paraphrase of his formulation — is an inference from the closing formulae and the genre's shape, not a quotation of ancient testimony. The inference is strong and standardly made; it remains an inference.

Rhapsodes and the Homeridae

The singers who performed such pieces were rhapsodes: professionals who recited epic verse at festivals and contests, staff in hand, in competition for prizes — the class of performer described, from the texts' side, in The Homeric Epics, Section X. Around the name of Homer there had formed, by the sixth century BCE, a guild or clan of such performers, the Homeridae of Chios, who claimed descent from the poet and custody of his works. The guild matters to this corpus because one hymn, the Hymn to Apollo, is attached by an ancient source to a named member of it — Cynaethus of Chios — and because the hymn's own blind singer of Chios was read by antiquity as Homer himself, performing inside the Homeridae's home tradition. Section V takes up that tangle. Here it is enough to register the institutional fact: the hymns survive because a profession sang them, and the profession had a corporate interest in the name under which they travelled.

Hesiod supplies the complementary picture from the singer's side. The proem of the Theogony — treated as a text in Theogony and the Succession Myth — is itself a hymn, to the Muses, over a hundred lines long, standing before a didactic epic exactly as a prooimion stands before a recitation; and the Works and Days opens with a shorter hymn to Zeus. The boundary between “hymn” and “epic” in archaic practice was thus a seam inside a performance, not a wall between kinds. The Homeric Hymns are what that seam looks like when the preludes are gathered up, written down, and preserved as a book.

III. Transmission: A Narrow Survival

The Iliad survives in hundreds of manuscripts and a blizzard of papyri; it was the schoolbook of the Greek world, and its transmission, traced in The Homeric Epics, Sections XI–XIII, is correspondingly broad and deep. The hymns had no such luck. They were nobody's schoolbook. They travelled as an appendix to Homer — copied in the company of the epics, the Battle of Frogs and Mice, the epigrams, and the fragments of Hesiod — and their whole medieval tradition hangs on a thread that this section follows from the archetype to the printed page. The account here follows the stemma as set out in the introduction to M. L. West's Loeb edition (2003), checked against the Allen–Halliday–Sikes commentary tradition.

The Archetype and Its Two Branches

All surviving manuscripts of the hymns are fifteenth-century copies, and all descend from a lost archetype whose text already bore the collection in its present order — an order that is itself a fact of the transmission, not of the poems' composition: somebody, at some date we cannot fix, gathered these thirty-three pieces into this sequence, and every copy we have inherits that act of gathering. From the archetype the tradition divides. On one side stands a lost hyparchetype, conventionally sigled Ψ (psi), probably of the twelfth or thirteenth century, from which twenty-eight manuscripts descend in three families. On the other side stands a single manuscript, sigled M, copied in the fifteenth century by the scholar-scribe Ioannes Eugenikos, probably in Constantinople sometime after 1439. Every other manuscript begins the collection at Hymn 3, to Apollo. M alone begins at Hymn 1.

// THE 1777 DISCOVERY //

M's modern history is the most famous accident in the hymns' survival. The codex reached Moscow — how, is not securely documented — and there, in 1777, the German philologist Christian Friedrich Matthaei found it in the imperial archive; a later anecdote, repeated in the scholarship with varying confidence, places it in a barn among farm animals, and the anecdote is given here as anecdote, not as record. Matthaei's transcription reached David Ruhnken, whose editions of the Hymn to Demeter appeared in 1780 and, in the form that held, 1782. The codex itself later came to Leiden, where it now rests as Universiteitsbibliotheek BPG 33 H — the Codex Leidensis that was the Codex Mosquensis. The two names in the literature denote one book.

What M uniquely preserves is the head of the collection. Its first hymn, to Dionysos, it preserves only in ruins: a gathering — a quinion, in West's account — had fallen out of the codex before it was copied or found, carrying away the body of the Dionysos hymn, so that only its last twenty-one lines stand on the page where the second hymn begins. And the second hymn is the Hymn to Demeter: 495 lines of major archaic poetry that survive in exactly one manuscript, unknown to the entire tradition — to antiquity after a point, to Byzantium, to the Renaissance, to the first printers — until 1777. The editio princeps of Homer, printed at Florence in 1488 by Demetrios Chalkokondyles, included the hymns as the Ψ tradition had them: beginning at Apollo. For nearly three centuries of print, the corpus that scholarship studied lacked its first two members. The Demeter hymn's arrival in 1782 was an event in European letters — it fed the Romantic rediscovery of Greek religion — and it rests, textually, on one fifteenth-century book and the care of its one modern finder.

Damage Inside the Survivor

Even M is not intact. One of its leaves — folio 35, in the modern study of the codex by Oliver Thomas (Mnemosyne 68, 2015) — was already damaged in antiquity's aftermath: part of the leaf was lost, taking with it stretches of the Demeter hymn around lines 387–404, 437, and 462–479, and a later reader pasted in a repair sheet and inked in replacement text, partly over the original scribe's letters. Editors distinguish that repairing hand (siglum m) from Eugenikos' own, and treat its supplements as what they are — an early reader's conjectures, some formulaic and plausible, none authoritative. The consequence deserves emphasis, because it is the kind of fact this hub exists to keep visible: at several points in one of the most-read religious poems of archaic Greece, the text in every modern edition is a repair, and the apparatus, not the page, is where the poem's true state can be seen. Lines are also missing or corrupt elsewhere in the hymn; Richardson's commentary (Section XI) marks the lacunae, and honest translations mark them too.

The rest of the transmission is quieter. The Ψ families transmit Hymns 3–33 with the ordinary quota of scribal error and a thin scholia tradition — nothing like the Alexandrian commentary mass that attended the epics, for the hymns were never edited by Aristarchus' school with the same intensity, and no ancient commentary on them survives whole. What antiquity quoted, it quoted piecemeal: Thucydides' two passages of the Apollo hymn (Section II) are the prize exhibit, valuable precisely because they let editors check the medieval text of those lines against a fifth-century BCE witness — and the check shows small divergences, the ordinary drift of a text copied for a thousand years. The hymns' survival, in sum, is the opposite of the epics': narrow where theirs is broad, late where theirs is anciently attested, and at its head dependent on a single book found once, in one archive, in 1777.

IV. The Hymn to Demeter as Text

The Hymn to Demeter (Hymn 2, 495 lines) is the collection's second poem and its most consequential survival. This hub's Mysteries article treats what the poem founded — its standing as the charter myth of Eleusis, the kykeon, Iambe's jests, the closing blessing on the initiated — and that cult reading is expressly left there. This section treats the poem as a made object: its date as argued, its architecture as built, and the underworld geography it assumes, with the narrative given in the summary the textual argument needs and no more.

The Date, as Argued

The hymn is conventionally dated to the late seventh or the sixth century BCE. The arguments, stated as arguments: its language and formulaic stock are of the same stratum as the epics' later layers and Hesiod's, which sets a broad archaic frame; it knows Eleusis as a place of local princes rather than as an Athenian state sanctuary, which most scholars take to precede, or to ignore, Athens' absorption of the cult — an argument from silence, and labelled as such whenever it is used; and it shows no trace of the Orphic and later theological developments that a Hellenistic pastiche would be expected to betray. N. J. Richardson's commentary (Oxford, 1974), the standard modern treatment, places the poem in that late-seventh-to-sixth-century window, and Richard Janko's statistical study of epic diction (Homer, Hesiod and the Hymns, 1982) put the Demeter hymn earliest among the four long hymns on his linguistic measures. The window is a consensus range, not a date; Section X returns to the method and its limits.

The Architecture: Four Movements

The poem is built in four movements, and its architecture is the tightest of any long hymn — a story of a loss that empties heaven and earth, resolved only when the cosmos renegotiates the terms. It opens in mid-catastrophe: the proem names Demeter and her daughter in a single sentence that already contains the crime.

“I begin to sing of rich-haired Demeter, awful goddess — of her and her trim-ankled daughter whom Aidoneus rapt away, given to him by all-seeing Zeus the loud-thunderer.” — HOMERIC HYMN TO DEMETER 1–3, TRANS. H. G. EVELYN-WHITE (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY, 1914)

First movement: the rape and the search (roughly lines 1–87). Persephone, gathering flowers with the daughters of Ocean — the narcissus, grown by Earth as a snare at Zeus's will, is the trap's centrepiece — is seized by Hades in his chariot as the earth splits open. Her cry reaches her mother. Demeter tears her headdress, casts off her dark veil, and searches land and sea for nine days, torches in her hands, tasting neither ambrosia nor nectar, never washing. Hecate, who heard the cry but saw nothing, meets her at dawn of the tenth day; together they go to Helios, who sees all things, and Helios tells the truth whole: Zeus gave the girl to his brother. The movement's engine is knowledge withheld and then granted — the poem's gods know, and the mother must find out.

Second movement: Eleusis (roughly lines 87–302). Demeter, in anger at Zeus, withdraws from Olympus and wanders in disguise — an old woman past childbearing — to Eleusis, where she is taken into the house of Celeus as nurse to the infant Demophon. The episode's centre is the failed immortalising: anointed with ambrosia by day, hidden in the fire by night, the child is being burned free of mortality when his mother Metaneira spies and cries out, and the working breaks. The goddess stands revealed in radiance, rebukes the household's folly, and commands a temple and altar below the citadel, promising to teach her rites. As narrative this is the poem's human panel — grief, service, a mother's error answering a mother's loss — and as foundation it is the charter the Mysteries article expounds; here it is enough to mark its structural place: the goddess, robbed of her daughter, nearly makes a mortal child a god, and the failure turns her grief outward into institution.

Third movement: the famine and the embassy (roughly lines 302–404). Seated in her new temple, Demeter makes a year “most terrible”: the earth will not sprout, the plough is dragged in vain, and mankind starves — which threatens the gods themselves, whose honours are fed by mortal sacrifice. The leverage is the poem's theology in one mechanism: the grain-goddess's grief is a strike that empties heaven's altars. Zeus sends Iris; Demeter refuses. He sends the whole company of the gods with gifts; she refuses them all, vowing never to let fruit rise until she sees her daughter. Zeus yields the only way left: Hermes goes down to Hades to bring Persephone up. Hades consents — and, smiling, gives his queen a pomegranate seed to eat, so that she cannot stay above forever. The underworld of this movement is the traditional house of Hades below the earth, reached by the messenger god and left by chariot; for the geography of Greek descents as a system, see Descents, where this hymn's version takes its place among the katabasis texts.

Fourth movement: the settlement and the foundation (roughly lines 404–495). Mother and daughter meet in a recognition scene the poem plays at full emotional stretch — Persephone springs from the chariot, Demeter runs to her “like a maenad down the mountain,” in paraphrase of the simile — and then, in the embrace, asks the question on which the world turns: did you eat anything below? The pomegranate confessed, Zeus's settlement arrives by Rhea, the grandmothers' envoy: for a third part of the year Persephone will go down to darkness, for two parts she will stay above with her mother and the gods. Demeter restores the fruit to the Rhaian plain, teaches her mysteries to the Eleusinian princes — Triptolemus, Diocles, Eumolpus, Celeus are named — and returns to Olympus. The closing lines bless the mortal who has seen the rites and darken the lot of the uninitiated after death, and the singer signs off with the corpus's formula: he will remember the goddess, and another song too.

A HYMN TO TWO GODDESSES

West's introduction makes the point editors now standardly repeat: the poem is in substance a hymn to Demeter and Persephone, the presiding pair of Eleusis, and its narrative is the foundation myth of their Mysteries. The daughter is not a subplot; the poem's subject is the bond the rape broke and the settlement re-made on new terms.

THE POMEGRANATE AS MECHANISM

The seed is the plot's legal instrument: food eaten in the house of the dead binds the eater to the house. The poem states the rule through its working, not as doctrine — Persephone herself reports that Hades “secretly put in my mouth pomegranate-food, honey-sweet, and compelled me against my will,” in paraphrase — and the settlement's thirds of the year are its consequence.

WHAT THE POEM DOES NOT SAY

It never describes the rites it founds. The Mysteries' content was secret, and the hymn — a public, festival poem — keeps the secret perfectly: it gives the myth, the temple, the teachers, and the blessing, and of the drōmena themselves, nothing. The distinction between the charter myth, which is literature, and the cult practice, which is not recoverable from it, is the Mysteries article's standing caution, repeated here because this poem is where readers most often blur it.

A TEXT WITH HOLES

The poem we read is M's poem, repaired: the damaged folio (Section III) cuts into the reunion movement itself, and the supplements of the repairing hand stand in the text of lines around 387–404, 437, and 462–479. Where a translation prints those lines smooth, an editor has chosen the repair; the Greek page, honestly read, has gaps.

V. The Hymn to Apollo: Two Hymns Joined at a Seam

The Hymn to Apollo (Hymn 3, 546 lines) is the longest of the hymns after Hermes, the earliest attested (Section II), and the only one whose composite nature is visible to the naked eye. It falls into two halves that differ in subject, in geography, in the god they celebrate, and in the audience they address — and between them, at line 179, there is a join that scholarship since Ruhnken has treated as the seam where two hymns were stitched into one. The Allen–Sikes commentary's judgement, paraphrased, states the consensus the subsequent literature has refined but not overturned: the hymn in its present form reads as continuous, but the continuity lies only on the surface, and the abrupt transition at verse 179 cannot be missed.

The Delian Half (Lines 1–178)

The first half celebrates Apollo of Delos: the god of the island festival, of the bow, of music and the assembled Ionians. It opens with a theophany in the house of Zeus — Apollo enters Olympus with his bow, and the gods tremble and rise from their seats until Leto unstrings the weapon and seats her son — then turns to the birth. Leto, pregnant by Zeus and hounded by Hera's jealousy, wanders the Aegean seeking a land that will receive her son; the lands refuse, fearing the proud god who will scorn them. Little Delos, poor and rocky, accepts — after bargaining, in one of the hymn's most human passages, for a temple and honours, and exacting from Leto a great oath that Apollo will honour her above all islands. The birth follows: Hera detains Eileithyia, the birth-goddess, until Iris fetches her with promise of a golden necklace; the god is born, the goddesses wash and swaddle him, and — in the detail every account keeps — he takes not the breast but nectar and ambrosia from Themis, bursts his swaddling bands, and speaks at once, claiming the lyre, the bow, and the declaring of Zeus's will to men. The half closes with the Delian festival itself — the Ionians assembled, the maidens' choir that can mimic every voice — and the singer's farewell and self-portrait, the blind man of rocky Chios, quoted by Thucydides and quoted in Section II above. Then, at line 178, the singer takes leave. The poem has ended.

The Pythian Half (Lines 179–546)

Except that it begins again. Line 179 opens a second invocation — “How shall I hymn you who are so well hymned?” in paraphrase — and a second subject: Apollo of Delphi, the oracular god, and the founding of his shrine. The god strides from Olympus seeking a site for his oracle, passing the cities and springs of Boeotia; at Telphusa's spring he chooses his place and lays foundations, but the nymph, jealous for her own honours, deceives him into moving on to a wilder site below Parnassus. There he kills the monstrous she-dragon that ravaged the district — the serpent later tradition names Python, whose rotting (pythesthai) in the sun gives Pytho its name in the hymn's own etymology — and returns to punish Telphusa, stopping her spring with rocks. Then, lacking priests, he provides them by piracy of a benevolent kind: taking the shape of a dolphin — the delphis from which the hymn derives Delphinios and Delphi — he leaps aboard a Cretan merchant ship, terrifies the crew by his transformations and the ship's miraculous sailing, and brings them round to Crisa, Delphi's port, where he reveals himself and appoints them his ministers: they are to guard his temple, speak righteousness, and live, as he dryly promises, on the sacrifices of others. The hymn ends with his charge to them and the closing formula. Birth and choir have given way to foundation, dragon-slaying, and the institution of a priesthood: a different poem, for a different sanctuary, with a different Apollo.

TWO GODS, ONE NAME

The Delian Apollo is the Ionian festival god, archer and musician, born on an island sanctuary shared by a sea-going confederacy. The Pythian Apollo is the mainland oracular god, dragon-slayer and founder, whose priests are landsmen imported by sea. The hymn that joins them is a theological act: one Apollo, both shrines, each half validating the other.

THE SEAM AT 179

The Delian half ends with a complete close — farewell to the god, the singer's sphragis, a prayer — and the Pythian half begins with a fresh invocation and a new question. Two closes and two openings in one poem are the formal signature of the join. Editors since Ruhnken print the division; the manuscripts know nothing of it.

WHO JOINED THEM, AND WHEN

Thucydides already knew the Delian passages as part of a hymn to Apollo in the fifth century BCE, and the Pythian half is certainly much older than Thucydides; the Allen–Sikes argument, paraphrased here, infers that the unity of the document extends back at least to the end of the fifth century. The join is therefore archaic or early classical, not a Byzantine editor's work.

THE BLIND MAN OF CHIOS

The sphragis at lines 165–172 — the singer asking the Delian maidens to remember him and to answer any future stranger that the sweetest singer is the blind man of Chios — was read by antiquity as Homer's self-portrait, and it is one of the root-texts of the blind-Homer tradition. Modern scholarship treats it as a rhapsode's signature within the Homeridae's tradition (below), not as biography of the epics' author.

Cynaethus of Chios

One ancient source names an author. A scholion on Pindar's second Nemean ode reports that the school of Cynaethus of Chios was prominent among the Homeridae and put out many compositions under Homer's name, and that Cynaethus himself composed the hymn to Apollo; the same notice dates his first recitation of the Homeric poems at Syracuse to the sixty-ninth Olympiad, 504–501 BCE. The notice is late, scholiastic, and uncorroborated, and this article reports it as such — a tradition, not a document. But it has proved unusually productive. Walter Burkert's well-known argument (in Arktouros, 1979) proposed that the combined hymn was made for a datable occasion: the double festival of Apollo held by Polycrates of Samos in 522 BCE, honouring Delian and Pythian Apollo together — precisely the two Apollos of the hymn's two halves — and that a Chian Homerid, Cynaethus, was the combining hand, joining a Delian hymn to a Pythian one for the festival that needed both. The proposal elegantly explains the join, the date fits the scholion's floruit within a generation, and M. L. West has argued in support of Cynaethus' authorship of the combined hymn. It remains a proposal: other scholars date the Delian half a century or more earlier and treat the join as anonymous and gradual. What is not in dispute is the shape of the evidence — two hymns, one seam, an archaic joiner, and a Chian guild in whose keeping the Homer-name travelled.

VI. The Hymn to Hermes: The Divine Comedy

The Hymn to Hermes (Hymn 4, 580 lines) is the longest of the hymns and the funniest poem in archaic Greek. Its subject is the youngest Olympian's first day of life, and its tone — reverent toward no one, delighted with its hero's criminal brilliance — makes it the corpus's standing proof that a hymn need not be solemn to be a hymn. Hermes is born at dawn in a cave on Mount Cyllene in Arcadia, to Zeus and the nymph Maia, a shy goddess living apart from the gods' company. By evening of the same day he has invented the lyre, stolen his brother Apollo's cattle, invented fire-making, sacrificed, lied to Apollo, been hauled before Zeus, and settled the affair in an exchange of gifts that fixes both brothers' provinces forever. The hymn is a biography as negotiation: a god with no honours acquires them, by theft, music, and charm, in twenty-four hours.

The Tortoise and the Lyre

The newborn god's first act is an invention. Stepping over the threshold of the cave — he does not stay in his cradle past the poem's first movement — Hermes finds a tortoise, and greets it, in paraphrase, with the poem's first joke: alive, it is a charm against witchcraft; dead, it will sing beautifully. He cuts off its limbs, scoops the shell, stretches ox-hide over it, fits horns and a crossbar, and strings it with seven sheep-gut strings — the first lyre, made between morning and noon by a god a few hours old. The episode is told with a craftsman's relish, and it is the hymn's credential scene: before Hermes steals anything, he makes something, and the thing he makes is the instrument of Greek song itself. The thief enters the story as a culture-bringer; the cattle raid that follows is the second half of a double debut.

The Cattle Theft

At evening Hermes goes to Pieria, where Apollo's divine cattle graze, and cuts out fifty of them. The theft is engineered like a trick in a folktale, and the hymn lingers on the engineering: he drives the herd backward, so their tracks point the wrong way; he fashions for himself huge sandals of woven tamarisk and myrtle boughs, which leave prints no tracker can read; and at the Alpheus he stops, kindles fire with fire-sticks — the poem credits him with the invention — slaughters two of the cattle, roasts the meat, and divides it into twelve portions, a sacrifice to the twelve gods among whom the newborn, pointedly, counts himself. Though the savour torments him, being a god he does not eat. He slips home through the keyhole in the likeness of mist, gets into his cradle, wraps himself in his swaddling clothes, and lies there holding his lyre, in the poem's portrait of innocence, when his mother — who has seen everything — reproaches him, and he answers her with the programme of his career: he will not stay in the cave in obscurity; he will have honours among the gods, by Apollo's wealth if it comes to that, or by plain robbery.

Apollo's Laughter and the Exchange

Apollo traces the herd — an old man who saw the infant driving cattle backward gives him the clue, and bird-omens confirm it — and storms into the cave, threatening the baby with Tartarus. Hermes, from the cradle, denies everything with an oath by his father's head: he was born yesterday, his feet are soft, he knows nothing of cattle. Apollo laughs, picks him up, and carries him to Olympus for judgement; before Zeus, Hermes delivers a defence of such brazen, polished falsehood that Zeus himself laughs and orders the boy to show where the cattle are hidden. The case is settled not by verdict but by music. At the hiding-place Hermes takes up the lyre and sings — a theogony in miniature, the gods in their order, his own birth among them — and Apollo is seized with helpless laughter and desire for the instrument: the cattle, all fifty, are cheap at the price. The exchange that closes the hymn apportions the brothers' worlds. Apollo takes the lyre and gives Hermes the herdsman's whip and the office of keeper of herds, the golden three-branched wand of wealth — the caduceus — and appointment as the gods' messenger; prophecy he keeps for himself, though he tells Hermes of the Thriai, the bee-maidens whose divination is a lesser, conditional gift. Hermes swears the great oath never to steal Apollo's possessions again — and becomes, in the settlement's fine print, the god of thieves by office rather than by practice. The hymn ends in concord: the brothers reconciled, the provinces divided, the youngest god installed.

// READING THE COMEDY STRAIGHT //

The comedy is the theology. In the hymns' world a god's timai — his honours, functions, and perquisites — are real property, won and registered; the Theogony apportions them by Zeus's decree after the Titanomachy, but Hermes, born too late for that settlement, must acquire his by transaction. The hymn tells, in the only register that fits its hero, how the pantheon's table of offices got its last and slipperiest entry: theft creates the dispute, music prices it, and the exchange ratifies it. Later readers who found the poem scandalous were reading it correctly and missing the point simultaneously — it is a foundation charter written as a caper.

A note on the poem's texture, since the hub's method requires keeping the registers distinct. The Hermes hymn is the most self-consciously literary of the long hymns: it knows the Iliad and Odyssey and plays against them, its diction is laced with words found nowhere else in epic, and its date has accordingly been argued later than the others' — Section X sets out the range, from the later sixth century into the fifth. None of that literariness makes it less a hymn. It makes it the clearest case in the corpus of the genre's double nature: a festival piece for a god's honour that is also, line by line, a poet's performance for an audience that knew its Homer by heart.

VII. The Hymn to Aphrodite: The Goddess Humbled, Aeneas Foretold

The Hymn to Aphrodite (Hymn 5, 293 lines) is the subtlest of the long hymns, and its premise is a reversal: the goddess whose power is desire is subjected to it. The proem defines her province with a lawyer's care — Aphrodite stirs desire in gods, mortals, birds, and beasts, and all are subject to her works — and then names the three exceptions, the virgin goddesses Athena, Artemis, and Hestia, whose hearts she cannot touch. Zeus, wearied of her boasting that she mates gods with mortal women, turns her own weapon on her: he casts into her heart a sweet longing for a mortal man, Anchises , a herdsman of the Dardanian royal house grazing his cattle on Mount Ida. The hymn is the story of the goddess's humbling — and, folded inside it, the charter of a dynasty.

Aphrodite adorns herself on Cyprus, in her temple at Paphos, where the Graces bathe and anoint her, and comes to Ida in beauty that makes the wolves and lions fawn and mate around her. To Anchises she tells a false tale — the hymn's centrepiece of dramatic irony — claiming to be a mortal princess, a Phrygian king's daughter, snatched from the dance by Hermes and carried over the fields to be Anchises' bride, her parents to be honoured with gifts in due form. Anchises, suspecting even as he yields that no mortal woman has such beauty, takes her to his bed. She wakes him afterward standing revealed in full divinity, her head reaching the roof-beams, and Anchises covers his face in terror: no man who lies with a goddess, he says, keeps his strength. Aphrodite comforts him — and then delivers the prophecy for which the hymn exists.

“You shall have a dear son who shall reign among the Trojans, and children's children after him, springing up continually.” — HOMERIC HYMN TO APHRODITE 196–197, TRANS. H. G. EVELYN-WHITE (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY, 1914)

The son is Aeneas — his name, the goddess explains, from the awful grief (ainon achos) his begetting has cost her. She will take the child to the nymphs of the mountain to be reared and bring him to Anchises at five years old; Anchises is to say the mother was a nymph, and — the condition on which his life depends — never to boast that he lay with Aphrodite. Around the prophecy the hymn sets two cautionary panels, Ganymede and Tithonus, mortals taken up for divine love: Ganymede made immortal, a grief to his father that Zeus assuaged with gifts; Tithonus granted immortality without youth, dwindling into endless, helpless age. The panels are the hymn's moral arithmetic: Aeneas' line will be, the goddess says, “very near to the gods” in paraphrase, generation after generation — but Anchises himself will get no apotheosis. The poem closes with her warning renewed and the singer's transition formula.

A Greek Hymn with Rome Inside It

The prophecy deserves its own paragraph, because of where it leads. A Greek hymn, composed for Greek festivals centuries before Rome mattered, promises that Aeneas' descendants will rule among the Trojans continually. The Iliad itself knows the same tradition — Poseidon, rescuing Aeneas in Book 20, declares that Aeneas' line will rule the Trojans when Priam's is extinguished — and the hymn shows the tradition already fixed in the archaic period: Aeneas survives the war, and his house endures. What Rome later did with that promise is another people's reception: the Julian family's descent from Aeneas and Venus, and Virgil's Aeneid, which takes the hymn's dynastic seed and grows from it the charter epic of an empire. That Roman development is treated in this hub's Aeneid article; the discipline to observe here is the hub's standing one — the Greek hymn promises Aeneas' line rule among the Trojans, in the Troad; it knows nothing of Italy, and reading Rome back into its lines is reception, not exegesis. The seed is genuinely in the Greek text. The tree is Roman.

For the goddess herself, the hymn is the archaic tradition's most searching portrait — the fullest account we have of Aphrodite's power stated as a system and then turned against its holder. Her shame is structural, not incidental: she who boasted of humbling gods is humbled before them, and she says so, dreading the reproach of the gods who will laugh at her defeat. The Olympians article draws on this hymn for her dossier; the textual point to keep here is that the humiliation is the hymn's gift to her — the story that gives the goddess of desire a biography, a wound, and a son.

VIII. The Shorter Hymns: The Rest of the Company

Beyond the four long hymns and the Dionysos fragment stand twenty-eight shorter pieces. They are not filler. Among them are the corpus's only hymn to Pan, its most vivid miracle-story after Demeter's, its fullest portrait of the Dioscuri as saviours of sailors, and a set of miniature theogonies and epiphanies that preserve, in a few lines each, cult titles and local traditions found nowhere else. This section takes the notable ones in the collection's own order of interest — Dionysos first, since the corpus gives him three hymns and its first page — and then surveys the rest; the full table of the thirty-three closes the section.

Dionysos Thrice: Hymns 1, 7, and 26

Hymn 1, to Dionysos, is the corpus's ruin: twenty-one lines surviving of a poem that must once have stood with the long hymns. What remains is its close: a catalogue of disputed birthplaces — Drakanos, Ikaros, Naxos, Thebes, and others are named as claimants — the poem adjudicating, in the lines that survive, for Nysa, and the birth of the god to Semele, with the singer's farewell. West has argued a reconstruction of the lost poem's scope (his 2001 study, discussed in his Loeb introduction); other scholars, Albrecht Dihle among them, have argued against the reconstruction's premises. The disagreement is flagged here as the scholarship presents it: the hymn's original scale is inferred, not known.

Hymn 7, to Dionysos (59 lines), is the short corpus's masterpiece, and it is told here in full, as its completeness deserves. Dionysos appears on a headland by the sea in the likeness of a young man in the first bloom of youth, dark hair flowing, a purple cloak on his strong shoulders. Tyrrhenian pirates, sweeping past in their ship, see him, take him for a king's son worth a ransom, and drag him aboard. The bonds will not hold him: the withies fall from his hands and feet of themselves, and he sits smiling with his dark eyes. The helmsman alone understands — he cries to his shipmates, in paraphrase, that they have seized a god, that the ship cannot bear him, that he is Zeus, Apollo, or Poseidon, and bids them set him ashore before he raises storms. The captain rebukes him and orders sail made. Then the miracles come in a rising sequence: fragrant wine streams through the ship, sweet as nectar, and amazement seizes the crew; a vine spreads both ways along the sail, heavy with grape clusters; ivy, dark with berries, wreathes the mast. The god turns into a lion, roaring in the bows, and in the middle of the ship creates a shaggy bear; the lion seizes the captain. The crew leap overboard — all but the helmsman — and in the sea become dolphins. The god, taking pity on the one man who knew him, holds the helmsman back, makes him blessed, and names himself in the hymn's epiphany formula: he is loud-roaring Dionysos, whom Semele bore to Zeus. The piece is a perfect miniature of the genre: appearance, recognition refused and granted, epiphany, transformation, and the god's self-naming — a whole theology of Dionysiac arrival in fifty-nine lines, and the archaic source from which the later tradition of the pirate story, told by Ovid and others, descends.

Hymn 26, to Dionysos (13 lines), is the third and briefest: an ivy-crowned portrait of the god as reveller, invoked as the singer asks to be kept glad for the seasons' round and to come again to the contest in joy. Three hymns to one god, in three registers — the fragmentary epic of his birth, the miracle of his epiphany, the festival invocation — give Dionysos, alone among the Olympians, a small corpus of his own inside the corpus.

Heracles, the Dioscuri, Pan

Hymn 15, to Heracles (9 lines), sings the lion-hearted son of Zeus and Alcmene in the briefest of biographies: he wandered endlessly over earth and sea at the bidding of Eurystheus, wrought and suffered much, and now dwells in bliss on Olympus with Hebe for his wife. Nine lines hold the whole arc that this hub's Heracles' Labours article unfolds; the hymn's compression is itself the evidence of how completely the audience was expected to know the story.

Hymn 33, to the Dioscuri (39 lines) — the second of two hymns to the twins, Hymn 17 being a five-line twin-piece — is the corpus's great hymn of rescue. Castor and Polydeuces, sons of Leda and of Zeus who took her in swan's shape, are sung as saviours of men and of swift ships: when a storm drives a vessel and the sailors pray with offerings, the twins appear through the air on swift wings, the winds drop, the waves smooth, and the sailors rejoice at the sign — the phenomenon later sailors would call St Elmo's fire, though the hymn speaks of the gods' own appearing, and the identification of the two is the tradition's, drawn here as the scholarship standardly draws it. The hymn is among the earliest literary witnesses to the twins' sea-rescue cult role.

Hymn 19, to Pan (49 lines), is the longest of the short hymns and the only hymn in the corpus addressed to a god outside the Olympian twelve. It tells Pan's birth: Hermes, in Arcadia, loves the daughter of Dryops, and the child she bears is a marvel — goat-footed, two-horned, noisy, sweetly laughing. The nurse flees in terror at the sight; Hermes takes the child to Olympus, where the gods delight in him, Dionysos above all, and name him Pan, the hymn says, because he delighted all (pantes) their hearts. The hymn then follows the grown god through his Arcadian landscape — the echoing hills, the hunt, the reed-pipe's music at evening, rivalled only by the nightingale — in the most purely picturesque writing in the collection. The etymology is the hymn's own; modern philology treats the derivation of Pan from pan, “all”, as folk-etymology, and the two statements are kept apart here, as the hub's method requires: the poem says it, and the poem's saying it is the archaic evidence.

The Remaining Company, in Brief

The rest reward a rapid muster. Hymn 6, to Aphrodite (21 lines), gives the goddess's sea-birth and her reception by the Seasons, golden-crowned — a second, lyric Aphrodite beside Hymn 5's narrative. Hymn 27, to Artemis (22 lines), shows the huntress shaking the mountains, then laying down her bow at Delphi to lead the Muses' and Graces' dance. Hymn 28, to Athena (18 lines), sings the birth of the goddess in full armour from Zeus's head, the gods frozen in awe, Olympus shaking, the sun's horses halted in the sky — the corpus's most concentrated epiphany. Hymn 30, to Earth (19 lines), addresses Gaia as mother of all and giver of livelihood, in the hymn that stands closest to a nature-prayer. Hymns 31 and 32, to Helios and Selene (19 and 20 lines), are a matched pair of celestial portraits — the Sun in his chariot's gleam, the Moon bathing and riding with her beams abroad — often judged, on style, among the latest pieces in the collection, a judgement labelled in Section X. Hymn 8, to Ares (17 lines), addresses the war-god in language so unlike the rest — cosmic, epithet-heaped, prayer-like — that scholars have suspected it a late, possibly Orphic or Neoplatonic-era insertion, filling a noticed gap: the corpus had no Ares. The suspicion is reported as suspicion; the hymn stands in the manuscripts, and the manuscripts are the corpus we have. The remaining miniatures — to Artemis again (9), Aphrodite again (10), Athena (11), Hera (12), Demeter in three lines (13), the Mother of the Gods with her lions and drums (14), Asclepius the healer (16), Hermes again (18), Hephaestus, teacher of crafts (20), Apollo in five lines (21), Poseidon the earth-shaker (22), Zeus in four (23), Hestia twice (24, 29) — the second of the Hestia hymns inviting her also to Apollo's house at Delphi — and the Muses and Apollo together (25) — are the prelude-stock proper: invocations a singer could set before an epic, each a god's dossier in a handful of lines.

IX. The Hymns as Sources: Where a God Gets a Life-Story

What are the hymns for, to a modern reader? Not primarily for their plots — the myths they tell are told elsewhere, often better stocked, in the Theogony, the epics, and the later mythographers treated in this hub's prose systematizers article. The hymns' unique evidentiary value lies in three things no other archaic genre supplies in the same concentration: the god's dossier, the god's biography, and the god's cult geography. This section sets out the three, with the hub's standing cautions attached.

Cult Epithets and the Dossier

A hymn opens by naming its god correctly — with the epithets, parentage, birthplace, and haunts by which the god is known — because in the logic of Greek prayer, correct naming is the condition of being heard. The openings are therefore dense deposits of cult language: Apollo of the silver bow and the far shooting, Hermes of the golden wand, Argus-slayer, robber and watcher by night, Poseidon the earth-shaker and earth-encircler, Demeter of the bright fruit and the awful mysteries. Where an epithet in a hymn matches an epithet in inscriptions or in a city's cult calendar, the hymn becomes a witness to actual worship and not only to poetry; where it does not, it remains a witness to what a festival audience expected a god to be called. Both are evidence, of different things, and the discipline is to say which. The hub's Olympians article builds the gods' dossiers from these deposits throughout.

The Biography: Birth, Acquisition, Province

The long hymns share a plot-shape so consistent that it amounts to the genre's grammar. A god is born, or arrives; the god lacks a settled place in the order of things; by deed, theft, suffering, or negotiation the god acquires a province — a function in the world — and the honours (timai) that go with it; and the poem ends with the god installed, the cosmos re-balanced, and the singer's farewell. Apollo gets Delphi and the lyre's counter-claim; Hermes gets the messenger's office, the herds, and the wand; Aphrodite gets, in place of her lost boast, a son and a dynasty; Demeter gets her daughter back for two-thirds of the year and a temple, rites, and priesthood at Eleusis. Even the short hymns obey the grammar in miniature: Athena's birth in Hymn 28 is an acquisition of place in eighteen lines; Pan's in Hymn 19 is an arrival and a naming. Set between epic, whose biographies are heroes', and lyric, whose gods are mostly presences invoked and released, the hymn is the genre in which a god gets a life-story — a beginning, a crisis, and a settled end. Much of what the Greek world knew as its gods' personalities — Hermes the trickster-infant, Apollo the founder, Aphrodite the humbled boaster — it knew from these poems.

// LITERARY MYTH IS NOT CULT PRACTICE //

The hymns are festival literature, and the hub's rule applies to them with full force: a story told in a hymn is evidence that the story was tellable, in that form, to a festival audience — not that any rite enacted it, any priest taught it, or any worshipper believed it in the form the poem gives. The Demeter hymn is the limiting case (Section IV): it founds the Mysteries in myth while revealing nothing of their practice. Conversely, the hymns' incidental furniture — the Delian festival in Hymn 3, the Dioscuri's rescue of sailors in Hymn 33 — can corroborate cult realities independently attested, and Thucydides uses the Apollo hymn in exactly that evidentiary way. Corroboration is not identity, and the articles of this hub keep the two apart.

Cult Geography

Finally, the hymns are maps. The Apollo hymn charts the Aegean of the Ionian festival network and the road to Delphi; the Demeter hymn fixes Eleusis; the Hermes hymn moves between Arcadian Cyllene, Pieria, and the Alpheus; the Aphrodite hymn joins Cyprus, Paphos, and Ida in one itinerary. A hymn composed for a sanctuary is an argument for that sanctuary — Delos' bargain with Leto in Hymn 3 is a charter for Delian honours, told as myth — and the competing birthplaces catalogued in the fragment of Hymn 1 show the argument's other side: cities bid for gods, and hymns were among the bids. Read as sources, then, the hymns document the religious geography of archaic Greece as its singers wished it sung. That is not the same as documenting it as it was; it is the next best thing, and for several places and epithets it is the only thing.

X. Dating the Hymns: Arguments, Not Dates

No hymn carries a date, an author's name that can be verified, or a reference to a securely datable event that all scholars accept. Every date in the literature is the conclusion of an argument, and this section's rule is the one the hub applies to the epics: the arguments are given with their kinds, the ranges with their holders, and no range is allowed to harden into a year.

The Instruments

Four instruments do most of the work. Linguistic stratification: the epic dialect changed over time, and statistical study of its features — the method of Richard Janko's Homer, Hesiod and the Hymns (1982) — yields a relative order for the poems it can measure, with the Demeter hymn earliest among the long four on his measures and the Hermes hymn latest; the method's relative orderings are widely used, its absolute conversions into years widely disputed. Intertextuality : a hymn that demonstrably knows the Iliad or Odyssey in something like our form is later than their crystallisation — the Hermes hymn's playful dependence on both epics is the standard case. Historical allusion: the Pythian half of the Apollo hymn has been dated by its Delphi politics — W. G. Forrest argued influentially for a date after the First Sacred War and the reorganisation of the Pythian games around 585 BCE — and Burkert's 522 BCE proposal for the combined hymn (Section V) is an argument of the same kind, from occasion rather than text. Institutions and things: the state of a cult, a city's standing, a practice's presence or absence — the Demeter hymn's pre-Athenian Eleusis (Section IV) is the type case — argued, always, from what the poem does not say as much as from what it does, and therefore the weakest of the four when it stands alone.

The corpus as a whole, then, is a stratified deposit of perhaps three centuries of festival song — the oldest hymns near-contemporary with the crystallisation of the epics themselves, the youngest possibly younger than the classical age they pretend to precede. That span is not an embarrassment to the collection's unity, because the unity was never the poems': it is the unity of the book that gathered them, and of the name under which the gathering travelled.

XI. Editions and Translations

The hymns are unusually fortunate in their modern instruments: one classic commentary that has served for nearly a century, one modern Loeb that puts text and translation in a single pocket volume, and one English version that has become the classroom standard. The reader who wants the corpus whole should know all three, and what each is for.

ALLEN, HALLIDAY & SIKES (1936)

The Homeric Hymns, edited with text and commentary by T. W. Allen, W. R. Halliday, and E. E. Sikes (Oxford; 2nd edition 1936, the first edition by Allen and Sikes having appeared in 1904). The classic commentary: introduction on the collection, its manuscripts, and the hymn-form; notes on every hymn, dense with the religion and antiquities of the older scholarship. Still the first place a disputed line is taken.

WEST'S LOEB (2003)

Homeric Hymns · Homeric Apocrypha · Lives of Homer, edited and translated by Martin L. West (Loeb Classical Library 496, Harvard University Press, 2003). The modern Greek text with facing English; the introduction's stemma and account of the collection are the authority this article follows for the transmission (Section III). The volume's company — the hymns bound with the other works that travelled under Homer's name — is itself a lesson in what “Homeric” meant.

ATHANASSAKIS (1976; 2ND ED. 2004)

The Homeric Hymns, translated by Apostolos N. Athanassakis (Johns Hopkins University Press; second edition, with Benjamin M. Wolkow, 2004). The standard modern English translation for readers without Greek: faithful, readable, and equipped with the notes a first reader needs. The version to give someone meeting the corpus for the first time.

EVELYN-WHITE (1914) AND THE SINGLE-HYMN COMMENTARIES

H. G. Evelyn-White's Loeb translation (1914, in the Hesiod volume) is dated in diction and indispensable in the archive's practice: it is the version quoted in this hub's Mysteries article and in Sections IV and VII here. For the long hymns singly: N. J. Richardson on Demeter (Oxford, 1974) and on Apollo, Hermes, and Aphrodite together (Cambridge, 2010); Andrew Faulkner's commentary on the Hymn to Aphrodite (Oxford, 2008) and his edited volume of interpretative essays on the corpus (Oxford, 2011); Athanassakis' text is complemented by the Italian edition of Filippo Càssola (1975), long prized for its introductions.

A practical word, in the hub's manner. Read the long hymns first in Athanassakis or West, whole, as poems; take disputed passages to Allen–Halliday–Sikes and the single-hymn commentaries; and when a translation prints the Demeter hymn smooth through its damaged folio, check the apparatus before quoting the lines as the poem's. The corpus rewards that order: pleasure first, then the instruments, then the holes in the page, honestly seen.

XII. Sources

The works below were consulted in the preparation of this article. Ancient texts are cited in the translations named; quotations are limited to the passages identified in the article as quoted — Hymn to Demeter 1–3 and Hymn to Aphrodite 196–197 in Evelyn-White's Loeb translation (1914), and the sphragis line of Hymn to Apollo as quoted by Thucydides in Jowett's translation. Where this article reports a scholarly position — West's stemma, Burkert's 522 BCE proposal, Forrest's Pythian dating, Janko's linguistic ordering, the suspicion attaching to Hymn 8 — the position is attributed to its holder in the text, and paraphrase is written as paraphrase throughout.
