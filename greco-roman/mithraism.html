MITHRAISM: THE ROMAN MYSTERIES OF MITHRAS

Rome · Ostia · The Rhine and Danube Frontiers · c. 90–400 CE

// A CULT WITHOUT A SCRIPTURE :: ROOMS — IMAGES — OUTSIDERS //

Mithraism was the most widely distributed mystery cult of the Roman world, and it left no book. No scripture, no liturgy, no myth told by an initiate, and no explanation of its central image survives from inside the cult — only hundreds of cave-shaped rooms, a strikingly uniform picture of a god killing a bull, some thousands of short dedications, and the remarks of outsiders, most of them hostile. This article is therefore built the way the evidence is built: rooms first, image second, outsiders third, and every interpretation labelled with the name of whoever made it.

I. The Evidence Problem, Stated First

Every other article in this hub's Mysteries sequence rests, at some point, on a text written by someone who stood inside the tradition it describes. Eleusis has the Homeric Hymn to Demeter; the Orphic current has the Derveni commentary and the gold tablets; the Isis cult has Apuleius, an initiate writing a novel about initiation. Those articles are gathered in The Mysteries, and the reader who comes to Mithras from them should be warned at the threshold: for Mithraism there is no equivalent text. Not a damaged one, not a fragmentary one — none. The cult flourished for roughly three centuries, in hundreds of communities, across an empire that produced paper the way it produced pottery, and it has not left one sentence of its own theology.

What survives falls into four classes, and the whole discipline of the subject consists in not confusing them.

1 · BUILDINGS

The mithraea themselves: small, cave-shaped, bench-lined halls, identified in the hundreds across the empire. They tell us where the cult met, how many could meet at once, and how the room was arranged. They say nothing, directly, about what the men in the room believed.

2 · IMAGES

Above all the tauroctony, the bull-slaying relief or painting that stood at the end of every mithraeum, in some seven hundred surviving examples of remarkable consistency. An image is evidence of what a cult showed its initiates. It is not a caption, and this cult left no caption.

3 · INSCRIPTIONS

Roughly a thousand short texts: dedications of altars, reliefs, and buildings, usually giving a man's name, his grade, his unit or profession, and the god's titles — Deo Soli Invicto Mithrae, “to the Unconquered Sun-God Mithras”, is the standard formula. They are prosopography, not doctrine: they tell us who, where, and when, almost never what was taught.

4 · OUTSIDERS

A handful of passages in authors who were not initiates: a Neoplatonist philosopher allegorising a cave, three Christian writers attacking a rival, a biographer repeating a story about pirates. Each has an agenda, each is treated in Section X under his own name, and none may be read as a report from inside.

The standard corpus makes the imbalance concrete. Maarten Vermaseren's Corpus Inscriptionum et Monumentorum Religionis Mithriacae (1956–1960) — the CIMRM, still the reference collection, cited by number throughout the scholarship — runs to some 2,400 entries of monuments and inscriptions, and the decades since have added substantially to it. Manfred Clauss's survey of the material counts, in round numbers, about a thousand inscriptions and seven hundred examples of the bull-slaying scene from some 420 find-spots. Against that mass of stone and paint, the insider literature of Mithraism is zero pages long. Two lost works are known by title and a few paraphrased lines — histories of the mysteries by authors named Euboulus and Pallas, cited by Porphyry — and even they were, so far as the citations show, accounts about the cult rather than scriptures of it. The gap is not an accident of survival alone. A cult that met in rooms built for a few dozen men, admitted by graded initiation, and guarded its rites as mysteries appears simply never to have circulated a written theology for a copyist to preserve.

// QUOTATIONS IN THIS ARTICLE //

Because the subject invites invented scripture, this article quotes almost nothing. The one extended quotation is the list of grade-names in Jerome's letter, given in Section VIII from the standard text of the letter. Everything else that renders an ancient author's sense — Porphyry, Plutarch, Justin, Tertullian, Firmicus, the Avestan hymn — is paraphrase, and is written as paraphrase, with the author and work named at the point of use. Modern scholars' positions are likewise paraphrased and named, never ventriloquised.

The consequence should be carried through every section that follows. When a handbook states flatly what Mithraists “believed” — that the bull-slaying released life into the world, that the soul climbed seven planetary spheres, that the initiate was promised resurrection — the reader is entitled to ask which of the four classes of evidence the statement rests on, and the honest answer is almost always the same: on an inference, drawn by a named modern scholar, from images and rooms, sometimes assisted by an outsider with an agenda of his own. Inference is not worthless; some of it is very strong. But in this subject the distance between the stone and the sentence built on it is the whole story, and this article keeps that distance visible. It is the same discipline this hub applies at Eleusis, where the secret was also kept — with the difference that at Eleusis we at least possess the myth the initiates heard. For Mithras we possess the furniture.

II. The Phenomenon: A Cult of the Roman World

Strip the interpretation away and the phenomenon itself is solid, datable, and mappable. A mystery cult — initiatory, oath-bound, meeting in dedicated rooms under a hierarchy of grades — centred on a god named Mithras, appears in the Roman world in the late first century CE, spreads with remarkable speed along the empire's military and commercial arteries, flourishes through the second and third centuries, and is extinguished in the course of the fourth. Its remains run from Hadrian's Wall to the Euphrates, and from the Danube to North Africa. It was, by distribution, the most successful of the Roman mysteries — the one a traveller in 250 CE was most likely to find, in some small vaulted room behind a barracks or a warehouse, in almost any garrison town or port of the Latin West.

The Chronology, at Its Real Strength

The earliest literary notice is a line of Statius, in the first book of the Thebaid (c. 80s–90 CE), which speaks — in paraphrase — of Mithras twisting the horns of an unruly bull beneath the rocks of a Persian cave: the god, the bull, and the cave are already together, in a Roman poem, by about 90 CE. The earliest datable monuments belong to the same generation: dedications and mithraea of the Flavian and Trajanic periods, clustering — and this matters for Section XI — in Rome and its port Ostia and on the Rhine and Danube frontiers, not in the Greek East. Through the second century the evidence thickens everywhere in the West; the mithraeum at Dura-Europos on the Euphrates, founded by Palmyrene archers in Roman service, carries dated dedications of 168 and 171 CE and shows the cult fully formed at the empire's eastern edge within a lifetime of its first certain appearance in Italy. The third century is the peak by every count — of new mithraea, of dedications, of named initiates. The fourth century is contraction, then suppression, treated in Section XIII.

Two earlier notices are often promoted into evidence for a first-century-BCE cult, and both must be kept at their real weight. Plutarch, writing about 100 CE, says in his Life of Pompey (24) that the Cilician pirates suppressed by Pompey in 67 BCE performed secret rites, among them rites of Mithras, which — he adds — continued to his own day. That is a claim made a century and a half after the event, by a writer with a taste for eastern colour, and no first-century-BCE mithraeum, relief, or inscription has ever been found to support it. It may preserve a genuine memory of Mithras-worship in Cilicia; it cannot document the Roman mystery cult, whose archaeology begins, abruptly and fully formed, some 150 years later. Similarly, Dio Cassius's story of the Armenian king Tiridates at Nero's court in 66 CE, addressing the emperor as one who had come to worship him “as Mithras”, attests the god's name and its Iranian royal associations in the East. It attests nothing about mysteries in Rome. The distinction between the name Mithras circulating in the eastern Mediterranean and the Roman mystery cult of Mithras is the single most important chronological distinction in the subject, and Section XI returns to it.

The great majority of mithraea are built, adapted, or redecorated; the epigraphic record of named initiates is densest; Ostia accumulates its exceptional cluster of sanctuaries (Section V).

4TH CENTURY

Fewer foundations, some violent destructions, some careful burials of cult images; imperial law turns against sacrifice in 391–392; the last mithraea close, are wrecked, or are built over (Section XIII).

The Map, and What It Argues

The distribution is not even, and its unevenness is itself evidence. The densest provinces are the military zones of the Rhine and upper Danube — Germania Superior, Raetia, Noricum, the Pannonias — together with Italy, Britain along the northern frontier, Dacia, and the port cities of the West. The Greek East, by contrast, is strikingly thin: a scatter of monuments in Asia Minor, very little in Greece itself, a modest presence in Syria largely explained by Roman garrisons like Dura. A cult diffusing westward out of Persia through Anatolia over centuries should be thickest where it started. The Mithraic map is thickest where the Roman army and the Roman customs service were thickest, and its earliest datable cluster sits in the capital's own port. Whatever Mithraism was, its vehicle was Roman: the legion on transfer, the customs official on posting, the merchant and the imperial slave moving along the empire's internal routes. The cult's own sociology (Section III) is written in its map.

One more feature deserves emphasis: the cult's uniformity. A mithraeum in Britain and one in Syria are recognisably the same institution — same plan, same image, same grades in the same order — though there was no Mithraic church, no central authority, and no scripture to enforce the pattern. How a textless cult standardised itself so tightly is a genuine puzzle. The most economical answer the evidence allows is that the pattern travelled with the men: soldiers and officials initiated in one post founded or reshaped the community at the next. That is an inference, and it is labelled as one; the uniformity it explains is a fact of the archaeology.

III. Who the Initiates Were — and the Question of Women

The inscriptions, thin as they are on doctrine, are rich in names, and Manfred Clauss's prosopography, Cultores Mithrae (1992), assembled them into the cult's social portrait: in round terms, a thousand or more named adherents whose status or profession can be read from the stones. The portrait is consistent from province to province.

SOLDIERS AND VETERANS

The largest single bloc: legionaries, auxiliaries, officers up to a middling rank, and veterans settled near their old stations. The frontier density of the mithraea (Section II) is this bloc made architectural. The older picture of Mithraism as the soldiers' religion nonetheless overstates it: soldiers are a plurality of the named adherents, not the whole, and in a civilian port like Ostia they are scarcely present at all.

THE IMPERIAL SERVICE

Imperial slaves and freedmen of the emperor's household administration, and — conspicuously in the Danube provinces — the staff of the customs service, the portorium: clerks, station-managers, and their slaves, whose postings moved them along the same routes the cult travelled. Mithraism maps onto the empire's junior bureaucracy as faithfully as onto its army.

MERCHANTS, SLAVES, FREEDMEN

Traders in the ports; privately owned slaves, who appear as dedicators in their own right and sometimes in the grades; freedmen in numbers. A slave and a freeborn officer could stand in the same grade hierarchy, ranked by initiation rather than by birth — one of the cult's real attractions, and one of the few social facts about it that the stones state plainly.

WHO IS MISSING

The high aristocracy is almost entirely absent for the cult's first two and a half centuries. A small group of senatorial adherents appears only in the later fourth century at Rome, in the milieu of the last pagan revival — the exception that marks how thoroughly Mithraism had been a cult of the empire's middle and lower ranks.

The communities were correspondingly small. A mithraeum's benches (Section IV) seat a few dozen reclining diners at most, and the membership lists that survive fit that scale: the bronze membership tablet from Virunum in Noricum, dated to 184 CE and recording names added year by year, lists a community numbered in the dozens, not the hundreds. Mithraism was not a mass movement with local branches; it was a network of small, intense, face-to-face cells, each gathered around its Pater, its Father — the senior grade who presided over a community (Section VIII) — and bound to the next cell by the mobility of its members and the uniformity of its forms, not by any organisation above the cell. There is no evidence for Mithraic provinces, synods, or any authority beyond the individual Pater.

The Question of Women, at Its Real Strength

The standard statement — Mithraism was an all-male cult — is one of the best-supported negative claims in the study of ancient religion, and it should still be stated precisely, because its support is of a particular kind. It rests on total epigraphic silence across a large corpus: among the thousand-plus named adherents, dedicators, and grade-holders in the inscriptions, and in the membership lists such as Virunum's, every name is male. Not nearly every name — every name. Cumont drew the conclusion in 1899 from the corpus he had just assembled, and a further century of finds has not produced a female initiate's dedication to set against it. For Ostia specifically, where the record is densest, the modern study of the cult there reports no evidence of even marginal female participation.

Against that silence stand exactly the kinds of scraps that keep a question technically open. A passage of Porphyry (On Abstinence 4.16), describing the animal names of the grades, has been read as assigning women a corresponding animal designation — the passage is compressed, its text and reference disputed, and it may concern the symbolism of the grades rather than the admission of women. A funerary inscription from North Africa appears to style a woman with a feminine form of the Lion grade, lea, “lioness”; its context is ambiguous and it may not be Mithraic at all. The scholar David Jonathan argued in 2000 that women were involved with Mithraic groups in at least some places; Aleš Chalupa's review of the whole dossier concludes that the traditional position still stands. The archive's verdict follows the evidence's weight: the cult of the mithraea was, so far as a large and consistent record can show, a cult of men; the two or three ambiguous scraps are recorded here because the discipline of this hub is to show the counter-evidence, not because they balance the corpus. What women in an initiate's household knew, believed, or practised at home is beyond the evidence entirely, and this article does not speculate on it.

IV. The Mithraeum: A Cave Built Indoors

The primary record of Mithraism is a room. The initiates called it, among other names, a spelaeum — a cave — and the word is the plan: a mithraeum is a cave manufactured indoors, in whatever material a town offered, inside buildings that from the street looked like nothing at all. Mithraea are found inserted into warehouses, baths, houses, barracks, and the basements of later churches; purpose-built examples are usually half-buried. Almost none presents a temple front to the street. The cult that left no book also built no skyline: its architecture is interior, windowless, and turned inward, and that is the first thing its remains say about it.

The Standard Plan

With local variation, the plan repeats from Britain to Syria. A rectangular hall, commonly in the range of ten to twenty metres long. Down each long side runs a continuous masonry bench or podium, raised, sloping slightly, on which the initiates reclined for the communal meal, facing inward across a central aisle. At the far end, in an apse or niche — often framed as a cave-mouth, sometimes roughened with pumice or tufa to imitate rock — stood the cult image: the tauroctony, as relief, painting, or free-standing group (Sections VI–VII). Before it, one or more small altars. The vault, where it survives or can be reconstructed, was in some cases painted with stars; the room was lit by lamps and by whatever light the doorway admitted, and in some mithraea by small openings contrived to throw a shaft of light toward the image — the specific claims made for particular light-effects at particular sites are modern reconstructions and are labelled as such in the site reports. Many mithraea add an ante-room for preparation and storage; some have a well or water basin, consistent with the water-miracle scene in the god's image-cycle (Section VII) and with ritual washing, though the use made of the water is nowhere described.

Two consequences follow from the plan, and both are firmer than most of what is said about Mithraic belief. First, the cult's centre of gravity was a meal eaten together in a ranked room: whatever else initiation conferred, it issued in a seat on a bench, in grade order, before the image. The Christian witnesses (Section X) who fixate on the Mithraic meal — bread, a cup, words spoken over them — were reacting to something architecturally real. Second, the room was a total environment: a man reclining in it saw the bull-slaying before him, the torchbearers beside it, stars (where painted) above him, and his graded fellows around him, and nothing of the ordinary street-world at all. Modern readings of the mithraeum as a model of the cosmos (Sections X and XII) begin from this real enclosure; how far the initiates themselves theorised it is the part that cannot be recovered.

Contrast the public temple of this hub's Roman Religion article: civic cult was conducted outdoors, before a crowd, at an altar in front of a god's house. The mithraeum inverts every term — indoors, hidden, initiates only, its image lit artificially in a built cave. That inversion is the mystery-form itself, shared with the cults of The Mysteries; Mithraism gave the form its most standardised architecture.

V. Ostia: The Urban Case

If the frontier mithraeum shows the cult in its military setting, Ostia — Rome's port, excavated as few Roman towns have been — shows it in the city, and Ostia is where the numbers become startling. Giovanni Becatti's catalogue of the Ostian mithraea, published in 1954 in the official excavation series, already listed fifteen; subsequent work has raised the count, and totals of seventeen or eighteen identified mithraea now circulate in the literature, the variation depending on how doubtful cases are counted. Either figure makes the point: in a town of perhaps fifty or sixty thousand people, archaeologists can walk the streets and point to a mithraeum every few blocks. No other town preserves anything like the density, because no other town of the cult's heartland survives as completely — Rome itself certainly held more mithraea, but Rome is buried under Rome.

The Ostian examples display the cult's whole urban repertoire. They are tucked into the ground floors of apartment blocks, into warehouses near the Tiber wharves, into the substructures of baths, into a converted house; one was installed in the premises of a guild, the rope-makers, under a patron named Fructosus. They are, with few exceptions, adaptations, not new buildings: a long room, a pair of benches, a niche, an altar — the kit of parts of Section IV, fitted into whatever shell the town provided. The dedicators are the port's working population: merchants, warehouse staff, slaves and freedmen, the occasional official. The army, dominant on the frontiers, is marginal here, because Ostia was not a garrison town. Ostia is therefore the standing correction to the “soldiers' religion” slogan: in the cult's most thickly documented community, Mithraism appears as a religion of the commercial and servile city.

THE MITHRAEUM OF FELICISSIMUS

A modest adapted room of the later third century, famous for one object: the black-and-white mosaic laid down its central aisle in seven panels of symbols, panel by panel the fullest material statement of the seven grades (Section VIII). The dedicatory panel names Felicissimus, the man who paid for the pavement.

THE MITHRAEUM OF THE SEVEN SPHERES

Named for its mosaic of the planetary spheres; its benches carry mosaics of the zodiac signs. It is among the most explicitly cosmic interiors the cult has left, and it is cited — with due caution — in every discussion of the astral readings (Section XII).

THE MITHRAEUM OF THE SEVEN GATES

Named for a mosaic showing seven gates. The name has inevitably been connected with the seven-gated ladder that the pagan critic Celsus, quoted by Origen, ascribed to the mysteries (Section VIII); the connection is a modern association, attractive and unprovable, and is labelled as such in the literature.

THE PAINTED MITHRAEA

The Mithraeum of the Painted Walls (Pareti Dipinte) and others preserve wall-painting where most sites preserve only masonry — processions and cult scenes comparable to the richer cycle at Santa Prisca in Rome (Section VIII).

What Ostia proves, beyond density, is plurality without unity. Fifteen or more mithraea in one town cannot have been one congregation meeting in fifteen places; they were fifteen communities, founded at different dates by different patrons, presumably recruiting from different streets, workplaces, and households, with no Ostian Mithraic authority above their several Fathers visible in any record. The cult reproduced by fission and fresh foundation, not by organisation. And yet a man initiated in one Ostian mithraeum would have found the room, the image, the grades, and the meal of any other immediately familiar. Holding those two facts together — cellular independence, formal uniformity — is the sociological form of the problem stated in Section II, and no surviving evidence resolves it further.

A last Ostian caution: the town's mithraea are mostly of the third century, the earliest belonging to the Antonine age, a generation after the cult's first appearance at Rome. Ostia shows the cult in maturity, at its densest — not at its origin.

VI. The Tauroctony I: The Central Group and the Animals

At the end of every mithraeum stood the same picture. Its modern name, tauroctony — Greek for “bull-slaying” — is a scholars' coinage; no ancient Mithraic term for the image survives, which is fitting, since no ancient explanation of it survives either. Some seven hundred examples are known, in marble relief, local stone, stucco, and paint, from the great carved altarpieces of the Rhine to small plaques in Dacia; and their agreement is the most extraordinary fact in Mithraic studies. Across three centuries and an empire, workshops that cannot have been in contact reproduce one composition, figure for figure, with the fidelity of a coin type. This section and the next read the image element by element, under a strict rule, stated once and kept throughout: first what the image shows, then — separately, and under a scholar's name — what has been inferred from it.

The Central Group: What Is Shown

A young man kneels on the back of a bull that has been forced to the ground. His left knee presses into the bull's spine; his right leg extends back to pin the bull's hind leg. His left hand grips the bull's muzzle or horns and hauls its head up and back; his right hand drives a short sword or dagger into the bull's neck, at the shoulder. His head is turned away from the killing — characteristically, he looks back over his shoulder, toward the figure of Sol in the corner of the scene. He wears the costume the Roman world read as Persian: a soft pointed cap (the “Phrygian” cap), a long-sleeved tunic, trousers, and a cloak that billows behind him, in finer examples studded with stars or lined with the signs of the zodiac. The setting is a cave, shown as a rocky arch over the group. This is Mithras, and the composition — the kneeling killer astride a bull, the averted face — is not an Iranian invention: art historians have long noted its descent from a classical Greek type, the bull-slaying Victory (Nike) known from Hellenistic sculpture, adapted and re-cast. The cult's central image, in its very anatomy, is a Greco-Roman artwork.

Three disciplines follow from the table. First: no ancient text explains any of these figures; every identification — dog as constellation, snake as earth, scorpion as autumn — is a modern proposal, and the proposals disagree. Second: the one thing the image itself insists on is a paradox, a killing from which grain grows. Fertility-from-death is shown; every fuller statement of it is a reconstruction, Cumont's in the first instance (Section XI). Third: there is no evidence that any bull was ever killed in a mithraeum. The rooms are far too small, their debris is feasting debris — chicken and other small-animal bone predominates where faunal remains have been analysed — and the tauroctony is a picture, not the record of a rite.

// THE AVERTED FACE //

Mithras's turned head has generated a literature of its own: reluctance, obedience to Sol's command, refusal to watch a necessary act. The image shows the turn; it also shows, in many examples, a ray or a direct line of sight linking Sol to Mithras, which makes “looking toward Sol” the minimal description. Everything beyond the minimal description — the god's emotions, his orders, his reluctance — is supplied by the interpreter. This article supplies none of it.

VII. The Tauroctony II: Torchbearers, Sol and Luna, the Ring of Signs

The central group does not stand alone. It is framed by a fixed company of witnesses and, in the grander reliefs, by the machinery of the sky itself. These framing elements are, if anything, more consistent than the animals, and they are the elements on which the cosmic readings of the image (Section XII) principally rest.

Cautes and Cautopates

Flanking the group stand two small figures dressed as Mithras himself — same cap, tunic, and trousers — each holding a torch. Cautes, conventionally on the viewer's right of the scene, holds his torch pointing up. Cautopates, on the left, holds his pointing down. Both commonly stand with legs crossed, in a posture as fixed as the torches. Their names are genuinely ancient — they appear in dedications — and they are the tauroctony's only named supporting figures. In a number of examples, about fifty catalogued cases, their positions are reversed, Cautopates taking the right and Cautes the left; the reversal is a fact of the corpus whose significance, if any, is unknown. In the mithraeum itself the pair often stood as statues in niches flanking the cult image, so that the initiate dining in the room sat, in effect, inside the picture.

What they mean is inference, and the inferences should be kept in their lanes. Raised and lowered torch most plainly suggest sunrise and sunset, dawn and dusk, rising and setting light; from there interpreters have gone on to spring and autumn, birth and death, life and the underworld, the two equinoxes. All of these are readings of a visual antithesis that the image certainly intends — the pair is too deliberate to be decoration — and none of them is confirmed by any Mithraic word. Where later writers, ancient and modern, have allegorised the torchbearers, this archive reports the allegory as theirs. The image gives two torches, one up, one down, and two names. It gives no more.

Sol, Luna, and the Zodiac Ring

In the upper corners of the relief — Sol on the viewer's left, Luna on the right — the busts of the Sun and Moon look on. In elaborate examples Sol appears in his four-horse chariot mounting upward, Luna in her two-horse chariot descending, each preceded by a torch-bearing youth, Phosphorus with torch raised before the Sun, Hesperus with torch lowered before the Moon: the Cautes-and-Cautopates antithesis written across the sky. Around the whole scene, in many reliefs, runs an arch or ring bearing the twelve signs of the zodiac, sometimes with the signs carved in full, sometimes abbreviated; stars stud the god's cloak and the cave's vault. The god's own titles, in the dedications, bind him to the sun: Sol Invictus Mithras, the Unconquered Sun Mithras, and Mithras and Sol appear together throughout the side scenes as allies who meet, clasp hands, and dine.

Here the line between showing and inferring can be drawn with unusual precision, and this article draws it. Shown: the scene is set under the sun and moon, inside the zodiac, among stars; its god is named with the sun's title; its room was a cave that a philosopher contemporary with the cult could describe as an image of the cosmos (Section X). The tauroctony is, demonstrably, a cosmic image — that much is in the carving, not in the interpreter. Not shown: any specific astronomical doctrine — which stars, which event, which teaching about the soul's road through the spheres. Those specifics are the work of the modern astral school (Section XII), and they stand or fall as modern arguments. The distinction matters because the cult's imagery is often raided, in both directions, by readers who want the zodiac ring to prove a whole astronomy, or who want to dismiss the astronomy because the whole cannot be proved. The ring proves a frame. The frame is real. What was taught inside it is gone.

The Side Scenes: A Myth in Pictures

On the grander reliefs the tauroctony is surrounded by small panels, read in sequence, which give the god something no text gives him: a story. The episodes recur across the corpus in a recognisable set, and their order can be reconstructed by comparing many examples — the reconstruction is modern work, principally of the corpus scholars, and individual panels are sometimes ambiguous; but the episode-list itself is read off the stones, and it is the closest thing to a Mithraic mythology that exists.

BORN FROM THE ROCK

The god emerges, already a youth, naked but for his cap, from a rock — the petra genetrix, the rock-that-gives-birth of the inscriptions — holding a dagger and a torch; shepherds sometimes watch. In an example from Housesteads on Hadrian's Wall he emerges instead from an egg ringed by the zodiac.

THE WATER MIRACLE

The god shoots an arrow into a rock, and water pours out, at which kneeling figures drink. The scene is regularly connected by interpreters with the wells and basins of the mithraea (Section IV) — a plausible link, and an inference.

THE HUNT AND THE CARRYING

Mithras hunts the bull, rides it, masters it, and hauls it — slung across his shoulders, dragged by the hind legs — toward the cave. The carrying scene, the transitus, explains how the bull arrives at the tauroctony's cave; the hunt gives the killing a narrative before.

SOL KNEELS; THE HANDSHAKE; THE BANQUET

Sol kneels before Mithras; the two gods clasp right hands as equals and allies; and they dine together, reclining on the hide of the killed bull, served by attendants — in some scenes by the torchbearers, in some by figures in animal masks. After the meal, in the final panels, Mithras ascends in Sol's chariot.

The banquet panel closes a loop the architecture opens: the god and Sol dine on the bull, on benches, in the cave — and below the relief the initiates dined on their benches in the same cave. That the meal understood itself against the pictured meal is the most natural inference the corpus offers. It remains an inference: no initiate wrote it down.

VIII. The Seven Grades

If the tauroctony is the cult's image, the grades are its skeleton: the one piece of Mithraic internal organisation that can be reconstructed in detail, because for once text and archaeology check each other. Mithraists were not simply members. They were initiated into, and advanced through, a sequence of named ranks, each with its own symbols, its own dress and implements in the cult's imagery, and — in the fullest evidence — its own tutelary planet. A man's grade is the thing his dedication most often records after his name. The hierarchy was the cult's social technology: it ranked a slave above a freeborn man if the slave stood higher on the ladder, and it gave every small community a Father, officers, and a path.

Corax · Nymphus · Miles · Leo · Perses · Heliodromus · Pater — THE SEVEN GRADE-NAMES IN ORDER, AS LISTED BY ST JEROME, LETTER 107.2 (403 CE)

The naming source is a single passage. Jerome, in his Letter 107 (§2), written in 403 CE to the Roman matron Laeta about her daughter's Christian education, remarks in passing — while praising a Christian city prefect who had smashed up a mithraeum and its images — the names of the grades through which, he says, the initiates pass: Corax (Raven), Nymphus (Bridegroom), Miles (Soldier), Leo (Lion), Perses (Persian), Heliodromus (Sun-Runner), Pater (Father). The passage is late, hostile, and incidental — Jerome is not describing the cult, he is celebrating its vandalism — and it is nonetheless the fullest ancient list we have. Even its text carries a small, instructive uncertainty: the second name has been read in the manuscript tradition both as Nymphus and as Cryphius, “the Hidden One”; the painted inscriptions of Santa Prisca (below) support Nymphus, and modern editions generally print it. The episode is a miniature of Mithraic method: the cult's most basic fact arrives through one enemy's aside, with a variant reading in it.

The Material Evidence: Santa Prisca and Ostia

Two sites turn Jerome's list into archaeology. The mithraeum beneath the church of Santa Prisca on Rome's Aventine — found in 1934 and excavated in the 1950s by Vermaseren and C. C. van Essen — preserves, uniquely, painted walls in two phases (c. 200 and c. 220 CE): processions of initiates in coloured tunics, some masked (one figure wears a raven's mask and carries a dish), bearing offerings toward enthroned figures of the cult, and beside the figures, painted inscriptions, the dipinti: acclamations hailing named men by grade and commending each grade to the protection of its planet — the Lions, for instance, acclaimed under Jupiter's guardianship, in the recurring nama formula, “hail!”, a word the cult took from the Iranian vocabulary. Here, in paint laid by initiates on their own wall, the grades walk in procession with their offerings in their hands: the hierarchy at work, not in an enemy's summary.

At Ostia, the Mithraeum of Felicissimus (Section V) lays the same system in stone: a mosaic running down the central aisle in seven panels, each filled with a cluster of symbols, ending in the panel that names Felicissimus, the pavement's donor. Read with Jerome's order and the Santa Prisca formulas, the panels resolve, grade by grade, into the ladder below — the assignment of panel to grade is an interpretation, but one in which text, painting, and mosaic, from three independent sources, converge.

The planetary column needs its label. The pairing of grade with planet rests on the Santa Prisca dipinti and on the symbols of the Ostia panels, and it is the standard reconstruction in the handbooks (Clauss's presentation is the usual reference). But note what the pairings do not do: they do not run in the order of the planets' spheres as ancient astronomy arranged them, and they do not match the one other ancient scheme that connects Mithras with a planetary ladder — the diagram the pagan critic Celsus described (preserved in Origen's Against Celsus 6.22), a ladder of seven gates of different metals, lead for Saturn up to gold for the Sun, with an eighth gate above, which Celsus connected with the soul's journey through the heavens. Whether Celsus's diagram was genuinely Mithraic, what it was for, and how it relates to the grades are all disputed. The handbooks' phrase “the planetary ladder”, implying that initiation was an ascent of the soul through the seven spheres, rung by rung, is an interpretation — a powerful one, held in various forms by Gordon and developed most fully by Beck — built by joining the grade-planet pairings to Celsus's ladder and to Porphyry's cosmic cave. The join is modern. The components are ancient. This article keeps them visibly separate, and the reader should too.

What the Grades Did

Of the grades' working life the evidence shows the outline and withholds the substance. The Pater presided: dedications name a community's Father, sometimes with a pater patrum, a Father of the Fathers, at Rome — a title whose authority beyond courtesy is unclear. The Leones, the Lions, appear in the Santa Prisca processions as the cult's offering-bearers and, in Porphyry's scattered remarks (Section X), as its central ritual grade, purified with honey rather than water — a detail Porphyry gives and no Mithraic source confirms. Painted and carved scenes at Santa Maria Capua Vetere and on a vessel from Mainz appear to show initiations themselves: a naked, blindfolded candidate, kneeling, hands bound, threatened with a sword or a torch, guided by an attendant. These are the only pictures of the rite that exist, they are fragmentary and partly restored, and every narrative built on them — ordeals, mock-deaths, oaths under threat — outruns them. Tertullian (Section X) reports one initiation detail from outside: the candidate for Miles was offered a crown on a sword's point and required to refuse it, declaring — in Tertullian's paraphrase of the formula — that Mithras was his crown. It is the most vivid ritual sentence to survive about the cult, and it survives in a Christian polemic about why Christians must not wear garlands. Such is the evidence for the grades: a ladder whose rungs are certain, whose climbing is almost wholly dark.

// SEVEN, EVERYWHERE? //

The seven-grade scheme is attested in full only in the Rome–Ostia evidence and Jerome. Inscriptions across the empire name Ravens, Lions, and Fathers in abundance, and several grades in between; whether every community, in every period, ran the full ladder of seven, or whether the complete system is a metropolitan form that the provinces knew in part, cannot be settled from the surviving record. The ladder in the table is the cult's best-documented form, not a certified universal.

IX. The Textual Near-Silence and the So-Called Mithras Liturgy

Section I stated the silence; this section walks around it, because the silence has a shape, and one famous text is forever being pushed into it. First, the shape. What the cult wrote, it wrote on stone and plaster: dedications, membership lists, the acclamations of Santa Prisca, a scatter of graffiti and painted verses — at Santa Prisca a few hexameter lines painted beside the cult image speak, in battered and disputed readings, of the god's deeds and the blood that saved; they are the nearest thing to Mithraic poetry in existence, and they amount to a handful of half-legible lines. A small number of brief ritual phrases survive at second hand in hostile authors (Section X). Beyond that: nothing. No hymn-book, no catechism, no myth-collection, no commentary, no letter of one initiate to another about the meaning of the bull. The contrast with the cult's neighbours is total — the Hermetic circles of the same centuries produced the treatises treated in this hub's Technical Hermetica, the Orphics produced commentaries and gold leaves, the Christians produced libraries. The Mithraists produced rooms.

Whether the silence means the cult had no texts, or had texts that perished unrecopied after its suppression, is a real question. What tips the balance toward “little was ever written” is the archaeology: in rooms that preserve altars, mosaics, painted acclamations, and cult vessels, no book-niche, scroll deposit, or doctrinal tablet cache has appeared — while the two Mithraic authors Porphyry names, Euboulus and Pallas, are known only as writers about the mysteries. The cult's chosen media were image, grade, and meal; the silence is continuous with everything else it did.

The “Mithras Liturgy”: A Name in Search of a Cult

Into this vacuum one text has been repeatedly inserted, and the hub's discipline requires it to be handled in the open. In the great Paris magical codex — the PGM IV of the Greek Magical Papyri, a fourth-century Egyptian spell-book — there stands a text, lines 475–829, describing a rite of immortalisation: breathing exercises, invocations, an ascent through the heavens past the planetary powers, a vision of a great god, and instructions for performing the whole. In 1903 the German scholar Albrecht Dieterich published it as Eine Mithrasliturgie — “A Mithras Liturgy” — because its presiding god is invoked as Helios Mithras, and he presented it as nothing less than a surviving liturgy of the Mithraic mysteries: the missing scripture, found.

It was not accepted then and it is not accepted now, and the reasons are evidential, not temperamental. Cumont himself rejected it: the text contains no bull, no cave-temple, no grades, no torchbearers, none of the cult's entire iconographic programme; its machinery — voces magicae, Egyptian gods, Greek philosophical vocabulary, the technology of the magical papyri — is the machinery of Graeco-Egyptian magic, in which “Helios Mithras” appears as one grand solar name among many. Its provenance compounds the problem: it was copied in Egypt, a province where the Roman mystery cult is archaeologically almost unattested. The positions in the modern literature, stated fairly, are three. The majority position (Cumont, and the main line of Mithraic scholarship after him, including Burkert and Gordon) holds that the text is not a Mithraic document at all, and that its conventional title is a misnomer that scholarship retains only because it is entrenched. Hans Dieter Betz, in his edition and commentary (2003), treats it as a product of the meeting of Greek, Egyptian, and Hermetic traditions in Egypt, in which Mithraic colouring is present but the cult of the mithraea is not the text's home. Marvin Meyer, its English translator in the standard PGM collection, has been the most willing of its modern editors to see real Mithraic connections in it. No Mithraic ritual text from a mithraeum, or from any controlled Mithraic context, exists against which the papyrus could be checked; until one does, the honest shelf-mark for PGM IV.475–829 is the one this archive uses: a Greek magical text of debated affiliations, conventionally but misleadingly called the Mithras Liturgy. The archive's own treatment of the papyri as a corpus stands in the Egyptology division, at The Greek Magical Papyri, and the text's Hermetic neighbours are treated at Technical Hermetica.

// WHY THE MISNOMER MATTERS //

The “Mithras Liturgy” is the single most common route by which invented Mithraism enters circulation: a real, vivid, quotable text — ascent, immortalisation, a supreme god — is quoted as the cult's own voice, and the whole theology of the papyrus is silently transferred to the men on the benches, who left no voice at all. The transfer is illegitimate even if some Mithraic influence on the papyrus is granted, for the same reason a Hermetic treatise cannot be quoted as the liturgy of a Hermes temple. In this hub the text is evidence for the religious imagination of Roman Egypt. For the mysteries of Mithras it is, at most, a disputed neighbour — and it is cited in this article only to be quarantined.

X. The Outsider Witnesses and Their Agendas

Everything written about Mithraism in antiquity was written by a non-initiate — or at best by a man whose initiation, if any, is unprovable — and each of the witnesses had work for the cult to do in his own argument. They are indispensable and unusable in the same way: each must be read with his agenda in view, credited where his detail is corroborated by the stones, and discounted where he is preaching. This section takes them one by one, in the order of their usefulness.

Porphyry: The Philosopher Who Read the Cave

Porphyry of Tyre (c. 234–c. 305 CE), Plotinus's pupil and editor, is the cult's most substantial ancient witness, and the most dangerous, because he is intelligent, sympathetic, and writing philosophy. His short treatise On the Cave of the Nymphs — a commentary on the cave of the nymphs in Odyssey 13 — digresses on the Mithraic cave, and in paraphrase its claims are these: that Zoroaster first consecrated a natural cave in the Persian mountains to Mithras, maker and father of all, as an image of the cosmos; that the mithraeum ever since has been such an image, its furnishings disposed as symbols of the cosmic elements and regions; that Mithras is seated at the equinoctial circle, bearing the sword of Aries and mounted on the Bull, as lord of generation; and that souls descend into generation and ascend again by the gates of the zodiac. In On Abstinence he adds the scattered grade-lore used in Sections III and VIII: the animal names, the Lions and their honey-purification, and references to the lost histories of Euboulus and Pallas.

Every word of that must be filed correctly. Porphyry is the source — often the only source — for the reading of the mithraeum as a cosmic model, and that reading has shaped the modern astral school (Section XII) more than any single ancient text. But On the Cave of the Nymphs is not a cult document. It is a Neoplatonic allegory of a Homeric passage, written by a philosopher whose system needed caves, descents, and cosmic symbols, in a tradition of allegoresis this hub traces in Neoplatonism and Theurgy. Porphyry may report genuine Mithraic self-understanding — he had sources now lost, and the cosmic furniture of the actual mithraea (zodiac rings, star-painted vaults) gives his reading material support no other allegorist enjoys. Or he may be doing to the mithraeum what his school did to Homer: finding his own philosophy in a venerable image. The evidence does not decide between these, and the difference between them is the difference between Mithraic doctrine and Neoplatonic reception. This article therefore cites Porphyry as what he demonstrably is: the cult's earliest systematic interpreter, standing outside it, in the third century — not its spokesman.

The Christian Witnesses: Rivals, Not Reporters

JUSTIN MARTYR (c. 150s CE)

In the First Apology (66) Justin says, in paraphrase, that in the mysteries of Mithras bread and a cup of water are set out with certain formulas in the rites of initiation — and explains the resemblance to the Eucharist as the work of demons who had imitated the Christian rite in advance. The detail (bread, cup, formulas, initiation) is early and plausible against the archaeology of the meal; the explanation is theology. The two must be separated by the reader, because Justin does not separate them.

TERTULLIAN (c. 200 CE)

Two passages carry weight. In On the Crown (15) he gives the Miles ordeal of the refused crown (Section VIII) — offered as a warning to Christian soldiers, and all the more credible as reportage for being incidental to his point. In On the Prescription of Heretics (40) he lists Mithraic parallels as diabolical counterfeits: Mithras, he says, marks his soldiers on their foreheads, celebrates an oblation of bread, and introduces an image of a resurrection. Forehead-marking and bread-oblation are the kind of detail polemic borrows from fact; the “image of a resurrection” is exactly the kind it invents, and no Mithraic source supports it.

FIRMICUS MATERNUS (c. 346 CE)

On the Error of the Pagan Religions, addressed to the emperors Constans and Constantius, is a prosecutor's handbook urging the extirpation of the cults, Mithras among them. Firmicus preserves what he presents as ritual watchwords and formulas of the mysteries he attacks — material that may derive from genuine leakage and cannot be verified from any insider source — inside rhetoric whose purpose is to have the temples closed. He is evidence for the fourth century's war on the cult (Section XIII) first, and for the cult's own practice only at a discount.

JEROME (403 CE)

The grade-list of Letter 107.2 (Section VIII) is the Christian witnesses' most valuable single gift to the record — valuable precisely because Jerome had no doctrinal use for the names and every reason of genre to get a throwaway list right. Incidental detail in a hostile source, corroborated by mosaics and wall-paintings, is as close to bedrock as this subject's texts come.

Plutarch: The Name Before the Cult

One pagan witness belongs to the cult's prehistory rather than its life. Plutarch, in On Isis and Osiris (46), explaining Persian dualism to Greek readers, says that the Persians call the good god Oromazes and the evil power Areimanios, and that between them stands Mithras, whom — he says — the Greeks therefore call the Mediator. It is a precious notice and a strictly limited one: it attests that an educated Greek of about 100 CE knew the Iranian Mithra as a cosmic intermediary in a dualist scheme. It says nothing about mysteries, grades, caves, or bulls, and it concerns the Persian god of Section XI, not the Roman cult — which, on the archaeological record, was just then coming into existence in the West. The pirates passage of the Pompey (Section II) is the same kind of evidence: real testimony to the name's eastern Mediterranean currency, endlessly over-promoted into a foundation charter for the Roman mysteries. Plutarch gives Mithraism a Persian prehistory of the name. He does not give it a Persian origin, and the question of origin must be settled, in Section XI, on other evidence.

// THE RULE FOR OUTSIDERS //

Credit an outsider where the stones agree with him (Jerome's grades, Justin's bread and cup, the meal on the benches). Note him, without crediting him, where he stands alone and interested (Tertullian's resurrection-image, Firmicus's watchwords). Treat him as a reader, not a witness, where he is expounding a system (Porphyry's cosmic cave, Celsus's ladder). Three different evidentiary values, three different uses — the whole textual method of Mithraic studies is in that sorting.

XI. The Persian Question: Miθra, Cumont, and Manchester 1971

No question in the subject has a longer history, or a cleaner reversal. The Roman god wears Persian dress, carries a Persian name, and was believed by Romans themselves to come from Persia. For the first seventy years of modern scholarship the matter seemed settled by those facts. It is now the position of the field's mainstream that the Roman mysteries are a Roman creation wearing Iranian colouring — and this section gives the question at its real strength, in its three acts: the Iranian god as he actually is in his own texts; the great reconstruction that derived the Roman cult from him; and the congress that dismantled it.

The Iranian Miθra, from the Avesta

The Iranian god is not shadowy; he has a scripture. In the Avesta, the Zoroastrian sacred corpus, Miθra (Middle Persian Mihr) is a yazata, a being worthy of worship, whose great hymn is the Mihr Yasht (Yasht 10), the longest of the Yashts — available in Ilya Gershevitch's standard edition and translation, The Avestan Hymn to Mithra (1959). The hymn's god is unmistakable, and his identity is in his name: miθra is the common noun for a contract, a covenant, a sworn bond, and the god is that bond personified and armed — Mithra of the wide pastures, of the thousand ears and ten thousand eyes, who cannot be deceived, who watches every agreement and falls in wrath on the oath-breaker, the miθrō.druj, the one who lies to the contract. He is guardian of cattle and of the waters that feed their pastures; he drives across the sky in a chariot, going before the sun, and in later tradition merges ever closer with the sun itself; he carries a mace against the demons. In the royal inscriptions he enters late, under Artaxerxes II, in the triad with Ahura Mazda and Anahita. He is a god of the open sky, of daylight inspection, of the herdsman's wealth and the king's sworn word.

Against that portrait, set the Roman mysteries, and the absences are as systematic as the correspondences are superficial. The Avesta knows no bull-slaying by Mithra — in the Zoroastrian creation myth told in later Pahlavi books, the primordial bull is killed by the evil spirit, and life proceeds from its body despite its killer, not by his hand. It knows no mystery initiation, no grades, no cave-temples, no communal reclining meal, no torchbearers, no community of men under a Father. The Roman cult's entire furniture is missing from the Iranian record, and the Iranian god's entire essence — the covenant, the oath, the contract watched by ten thousand eyes — is missing from the Roman one: no Mithraic inscription invokes Mithras as guardian of contracts. What the two share is the name, a solar association, cattle in the god's orbit, and the Persian costume in which the Roman god is dressed. That is the whole of the continuity the texts attest.

Cumont's Reconstruction, 1896–1899

Modern Mithraic studies were founded by one man's work of genius. Franz Cumont, the Belgian scholar, published his Textes et monuments figurés relatifs aux mystères de Mithra in 1896–1899 — the first full corpus — and with it a reconstruction that organised everything: Roman Mithraism, he argued, was Mazdaism in Roman dress, the ancient Iranian religion carried west by Iranian communities in Anatolia, preserved among the Magi of Cappadocia and the pirate-and-mercenary world of Cilicia (Plutarch's pirates were his transmission link), Hellenised in dress, Romanised in organisation, but in doctrine the old Persian faith — dualist, eschatological, centred on the bull-slaying as a cosmic act of salvation by which Mithras released the world's life and the souls of men. Cumont's Mithraism had a theology, a myth, a soteriology, and a history, and for seventy years it was Mithraism: in the handbooks, in the museums, in the imagination of everyone who wrote about the cult, including — this is the measure of its power — most of the popular claims this article declined to repeat in Section I.

Its weakness was visible from the start to anyone who checked its joints: the doctrine was not in the Roman evidence. Cumont had read the Iranian texts into the Roman images — supplying the tauroctony with the Zoroastrian creation myth (in which, as noted, Mithra does not kill the bull), the grades with a Persian priesthood, the cult with an eschatology no inscription mentions. The reconstruction was, in the strict sense, circular: the images were explained by the Iranian texts, and the Iranian texts' relevance was proved by the images. For two generations the circle held, because Cumont's corpus was indispensable and no rival account organised the material as completely.

Manchester 1971 and the Modern Position

The break came at the First International Congress of Mithraic Studies, held at Manchester in 1971 — the proceedings, edited by John R. Hinnells as Mithraic Studies (1975), remain the turning point's document. Two papers did the decisive work. Hinnells, an Iranist, examined Cumont's Iranian evidence and concluded that the reconstruction would not stand: the Roman cult's supposed Persian doctrine was unsupported by, and at points in conflict with, the Iranian sources themselves. Richard L. Gordon's paper, “Franz Cumont and the Doctrines of Mithraism” (published in the 1975 proceedings), dissected the method: Cumont had imposed a speculative theological system on fragmentary material, and the field had mistaken the system for the evidence. Roger Beck's great survey, “Mithraism since Franz Cumont” (1984), codified the post-congress situation, and the scholarship since — Beck's own work on the cult's astral language, Clauss's social and prosopographical studies, Gordon's essays on the grades and the imagery, Reinhold Merkelbach's account of the cult as a Roman foundation of the late first century — works within the space Manchester opened.

The modern position, stated with its proper edges, is this. The Roman mysteries of Mithras were a new creation of the Roman world in the late first century CE — most probably, on the distribution of the earliest evidence, in or near Rome itself — which took from Iran the god's name, his Persian costume, a handful of loan-words (nama; the grade-name Perses; the torchbearers' names have also been claimed as Iranian), and the prestige of an ancient eastern wisdom that Romans credited whether or not it was delivered. The mysteries' form — initiation, grades, communal meal, cave-room, bull-slaying image — is Greco-Roman work, built from materials the Roman world had at hand, including a classical sculptural type for its central image (Section VI). On this account the cult's Persian-ness is itself a Roman artefact: an invented antiquity, worn the way the god wears his cap.

// THE CONSENSUS, WITH ITS DISSENTERS //

The position above is the mainstream of the field since 1971 — Beck, Clauss, and Gordon are its representative names — but it is a mainstream with named dissent, and the archive records it. Robert Turcan defended, across a long career, a modified Cumontian view: a substantially Iranian cult transmitted through Anatolia and genuinely continuous in more than name. Others have argued for real contributions from the Iranian diaspora in the eastern provinces, or from the Greco-Iranian royal cults of Commagene, whose king Antiochus I set up images of a god he called Mithras in the first century BCE — images that are royal and public, not mysteries, but that prove the name's Anatolian currency. The dissent keeps the eastern contribution larger than the mainstream allows; it does not restore Cumont's system, and no current scholar defends the tauroctony as an Iranian myth. Where this article says “the modern position”, it means the position stated above, with this paragraph attached.

XII. The Astral Readings: Star-Map, Precession, and Their Critics

That the tauroctony is set among stars, zodiac signs, sun, and moon is in the carving (Section VII). What the stars say is the most contested question in modern Mithraic studies, and the home of its boldest thesis. This section presents the astral readings as what they are — modern constructions, of very different strengths — beginning with the correspondence table on which they all rest.

The readings then diverge, completely, on what the pattern means and who Mithras is within it.

SPEIDEL: MITHRAS-ORION (1980)

Michael P. Speidel's Mithras-Orion (1980) identified the god with Orion, the great equatorial constellation standing over Taurus — a soldier-scholar's reading for a soldiers' cult, argued from the god's position above the bull. It opened the modern astral debate; its specific identification has won few adherents against its rivals.

ULANSEY: PRECESSION AND PERSEUS (1989)

David Ulansey's The Origins of the Mithraic Mysteries (1989) is the boldest thesis in the field. Mithras, he argues, is Perseus, whose constellation stands directly above Taurus, Phrygian-capped and sword in hand; and the bull-slaying encodes the discovery of the precession of the equinoxes — the slow rotation of the whole celestial sphere, announced by Hipparchus in the second century BCE, by which the equinox had passed out of Taurus. The cult, on this reading, was founded in the intellectual shock of that discovery, among the Stoic circles of Tarsus in Cilicia, as the mystery of a god strong enough to have moved the cosmos itself: the killing of the Bull is the ending of the Age of Taurus. Noel Swerdlow, an historian of astronomy of the first rank, found the astronomical core of the thesis serious; the field as a whole has not followed Ulansey to his conclusion.

BECK: STAR-TALK, NOT A STAR-MAP

Roger Beck, in The Religion of the Mithras Cult in the Roman Empire (2006) and the papers around it, accepts the astral dimension and refuses the map. The tauroctony, for Beck, is not a chart to be decoded point for point but a composition in what he calls the cult's “star-talk” — a symbolic language of sun, moon, planets, and signs in which the mithraeum itself, the cosmic cave of Porphyry's reading, was the primary text, and in which Mithras needs no constellation because he is the sun-god in the scene, not a figure pasted onto the sky. Beck has been Ulansey's most consequential critic from inside the astral camp: the precession thesis, he argues, requires the cult to have preserved, as its central secret, an astronomical doctrine no ancient source attributes to it.

THE SCEPTICS

Other scholars — Gordon among them, in his essays on the imagery — resist the decoding enterprise itself: the correspondences, they note, are assembled by choosing, for each animal, the constellation that fits, from a sky full of animals; no ancient author says the tauroctony is a star-map; and a method that can identify the god as Perseus, Orion, or the Sun with equal facility has not identified him at all. On this view the image's astral furniture is real but generic — the cosmic frame of Section VII — and the specific decodings are modern system-building of the same species as Cumont's, with astronomy in place of Iran.

The archive's assessment, for the record: the astral frame is attested in the images themselves; the equatorial correspondence of the animal set is a genuine and unexplained datum; Ulansey's precession thesis is a modern construction of great elegance that no ancient text supports and the field has not adopted; Beck's star-talk is the most influential current framework precisely because it claims less. Readers who meet the tauroctony-as-star-map presented elsewhere as settled fact — it is so presented constantly — should know that its three leading proponents cannot agree on who the central figure is. The zodiac imagery of the cult is real, and its cautious use in this hub's Greek Astrology article follows the same rule: the frame is evidence, the decoding is argument.

XIII. The End: Suppression, Burial, and the Walbrook Mithraeum

Mithraism did not fade; it was ended, in the general ending of Rome's public paganism in the later fourth century — though its archaeology is more various than the word “suppression” suggests. The legal frame is clear: Gratian removed state funding from the old cults in 382, and the Theodosian decrees of 391–392 prohibited sacrifice and temple worship outright — the act under which rooms whose whole life was sacrifice and a sacred meal became impossible. No decree named Mithras, and none was needed.

On the ground, the endings differ by site, and the differences are data. Some mithraea were violently destroyed: cult images smashed, heads struck from reliefs, rooms wrecked and filled — destruction layers at a number of sites, some of them coin-dated to the late fourth century, record the physical fact, though whether the wreckers were Christian zealots, officials, or neighbours is rarely determinable, and the older literature's habit of assigning every smashed relief to a Christian mob outruns the evidence. Some mithraea were simply abandoned, their roofs fallen in on undisturbed benches. And some were closed with care: at Santa Prisca the mithraeum was walled up around 400 as the church rose above it; at several sites the cult sculptures were buried whole, in pits within the room — an act that reads, on its face, as the initiates' own last rite for their images, hiding them from the wreckers or laying them to rest. A cult that had never written its faith down ended, in places, by burying its pictures. Mithraism's last datable traces belong to the years around 400; claims of survival into the fifth century, in remote districts, rest on evidence Cumont himself advanced and later scrutiny withdrew.

Walbrook: The Famous Excavation

The one mithraeum the modern world knows by name stands in London. The Temple of Mithras at Walbrook was built about the middle of the third century on the bank of the Walbrook stream in Londinium, a standard mithraeum of the bench-and-aisle plan, which served its community for over a century; in its last phase, in the later fourth century, the building appears to have been rededicated — its Mithraic sculptures, including a fine marble head of the god and a tauroctony relief, were carefully buried beneath its floor, and figures of other gods, Bacchus among them, take the stage in the final layers. John Shepherd's monograph, The Temple of Mithras, Walbrook (1998), is the site's standard publication; even the rededication is an interpretation of the buried marbles, and it is labelled as one here.

The temple's second life began on 18 September 1954, when excavators under W. F. Grimes, clearing a bomb-site for the building of Bucklersbury House, uncovered the walls — and, on the dig's last scheduled day, the marble head of Mithras in his Phrygian cap. The discovery became a public sensation of a kind archaeology rarely achieves: contemporary reports describe queues in the tens of thousands to view the muddy foundations, and the temple was, by general consent, the most famous Roman find of twentieth-century London. Fame did not keep it in place. In 1962 the remains were dismantled and rebuilt at street level some hundred metres away, on Queen Victoria Street — a reconstruction on a car-park podium, at the wrong depth, in the wrong orientation, widely regretted. There the story reverses: the site was bought for Bloomberg's European headquarters, the 1962 reconstruction was taken down stone by stone, and the temple was rebuilt once more, this time on its original footprint and at its original depth, some seven metres below the modern street. It reopened in 2017 as the London Mithraeum, a public exhibition in the Bloomberg building — the buried god's room restored, as nearly as the twenty-first century can manage it, to the darkness it was built for. No other mithraeum has had so public a biography; and it is fitting that the cult's most famous survival should be a room, not a text.

XIV. What Cannot Be Known

This hub's Mysteries discipline ends every treatment with the negative inventory, and Mithraism's is the longest in the archive — not because the cult is poorly evidenced, but because its evidence is so copious in the classes that do not speak and so absolute in its silence in the class that would. It is set down here plainly, as the article's conclusion, because a reader who knows exactly what is not known knows Mithraism better than a reader furnished with a complete theology.

THE MYTH, AS TOLD

The side panels give a sequence of episodes — rock-birth, water-miracle, hunt, killing, banquet, ascent — arranged by modern comparison of the reliefs. The story as the initiates told it, with its names, speeches, and point, does not exist. We have the illustrations to a book that was never written, or was written and wholly lost.

THE MEANING OF THE KILLING

Creation, fertility, salvation, cosmic rotation, a star event, a loyalty emblem: the tauroctony has carried every one of these in the literature, each under a scholar's name, none under the cult's. What a Pater told a new Raven the image meant is unrecoverable, and may never have been a single thing in every community.

THE WORDS AND THE RITES

No liturgy, prayer, hymn (beyond Santa Prisca's painted fragments), oath, or password survives from inside, the hostile quotations of Firmicus excepted at their discount. The initiation's ordeals are known from two fragmentary picture-cycles and one sentence of Tertullian. The meal's formulas, beyond Justin's “certain words”, are unknown.

THE PROMISE

What initiation was believed to confer — protection in this life, a better road for the soul after death, fellowship, advancement, some combination — is inference from Porphyry's philosophy, Celsus's ladder, and the general shape of the mysteries. No Mithraic statement of the cult's promise exists. Tertullian's “resurrection” is his word, in his polemic, and stands in this inventory as the type of everything that must not be built on it.

THE FOUNDER AND THE FOUNDING

No founder is named in any ancient source; no founding event, place, or decade within the late first century can be fixed; whether the cult was one man's invention, a committee's, or a rapid crystallisation of existing materials is beyond the evidence. Merkelbach's Roman founder and Ulansey's Tarsian astronomers are both portraits painted into a blank.

THE INTERIOR LIFE

What it felt like to recline in the cave, to climb the grades, to call a customs clerk from the next station “Father” and mean it — the whole experiential substance of the cult — survives only in the arrangement of its rooms. The rooms are eloquent. They are not testimony.

Set against the inventory, the positive residue is not small. We know that for three centuries, in hundreds of small rooms from Britain to Syria, groups of a few dozen men — soldiers, clerks, merchants, slaves, and freedmen, ranked in seven grades under a Father — met in the dark before an image of a god in Persian dress killing a bull beneath the sun, the moon, and the ring of the zodiac, and ate together in his presence. We know the rooms, the image, the grades, the names, the map, and the end. Everything beyond that which calls itself Mithraic doctrine is a modern construction, standing on a labelled scholar's authority — and this cult, present everywhere in stone and silent in its own voice, is the archive's standing emblem of why the labels matter.
