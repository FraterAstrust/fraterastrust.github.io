I. A Workshop, Not a System: What This Stratum Is

Greek alchemy does not begin with a theory of the universe. It begins with a set of workshop procedures for making inexpensive materials look, weigh, ring, and wear like expensive ones: copper alloys coloured yellow until they pass for gold, white alloys that pass for silver, clear quartz coloured green until it passes for emerald, and wool coloured with cheap dyestuffs until it passes for Tyrian purple. The oldest stratum of the Greek alchemical tradition — the material gathered under the names of Bolos of Mendes and of the pseudo-Democritus, the anonymous author (or authors) who wrote as Democritus of Abdera — is a craft literature. Its founding text, the Physika kai Mystika, is lost as a complete work and survives only in fragments, epitomes, and quotations. What those fragments contain, overwhelmingly, is recipes.

That plain statement needs defending, because later centuries loaded this material with meanings it did not originally carry. There is, in this stratum, no philosopher's stone. There is no elixir of life, no universal medicine, no programme for the perfection of the soul through the perfection of metals. Those ideas belong to later developments — to the generation of Zosimos of Panopolis around 300 CE, who quotes pseudo-Democritus constantly and builds something larger on him, and to the Arabic and Latin traditions after that. What the Bolos / pseudo-Democritus stratum does have is three things that proved durable: a fourfold organisation of the art (gold, silver, stones, purple); a physical framework — sympathy and antipathy between “natures” — that purported to explain why the recipes work; and an initiatory frame story, in which the recipes are temple secrets revealed by a Persian master. Craft, physics, and legend arrive together, in the same short texts. Keeping them distinct, while showing how they interlock, is the work of this article.

A note on method, because this archive's standing rule for repeatable processes applies here with full force: the recipes are presented below as workshop texts, step by step, in the sequence the sources give, with quantities where the sources give quantities. Where a modern chemical explanation helps the reader see what a procedure actually does to the metal or the stone, that explanation is offered — and is flagged, every time, as a modern explanation. The practitioners did not think in terms of oxidation, selective dissolution, or copper salts, and this article does not dress modern chemistry in their clothes. Conversely, where the ancient text explains itself — in terms of natures delighting in, conquering, and mastering one another — that explanation is given in its own terms, in Section XVII. The fullest surviving parallels to this recipe tradition, the third- to fourth-century CE workshop papyri from Thebes, receive their complete treatment in the Egyptology division — in the Zosimos article (§§IX–X) and in the Alchemy article (§XXIV) — and are used here to illuminate the earlier material, not duplicated.

ATTESTED

A recipe tradition organised in four books — on gold, on silver, on precious stones, on purple — ascribed to Democritus, quoted extensively by Zosimos, Synesius, and the later Greek commentators; substantial recipe overlap with the Leyden Papyrus X and the Stockholm Papyrus.

DEBATED AMONG SPECIALISTS

The date of Bolos (3rd or 2nd century BCE, conventionally c. 200 BCE); whether Bolos himself wrote any of the alchemical recipes; and the date of the alchemical pseudo-Democritus, whom Matteo Martelli's edition places in the second half of the 1st century CE, most likely c. 54–68 CE.

LEGEND, LABELLED AS SUCH

The initiation of Democritus by the Persian mage Ostanes in the temple at Memphis; the dead master's books discovered inside a temple column; the evocation of Ostanes from Hades. These are the texts' own frame stories, reported in Section VII as the tradition's self-presentation, not as biography.

NOT PRESENT IN THIS STRATUM

No philosopher's stone; no elixir; no transmutation doctrine in the later sense of a universal agent perfecting all metals; no apparatus literature of the kind Maria the Jewess and Zosimos introduce. The art here makes surfaces, alloys, and imitations — and says so.

II. Mendes and the Delta: The World Behind the Recipes

Mendes — Egyptian Djedet, Greek Mendēs — was a substantial city of the north-eastern Nile Delta, capital of the sixteenth Lower Egyptian nome, sacred to the ram god Banebdjedet. By the Hellenistic period it sat inside the most technically accomplished craft economy of the Mediterranean world. Egypt's temple and palace workshops had been working gold, silver, electrum, bronze, faience, glass, and coloured stones for three thousand years before Bolos; its dyers handled linen and wool with mordants and vat dyes of great sophistication; and its Delta ports traded with Phoenicia — the source of the murex purple that gave the fourth book of the pseudo-Democritean corpus its subject — and with the Aegean, Cyprus, and the Levant. The recipes in this tradition presuppose that economy at every line: Cyprian copper, Chian earth, Cappadocian salt, Italian antimony, misy and sory from the mines, Cadmean earth from Cyprus, saffron from Cilicia and Cnidus. The ingredient lists are trade maps.

Over that Egyptian craft base lay a Greek intellectual superstructure. After Alexander's conquest (332 BCE) and the founding of Alexandria, Greek natural philosophy — above all the atomism of Democritus, the physics of the Stoics with its cosmic sympathy, and the pharmacology descending from Theophrastus and Dioscorides — met Egyptian workshop practice in the same cities and, this article will argue following Jack Lindsay's The Origins of Alchemy in Graeco-Roman Egypt (1970), in the same texts. The characteristic product of that meeting is a recipe that comes with a reason: not merely “do this,” but “do this, because this nature delights in that one.” F. Sherwood Taylor, in his histories of Greek alchemy, made the same point from the other side: the earliest alchemical writings are continuous with the technical papyri and the dyers' and goldsmiths' handbooks, and only gradually acquire the mystical vocabulary of the later corpus.

// WHY EGYPT, WHY THEN //

The conjunction that produced this literature — hereditary temple crafts, a monetised market hungry for precious materials, Greek philosophical vocabularies available for hire, and a book trade that rewarded famous ancient names — existed in Ptolemaic and Roman Egypt and nowhere else in quite that form. The recipes' geography is not an accident of survival; it is the tradition's birthplace showing through.

One more feature of the setting matters for everything that follows: precious materials were expensive, tested, and policed. Gold and silver coinage and plate were assayed — the Leyden Papyrus includes recipes for testing the purity of gold, silver, and tin, and for detecting adulteration — and the same handbook that teaches a workman how to double his asem teaches him how the fraud can be found out. The line between the legitimate jeweller stretching a costly material, the dyer imitating a costly colour openly for a cheaper market, and the counterfeiter was drawn differently in antiquity than a modern reader expects; the papyri themselves frequently acknowledge that their products are imitations. Section XX returns to the question of fraud. For now it is enough to hold the economic fact: a technology that could make eight drachmas of silver-white alloy do the work of sixteen was worth money, worth writing down, and worth keeping secret — which is precisely the social setting the word mystika describes.

III. Bolos of Mendes: The Man and the Dating Problem

Almost everything known about Bolos comes at second or third hand, and the honest way to present him is to present the dossier and its cracks together. The Byzantine encyclopedia known as the Suda (10th century CE) contains two entries under the name Bolos: one for a Pythagorean philosopher of Mendes who wrote on marvels, potent remedies, and astronomical phenomena, and one for a Bolos of the school of Democritus, author of an Inquiry and a Medical Art containing material on natural sympathies. The Roman agricultural writer Columella (1st century CE), who cites a Bolos of Mendes on farming matters, supplies the passage from which scholars have generally concluded that the two Suda entries concern one man: a Mendesian writer of the Democritean persuasion, with Pythagorean colouring, who compiled works on natural properties, medicine, and agriculture. The standard modern reference summary — the Dictionary of Scientific Biography entry on Bolos of Mendes, consulted for this article — concludes the same, and adds the judgement that in antiquity Bolos was “widely read,” his reputation as an authority in natural history at times rivalling Aristotle's.

The date is genuinely debated, and this article gives it as a range. Bolos is conventionally placed around c. 200 BCE — floruit in the 3rd or 2nd century BCE. The fixed points are these: he appears to have known Theophrastus' Historia Plantarum (Theophrastus died c. 287 BCE), which puts him after the early 3rd century; and he was already an authority for writers of the later Hellenistic and early Roman periods. Beyond that, precision dissolves. Matteo Martelli, in The Four Books of Pseudo-Democritus (2013) — the standard modern edition of the alchemical material — reviews the proposals for Bolos' date at pages 37–41 and treats the conventional placement as exactly that: conventional. Some discussions of the Democritean material have argued for later dates for parts of the corpus circulating under his name. Where the older literature is quoted below, the equation it assumes is flagged.

IV. The Works Under Bolos' Name: Sympathies, Things Made by Hand, Farming

None of Bolos' works survives complete. What survives is a wake: citations, excerpts, and attributions in Pliny the Elder, Aelian, Columella, the Hermetic compilations, and the Byzantine agricultural collection known as the Geoponica. From that wake, four bodies of material can be reconstructed with reasonable confidence.

1. On Sympathies and Antipathies — the Physika

Bolos' best-known and most influential work circulated under titles rendered as Natural Properties (Physika) and as On Sympathies and Antipathies. Its project, as the Dictionary of Scientific Biography summary puts it, was to catalogue observed biological and ecological relationships — which creatures, plants, and stones help, harm, attract, or repel one another — and to explain them through supposed affinities and aversions between the entities concerned, figured almost as conscious “loves and hates.” The remora that stops the ship, the elephant's fear, the herb that counteracts the venom: the Physika gathered hundreds of such pairings, largely marvellous in content, and organised them by a single explanatory principle. Section XVII examines that principle as the physics of the later recipe books; here the point is bibliographical. The sympathy framework is Bolos' signature contribution to the tradition, and it is older than any surviving alchemical recipe ascribed to the Democritean school.

2. Cheirokmēta — Things Made by Hand

The Cheirokmēta — the title means, roughly, “things made or effected by hand,” artificial or hand-worked products — was usually cited under Democritus' name rather than Bolos' own. It dealt, on the surviving evidence, with medical and magical herbs and with agricultural practices: the manipulation of natural materials by art to produce effects nature alone does not supply. The word matters for the history of alchemy beyond this article: cheirokmēta is also the title Zosimos gives to parts of his own encyclopedic corpus, a deliberate act of affiliation with the Democritean workshop tradition, treated in the Egyptology division's Zosimos article (§V). Already in the title, hand-work — cheir, the hand — is the point: these are things made, not things found.

3. On Farming — the Georgika and the Geoponica Debt

Bolos wrote on agriculture — a work cited by Columella as On Farming (Peri geōrgias) — and the Democritean agricultural material descending from him was excerpted for a millennium. Wellmann's 1921 edition assembled eighty-two surviving fragments of the Democritean Georgika. The tenth-century Byzantine Geoponica, the great surviving Greek farming compilation, drew heavily and repeatedly on the Democritus/Bolos tradition: its precepts on grafting, pest control, weather signs, and sympathetic plant lore carry the Mendesian compiler's fingerprints throughout, transmitted through intermediaries such as Vindonius Anatolius and Cassianus Bassus. H. J. Rose's 1933 study of the folklore of the Geoponica states the attribution plainly, in the form this article adopts: the supposed extracts from Democritus treating of magic, sympathy, and antipathy have been referred — plausibly, in Rose's word — to Bolos of Mendes, an author of the Alexandrian period. The agricultural afterlife matters here because it shows the scale of the pseudepigraphic machine: by the time the Geoponica was compiled, “Democritus” on farming meant Bolos' tradition, and nobody involved found that remarkable.

4. On Stones, and the Rest of the Dossier

A On Stones (Peri lithōn) is also ascribed to Bolos: a catalogue of precious and semiprecious stones and their powers, in the manner that foreshadows the later lapidary tradition — the direct ancestor, in spirit, of the stones book of the Four Books, where the interest shifts from the stone's occult virtues to the workshop problem of counterfeiting its colour (Section XIV). Separate writings on medicine, on the history of the Jews, and on other subjects were credited to him in antiquity as well; of most of them, few identifiable fragments remain. The dossier as a whole describes not a specialist but a compiler-encyclopedist of the hidden properties of things — exactly the profile that could plausibly stand behind a recipe collection organised by sympathy, and exactly the profile that made his adopted name worth borrowing.

V. Why Democritus? The Pseudo-Democritus Problem

Democritus of Abdera (c. 460–370 BCE) was the co-founder, with Leucippus, of atomism: the doctrine that reality consists of indivisible atoms moving in void, and that the sensible qualities of things — colour, taste, heat — are arrangements, not essences. He wrote nothing about making gold. Yet by the Hellenistic period his name had become a magnet for precisely the literature he never wrote: works on sympathies, on agriculture, on stones, on the making of precious materials. Modern scholarship files all of it under pseudo-Democritus, and the question is why this name, of all names, attracted the workshop corpus.

Three reasons, mutually reinforcing, are visible in the evidence. First, suitability of doctrine. Atomism makes material transformation thinkable in a way few other Greek systems do: if the difference between copper and gold is a difference of arrangement and admixture, then an art that rearranges and admixes is, in principle, imitating nature's own method. Later alchemists who presented their theories as interpretations of Democritus were exploiting a real affinity between the borrowed name and the borrowed doctrine. Second, the traveller legend. From the first century BCE onward, Greek and Roman authors increasingly credited early philosophers with formative journeys to Egypt, Persia, and Babylon. Democritus was swept into that pattern early and thoroughly: he was said to have learned from the Chaldaeans, from the Magi who accompanied Xerxes, and above all from Ostanes, the legendary Persian mage. A philosopher who had been initiated in Egypt and Persia could plausibly possess Egyptian temple recipes; the legend manufactured the credentials the recipes required. Third, the economics of authority. In a manuscript culture, an anonymous recipe is a claim; a recipe by Democritus is an inheritance. The name sold, protected, and authorised the text — and, once attached, was defended by every copyist and commentator whose own work leaned on it.

// PSEUDEPIGRAPHY IS NOT A SCANDAL HERE //

Ancient readers did not experience attribution the way modern scholarship does. A work “of Democritus” could mean: from his school, from his tradition, in his manner, or simply — shelved under his name. The fraud question is real for individual recipes sold as gold-making (Section XX), but the borrowed name itself was a normal literary institution of the Hellenistic book world, applied to Pythagoras, Hermes, Zoroaster, and Moses with the same freedom.

The consequence for the reader of this archive is a discipline of naming. In what follows, “Democritus” inside a summary of an ancient claim means the figure the ancient text presents; pseudo-Democritus means the anonymous historical author(s) of the alchemical Four Books as modern scholarship reconstructs them; and Bolos means the Mendesian compiler of the 3rd–2nd century BCE, whose relation to the alchemical text is that of fountainhead to a later redaction — close, formative, and not identity. Martelli's edition is the authority followed for that three-way distinction throughout this article.

VI. The Physika kai Mystika: A Lost Book and Its Fragments

The founding text of Greek alchemy does not exist. That sentence is not a flourish; it is the bibliographical situation, and this article states it as plainly as the task of the archive requires: the Physika kai Mystika is a lost work. No manuscript of the complete Four Books survives. What survives, on Martelli's reconstruction, is a set of epitomised extracts preserved in two extant treatises — the Physika kai Mystika itself, in the abridged form transmitted by the Byzantine alchemical corpus, and a separate treatise On the Making of Silver (Peri asēmou poiēseōs) — together with a collection of substance lists, the Katalogoi, and a sediment of quotations, summaries, and paraphrases in Zosimos, Synesius, pseudo-Olympiodorus, Stephanus, Christianus, and the anonymous commentators. The oldest great manuscript of the corpus, the Marcianus graecus 299 (10th–11th century, Venice), already contains the work in epitome: even our earliest witness is a digest of a digest, a thousand years downstream of its author.

The Title, Translated Carefully

Physika kai Mystika (Φυσικὰ καὶ μυστικά) is conventionally Englished in several ways, and the differences are not cosmetic. Lawrence Principe, in The Secrets of Alchemy (2013), discusses the title directly: it is often translated “Physical and Mystical Things,” but that rendering misleads, because Greek mystika in this period did not mean “mystical” in the modern sense of inward spiritual experience; it meant things to be kept secret — the adjective of the mysteries, of what is disclosed only to initiates. Principe's preferred rendering is “Natural and Secret Things”: physika, matters of nature, and mystika, the secret parts of the craft — secret, on his reading, in the practical sense that lucrative artisanal processes are trade secrets. Other translators, keeping the initiatory colouring of mystika in view, render the title “Natural and Initiatory Matters.” This article uses Physika kai Mystika untranslated after this section, and asks the reader to hold both halves of the word's range at once: these are nature-recipes that present themselves as initiates' knowledge. The frame story of Section VII shows the text insisting on the second half; the recipes of Sections X–XV show the first half doing the work.

Berthelot and Ruelle: How the Fragments Reached Us in Print

The modern study of the text begins with Marcellin Berthelot and Charles-Émile Ruelle's Collection des anciens alchimistes grecs (Paris, 1887–88) — the edition, French translation, and vast introductory study that first made the Greek alchemical corpus, pseudo-Democritus included, available as a whole. Berthelot's accompanying Les origines de l'alchimie (1885) argued the thesis that still structures the field: that alchemical doctrine grew out of the documented practices of Egyptian goldsmiths, dyers, and glassworkers — out of imitation and falsification techniques preserved in the Leyden papyrus — rather than descending from pure speculation. The English renderings of the Physika kai Mystika quoted and paraphrased in this article follow the Berthelot–Ruelle text as transmitted in that edition (the Marcianus manuscript being their principal witness); Martelli's 2013 edition, with its own text and translation of the Physika kai Mystika, the On the Making of Silver, the Katalogoi, and Synesius' commentary, is the current scholarly standard and the authority for the datings and attributions used here.

// READING A FRAGMENTARY TEXT HONESTLY //

Because the work survives in epitome, arguments from its silence are worthless: that the surviving extracts say little about (for example) stones compared with gold reflects, as Martelli notes, the interests of the later excerptors, who privileged gold- and silver-making — not necessarily the proportions of the lost original. Every claim in Sections VIII–XV is therefore framed as a claim about the transmitted tradition, whose centre of gravity we can see, rather than about a book we cannot open.

VII. The Frame Story: Ostanes, Memphis, and the Column in the Temple

What follows in this section is the text's own frame story — the legend the tradition tells about itself. It is labelled legend throughout, because that is what it is: a charter myth for a recipe book, composed to explain where secret knowledge comes from and why the reader may trust it.

The summary given by Synesius (c. 373–414 CE) in his commentary-letter on Democritus' book — preserved and edited by Martelli — runs, in paraphrase, as follows. Democritus came from Abdera in Thrace, a natural philosopher who investigated all natural questions; but he became truly wise when he went to Egypt and was initiated in the temple of Memphis, together with the Egyptian priests, by the great Ostanes, the Persian mage. From Ostanes he took his first principles, and he composed four books on dyeing: on gold, on silver, on stones, and on purple. The initiation claim does double duty in the legend: it gives the Greek philosopher Egyptian temple credentials — Memphis being among the most venerable cult centres of Egypt, seat of Ptah, patron of craftsmen — and Persian magical credentials in a single scene, welding the two prestige traditions of secret knowledge into one pedigree.

The Physika kai Mystika's own opening narrative, as transmitted in the Berthelot–Ruelle text, is stranger and more specific, and is paraphrased here closely because its details became canonical for the whole later tradition. The author and his fellow pupils had gathered their master's teaching on the diversity of matter and were labouring to make the natures agree when the master died before they were initiated — before, that is, he had imparted the final secret. They attempted to evoke him from Hades. The shade, constrained, could say almost nothing: in the Berthelot–Ruelle rendering, its whole utterance is the sentence, “The books are in the Temple.” Returning to the temple and finding nothing by inquiry, the pupils were later feasting together in the sanctuary when a column suddenly opened — and inside it, where no one had told them anything was hidden, they found the master's books, and in them the whole of his writing gathered into a single short formula: the aphorism of the natures, treated in Section XVIII. The narrative then pivots, without transition, into recipes — as if to say: the formula is the theory, and the rest of the book is what it looks like at the bench.

// WHAT A FRAME STORY IS FOR //

Read functionally, the legend solves three practical problems for a trade-secret text. It explains why the knowledge is written at all (the master died; the books are his testament). It explains why it is incomplete (the initiation never happened; the reader, like the pupils, must work out the compositions for himself). And it sets the price of entry: the recipes that follow are temple property, to be handled as initiates handle sacred things. Every later alchemical preface that complains of a dead master, a hidden book, or an oath of secrecy is spending currency minted here.

Ostanes himself belongs to the legend's furniture. A figure of that name — a Persian magus attached, in various accounts, to the courts of Xerxes or of Alexander — recurs across Hellenistic magic, astrology, and pharmacology as the fountainhead of Persian secret wisdom for Greek readers. Martelli's discussion (pp. 69–73) treats the Ostanes of the Four Books as a literary authority-figure of that tradition, not as a recoverable historical teacher. The archive's rule applies: the initiation at Memphis is evidence — excellent evidence — for how the Roman-period craft imagined its own origins. It is not evidence for an event in the life of a fifth-century atomist who, on the chronology alone, had been dead for some four centuries when the scene is set.

VIII. The Four Books: Gold, Silver, Stones, Purple

Synesius' summary, Zosimos' citations, and the surviving epitomes agree on the architecture of the lost work. The Four Books were four books on dyeing:

BOOK I — ON GOLD

Chrysopoeia, gold-making: how to give base metals a yellow tinge — to dye them gold — and how to augment and double gold itself. The longest shadow in the tradition; the surviving Physika kai Mystika epitome is dominated by it.

BOOK II — ON SILVER

Argyropoeia, silver-making: whitening copper, iron, tin, and lead into silver-like bodies, and the manufacture of asem, the white alloy that stood in for silver. A separate epitome, On the Making of Silver, preserves this book's line of transmission.

BOOK III — ON STONES

The dyeing of stones: colouring rock crystal and other cheap transparent minerals to pass as emerald, ruby, beryl, amethyst, and the rest; improving and imitating pearls. Preserved most fully not in the epitomes but in the Stockholm Papyrus (Section XIV).

BOOK IV — ON PURPLE

The dyeing of wool purple: genuine murex dyeing and, at greater length, its imitation with lichens, madder, alkanet, and other cheaper dyestuffs fixed with mordants. The Physika kai Mystika as transmitted opens with purple recipes (Section XV).

Two features of that table deserve emphasis. First, the unity of the four subjects. To a modern reader, metallurgy, gemmology, and textile dyeing are three trades. To this tradition they are one art, because they are one operation — the fixing of a precious colour in a receptive body — performed on four kinds of matter. The same vocabulary (baphē, dyeing; katabaphē, immersion-dyeing) runs through all four books; the same mordant-like auxiliaries (alum, urine, vinegar, brine) recur in the metal recipes and the wool recipes alike; and the same explanatory refrain follows the recipes in every book. Section IX takes up that unity as the tradition's central idea. Second, the centre of gravity is imitation. Each book is organised around a precious material and the problem of producing its appearance — and, so far as the workshop could manage it, its working properties — from cheaper stock. The art begins, and in this stratum remains, the production and imitation of precious materials. Later alchemy's claim to transmute in earnest is a reinterpretation of this workshop inheritance, not its original content.

The preserved extracts, as Martelli notes, skew heavily toward Books I and II: the later Greek alchemists who excerpted and commented on pseudo-Democritus cared about gold and silver above all, and the stones and purple books survive disproportionately through the parallel papyrus tradition rather than through the commentary tradition. That skew is itself a historical datum — it is the moment the fourfold workshop art begins to narrow into the gold-obsessed art of the later corpus — and it is why this article leans on the Leyden and Stockholm papyri for Sections XIV and XV: they preserve, in working form, the half of the Four Books that the commentators let slip.

IX. The Language of the Art: Baphē — Everything Is a Dyeing

The technical vocabulary of the Four Books is the vocabulary of the dye-house, and learning to hear it is the fastest way into the practitioners' own understanding of what they were doing. The master-word is baphē (βαφή): a dipping, a dyeing, the colour taken up by a body immersed in a prepared liquor. A metal treated with a yellowing preparation is not, in the text's own terms, transmuted; it is dyed. The recipes speak accordingly of dyeing silver yellow to make it gold, of dye liquors (bammata) compounded to a wax-like consistency and smeared on leaf, of immersion times counted in days, of colours that must “penetrate to the centre” of the leaf, and of results judged by whether the colour pleases and whether it resists fire. The wool-dyer's criteria — fastness, depth, evenness — are the gold-maker's criteria, unchanged.

Around that master-word cluster the operation-words of the bench. Projection (epibolē in the later vocabulary; the epitome speaks of throwing or projecting a preparation onto a molten or heated body) is the moment a prepared powder or paste meets the metal. Fixing is the rendering of a volatile ingredient — above all mercury — non-volatile by combination, so that it stays in the work instead of flying off in the fire. Whitening and yellowing name the two great colour-operations on metals, exactly parallel to bleaching and dyeing in the cloth trade. And presiding over the whole vocabulary stands the word physis, “nature”: every ingredient and every metal is a nature, with its own affinities, and a recipe is a choreography of natures. When the epitome pauses after a recipe to remark that salt cleans copper inside and out, that mercury whitens a body within and on its surface, that a drop of oil can destroy a quantity of purple and a little sulphur burn many bodies — it is thinking, in its own idiom, about penetration, dosage, and disproportionate effect. The idiom is not ours. It is not empty, either.

X. Chrysopoeia in Practice: Yellowing Metals, Step by Step

The surviving epitome of the Physika kai Mystika (Berthelot–Ruelle text, §§4–19 in the numbering of the translation consulted) presents its gold-making as a repertoire, not a single process: a family of yellowing preparations, each applicable to several bodies, each closed by the refrain of the natures. What follows reconstructs the working sequence of that repertoire faithfully from the transmitted recipes, paraphrased — the epitome's own prose is too compressed, and its ingredient names too contested, for responsible verbatim quotation at length. Three representative procedures carry the whole logic of the book.

Procedure 1: Fixing Mercury, Whitening Copper, Yellowing to Gold (§§4–5)

Step 1 — fix the mercury. Take mercury and “fix” it — render it solid and fire-resistant — by combining it with the “metallic body of magnesia,” or with Italian antimony (stibnite), or with unburnt (“apyrous”) sulphur, or selenite, baked limestone, alum of Melos, or arsenic. The recipe offers the alternatives without ranking them: the workshop is expected to know its stock. Step 2 — whiten the copper. Lay the white earth so prepared on copper, and the copper becomes, in the text's phrase, copper “without shade” — bright, its dark surface gone. Step 3 — yellow to gold. Add “yellow silver” (a gold-bearing silver, i.e. electrum-grade material) to the whitened copper and the product is gold; applied to gold itself, the same preparation yields the richest grade, called in the text chrysocorail, “gold-coral.” The recipe then generalises: the same effect is obtained with yellow arsenic (orpiment) and sandarac (realgar) properly treated, and with cinnabar “completely transformed” — and it closes with the refrain, in the Berthelot–Ruelle rendering: nature triumphs over nature.

Modern explanation (flagged as such). Nothing in this procedure creates gold from copper. What the ingredients can genuinely do is transform the surface and the alloy colour: mercury amalgamates with and brightens copper surfaces; arsenic and sulphur compounds form coloured surface films and, alloyed in small percentages, shift copper's colour toward pale gold; and the final step frankly adds a gold-bearing material, so that the product is a gold-bearing alloy whose surface the earlier steps have prepared to show the colour well. The recipe's internal logic — whiten first, then yellow — mirrors the dyer's logic of preparing a ground before laying a colour, and it is that logic, not any transmutation, that the sequence actually executes.

Procedure 2: The Yellowing Earths — Pyrites, Cadmia, Cinnabar (§§6–10)

A second family of recipes works not with mercury but with roasted mineral earths. Take silver pyrites (also called siderite): make it fluid with grey or white litharge or Italian antimony, sprinkle in lead — the text is precise: not any lead, but “the lead of Coptos” — and black litharge, heat, add “artificial yellow,” and dye. Or treat pyrites until it is incombustible and has lost its black colour, working it with brine, uncorrupted urine, sea water, or oxymel (honey-vinegar) until it resembles gold flakes that have not seen fire; then mix in unburnt sulphur, yellow alum, or Attic ochre — and add silver to obtain gold, gold to obtain the “golden shell.” Or take Cyprian cadmia (the zinc-bearing furnace earth), bleach it by custom, then yellow it with calf's bile, turpentine, castor oil, radish oil, or egg yolks — the recipe lists the yellowing agents as interchangeable carriers of the yellow nature — and throw the mixture on gold. Each recipe ends with the refrain, varied: nature enjoys nature; nature rules nature; nature victorious dominates nature.

Modern explanation (flagged as such). Roasting sulphide ores such as pyrites drives off sulphur and converts dark minerals into pale, friable oxides and sulphates — a real and dramatic colour change, from black-brass to gold-flecked, that a workshop could watch happening in the crucible. Brine, urine, and vinegar are pickling liquors: they clean oxides and brighten surfaces. Bile, oils, and egg yolk are organic media that carry fine powders onto a surface and carbonise or glaze in gentle heat. None of this makes gold; all of it makes colour — which is what the book's own title for the operation, dyeing, always said.

Procedure 3: The Liquors — Dyeing Leaf by Immersion (§§16–18)

The epitome's third family abandons the crucible for the dyepot. Grind Pontic rhubarb in harsh Aminian wine to a wax-like consistency; spread it on silver leaf to a fingernail's thickness — then thinner; seal the leaf in a new, well-luted vessel and heat gently “until it penetrates to the centre of the leaf”; return the leaf to the rest of the preparation, then melt — “and you will find gold.” If the rhubarb is old, restore it with an equal quantity of celandine, “which has an affinity for rhubarb.” Variants use Cilician saffron diluted in vine juice as an immersion liquor for silver or (better, the text says) purified copper leaf, and a compounded liquor of saffron, safflower, celandine, and birthwort in strong vinegar, with a little sulphur, in which lead is soaked “and you will find gold.”

Modern explanation (flagged as such). Rhubarb root, celandine, and saffron are all powerful yellow dyestuffs of the cloth trade; prolonged immersion of thin metal leaf in hot, acidic, dye-laden liquor, followed by gentle sealed heating, stains and films the surface a durable yellow. On leaf thin enough, a surface film is most of what there is — the recipe's insistence on thinness and on penetration “to the centre” is the practitioner's recognition of exactly that. The product would not survive an assayer's touchstone or cupel, and the tradition knew about touchstones; what it would survive is the eye, at the distance of a market stall or a dinner table. That was the specification the recipe was written to meet.

Again and again the recipes say “according to custom,” “as you will hear,” “treat as usual.” These are not evasions; they are the marks of a text written for practitioners who already possessed the tacit half of the art — furnace management, timing, the look of a melt — and needed only the proportions and the order of operations. A recipe tradition that must say everything says nothing of the kind. The compression is evidence that a living workshop stood behind the book.

XI. Argyropoeia: Whitening Metals and the Making of Asem

Book II's operation is Book I's in mirror image: where chrysopoeia yellows, argyropoeia whitens — and whitening, in this technology, is the more achievable art. The transmitted On the Making of Silver and the silver sections of the epitome (§§20–28) work with a stable cast of whitening agents: mercury (especially mercury “fixed” from arsenic or sandarac), white arsenic, bleached magnesia, calcined cadmia, white lead, litharge ground with sulphur, and the vapours of sulphur itself — “for the smoke of sulphur being white, whitens everything.” Projected onto copper or iron pre-treated with sulphur, the fixed mercury preparation makes the metal white; cooked in castor or radish oil with a little alum, the mercurial “steam” yields a product “similar to white lead” that whitens all metallic bodies it is mixed into. A worked example in the epitome: melt four ounces of whitish copper (orichalcum), stir in, little by little, one ounce of pre-purified tin, then project half the white magnesia preparation — the whitened magnesia, the text notes approvingly, does not make the body brittle and does not tarnish copper's lustre.

Modern explanation (flagged as such). The whitening repertoire rests on real effects, and they are worth naming precisely. Mercury amalgamates readily with copper, tin, and lead, forming silvery surface layers. Arsenic alloyed into copper in small percentages produces a silvery-white arsenical bronze — a material with a long independent history in ancient metallurgy. Tin-rich copper alloys are pale and hard. Sulphur fumes and pickling liquors strip dark oxides. A workshop commanding all four effects could produce white metal of convincing colour and ring from base stock — which is why silver-imitation, of all the four books' arts, had the longest and most respectable commercial life.

Asem: The Imitation That Had Its Own Name

The product at the centre of Book II is asem (Greek asēmon, “unstamped” metal — metal not struck into coin). The word originally denoted electrum, the natural alloy of gold and silver, which could be parted into either precious metal and so seemed to contain both natures within it; Berthelot remarked that in this fact lies one root of the whole alchemical idea. In the papyri the word has slid down-market: asem now names the manufactured white alloys intended to imitate silver (and occasionally gold). Earle Radcliffe Caley's English translation of the Leyden Papyrus (made from the Greek text and the earlier German scholarship, and the version quoted here) gives the papyrus's asem recipes in full working form. Two, in Caley's translation, show the genre exactly:

LEYDEN X, RECIPE 5 — MANUFACTURE OF ASEM (CALEY TRANSLATION, ABRIDGED QUOTATION)

“Tin, 12 drachmas; mercury, 4 drachmas; earth of Chios, 2 drachmas. To the melted tin, add the crushed earth, then the mercury, stir with an iron, and put (the product) in use.” — A tin amalgam, given body by the white Chian clay: silver to the eye, at a fraction of silver's price.

LEYDEM X, RECIPE 8 — MANUFACTURE OF ASEM (CALEY TRANSLATION, PARAPHRASE)

Take soft tin in small pieces, purified four times; take four parts of it and three parts of pure white copper (bronze), and combine. The recipe's care is in the preparation of the stock — purification repeated to a number — not in any exotic ingredient.

Caley's general introduction to the two papyri states the essential fact about this whole literature without embarrassment: both papyri explicitly acknowledge that the alloys and gems they describe are imitations and not the real thing. The asem recipes are not failed transmutations. They are successful imitations, written down by and for people who knew the difference and had customers for both grades of goods. It is the later commentary tradition — and some modern popular writing — that blurs the distinction the papyri themselves keep.

XII. Diplosis: The Doubling of Gold and of Asem

Between honest imitation and outright fraud stands the operation the Greeks called diplosis — doubling. Diplosis starts from genuine precious material and stretches it: a quantity of gold (or of asem) is alloyed with cheaper metals in such proportions, and its surface then so treated, that the doubled mass keeps the colour, and as far as possible the other saleable properties, of the original. The mass of precious metal has doubled; its nature, the tradition claims, has not changed — nature has mastered nature, and the added base metal has been conquered into the dominant nature of the gold. The claim and the operation must be examined separately, because the operation is real, well documented, and technically interesting, while the claim is the point at which workshop practice shades into the doctrine later alchemy would inherit.

The Doubling of Asem: Leyden X, Recipe 6, Step by Step

Caley's translation of the Leyden Papyrus gives the operation in full. The ingredients, quoted from that translation: “refined copper, 40 drachmas; asem, 8 drachmas; tin in buttons, 40 drachmas.” The procedure, in the recipe's own order:

Step 1. Melt the refined copper first — the highest-melting ingredient takes the fire alone. Step 2. After two heatings, add the tin. Step 3. Then add the asem — the precious seed, eight drachmas against eighty of base stock, added last so it spends the least time in the fiercest heat. Step 4. When all are softened together, remelt the whole several times — repeated melting homogenises an alloy that would otherwise segregate as it cools. Step 5. Cool the melt by means of the preceding composition (Recipe 5's tin–mercury–Chian earth preparation, on Caley's identification) — a quench that also contributes the amalgamating, whitening agent to the surface. Step 6. Clean the finished metal with talc (the translation's note gives the Greek term rendered by Berthelot as talc or selenite). The recipe then adds, in the same breath, that tripling (triplosis) is effected by the same procedure, with the weights proportioned accordingly — and the following recipe (Leyden X, Recipe 7, “Inexhaustible Stock”) explains how to draw off eight drachmas from the doubled stock, restore the reserve by remelting with four drachmas of asem, and repeat: a self-renewing inventory, maintained at grade by periodic additions of the genuine article.

Modern explanation (flagged as such). Eight drachmas of asem in eighty-eight of product is a nine-per-cent precious content. What carries the imitation is the alloy system itself: copper–tin bronze at roughly equal weights is pale; the mercury-bearing quench amalgamates and brightens the surface; and repeated melting genuinely does homogenise the melt. The product is a pale bronze disciplined to look like silver — and the recipe's own framing concedes the mechanism, since the “inexhaustible” stock must be periodically refreshed with real asem to stay convincing. Diplosis of gold worked the same way at higher stakes: gold alloyed down with silver and copper toward half or a third of its original fineness, then surface-enriched (Section XIII) until the skin assayed rich again. Contemporary reviewers of the papyri already saw the point clearly: the mass was doubled while the colour remained unchanged, and — as the older summaries of the Leyden manual remark — a skilled workman would find the result difficult, even impossible, to detect by eye.

// WHY DIPLOSIS MATTERS TO THE HISTORY //

Diplosis is the hinge between the workshop and the doctrine. A practice that visibly stretches gold while preserving its colour teaches its practitioner — in his hands, not in a book — that a small quantity of a noble nature can dominate a large quantity of base matter. Generalise that lesson, remove the honest bookkeeping of the refresh-recipe, and you have the seed of the later theory of the ferment, the tincture, and ultimately the philosopher's stone: a little of the perfect thing perfecting much of the imperfect. Zosimos' generation performed that generalisation. The papyri had not.

XIII. Surface Colouring and Enrichment: The Golden Skin

A large share of both the Physika tradition and the Leyden Papyrus is devoted neither to alloying through the mass nor to immersion dyeing, but to a third strategy: treating the surface of a finished object so that the skin of the metal carries a colour the bulk does not. The papyrus's recipes for colouring the surfaces of metals, for gilding and silvering, and for writing in gold and silver letters (fifteen recipes in Leyden X) belong to this family, as do the Physika's pastes smeared on leaf and heated in luted vessels. The practitioner's own account of what he is doing, where the texts give one, is consistent: the preparation cleans, opens, and dyes the surface; salt, the epitome observes, cleans copper's surface and even its inward parts, and rusts it after pickling; mercury whitens the outsides of chrysochalcum and cleanses them, and whitens the inward parts too. Surface and interior are distinguished, and the art is frankly located at their boundary.

The Enrichment Principle, in the Practitioners' Terms

Consider a finished object of gold alloyed — as nearly all worked gold was — with silver and copper. The workshop possessed two ways to make its surface richer than its bulk. The first adds: a gold-bearing preparation (a paste of gold filings, mercury, and auxiliaries; a dye liquor carrying a yellowing earth) is applied and heated, leaving gold concentrated at the skin. The second subtracts: the object is packed or steeped in an aggressive medium — salt mixtures, alum, vinegar and misy liquors, the pickling baths the epitome prescribes for three, five, or six days “until rust forms, then dye” — which attacks the copper and silver at the surface and leaves the gold standing. The epitome's rust recipe states the subtraction logic in its own triumphant idiom: couperose (iron/copper vitriol) “makes gold out of rust.” Burnishing then compacts the treated skin. In the practitioners' terms, a baser nature has been drawn out of the surface and a nobler nature left in possession of it: nature has conquered nature, measurably, at the depth of a fingernail's paring.

Modern explanation (flagged as such). The subtractive method is the process modern archaeometallurgy calls depletion gilding or surface enrichment, documented in ancient goldwork worldwide and analysed in detail in the modern conservation literature (the standard review by Grimwade on the surface enrichment of carat gold alloys takes the Leyden Papyrus as one of its earliest textual witnesses). Gold resists oxidation and most acids; copper and silver do not. Heating a gold–silver–copper alloy oxidises copper at the surface; pickling in salt, alum, or acidic liquors dissolves the copper and silver compounds so formed; repetition leaves a spongy, microscopically pitted layer of nearly pure gold, which burnishing consolidates into a continuous rich skin. Pliny already describes a cementation refining of gold with salt and brick dust in the first century CE. The ancients who ran this process had no concept of selective dissolution; they had something nearly as good — a reliable recipe, a correct observation (the surface assays richer than the bulk), and an explanatory frame, sympathy, that told them why the treatment had to be repeated until the natures had finished their contest.

The testing recipes close the loop. The archive reads that juxtaposition without cynicism: in a market where every grade of goods circulated, the assay and the imitation were both professional knowledge, and the same man might need each on successive days. What the handbook will not do is call the imitation the real thing. That reticence, again, is the papyri's own.

XIV. Dyeing Stones: Quartz into Emerald

Book III of the Four Books — on stones — is the least well preserved in the epitomes and the best preserved in the papyri. The Stockholm Papyrus devotes some seventy of its 154 recipes to the imitation of precious stones and the improvement of genuine ones, and its procedures, which descend from the same tradition Synesius summarises, can be reconstructed in full working order. The raw material throughout is rock crystal — clear quartz — or another cheap transparent mineral or glassy stone, and the problem throughout is the dyer's problem in a harder medium: quartz has no fibre for a dye to grip. The tradition's solution has three stages, and the recipes are explicit about all of them.

Stage 1: Open the Stone

First the crystal must be made receptive — in the recipes' language, corroded, roughened, or opened up. The transmitted methods include steeping the stone in mordanting liquors (the Stockholm Papyrus's mordant recipe — boil chalcanthum, copper vitriol, with skorpiurus — is recommended, in Caley's translation, for “any desired color,” and is said to mordant “all kinds of stones and skins” as well as wool); soaking in urine (one pearl recipe specifies, with a straight face, the urine of a young boy); and heat-shocking: heating the crystal and quenching it so that a network of fine cracks — crazing — runs through the surface layers. The crackle is not damage; it is the point. A crazed stone drinks.

Stage 2: Make the Colour — Verdigris for Emerald

For emerald — the most treated stone in the papyrus, with more than twenty recipes — the colouring matter is always verdigris, and the papyrus includes a stand-alone recipe for preparing it, quoted here in the form given in Marjolijn Bol's study of the varnish-and-glaze recipe tradition, following the Stockholm text: clean a sheet of Cyprian copper with pumice and water, dry it, smear it very lightly with oil, and hang it by a cord in a cask over sharp vinegar, not touching the vinegar; seal the cask against evaporation; if the sheet went in in the morning, scrape off the verdigris in the evening, if in the evening, scrape in the morning; rehang, and repeat until the sheet is used up. Modern explanation (flagged as such): vinegar vapour corrodes copper to basic copper acetate — verdigris — a blue-green pigment whose colour, ground fine and carried in oil, resin, or vinegar, sits in quartz's cracks and reads, through the transparent body of the stone, as the green of emerald.

Stage 3: Dye, Seal, and Finish

The opened stone is then steeped or boiled in the colouring preparation — for emerald, verdigris compounded variously with celandine juice and other adjuncts in liquid resin, on the recipe Bol excerpts — until the cracks and surface layers have taken the colour through. Excess is cleaned off; some recipes finish the stone with a coloured backing or a surface glaze, the ancestor of the foiled doublet. Other stones follow the same grammar with other colours: reds for ruby and carbuncle, violets for amethyst, the blue of hyacinth; and a parallel group of recipes treats pearls — whitening smoked or brownish pearls by boiling in honey-water with pounded fig root, roughening dull pearls in urine to restore their bite, and manufacturing artificial pearls outright from prepared crystal by the mordant-and-dye sequence above. Kurt Nassau's history of gemstone treatments, which studied the Stockholm text closely, remarks on how many of these procedures — crackling and dyeing quartz, foil-backing, oiling — are, in modernised dress, still the trade's procedures. The book of stones is not a curiosity appended to the art. It is the art's proof that its central operation is colour, wherever colour can be made to stay.

No one has ever supposed that a dyed quartz was transmuted into emerald; the recipes never claim it, and the product's value lay in appearance alone. The stones book therefore anchors the interpretation of the whole Four Books: whatever the gold and silver books are doing, they belong to a work whose third book is openly, technically, and skilfully about imitation. Interpretations of Books I and II that require literal transmutation must explain why the same author, in the same work, with the same vocabulary, settles for dye in Book III.

XV. Purple: Dyeing Wool With — and Without — the Murex

Tyrian purple — the secretion of the murex and related sea snails, harvested by the ten-thousand, fermented, and vat-dyed at a cost that made purple cloth a currency of rank — was the ancient world's most prestigious colour and its most imitated. Book IV of the Four Books treats its production and, characteristically, its substitution. Fittingly, the transmitted Physika kai Mystika itself opens with purple: the first recipes of the epitome as Berthelot and Ruelle print it are purple recipes, and they give the fullest surviving view of the tradition's dye-house procedure. They are paraphrased here in sequence.

The Physika's Purple Sequence (Berthelot–Ruelle Text, §§1–2)

Step 1 — prepare the ground. Macerate iron slag (two obols by weight) in urine (seven drachmas); bring a pound of the purple material to the boil in the liquor; take it off the fire and let the whole stand in a vessel; then pour the decoction over the purple and leave it to soak a night and a day. Step 2 — the lichen bath. Take four pounds of marine lichen (the orchil-bearing seaweed of the substitute-purple trade), cover with water to four fingers above the material, and hold it until it thickens; filter, heat, and pour over the wool laid out beforehand; tread the loose parts so the juice penetrates thoroughly; leave two nights and two days. Step 3 — repeat and finish. Rebuild the bath at the original proportion from the same juice; steep the wool again, a night and a day; rinse in urine; dry in the shade. A companion recipe works with alkanet: crush it; boil four pounds of sorrel in urine until dissolved; filter; add the alkanet and cook until thickened; filter again; enter the wool; wash in urine, then in water; dry in the shade; and expose the urine-soaked wool to the vapours of seaweed for two days. The text then appends a dyer's catalogue of purple materials, esteemed and worthless distinguished with a professional's bluntness: the seaweed called false purple, kermes (“coccus”), the marine colour, alkanet of Laodicea, madder of Italy and the rest are esteemed; dyestuffs that “do not give a fixed dye are of no value” — a list of failures follows, with a grudging exception made for isatis (woad).

Modern explanation (flagged as such). Almost every element of that sequence has a function a modern dyer recognises. Urine, standing, generates ammonia: an alkaline assistant that swells wool fibre and helps both the extraction of lichen dyes (orchil, from the very lichens named) and their uptake. Iron slag in an acid-alkaline liquor is an iron mordant source: iron saddens and fixes reds toward purple-brown. Sorrel is rich in oxalic acid, a cleaning and mordanting assistant. Repeated long steeps with treading are how level colour is built from weak dyestuffs; shade-drying protects fugitive lichen purples from light. The product is not murex purple and does not wear like it — orchil is famously less fast — but at the price point the recipe serves, it is purple, and the text's own catalogue admits the hierarchy it is working within.

The Papyri's Dye-House: Mordant First

The Stockholm Papyrus, with seventy-odd dye recipes, shows the same craft at industrial completeness, and its procedures (in Caley's translation) can be summarised as a fixed order of operations that any modern natural dyer will recognise on sight: (1) scour — clean the wool with soapwort: bruise it, heat it in water, enter the wool, shake, lift, dry (Recipe 85); (2) mordant — boil chalcanthum and skorpiurus and use the liquor for any colour (Recipe 87); a separate instruction notes that alkanet is best dissolved in camel's urine, which makes the dye “not only fast, but durable” (Recipe 88); (3) dye — one bath, three colours: a liquor of krimnos and dyer's alum gives scarlet; add ground sulphur and water for leek-green; add unadulterated natron for quince-yellow (Recipe 84) — the mordant-and-assistant system shifting one dyestuff across the spectrum; (4) purple by substitution — prepare the wool in a bath of asphodel and natron, rinse, then enter it in a boiled liquor of grape skins and vinegar stood six hours (Recipe 86). And presiding over the whole section, a dyer's quality control: the papyri teach how to test dyestuffs before buying — good woad is heavy and dark blue, poor woad light and whitish; the best madder rubs up purple and fast.

The Leyden and Stockholm versions of the Phrygian-stone purple recipe stand side by side in the older literature as the textbook proof of the tradition's unity: roast the Phrygian stone (an aluminous mordant stone, on Berthelot's identification an alunite), boil it, steep the wool until cold; then a second bath of orchil lichen (and, in the Stockholm version, amaranth), one mina, boiled, the wool cooled in it. Two manuscripts, one recipe, trivially varied — the fingerprint of a copying tradition serving working dyers, of which the Four Books' purple book is the literary head.

XVI. The Theban Witnesses: The Leyden and Stockholm Papyri

Everything claimed in Sections X–XV about the workshop character of this tradition can be checked against two documents that are not fragments, quotations, or epitomes, but whole working manuscripts: the Leyden Papyrus X and the Stockholm Papyrus (Papyrus Graecus Holmiensis). Both are Greek recipe collections written in Egypt in the late 3rd or early 4th century CE; both come from Thebes, where they formed part of the cache of papyri — magical and technical together — collected in the early nineteenth century by Giovanni Anastasi, Swedish vice-consul at Alexandria, the Leyden manuscript passing to the Netherlands in 1828 and the Stockholm manuscript to Sweden. The two are so close in hand, format, and content that they have long been treated as products of the same workshop milieu, in part perhaps written by the same hand; the Leyden papyrus, published by Leemans in 1885, holds a hundred-odd recipes weighted toward metals — alloys, solders, surface colouring, assays, metallic writing, a handful of dye recipes — while the Stockholm papyrus, published by Lagercrantz in 1913, holds 154 recipes weighted toward dyeing and the imitation of stones and pearls. Caley's English translations (1926–27; reissued with William B. Jensen's introduction in 2008) are the versions quoted in this article.

Their relation to the Four Books is the scholarly crux of this article, and it can be stated precisely. The overlap is extensive and specific: dozens of the papyri's recipes parallel the Democritean material — the Jensen introduction to the Caley reissue counts twenty-seven similar recipes shared with the Physika tradition — the same fourfold subject division organises both, the same ingredients, proportions, and operations recur, and the papyri even preserve stray attributions into the older literature (a Stockholm recipe on purifying copper cites Anaxilaus of Larissa, a contemporary of Bolos' tradition). The differences are equally instructive. The papyri are more precise than the epitomised Physika: fuller weights, fuller sequences, none of the frame story, none of the aphorism, no Ostanes, no temple. And they are later by two to four centuries than the strata they illuminate. The correct inference — the one Berthelot drew, and the one this archive follows — is not that the papyri copy the Four Books or that the Four Books copy the papyri, but that both draw on a continuous Egyptian workshop literature, older than either, of which the Democritean books are the literary redaction and the papyri the bench copies. Where the epitome is compressed to the point of obscurity, the papyri show what the full recipe looked like; where the papyri are mute about meaning, the Physika supplies the framework the workshop worked within.

It is often repeated that such recipe books were the kind of text Diocletian ordered burned after the Egyptian revolt of 297–98 CE, lest the Egyptians grow rich by the art of making gold and silver and finance further rebellion. The story comes from John of Antioch, writing centuries later, and Berthelot's generation connected it romantically to the papyri's survival in a tomb. The archive records the story as what it is: a late testimony that Roman authority took Egyptian gold-making literature seriously — itself a datum about the art's economic weight — and not as a documented fact about these two manuscripts.

XVII. Sympathy and Antipathy: The Physics of the Workshop

Why should brine clean pyrites, mercury seize copper, a lichen dye wool and not linen, celandine restore rhubarb? The tradition's answer is Bolos' answer, and it is the only theoretical apparatus this stratum possesses: the natures of things stand in relations of sympathy (sympatheia) and antipathy (antipatheia) — affinities and aversions, inherent in the things themselves, which the practitioner does not create but discovers, combines, and exploits. Bolos' Physika had catalogued those relations among living things; the recipe books extend the catalogue to minerals, metals, and dyestuffs, and the extension is seamless in the texts' own presentation: the magnetic stone “has an affinity for iron”; celandine “has an affinity for rhubarb”; sulphur mixed with sulphur makes bodies sulphurous “because they have a great affinity for it”; the smoke of sulphur, being white, whitens — like acting on like, the oldest clause in the sympathy physics.

Three features of this framework deserve the reader's respect, because condescension toward it is the commonest error in popular accounts of this material. First, it is genuinely explanatory in intent. Sympathy is offered as the reason recipes work, and it generates expectations: if two natures sympathise, they will combine, penetrate, and hold; if they are antipathetic, no amount of fire will marry them, and the failure will be legible in the crucible. Second, it is continuous with respectable Hellenistic physics. Stoic cosmology ran on cosmic sympathy — the universe as one animal, its parts in mutual affection — and the medical tradition from which Bolos also descends dosed by sympathy and antipathy as a matter of course. The workshop version is not a corruption of philosophy; it is philosophy's country cousin, raised in the same house. Third, it encodes real phenomenology. “Affinity” in these texts reliably tracks what a modern chemist would call reactivity and miscibility: mercury really does seize copper; sulphur really does attack metals; mordants really are substances with a foot in both the fibre and the dye. The framework is wrong as physics and serviceable as a mnemonic organisation of bench experience — which is to say, it functioned the way a good pre-modern theory should.

// THE PHYSICIAN ANALOGY IN THE TEXT ITSELF //

The epitome defends its method with a comparison worth recovering (paraphrased from the Berthelot–Ruelle text, §14–15): the children of physicians, preparing a medicine, do not proceed by enthusiasm; they first learn which substance is hot, which cold, which humid, and in what condition each must be to favour a balanced mixture — and only then compound the cure. So too the dyer of metals must learn which species cleans, which dyes, which gives shine, which resists fire, before he compounds. The passage is the tradition describing itself: an empirical art of classified materials and tested combinations, indignant at the charge of mere fable. It is as close as this stratum comes to a methodological manifesto, and it is entirely about the bench.

XVIII. The Aphorism: Nature Delights in Nature

One sentence from this tradition outlived everything else in it. In the frame story it is the whole of the dead master's secret, found written inside the temple column; in the recipe epitome it recurs after procedure after procedure, varied like a bell; in Zosimos and the later corpus it becomes the most quoted sentence in Greek alchemy. It is quoted here in the form given by the edition this article follows for the Four Books:

“Nature delights in nature, nature conquers nature, nature masters nature.” — APHORISM OF THE FOUR BOOKS, ASCRIBED WITHIN THE TEXT TO OSTANES; GREEK: HĒ PHYSIS TĒI PHYSEI TERPETAI, KAI HĒ PHYSIS TĒN PHYSIN NIKAI, KAI HĒ PHYSIS TĒN PHYSIN KRATEI. TRANSLATION AS GIVEN IN MATTEO MARTELLI, THE FOUR BOOKS OF PSEUDO-DEMOCRITUS (2013), P. 11

The reader should know that the sentence exists in a family of renderings, because the Greek verbs carry ranges their English equivalents split. The Berthelot–Ruelle text, in the English rendering of their translation consulted for this article, gives the column inscription as: “Nature enjoys nature; nature triumphs over nature; nature masters nature” — and the epitome's refrains cycle through enjoys, charms, triumphs, rules, and dominates for the same three verbs. Principe's discussion gives “delights”, “triumphs”, and “masters.” These are not rival sentences; they are one Greek sentence, terpetai / nikai / kratei — is delighted, conquers, holds fast — refracted through three honest translations. This article quotes Martelli's form, names it, and treats every other occurrence of the saying in its pages as a transmitted saying of the tradition rather than a fresh quotation.

What does it mean? Within the text's own economy, it is the sympathy physics of Section XVII compressed into a rule of the bench, and its three clauses map onto three things a practitioner watches for. Nature delights in nature: like joins like — the affinity clause, the reason a preparation takes. Nature conquers nature: one nature overcomes and transforms another — the sulphur that burns, the pickle that strips, the dye that displaces a body's own colour. Nature masters nature: one nature fixes and holds another fast — the mercury fixed, the colour made fast against fire and washing, the doubled gold that keeps its conquered base metal in subjection. Martelli notes that the saying also occurs in earlier Hellenistic writings on sympathy and antipathy ascribed to Persian authorities — to pseudo-Zoroaster and Ostanes material — which is to say the Four Books did not coin it; they canonised it, by putting it in the column, in the temple, at the head of the recipes. Later alchemy, which quoted it for fifteen hundred years, usually quoted it as metaphysics. In its first home it is a workshop mnemonic with a legend wrapped around it, and the legend is the tradition's way of saying: this sentence is worth the whole book.

XIX. Transmission: Zosimos, Synesius, and the Commentators

The Four Books survived the way they did because later alchemists could not leave them alone. Zosimos of Panopolis (fl. c. 300 CE) cites pseudo-Democritus more than any other authority; his own account of the art's pedigree, and his encyclopedic Cheirokmēta — the title itself a homage to the Democritean hand-work book — present his work as the continuation of the Democritean tradition, and it is largely through Zosimos' quotations, and those of his successors, that the lost books' contents can be triangulated at all. The hub's Zosimos of Panopolis article takes up the story at exactly this hand-off, as does the Egyptology division's fuller Zosimos treatment. Synesius (c. 373–414 CE), the Neoplatonist who became bishop of Ptolemais, cast his Democritean work as a letter to the priest Dioscorus — The Philosopher Synesius to Dioscorus: Notes on Democritus' Book — which survives entire and supplies, among much else, the Memphis initiation summary of Section VII. A commentary by one Petasius is lost; the works of pseudo-Olympiodorus, Stephanus of Alexandria, Christianus, and the philosopher Anonymous continue the commentary chain into the Byzantine period, each presenting its own doctrine, in Martelli's observation, as an interpretation of the Four Books.

Alongside the commentary chain ran the other transmission, the one that never made it into the Byzantine corpus: the bench transmission of the papyri and their descendants. The recipe literature of late antiquity and the early Middle Ages — the Latin Mappae clavicula tradition among it, which scholarship derives from a lost Greek source of this same family — carried the alloy, dye, and stone recipes forward as craft knowledge long after the commentators had allegorised their Democritus beyond recognition. And a third figure of the founding generation belongs in this section as the bridge to the hub's next article: Maria the Jewess (Maria Hebraea), placed by the tradition in the first centuries CE, to whom Zosimos attributes the apparatus — the kerotakis, the tribikos, the water bath that still bears her name — that the Democritean books conspicuously lack. The recipe tradition gave the art its materials and its physics; Maria's generation gave it its instruments. Her article in this hub is Maria the Jewess; the apparatus is treated in full in the Egyptology division's Zosimos article (§§XI–XIII).

XX. Assessment: What This Stratum Is and Is Not

The Bolos / pseudo-Democritus stratum is the root of Western alchemy, and roots are judged by what they actually are, not by the tree. What this stratum is: a craft tradition of the first order — organised (four books, one operation, one vocabulary), literate (recipes with reasons, catalogues with quality judgements, testing procedures beside manufacturing procedures), theoretically framed (sympathy and antipathy as a working physics of combination), and socially framed (temple secrecy, initiatory legend, a borrowed name of immense authority). Its practitioners could double gold, whiten copper into silver's likeness, enrich a surface until the touchstone was satisfied, dye quartz into emerald's likeness, and purple wool without a single snail — and could explain, in their own terms, why each operation worked. F. Sherwood Taylor's judgement on the early Greek material stands: this is the point at which the accumulated technique of the Egyptian workshops becomes a literature, with everything that follows from that transformation — commentary, canon, and eventually doctrine.

What it is not must be said with equal plainness, because the later tradition's prestige constantly back-projects itself. There is no philosopher's stone in these texts: no single agent that perfects all metals, no universal solvent, no elixir, no medicine for the body or the soul compounded from metals. There is no doctrine of transmutation as the later centuries understood it — the conversion of a base metal's innermost nature into gold by a perfected agent — though there is, in diplosis, the workshop experience from which that doctrine would be generalised. There is no apparatus beyond the crucible, the luted vessel, and the dyepot; the still, the reflux apparatus, and the water bath belong to the next generation. And there is no fraud doctrine either: the papyri call their products imitations, the testing recipes sit unembarrassed beside the manufacturing recipes, and the line the tradition itself draws is not between true and false making but between work that holds its colour — in fire, in wear, under the stone — and work that does not. Judged by its own standard, fastness, the stratum is honest about its aims in a way its descendants were not always.

From this root, everything in the hub's Alchemy wing grows. Zosimos takes the recipes, the aphorism, and the Democritean pedigree, and adds apparatus, a doctrine of bodies and spirits, and the visionary literature that turns the art inward. Maria's attributed instruments make vapour work — sublimation, reflux, gentle digestion — into routine operations, and with vapour work the whole later technology of the art becomes possible. The Arabic and Latin traditions take Zosimos' enlarged art and, across a millennium, build from it the stone, the elixir, and the laboratory science that eventually dissolves alchemy into chemistry. None of that is in Bolos. All of it cites him — under the name he borrowed. The workshop root holds the tree up precisely because it never pretended to be the tree: four books on dyeing, written for people whose hands were already in the work.
