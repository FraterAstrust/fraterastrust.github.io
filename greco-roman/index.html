I. Introduction — Scope of This Hub

This hub covers the Greek and Roman world as a single, long civilisation with two languages, many cities, and one continuous argument with itself about the gods, about nature, and about what human beings can make and remake. It begins in the Bronze Age background — Minoan Crete and Mycenaean Greece, the palace worlds whose languages, gods and crafts stand behind everything later Greeks told about their own past — and it ends at a threshold in 1144 CE, when an Arabic alchemical book was put into Latin and the art that Alexandria had founded entered the Latin West. Between those points lie the archaic and classical city-states, the Hellenistic kingdoms, the Roman absorption of the Greek world, and the long late-antique centuries in which Greek learning was copied, commented on, and carried into new hands.

The hub has a defined place in this archive, and it is worth stating plainly. It follows the Egyptology division — the reader who arrives from Egyptology will find the story picked up where the pharaonic and Ptolemaic material leaves it — and it follows the Before Kemet hub, which treats everything before dynastic Egypt and tiers its evidence as attested, ancient-traditional, and modern-constructed. This hub needs no tier system of that kind, because its whole period is documented: there are texts, inscriptions, buildings and workshop remains throughout. What it needs instead — and what its method section sets out — is discipline of a different kind: the discipline of distinguishing a poem from a cult, a Greek original from a Roman reception, and a verified quotation from a paraphrase.

One debt of continuity deserves emphasis. The Egyptology division already treats Egyptian alchemy, the Hermetica, and Zōsimos of Panopolis from the Egyptian side: see its Alchemy article for the sacred art of Kemet, its temple crafts and its recipe papyri. The alchemy wing of this hub carries that same history forward — from the point where Greek-speaking practitioners in Roman Egypt turned Egyptian craft knowledge, Greek natural philosophy and a new apparatus tradition into the discipline the later world would call alchemy, and onward through its Byzantine, Syriac, Arabic and Latin transmission. The two treatments are companions, not duplicates: Egyptology asks what the art was in Egypt; this hub asks what the Greek and Roman world made of it, and where it went next.

The mythology wing now carries the great story-cycles in full — the Argonauts, the labours of Heracles, Theseus, Perseus, the wanderings of Odysseus, the Trojan and Theban cycles, the descents to the dead, the makers and fire-stealers, Cupid and Psyche, and the legendary lives of the sages. There is a reason beyond completeness. In the alchemical tradition these stories were not decoration: they were a teaching vehicle, passed from master to student — much of the transmission oral — with their lessons deliberately obscured by allegory, so that the tale could be heard by anyone and understood only by those prepared for it. That is the tradition's own account of why it veiled its teaching in story, and this hub documents it as such: Wing I tells the myths whole, as texts with their transmission and their cult footing stated honestly, and Wing II's Alchemical Allegory sets out how the alchemists themselves read them — a documented interpretive tradition with its own rules, presented as the alchemists' reading, never as the myths' original meaning.

// THE HUB IN ONE LINE //

Mythology is read here as literature and as lived religion; alchemy is read here as philosophy and as bench work. Neither wing is allowed to swallow the other, and neither is reduced to the other.

A note on names and spelling. Greek words are transliterated consistently throughout the hub, with macrons marking long vowels where the standard scholarly spelling carries them: Zōsimos, kērotakis, tribikos, chēmeia. Personal names follow the familiar Latinised forms (Hesiod, Plutarch, Ptolemy) except where the Greek form is itself the subject. Dates are BCE and CE throughout; a date preceded by c. is approximate, and a date given as a range is a floruit or a construction period, not a pair of anniversaries. Where scholars disagree about a date that matters — the dating of the alchemical pseudo-Democritus is the standing example — the timeline says so in the entry itself rather than silently choosing a side.

Finally, a note on what this hub is not. It is not a retelling of the myths for their own sake, though the myths are told here carefully and in full. It is not a celebration of alchemy as lost super-science, nor a dismissal of it as fraud: the recipe literature is explicit that many of its products were imitations, and the hub reports that plainly, because a tradition honest about its own imitations deserves to be read honestly. And it is not a general history of Greece and Rome — politics and war appear on the timeline where they change the conditions of cult, of learning, or of the art, and not otherwise.

II. The Two Wings

The hub is organised in two wings. The division is not decorative: the two wings use different primary evidence, ask different questions, and are held to the same standard by different means. Wing I works from poems, hymns, inscriptions, sanctuaries and ritual calendars. Wing II works from treatises, recipe collections, apparatus descriptions and manuscripts. Every article in the index belongs to one wing, and the timeline is written so that a reader can follow either wing down its length — or watch the two converge, as they do in Alexandria, where the god Hermes of Wing I becomes Hermes Trismegistus, the named author of Wing II's philosophy.

WING I

MYTHOLOGY — gods, heroes and epics as texts and as cult. The succession myth and the Olympian order; the heroes and the epics that carry them; and the mystery cults — Eleusinian, Orphic, Dionysian, Samothracian — where myth stopped being only a story and became something a person underwent. A myth is treated first as a text: who composed it, in what genre, surviving in what edition. It is treated second as cult: who sacrificed, processed, swore, initiated, and where. The wing's standing warning is that the two do not map onto each other neatly, and any sentence that confuses a poet's invention with a city's practice is rewritten until it no longer does.

WING II

ALCHEMY — the Alexandrian art and its transmission. The art that Greek-speaking Egypt built out of older Egyptian and Near Eastern craft: pseudo-Democritus and the Physika kai Mystika, Maria the Jewess and the apparatus tradition, Zōsimos and the vision literature, the Hermetica, and the commentators who carried the corpus through Byzantium into Syriac and Arabic and finally through the Latin door of 1144. Workshop procedure stands at the centre of this wing: furnaces, stills, the kērotakis, quantities, sequences, tests. A claim about what alchemists believed is only admitted here alongside the evidence of what they actually did at the bench.

WING I — ITS KEY DISTINCTION

Literary myth is not cult practice. Homer and Hesiod are evidence for what archaic audiences heard; calendars, decrees and sanctuary remains are evidence for what cities did. Burkert's Greek Religion is the wing's model for holding the two together without merging them.

WING II — ITS KEY DISTINCTION

Recipe is not theory, and theory is not recipe. The Leyden and Stockholm papyri describe making and testing saleable imitations; Zōsimos describes vessels, vapours and visions in one breath. The wing keeps both registers visible and names which one a given text is speaking in.

The wings meet at three points, and the hub marks each meeting rather than blurring it. They meet in Hermes: Olympian messenger in Wing I, thrice-greatest sage and supposed author in Wing II. They meet in the mysteries, where Wing I's cult history borders the initiatory language Wing II later borrowed. And they meet in Alexandria itself — a Greek city on Egyptian soil, ruled from a Macedonian court, taxed by Rome — the one place where a single practitioner could plausibly read Hesiod in the morning and tend a kērotakis in the afternoon. Plato's Atlantis, treated in the Before Kemet hub at Plato's Atlantis, is the archive's standing example of a Wing I text that later ages misread as a Wing II-style technical record; it is cross-referenced there and not duplicated here.

III. Method — The Rules of This Hub

Every archive in this project keeps its rules on its hub page, where a reader can hold the articles to them. These are the rules of the Greco-Roman hub. They are stricter than they look, and they bind the timeline below as much as the articles it indexes.

// THE HUB'S RULE //

No invented quotations anywhere in this archive. A sentence inside quotation marks is a verified quotation from a named published translation, or it does not appear. Everything else is labelled paraphrase — including this hub's own summaries of the arguments it admires most.

Texts are named in named editions and translations. Hesiod is cited from M. L. West's editions of the Theogony and the Works and Days; the Greek alchemists from Berthelot and Ruelle's Collection des anciens alchimistes grecs and from Michèle Mertens's Les alchimistes grecs; the Corpus Hermeticum from Brian Copenhaver's Hermetica. Where an article relies on a different edition, that edition is named on the article. A claim that cannot be traced to a named text is not made.

Literary myth is distinguished from cult practice. That a god does something in a poem establishes that the poem says it. It does not establish that any Greek sacrificed, processed or prayed on that basis. Cult claims in this hub rest on cult evidence: inscriptions, calendars, votives, sanctuary archaeology, and ancient authors writing about ritual as ritual — Pausanias describing a sanctuary, a decree regulating a festival, Plutarch writing as a priest of Delphi.

Greek and Roman reception are distinguished. Rome did not simply inherit Greece; it translated, renamed, re-founded and sometimes invented Greek material for Roman purposes. Zeus and Jupiter, Heracles and Hercules, the Greek chēmeia and the Latin alchimia that reached the West through Arabic — in each pair the second term is a reception, with its own date and its own agenda, and this hub dates receptions separately from their sources wherever the difference matters. The Atlantis of Plato and the Atlantis of later centuries are, by this rule, two different subjects.

Quotations are only from verified published translations, and are named as such. No wording is reconstructed from memory, none is silently modernised, and none is manufactured to give an argument a classical accent it has not earned. Where a famous saying turns out to be a later paraphrase — a common fate of alchemical maxims in particular — the hub says so and prints the paraphrase labelled as paraphrase, with its actual, later source where that source is known.

Speculation is labelled as speculation. The date of Homer, the identity of Bolos of Mendes, the historicity of Maria the Jewess, the authorship of works ascribed to Stephanus of Alexandria: on each of these the scholarship is divided or the evidence thin, and this hub presents them as open questions with the main positions named, not as settled facts with the disagreement footnoted away. A reader should be able to tell, from any sentence in this hub, whether they are standing on a document, on a scholarly consensus, or on an argument.

// A NOTE ON THE ALCHEMICAL SOURCES //

Pseudepigraphy is the normal condition of Wing II's library, not an embarrassment to be explained away. Works travel under the names of Democritus, Hermes, Moses, Cleopatra and Aristotle that their named authors did not write. This hub cites such works as pseudo-Democritus, the Hermetica ascribed to Hermes Trismegistus, and so on — the attribution is part of the historical evidence, and silently treating it as authorship would falsify the record the hub exists to keep.

IV. The Greco-Roman Timeline

Forty-nine entries, from the palace of Knossos to the great Byzantine codex that preserved the Greek alchemists. Read it as two interleaved histories: the Wing I history of gods, poems and cults, and the Wing II history that begins, quietly, in Hellenistic Egypt and ends by walking out of the Greek world altogether — into Syriac, into Arabic, and at the threshold, into Latin. Every date was checked for this hub against the standard references named in the Sources section; approximate dates are marked as approximate.

c. 2000–1450 BCE
Minoan Crete — the first Aegean palace civilisation. On Crete, a non-Greek-speaking people — Minoan is a modern name, taken from the legendary King Minos — built unfortified palace complexes at Knossos, Phaistos, Malia and Zakros, administered in the undeciphered Linear A script. Their art, their sea trade and their religious imagery — bulls, double axes, goddess figures — formed the deep background that later Greek myth remembered, transformed, as the age of Minos, the labyrinth and the Minotaur.
c. 1600–1100 BCE
Mycenaean Greece — Greek-speaking palace kingdoms. On the mainland, fortified palace centres at Mycenae, Tiryns, Pylos, Thebes and Athens ruled through a redistributive bureaucracy whose accounts survive in Linear B — deciphered in 1952 as an early form of Greek. The tablets already name gods the classical world would worship, among them Zeus, Hera, Poseidon and Dionysos. Around 1450 BCE the Mycenaeans took over Knossos, and Minoan and Mycenaean culture fused in the late palaces.
c. 1200–1100 BCE
The Bronze Age collapse. Within a few generations the palace system failed across the eastern Mediterranean: Mycenaean centres were destroyed or abandoned, the Hittite Empire fell, and trade networks contracted sharply. In Greece, Linear B writing was lost entirely, and the centuries that followed — poorer, smaller in scale, and without writing — are conventionally called the Greek Dark Age. The causes of the collapse remain debated; its reality, in the ground, is not.
c. 800 BCE
The Greek alphabet adapted from Phoenician script. Greek communities adapted the Phoenician consonantal script and, decisively, repurposed some of its signs as vowels — producing the first fully alphabetic writing system. Within a generation or two, writing in Greek was being used not only for accounts and ownership marks but for verse: some of the earliest surviving Greek inscriptions are poetic. The recovery of writing is the precondition for everything Wing I studies.
c. 750–700 BCE
Homer and Hesiod — the foundational poems take shape. The Iliad and Odyssey, composed in an oral-formulaic tradition and conventionally attached to the name Homer, reached something close to their surviving form in the later 8th century BCE; their exact dating remains debated. Hesiod of Ascra, a near-contemporary, composed the Theogony — the first systematic Greek account of the gods' generations — and the Works and Days. Between them, these poets gave the Greek world its shared divine genealogy and its heroic past.
776 BCE
Traditional date of the first Olympic Games. Later Greek chronographers fixed the first Olympiad, and with it the starting point of Greek historical dating, to 776 BCE, when the victor list for the games at Olympia begins with Koroibos of Elis, winner of the stadion foot-race. The sanctuary of Zeus at Olympia was older than the games' traditional start, and the date is best understood as the beginning of a reliable victor record rather than the invention of the festival itself.
734–733 BCE
Greek colonisation reaches Sicily — Naxos and Syracuse founded. In the great archaic movement of settlement around the Mediterranean and Black Sea coasts, Chalcidians founded Naxos in Sicily in 734 BCE and Corinthians founded Syracuse the following year, by the traditional chronology Thucydides reports. The colonies carried Greek cults, calendars and myths into new landscapes — and brought Greek speakers into permanent contact with the peoples, crafts and materials of Italy, North Africa and the Near East.
594/593 BCE
Solon, archon of Athens. Chosen as archon and mediator in a crisis of debt and faction, Solon cancelled debts, abolished debt-bondage for Athenians, reordered political rights by wealth classes, and — unusually — set his reforms down in verse, from which later authors quote him. His settlement did not create democracy, and tyranny followed within a generation; but it established the principle, foundational for classical Athens, that law is a public, written thing standing above faction.
508/507 BCE
Cleisthenes' reforms — Athenian democracy founded. After the fall of the Peisistratid tyranny, Cleisthenes reorganised the Athenian citizen body into ten new tribes drawn across the old regional divisions, with a Council of Five Hundred preparing business for the assembly of citizens. The reforms are conventionally taken as the founding act of Athenian dēmokratia — a citizen body, excluding women, slaves and resident aliens, governing itself through assembly, council and courts.
490 BCE
Marathon — the first Persian invasion repelled. A Persian expeditionary force sent by Darius I landed at Marathon to punish Athens and Eretria for aiding the Ionian Revolt. On the plain of Marathon, Athenian and Plataean hoplites defeated it decisively; Herodotus gives the Athenian dead as 192, a figure the burial mound on the field still commemorates. Marathon became, in Athenian memory, the founding victory of the democracy's self-image — the generation of the Marathonomachai, the Marathon-fighters.
480 BCE
Thermopylae and Salamis — Xerxes' invasion turns. Xerxes' far larger invasion crossed into Greece in 480 BCE. A Greek rearguard under the Spartan king Leonidas was destroyed holding the pass of Thermopylae; Athens was evacuated and its Acropolis burned. Weeks later, in the straits of Salamis, the allied Greek fleet — its largest contingent Athenian, its strategy Themistocles' — destroyed the Persian fleet in confined water where numbers told against the invader. The invasion was not over, but its momentum was broken.
479 BCE
Plataea and Mycale — the Persian Wars end in Greece. In 479 BCE a pan-Hellenic army under the Spartan Pausanias defeated the Persian land force left in Greece at Plataea in Boeotia, and Greek forces destroyed the Persian fleet beached at Mycale across the Aegean on, by tradition, the same day. Mainland Greece was free of Persian occupation. The wars' memory — a small world of free cities defeating an empire — shaped Greek, and then European, political imagination out of all proportion to the forces engaged.
478 BCE
The Delian League founded. Athens and its Aegean allies formed a league, its treasury on Delos and its purpose the continuation of war against Persia, with members contributing ships or money. Within a generation the league had become, in substance, an Athenian empire: the treasury moved to Athens in 454 BCE, allied contributions became tribute, and the league's funds underwrote the building programme that remade the Acropolis — a fact Athens' critics, ancient and modern, have not let it forget.
c. 450 BCE
Empedocles of Acragas — the four roots. A Sicilian philosopher-poet, writing in verse a generation before Socrates, set all nature on four eternal “roots” — fire, air, water and earth — mixed and parted by two powers, Love and Strife. Plato and Aristotle will each rebuild the scheme rather than replace it, and the tetrad Empedocles fixed underwrites, in the end, the physics of the alchemists.
447–432 BCE
The Parthenon built on the Athenian Acropolis. Under Pericles' leadership Athens rebuilt the sanctuary the Persians had burned, raising the Parthenon — temple of Athena Parthenos, designed by Iktinos and Kallikrates, its sculpture overseen by Pheidias — between 447 and 432 BCE. It housed Pheidias' colossal gold-and-ivory Athena and functioned in part as the treasury of the Athenian state. As cult building, war memorial and imperial statement at once, it is Wing I's central case of a temple that is also an argument.
c. 460–370 BCE
Hippocrates and the Hippocratic corpus. Under the name of the Coan physician, some sixty treatises by many hands bind the body's four humours — blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile — to the same qualities that order the elements: hot, cold, wet and dry. Temperament, the body's individual mixture of those qualities, becomes the shared physics of Greek medicine and, centuries later, one of alchemy's parent languages.
431–404 BCE
The Peloponnesian War. Athens and Sparta, at the head of their respective alliances, fought a generation-long war recorded by Thucydides and, after his history breaks off, by Xenophon. Plague devastated Athens from 430 BCE; the Athenian expedition to Sicily ended in catastrophe in 413 BCE; and in 404 BCE, starved by blockade after its fleet was destroyed at Aigospotami, Athens surrendered. The war ended Athenian empire and began a century in which no Greek state could hold the hegemony all of them sought.
399 BCE
The trial and death of Socrates. An Athenian jury convicted Socrates, aged about seventy, on charges of not recognising the gods of the city, introducing new divinities, and corrupting the young; he was executed by drinking hemlock. Socrates wrote nothing. His pupil Plato made the trial and death — in the Apology, Crito and Phaedo — the founding scene of Western philosophy: a city that could kill its questioner, and a questioner who would not stop questioning to save his life.
387/386 BCE
Plato founds the Academy at Athens. On returning to Athens, Plato established his school in the grove of the hero Akademos outside the city walls — the institution whose name every later academy borrows. There Aristotle studied for twenty years before founding his own Lyceum in 335 BCE. The Academy endured, through many changes of doctrine, for over nine centuries: its closure in 529 CE is one of this timeline's closing entries, and the span between the two dates is a fair measure of Greek philosophy's institutional life.
384–322 BCE
Aristotle — matter, mixture, and the subterranean fire. Plato's pupil of twenty years founds his own school, the Lyceum, in 335 BCE, and gives the world below the moon its lasting physics: form realised in matter, mixture as a true union of qualities, and — in the Meteorologica — metals generated within the earth from a moist exhalation, the Greek root from which the sulphur–mercury theory will grow.
338 BCE
Chaeronea — Macedon masters Greece. At Chaeronea in Boeotia, Philip II of Macedon defeated the combined forces of Athens, Thebes and their allies; the Theban Sacred Band was annihilated where it stood. In 337 BCE Philip organised most Greek states into the League of Corinth under Macedonian leadership, formally to make war on Persia. The independent foreign policy of the classical polis — the world that had produced Marathon, the Parthenon and Socrates' jury — was effectively over.
336–323 BCE
Alexander the Great — reign and conquests. Succeeding his assassinated father at twenty, Alexander crossed into Asia in 334 BCE and in eleven years destroyed the Achaemenid Persian Empire — Granicus, Issus, Gaugamela — took Egypt without a battle, and campaigned as far as the Indus before his army refused to go further. He died at Babylon in 323 BCE, leaving no adult heir. His conquests carried Greek language, cities and cults across the Near East and created the Hellenistic world in which Wing II begins.
331 BCE
Alexandria founded in Egypt. On the western edge of the Nile Delta, Alexander founded the city that bears his name — laid out, by tradition, with his own hand marking its streets. Under the Ptolemaic dynasty it became the largest Greek city in the world and its greatest centre of learning, governing Egypt through a Greek-speaking court while ruling an Egyptian countryside of temples and villages. No single place matters more to this hub: Alexandria is where Wing I's gods were re-imagined and Wing II's art was invented.
early 3rd century BCE
The Museum and Library of Alexandria founded. Under Ptolemy I Soter and Ptolemy II Philadelphus — the project is associated with Demetrius of Phalerum, an exiled Athenian statesman formed in Aristotle's school — Alexandria acquired the Mouseion, a royally salaried research community, and its famous Library. Scholars there edited Homer and the tragedians into the texts we still read, and Callimachus catalogued the collection in his Pinakes. Royal patronage of learning on this scale was itself a Hellenistic invention.
c. 300 BCE
Euclid's Elements — Alexandrian science at full stretch. Working in Alexandria around 300 BCE, Euclid organised geometry into the axiomatic Elements, the most durable textbook in the history of science. He stands here for a constellation: Eratosthenes, librarian at Alexandria, who measured the Earth's circumference from shadow lengths; Aristarchus, who proposed a heliocentric system; Herophilus and Erasistratus, who founded systematic anatomy. Hellenistic Alexandria did not only preserve Greek learning — it extended it further, faster, than the classical world ever had.
c. 250 BCE
Apollonius Rhodius composes the Argonautica. In Ptolemaic Alexandria, a scholar-poet of the Library retold the oldest of Greek voyage-tales — Jason, Medea and the Golden Fleece — as a learned epic in four books. The saga was already archaic when the Odyssey mentioned the Argo as "known to all"; Apollonius's poem is the fullest surviving telling, and the form in which the story reached every later reader, the alchemical readers included.
c. 200 BCE
Bolos of Mendes — the name behind pseudo-Democritus. Bolos, a Hellenised Egyptian writer from Mendes in the Delta, flourished around 200 BCE — the floruit is also given as the 3rd century BCE — writing on sympathies and antipathies in nature under the authority of the philosopher Democritus. Later scholarship has attached his name to the alchemical Physika kai Mystika. The connection is genuinely disputed: the surviving alchemical text, in the form we have it, is now generally placed much later, in the 1st century CE. This hub therefore treats Bolos and the alchemical pseudo-Democritus as related but distinguishable problems — see the timeline entry for the 1st century CE below, and the Wing II article on the origins of the art.
146 BCE
Corinth destroyed — Rome absorbs mainland Greece. After defeating the Achaean League, the Roman general Mummius sacked Corinth — among the richest and oldest Greek cities — and its territory was annexed; Macedonia had already become a Roman province. Greece kept its language, its schools and its prestige, and Roman aristocrats would soon be sending their sons to Athens to be educated. But political sovereignty had passed, permanently, to Rome: the Greek world would be governed from outside itself for two thousand years.
31 BCE
Actium — Octavian defeats Antony and Cleopatra. Off the western coast of Greece, the fleet of Octavian — the future Augustus — defeated the combined forces of Mark Antony and Cleopatra VII of Egypt. The battle decided the Roman civil wars and, with them, the fate of the last great Hellenistic kingdom. Antony and Cleopatra fled to Alexandria and held it for one more year; the Hellenistic age, conventionally dated from Alexander's death in 323 BCE, ends with theirs.
30 BCE
Alexandria falls — Egypt becomes a Roman province. Octavian entered Alexandria in 30 BCE; Antony and Cleopatra died by their own hands, and Egypt was annexed not as an ordinary province but as the emperor's personal possession, governed by an equestrian prefect and closed to senators. Alexandria remained a Greek metropolis — restless, brilliant, and now the intellectual capital of a Roman world. It is under Roman rule, in this Greek city on Egyptian soil, that alchemy as a written discipline appears.
19 BCE
Virgil's Aeneid published after his death. Virgil (70–19 BCE) left the Aeneid unfinished, and Augustus had it published as it stood. Its sixth book — Aeneas's descent to the dead, guided by the Cumaean Sibyl — is the most complete underworld the ancient world produced: the geography of Hades, its judges, punishments and Elysian rewards systematised into a single journey, and the katabasis tradition's Roman summa.
1st century CE
The Physika kai Mystika in its surviving form — the Roman-era workshop. The oldest substantial alchemical treatise we possess, the Physika kai Mystika (Natural and Secret Questions) ascribed to Democritus, survives as epitomised extracts of a work in four books on dyeing — metals gold and silver, stones, and wool purple. Recent scholarship places its anonymous alchemical author in the second half of the 1st century CE, often c. 54–68 CE, centuries after both Democritus and Bolos. Its subject throughout is the workshop operation the Greeks called baphē, dyeing or tinting: giving a base material the colour, and so the appearance and standing, of a precious one. Theory and recipe are already inseparable in it.
1st–3rd century CE
Maria the Jewess and the apparatus tradition. Known only through quotation and report — above all by Zōsimos, who treats her as an ancient authority — Maria is placed by the tradition that cites her somewhere in the first three centuries CE, in Alexandria. To her are credited the descriptions or inventions that define early alchemical equipment: the tribikos, a three-armed still; the kērotakis, a sealed reflux vessel in which metal leaf was exposed to vapours; and the gentle water bath that still bears her name in the French bain-marie. Whether one historical woman stands behind the attributions cannot be proved; that the attributions shaped fifteen centuries of laboratory practice is not in doubt.
c. 46 – after 119 CE
Plutarch of Chaeronea — priest, biographer, Platonist. Plutarch, who served for many years as a priest of Apollo at Delphi, wrote the Parallel Lives — paired biographies of Greek and Roman statesmen that became Europe's schoolbook of antiquity — and the wide-ranging essays of the Moralia, including major works on Isis and Osiris and on the Delphic oracles. He is this hub's model Roman-era Greek witness: a man of the classical tradition writing, observantly and from inside, about cults far older than it.
2nd century CE
Ptolemy of Alexandria — the ancient cosmos systematised. Claudius Ptolemy, working in Alexandria — his dated astronomical observations run from 127 to 141 CE — composed the Almagest, the mathematical astronomy that governed the science until Copernicus, together with the Geography, the Tetrabiblos on astrology, and works on optics and harmonics. His synthesis drew on Babylonian records and Greek geometry alike. Through Arabic and then Latin translation, Ptolemy — not any classical Athenian — was the Greek scientist the medieval world actually read.
129–c. 216 CE
Galen of Pergamum — temperament systematised. Physician to Marcus Aurelius and the most prolific medical author of antiquity, Galen canonises the four humours and grades every simple drug by degrees — first to fourth — of heating, cooling, drying and moistening. That degree language becomes the quantitative idiom of Arabic and Latin medicine and alchemy alike.
late 2nd century CE
Apuleius writes the Metamorphoses. Apuleius of Madaura (c. 124 – c. 170 CE), Platonist philosopher once tried on a charge of magic, composed the only Latin novel to survive entire — the Golden Ass — and set inside it, in Books 4–6, the tale of Cupid and Psyche, antiquity's fullest story of the soul's trials, before closing the novel with its hero's conversion to Isis in Book 11.
2nd–3rd century CE
The Hermetica composed in Roman Egypt. In Greek, in Egypt, teachers whose names we do not know composed the dialogues ascribed to Hermes Trismegistus — the figure formed by merging the Greek Hermes with the Egyptian Thoth. Seventeen Greek treatises survive as the Corpus Hermeticum, with the Latin Asclepius and further fragments and excerpts beside them. Their setting is an Egypt of deep antiquity; their language, philosophy and date are those of the Roman-era Alexandrian world, and modern scholarship — Copenhaver, Fowden — reads them precisely as that world's religious philosophy, not as survivals from the pharaonic age they evoke.
204/205–270 CE
Plotinus — the Neoplatonist turn. An Egyptian-born philosopher teaching in Rome orders all reality as the procession of Soul and Intellect from an ineffable One, and the soul's task as return. His pupil Porphyry edits the lectures as the Enneads; within a generation Iamblichus answers contemplation with theurgy — rites worked with material tokens — and late-antique alchemy breathes the air of both arguments.
c. 300 CE
The Leyden and Stockholm papyri — the workshop on paper. Two Greek recipe collections, written about 300 CE, probably by the same hand, and recovered from a Theban burial context, preserve the bench literature of the Roman-era craft. Leyden Papyrus X gives recipes for alloys, for colouring metal surfaces, for testing purity and for writing in gold and silver; the Stockholm Papyrus gives 154 recipes for dyeing, for imitation gems, and for cleaning and fabricating pearls. Both are candid that many products are imitations — and both draw, in part, on the pseudo-Democritean tradition. They are Wing II's ground truth: this is what the art looked like with its sleeves rolled up.
c. 300 CE
Zōsimos of Panopolis — the first alchemist we can read whole. From Panopolis in Upper Egypt, writing around 300 CE, Zōsimos compiled, commented on and argued with the whole earlier tradition — Democritus, Maria, Hermes — in works that survive in Greek and in Syriac and Arabic transmission. His Visions, dream-accounts of a sacrificial altar that is also a vessel and a man, fuse apparatus description with spiritual autobiography as no earlier text does. Through Zōsimos, uniquely, we can watch an ancient alchemist thinking: citing authorities, describing furnaces, and interpreting his own dreams of the work.
391 CE
The Serapeum of Alexandria destroyed. After street fighting between pagans and Christians, and under imperial authorisation following Theodosius I's prohibitions of pagan cult, a Christian crowd led by the patriarch Theophilus destroyed the Serapeum — Alexandria's great temple of Serapis and one of the wonders of the city, which also housed a book collection. The destruction became, already in the ancient sources, a symbol fought over by both sides; soberly, it marks the moment public pagan cult in Alexandria ceased to be defensible, a generation before Hypatia's murder.
415 CE
Hypatia murdered in Alexandria. Hypatia — mathematician, astronomer, and the city's leading teacher of Neoplatonic philosophy, daughter of the mathematician Theon — was seized by a Christian mob in March 415, in the midst of a political feud between the patriarch Cyril and the imperial prefect Orestes, dragged into a church and killed. The ancient sources, including the church historian Socrates, treat the murder as a disgrace to the city. Later ages made her a martyr variously of science, of philosophy or of paganism; the record supports a colder verdict — a scholar killed by the politics of her city and her moment.
529 CE
Justinian closes the Academy at Athens. The emperor Justinian's measures against pagan teaching in 529 ended the Neoplatonic school at Athens — the institutional descendant, through many transformations, of the Academy Plato had founded over nine centuries earlier. Its last head, Damascius, and six colleagues famously withdrew for a time to the Persian court. Philosophy did not end: it continued in Alexandria, increasingly in Christian hands. But 529 remains the conventional closing date of ancient Greek philosophy as a pagan institution.
6th century CE
Olympiodorus and the commentators — the corpus takes shape. In 6th-century Alexandria, the Neoplatonic philosopher Olympiodorus the Younger lectured on Plato and Aristotle and commented on the alchemical authorities, Democritus and Zōsimos among them; Synesius and others of the same milieu did similar work before and beside him. It is to this generation of commentators — Christian-era scholars expounding pagan masters — that we owe the shape of the Greek alchemical corpus: an anthology of extracts, authorities and commentaries rather than a shelf of single-authored books. Systematisation, here, is itself a form of preservation.
7th century CE
Stephanus of Alexandria — the last Alexandrian master. Stephanus (fl. c. 580–640) taught philosophy at Alexandria and then, after Heraclius became emperor in 610, at Constantinople, lecturing on Plato, Aristotle, astronomy, astrology and alchemy. Nine alchemical lectures (praxeis) on the making of gold are transmitted under his name, the ninth addressed to Heraclius himself; whether all are authentically his is debated, and F. Sherwood Taylor argued the case for authenticity in editing them. In the Morienus tradition, Stephanus is the teacher from whom the art passed to the Arabic-speaking world — a legend built, as legends are, on a real eminence.
641 CE
The Arab conquest of Egypt completed. Arab armies under ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ took Egypt from the Byzantine Empire between 639 and 642; Alexandria capitulated by treaty, and the conquest is conventionally dated to 641 CE. Greek remained an administrative language for decades, and Alexandria's scholars, craftsmen and manuscripts did not vanish — they acquired new rulers and, in time, a new language of learning. For Wing II this is not an ending but a change of custody: the Alexandrian art now begins its Syriac and Arabic life, from which the Latin West would eventually receive it.
8th–9th century CE
Balinas and the Secret of Creation — the Emerald Tablet's earliest home. The Arabic Kitāb sirr al-khalīqa (Book of the Secret of Creation), attributed to Balinas — Apollonius of Tyana in his Arabic afterlife — frames its cosmology with a discovery story: a vault beneath a statue of Hermes, an enthroned elder, and a tablet of emerald. No Greek or Latin manuscript of the Emerald Tablet survives from antiquity; this Arabic book is its earliest attested witness, and the Latin career that made it famous begins only in the 12th century.
10th–11th century CE
The Marcianus codex — the Greek alchemists preserved. In Constantinople, a Byzantine compiler's great anthology of Greek alchemy was copied into the manuscript now in Venice as Codex Marcianus graecus 299, dated to the 10th or 11th century — the oldest and most important surviving collection of the Greek alchemical corpus, with the Paris manuscripts (Parisinus graecus 2325 and 2327) beside it. Its famous single leaf, the Chrysopoeia of Cleopatra, preserves early apparatus drawings and the ouroboros image. Berthelot and Ruelle's great 19th-century edition, the foundation of all modern work on Wing II, rests principally on these codices: what Byzantium copied, the modern world can read.
◆ THRESHOLD — 1144 CE · THE LATIN DOOR OPENS ◆
On 11 February 1144, by its colophon, Robert of Chester completed the Liber de compositione alchemiae — the dialogue of the monk Morienus with the Umayyad prince Khalid ibn Yazid, translated from Arabic into Latin, and generally counted the first alchemical book of the Latin West. The chain the Morienus itself claims — Stephanus teaching Morienus, Morienus teaching Khalid — is legend built on real names; the translation is documentary fact. Through it, and the flood of Arabic-to-Latin translation that followed, the Alexandrian art entered the world of Albertus Magnus, Roger Bacon and the medieval Latin tradition. This hub's remit ends at that door. What walked through it belongs to the archive's later wings.
// WHAT THE TIMELINE TEACHES //

Read down the entries and the hub's wager becomes visible. Wing I's world — the world of Hesiod's genealogy and the Parthenon — is complete by the time Alexander dies. Wing II's world has barely begun at that date, and it is made entirely inside the first wing's ruins and libraries: Greek philosophy, Egyptian craft, Roman peace, Alexandrian scholarship. Alchemy is not a survival from the Bronze Age and not an import from outside the classical world. It is one of the classical world's last inventions — and, through Byzantium, Baghdad and Toledo, one of its longest-lived.

V. Article Index

Thirty-seven full articles develop the hub's two wings — seventeen for mythology, twenty for alchemy. Wing I carries, at its centre, a Myths & Legends cluster: the great story-cycles told in full, because the alchemical tradition itself transmitted its teaching through these stories, veiled in allegory. Wing II rests on a Foundations cluster: the element theory, matter theory, physics, medicine, astrology and theology the Alexandrian practitioners inherited, expounded in their own right ahead of the articles that use them. Each article is listed here exactly once, under its wing, and each follows the hub's method: named editions, cult distinguished from literature, Greek distinguished from Roman reception, no invented quotations, speculation labelled.

Wing I — Mythology

The Theogony and the Succession Myth

Hesiod's Theogony in West's edition: Chaos, Gaia, Ouranos, Kronos and Zeus read as a succession myth with Near Eastern parallels, and as the charter genealogy that ordered the Greek divine world for a thousand years.

The Olympian Gods — System and Cult

The twelve Olympians as a poetic system and as a lived pantheon: epithets, sanctuaries, sacrifices and festivals, with Burkert's Greek Religion as the guide to where the poems end and the cults begin.

Heroes and Epic

Heracles, Theseus and Jason between shrine and story, and the Homeric question itself — who, if anyone, Homer was, how oral epic became written text, and what hero cult did that epic never mentions.

The Mysteries

Eleusis, the Orphica, the Dionysian and Samothracian mysteries: initiation, secrecy and the promise of a better lot after death, reconstructed from the hymns, the gold tablets, the archaeology and Burkert's Ancient Mystery Cults — with what cannot be reconstructed said plainly.

The Homeric Epics — The Iliad and Odyssey as Texts

Two poems, three kinds of object: the oral-formulaic account of Parry and Lord, the Homeric Question from Wolf to Nagy and West, the Alexandrian editors and the Venetus A, and Homer's thousand years as the Greek schoolbook — the text every later reader learned to read through.

Roman Religion — The Rites of the City and the Household

Rome's religion on its own terms, not Greek myth in Latin dress: pontifices and augury, the inscribed calendar, lares and penates, the Capitoline, the imported cults under licence, and the imperial cult — a system of exact performance maintaining the pax deorum.

Wing I — Myths & Legends

The cycles told whole: the stories the tradition itself used as teaching vehicles, each presented as a text with its transmission, its variants kept visible, and its cult and historical footing stated at its real strength. How the alchemists read these same stories as veiled instruction is Wing II's Alchemical Allegory.

The Argonauts — Jason, Medea, and the Golden Fleece

Apollonius's Argonautica and Apollodorus: the voyage in full, the Fleece's nature debated already in antiquity, and Medea — her pharmaka, her cauldron of rejuvenation, and the trick turned murderous at Corinth.

The Twelve Labours of Heracles

How scattered local feats became a canonical twelve: Apollodorus's order against Diodorus's, the Olympia metopes as the earliest visual canon, the two disqualified labours, and Heracles worshipped as both god and hero.

Theseus — The Labyrinth and the Making of Athens' Hero

Ariadne's thread, the Minotaur, the black sail, and the ship whose planks were replaced one by one; behind the tale, the Cretan background at its real strength and Cimon's recovery of the hero's bones as democratic statecraft.

Perseus — Medusa, Pegasus, and the Hero of Argos

The quest as a system of tools and indirect seeing: the Graeae, the mirrored shield, the gorgoneion turned protective on Athena's aegis, and Bellerophon — the sequel the tradition itself attached to Pegasus.

The Wanderings of Odysseus

The sea-tale stratum of the Odyssey, Books 9–12 told in full: the Cyclops, Circe, the Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, the cattle of the Sun — and Eratosthenes' cobbler, antiquity's own warning against mapping the voyage.

The Trojan Cycle — The War as Tradition, Text, and Ruin

Not two poems but a ring of them: the lost Epic Cycle from Proclus's summaries, the story told whole once, its afterlife in tragedy and cult, and Hisarlik's strata at their real strength — literature built on a real place.

The Theban Cycle — Cadmus, Oedipus, and the Seven

The other great cycle, and the darker: Cadmus bringing the alphabet, the sown men, the house of Laius eating itself across four generations, the Seven and the Epigoni — a family saga on a real Mycenaean ruin.

Descents — Katabasis and the Geography of the Dead

The underworld mapped river by river, then walked: Odysseus's nekyia, Heracles for Cerberus, Theseus in the chair of forgetfulness, Orpheus's backward look, and Aeneid VI as the tradition's Roman summa.

Makers and Fire — Prometheus, Hephaestus, and Daedalus

The craft-and-fire cluster: the trick at Mecone, the fennel stalk, Pandora's jar, the smith-god's masterworks, and the mortal maker whose wings melted — the myths a fire-working tradition naturally claimed as its own.

Cupid and Psyche — Apuleius and the Soul's Trials

Apuleius's tale in full: the unseen husband, the lamp, the four tasks, the descent for Proserpina's box — set in its Isiac frame in Book 11 and followed through its long afterlife as an allegory of the soul.

Legendary Lives — Sages, Wonder-Workers, and the Romance of History

Legend as a genre distinct from myth: Pythagoras's golden thigh, Empedocles' leap, Philostratus's Apollonius, the Alexander Romance, Aesop — named lives that accreted wonders, and what the accretion was for.

Wing II — Foundations

The doctrines the alchemy articles presuppose, given articles of their own: the tetrad and its quality square, Aristotle's matter and mixture, Plato's receptacle and solids, Stoic pneuma and sympathy, the atomism the corpus both borrowed and refused, the humours and Galen's degrees, the astrology that timed the work, the Neoplatonism and theurgy of the art's late centuries, the technical Hermetica at the bench, and the fire-assays by which every claim was tested. Each article closes by naming what the Alexandrians actually took from the foundation it expounds.

The Four Elements — The Tetrad as a System

Empedocles' four roots and the powers that mix them, Love and Strife; the quality pairs assigned — fire hot and dry, air hot and wet, water cold and wet, earth cold and dry — and the square they form; Plato's and Aristotle's rival systematisations of the scheme that underwrites nearly every text in this wing.

Aristotle and Matter Theory

Hylomorphism and the disputed prime matter; mixis against mere juxtaposition; the two exhalations and Meteorologica IV as the Greek root of the sulphur–mercury theory; and the alchemical works under Aristotle's name, flagged as the pseudepigraphy they are.

Plato's Timaeus — The Receptacle and the Solids

The demiurge, the world-soul's ratios, and the chōra that receives all forms; the elements built as regular solids and transformed by triangle arithmetic, earth alone excluded; Calcidius's channel to the Latin West, and the dialogue's authority for Hermetists and alchemists.

Stoic Physics — Pneuma, Tension, and Sympathy

The two principles, active breath and passive matter; pneuma and its tension grading hexis, nature and soul; total blending through and through; sympatheia as a physical doctrine of the one cosmic animal, and the conflagration that ends each world — the substrate of the workshop's sympathy language.

Atomism — Democritus, Epicurus, Lucretius

Atoms and void from Leucippus and the Abderite through Epicurus's swerve to the De Rerum Natura; and the productive tension at this hub's origin — a physics of qualities and sympathies travelling for centuries under the atomist's name, the three Democrituses kept carefully distinct.

The Humours and Galen — Temperament and the Degrees of Simples

The Hippocratic corpus as a library, not a man; the four humours bound to seasons, ages and the quality tetrad; krasis and Galen's nine mixtures; and the grading of simple drugs by degrees of heating, cooling, drying and moistening — the quantitative language medicine and alchemy share.

Greek Astrology — The Timing Science

The Hellenistic synthesis and its charter horoscope, the Thema Mundi; the working apparatus of signs, places, aspects, lots and decans; Dorotheus on elections, Valens's practitioner's Anthology, and Ptolemy's Tetrabiblos with its natural-philosophy defence — astrology as the science by which the workshop timed its operations.

Neoplatonism and Theurgy — Plotinus to Iamblichus

The One, Intellect and Soul; emanation and the soul's return; Iamblichus's answer in the De Mysteriis — the fully descended soul and theurgy as god-worked rite; the chains of sympathy running through stones, plants and names, and the animation of statues — the philosophical air late-antique alchemy breathed.

The Technical Hermetica — Hermes at the Bench

The practical family the Hermetica article promises: Nechepso and Petosiris, Hermetic astrology and iatromathematics, the Kyranides with its alphabetised sympathies of bird, fish, plant and stone, and the alchemical Hermetica — Hermes not as philosopher but as the name on the recipe, the election and the remedy.

Fire and Assay — Testing in the Greek World

Proof as procedure: the touchstone and its graded needles, cupellation in the bone-ash cupel, parting at Sardis, the hydrostatic test and the truth about Archimedes' crown, and Athens' public coin-tester under law — the tradition of testing that the recipe papyri write down for the workshop.

Wing II — Alchemy

Origins — Bolos and Pseudo-Democritus

Bolos of Mendes, the Democritus attribution, and the Physika kai Mystika: how Egyptian craft, Greek natural philosophy and the doctrine of sympathies met, and why the dating of the earliest alchemical book is still an honest scholarly argument.

Maria the Jewess and the Apparatus Tradition

The tribikos, the kērotakis and the bain-marie: the earliest named laboratory tradition in the West, what Zōsimos actually reports of Maria, and where reverent attribution ends and evidence begins.

Zōsimos of Panopolis

The Visions, the letters to Theosebeia, and the Greek, Syriac and Arabic transmission of the most substantial ancient alchemist: apparatus, allegory and autobiography in the works Mertens edited and Berthelot first printed whole.

The Commentators — Alchemy in the Lecture Hall

Synesius, Olympiodorus, Christianus and Stephanus of Alexandria: the art's move from workshop letter to commentary, the canon fixed and the vocabulary systematised — and the three identity debates the scholarship still argues, stated fairly.

The Chrysopoeia of Cleopatra — One Leaf, One Serpent

The most famous single page in Greek alchemy read element by element: the ouroboros and its "one is the all," the ring diagram, the apparatus sketches beside the text — and what one leaf in one codex can and cannot prove.

The Hermetica — From Alexandria to Ficino

The Corpus Hermeticum and the Asclepius: composed in Roman Egypt, lost to the Latin West for a millennium, carried in Byzantine manuscripts, and translated by Marsilio Ficino in 1463 — printed 1471 — in Copenhaver's and Fowden's readings.

The Emerald Tablet Before the Latin West

Balinas in the vault beneath Hermes' statue: the Tablet's earliest surviving home in the Arabic Secret of Creation, its text walked through sentence by sentence — thirteen in the usual segmentation — and the Greek original named as inference, not document.

Transmission — Byzantium, Syriac, Arabic, Latin

How the art survived its world: the Byzantine commentators and the Marcianus codex, the Syriac translators, the Arabic masters who transformed what they received, and Robert of Chester's 1144 Morienus at the Latin door.

Alchemical Allegory — Myth as the Work's Veil

The keystone of this expansion: how alchemists actually read myth as veiled instruction — from Greek allegoresis through the Fleece-as-book claim and Medea's cauldron to Maier's Atalanta Fugiens and Pernety's mytho-hermetic dictionary — presented as the alchemists' own documented reading, in its own terms.

Decknamen — A Glossary of Greek Alchemical Cover-Names

A working glossary of the corpus's cover-names: term, literal sense, and referent with the evidence graded for each — drawn from the ancient lexica, Zōsimos, Synesius and the recipe papyri, with the genuinely unknown names kept as unknowns.

VI. Sources and Further Reading

The works below are the backbone scholarship of this hub — the books its timeline was checked against and its articles are built on. Wing I rests on Burkert and West; Wing II on Berthelot and Ruelle's edition of the corpus, on Mertens, and on the modern histories of Taylor, Lindsay and Principe; the Hermetica on Copenhaver and Fowden. Primary texts are cited throughout in the editions named here.

Wing I — Myth, Religion and the Texts

  • Burkert, W., Greek Religion: Archaic and Classical (English translation, Harvard University Press, 1985) — the standard account of Greek cult practice, festival and sanctuary, and this hub's model for distinguishing cult evidence from literary myth.
  • Burkert, W., Ancient Mystery Cults (Harvard University Press, 1987) — Eleusis, Dionysian and Orphic initiation, the Samothracian and Isiac mysteries, and Mithras, compared without conflation; the backbone of the Mysteries article.
  • West, M. L. (ed.), Hesiod: Theogony (Oxford, 1966), with introduction and commentary; and West, M. L. (ed.), Hesiod: Works and Days (Oxford, 1978) — the editions of Hesiod cited throughout Wing I, including the Near Eastern parallels of the succession myth.
  • Burkert, W., The Orientalizing Revolution (English translation, Harvard University Press, 1992), and West, M. L., The East Face of Helicon (Oxford, 1997) — consulted for the Near Eastern background of Greek myth and poetry in the Bronze Age and archaic entries of the timeline.

Wing II — The Greek Alchemists and Their Historians

  • Berthelot, M. & Ruelle, C.-É., Collection des anciens alchimistes grecs, 3 vols. (Paris, 1887–1888) — the foundational edition of the Greek alchemical corpus from the Marcianus and Paris manuscripts; dated 1888–1889 in some catalogues. Still the point of departure for every text in Wing II.
  • Mertens, M., Les alchimistes grecs (Paris, Les Belles Lettres) — the modern critical editions, notably tome IV, Zosime de Panopolis: Mémoires authentiques (1995), with translation and commentary; the edition of Zōsimos cited in this hub.
  • Taylor, F. Sherwood, The Alchemists: Founders of Modern Chemistry (1949), and Taylor's editions and studies of Stephanus of Alexandria in Ambix (1937–1938) — the case for the authenticity of Stephanus's alchemical lectures, and a lucid first map of the Greek tradition.
  • Lindsay, J., The Origins of Alchemy (1970) — the Alexandrian origins of the art set in their full Egyptian, Greek and artisanal context; dated in argument, unmatched in range, and read here alongside the scholarship that has revised it.
  • Principe, L. M., The Secrets of Alchemy (University of Chicago Press, 2013) — the current standard short history, from the Greco-Egyptian beginnings through the Arabic and Latin traditions to the laboratory reconstructions of early practice; this hub's check on its own Wing II chronology.

Wing II — Foundations

  • Long, A. A. & Sedley, D. N., The Hellenistic Philosophers, 2 vols. (Cambridge University Press, 1987) — the testimony collection behind the Foundations cluster's Stoic physics and atomism: pneuma, total blending, sympathy and the conflagration in the sources' own words.

// A STANDING CORRECTION //

Two datings repeated in popular writing are checked and not repeated in this hub: Bolos of Mendes belongs to around 200 BCE, but the alchemical Physika kai Mystika in its surviving form belongs to the 1st century CE; and the Corpus Hermeticum was composed in the 2nd–3rd centuries CE, not in the age of Moses — a point settled for the Latin tradition when Isaac Casaubon redated the treatises on linguistic evidence in 1614, after Ficino's 1463 translation had presented them to Europe as primordial wisdom.

Dates in this hub will be revised as the literature moves — the dating debate over the alchemical pseudo-Democritus has moved once within living scholarship, and may move again. Where it does, the timeline entry will be rewritten to say what changed and why, in line with the method set out above.
