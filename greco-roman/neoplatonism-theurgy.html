I. Foundations: The Air Alchemy Breathed

This article belongs to the Foundations cluster of the Alchemy wing, and like its sibling on the Hermetica it is not, strictly speaking, an article about alchemy. No furnace is lit in it, no apparatus is drawn, no metal changes colour. Its subject is prior to all of that: the philosophy inside which Alexandrian alchemy became thinkable as a sacred science rather than a workshop trick. When Zosimos of Panopolis, writing around 300 CE, dreams of altars that are also men, of metals that suffer, die, and rise, and of a cosmos in which every material thing is bound to every other by hidden kinship, he is not inventing a private mythology. He is drawing, often without citation, on the common intellectual stock of his century — and the largest single deposit in that stock is the philosophy modern scholarship calls Neoplatonism.

The word itself is modern. No ancient philosopher called himself a Neoplatonist; Plotinus, Porphyry, Iamblichus, and Proclus all understood themselves simply as Platonists — interpreters of Plato, recovering what Plato had meant. The modern label marks a real difference of temper and system all the same. Between roughly 250 and 530 CE the Platonic tradition produced something Plato himself had not: a complete, hierarchical metaphysics in which all reality proceeds from a single, utterly simple first principle — the One — through the divine Intellect and Soul, down to the last dregs of matter, and in which the human soul’s whole task is the journey back. That metaphysics is the first half of this article’s subject. The second half is what happened to it when one of its greatest systematicians concluded that the journey back could not be made by thought alone, and that the gods themselves had provided the missing vehicle: theurgy (theourgia), literally “god-work” — rite understood not as human bargaining with the divine but as divine action performed through human hands, materials, and names.

NEOPLATONISM

The systematic Platonism of Plotinus (204/5–270) and his successors: reality as an ordered procession from the One through Intellect and Soul to matter; salvation as the soul’s contemplative return (epistrophē) to its source. The metaphysical frame of nearly all late-antique learned religion, pagan and — by adoption and argument — much Christian and later Islamic and Jewish thought besides.

THEURGY

The ritual system defended above all by Iamblichus (c. 245–c. 325) in the De Mysteriis: divinely instituted rites — sacrifice, invocation, divination, the consecration of statues — held to purify and elevate the embodied soul by the gods’ own power working through material tokens (symbola, synthēmata) sown through the cosmos. Not, in its own account, coercion of the gods: receptivity prepared, divinity received.

Why an alchemy hub needs this article can be put in one sentence each for its two halves. From Neoplatonism, alchemy inherited its cosmos: a living, hierarchically ordered whole in which the lowest things genuinely participate in the highest, so that work done on matter could be, in principle, work done on reality itself and not mere tinkering with appearances. From theurgy, it inherited its method’s dignity: the conviction that material operations — the right substance, prepared the right way, at the right time, under the right name — are not a descent from philosophy into superstition but one of the places philosophy happens. The alchemist who seals a vessel, times an operation by the heavens, and addresses the work in prayer is not a philosopher who has lapsed. In the world this article describes, he is applying the metaphysics.

One framing caution before the system itself. It is tempting to tell this story as a decline: pure philosophy first, ritual superstition after — the so-called “irrational turn” of late antiquity. That telling has a distinguished pedigree, and Section XV gives it its due. But it is a verdict, not a fact, and the revisionist scholarship of the last half-century — John Dillon’s work on Iamblichus, Gregory Shaw’s Theurgy and the Soul, Ilinca Tanaseanu-Döbler’s Theurgy in Late Antiquity — has made the opposite case with the texts open on the desk: that Iamblichus’s turn to rite follows from premises Plotinus himself supplied, argued with philosophical rigour, and that the difference between the two men is a disagreement inside Platonism about one precise question — how far the soul falls when it enters a body. This article states both readings and adjudicates where the evidence allows. It does not smuggle either verdict into its descriptions.

II. Plotinus and Porphyry: The Life, the School, the Book

Plotinus (204/5–270 CE) was born in Roman Egypt — the tradition gives Lycopolis, in the Delta, though he himself refused to speak of his birthplace, his parents, or his birthday, and the refusal is part of the evidence. Nearly everything known of his life comes from one source: the Life of Plotinus written by his pupil Porphyry (c. 234–c. 305) as the preface to Porphyry’s edition of the master’s writings. The Life is a devoted pupil’s work, not a neutral record, and this article treats its anecdotes as Porphyry’s testimony rather than as court evidence. But it is testimony of the first order, from a man who lived in the circle for some six years, and its main lines have never been seriously displaced.

In paraphrase of Porphyry’s account: Plotinus came to philosophy at twenty-eight, in Alexandria, working through the city’s famous teachers in disappointment until he was brought to Ammonius Saccas, the Platonist who taught without writing — and recognised his teacher at once, remaining with Ammonius for eleven years. At thirty-nine he joined the emperor Gordian III’s Persian expedition in the hope of reaching the philosophers of Persia and India; the expedition collapsed with Gordian’s murder, and Plotinus escaped to Antioch and then to Rome, where from about 244 he taught for the rest of his life. He began to write only at about fifty. Porphyry’s famous opening judgement — in paraphrase, that Plotinus seemed ashamed of being in the body, and would not sit for a portrait, since a portrait would be an image of an image — is the biographical seed of the whole philosophy that follows: a man for whom embodiment was the problem to which thought was the answer.

The school in Rome was a circle more than an institution: lectures built around the reading of Plato and Aristotle and their commentators, open discussion in which, Porphyry says, the master welcomed interruption and objection, and a household that took in orphans and administered their property with an exactness Porphyry praises — the otherworldly teacher as meticulous trustee. Plotinus wrote in Greek, badly spelled and worse punctuated by his own cheerful admission, in treatises that read as the school’s internal arguments set down: objections raised and answered, positions tried, abandoned, resumed. He died in 270, in Campania, having withdrawn as illness — probably a form of leprosy, on the usual modern reading of Porphyry’s symptoms — made company difficult. Porphyry records his last words, addressed to his physician Eustochius; the Greek permits two construals, and translations divide between a confession (“I am striving to give back the divine in myself to the divine in the All”) and an exhortation (“Strive to bring back the god in you to the divine in the universe”). The ambiguity is preserved here deliberately: it is a small, exact emblem of how much of Plotinus reaches us already interpreted.

Porphyry the Editor: How the Enneads Were Made

Plotinus left no book. He left fifty-four treatises, written across the last seventeen years of his life, in no published order. The book the world reads — the Enneads — is Porphyry’s construction, made some thirty years after the master’s death, around 301 CE. Porphyry tells us in the Life (chapters 24–26) exactly what he did, and the candour is the point: he edited, titled, divided, and arranged. To reach the number fifty-four he split some long works into several treatises and joined others; he then grouped the fifty-four into six sets of nine — six enneads, from the Greek ennea, nine — and disposed the whole in three volumes. The arrangement is not chronological. It is a curriculum: an ascent for the reader, deliberately modelled on the ascent the philosophy describes.

Two features of the edition matter for everything after. First, Porphyry also preserved, in the Life, the chronological order in which the treatises were actually written — twenty-one before his own arrival in Rome, twenty-four during his years in the circle, nine after his departure for Sicily — so modern scholarship can read Plotinus twice over: in the master’s developing order, or in the pupil’s pedagogical one. Citations in this article follow the universal convention: Ennead, treatise, chapter — thus V.1 is the first treatise of the Fifth Ennead, “On the Three Primary Hypostases”, the tenth treatise in chronological order and the system’s own best summary. Second, the Life itself became the tradition’s front door. Every later Platonist, and every modern reader since, has met Plotinus through Porphyry’s framing: the ashamed-of-the-body sage, the four ecstasies (Porphyry reports that in their six years together Plotinus attained union with the One four times, Porphyry himself once) — a biography that is already an interpretation, and one its subject, who would not even sit for a portrait, might have declined to authorise. The editor is the first commentator. Remember that when Section VII reaches the pupil’s second career: as the questioner whose doubts provoked the theurgic turn.

III. The Three Hypostases: One, Intellect, Soul

The system can be stated in a single paragraph, and Plotinus states it himself, in paraphrase, at the opening of V.1: there are three primary realities — hypostases, the word means something like “that which stands under”, a real, subsistent level of being — and everything else is their product, image, or failure. At the summit, the One (to Hen), also called the Good. Below it, Intellect (Nous), the realm of true being and of Plato’s Forms. Below Intellect, Soul (Psychē) — first the World Soul that animates the cosmos, then, as its powers and offspring, the souls of stars, of daimones, of human beings, of animals and plants. Below Soul, the sensible world of bodies; and at the very floor, matter, of which Section V treats. The hierarchy is not a ladder built once and left standing. It is a continuous, eternal dependence: each level exists, at every moment, by looking toward the level above it.

The One: Beyond Being, Beyond Predication

The One is not a being. That is the first and hardest sentence of the system, and Plotinus means it strictly: being involves form, form involves determination, determination involves duality — a thing and its form, a thinker and a thought — and the first principle can contain no duality at all, or it would not be first. The One is therefore beyond being (epekeina tēs ousias — Plato’s own phrase for the Good in the Republic, which Plotinus takes as his charter), beyond intellect, beyond self-knowledge: it does not think, because thinking divides; it does not will in our sense, because willing implies a lack or an object. Every name for it — One, Good, First, Cause, Father — is, in Armstrong’s and Gerson’s shared rendering of Plotinus’s repeated warning, a pointer and not a description: we say what it is not, and the negations are truer than the affirmations. And yet it is, in the treatise’s famous phrase as both translations render the sense, the productive power of all things (dynamis pantōn): not one item in the inventory of reality but the reason there is an inventory. The paradox is deliberate and structural. A first principle that could be described would be one more thing among things; the One explains everything precisely by being nothing in particular.

Intellect: Being Thinking Itself

From the One — Section IV examines the “from” — comes Intellect, the second hypostasis, and with Intellect, being itself begins. Intellect is Plato’s realm of Forms gathered into a single living mind: it does not have thoughts about the Forms from outside, as a scholar consults a catalogue; it is the Forms, thinking itself, each Form transparent to all the others, thinker and thought one act. Plotinus’s V.5 insists, against any picture of the intelligibles as external standards, that the intelligibles are not outside Intellect: truth is not correspondence to something elsewhere but the identity of mind and being. Intellect is eternal, not in the sense of lasting a very long time but of standing outside time altogether — all its life at once, nothing awaited, nothing lost. It is also the first multiplicity: perfectly unified, but a unity that contains the articulated whole of what can be — being, sameness and difference, rest and motion, the great kinds of Plato’s Sophist — and so it stands one step, and exactly one step, below the absolute simplicity of the One.

Soul: The Amphibious Hypostasis

From Intellect comes Soul, the third hypostasis, and the system’s great mediator. Soul faces both ways. Turned upward, it contemplates Intellect and receives the Forms as rational principles (logoi) — the same contents Intellect holds timelessly, unfolded by Soul into sequence, discourse, and life. Turned downward, it gives those principles body: the World Soul pours them into the cosmos as nature, growth, sensation, and motion, and individual souls administer individual living things. Soul is the origin of time: in the celebrated account of III.7, in paraphrase, time is the life of Soul in movement — Soul, unable to hold Intellect’s all-at-once, spreading it out into before and after, and the sensible cosmos is that spreading made visible. Hence the description of soul that the whole later tradition, Iamblichus included, inherits: amphibious. The human soul lives in two worlds at once, intelligible and sensible, and its misery and its dignity are the same fact — it can forget which world is home. Everything in Sections VI–X of this article is a dispute about exactly how amphibious, and how forgetful, the soul can be.

THE ONE

Beyond being and thought; absolutely simple; the Good; productive power of all things. Known, if at all, by negation and by union — not by description. Enneads V.1, V.4, VI.9 are its charter treatises.

INTELLECT (NOUS)

Being itself as self-thinking mind; the Forms internal to it; eternal, all-at-once, the first multiplicity-in-unity. The soul’s true home and the object of its contemplation. Ennead V throughout.

SOUL (PSYCHĒ)

Image of Intellect; source of time, life, and the sensible cosmos; amphibious between the intelligible and the bodily. World Soul above, individual souls below — distinct, yet not severed. Ennead IV throughout.

A scholarly caution, owed to John Dillon’s The Middle Platonists (1977; revised 1996) above all: none of this was invented from nothing in 250 CE. The raw materials — a supreme principle beyond the demiurge, a mediating world-soul, the Forms as God’s thoughts, the language of procession and return — were assembled across the four centuries of Middle Platonism that Dillon’s book maps: Plutarch of Chaeronea, Alcinous, Numenius of Apamea (whose triad of gods stands nearest to Plotinus’s scheme), the Neopythagoreans, and behind them the Alexandrian Judaism of Philo. Plotinus’s originality is not the parts but the engineering: the strict derivation of each level from the contemplation of the one above, the location of the Forms inside Intellect, and the uncompromising simplicity of the One. “Neoplatonism” names the moment a tradition’s materials became a system — which is why the tradition’s later members, quarrelling over the system, could all plausibly claim to be restoring Plato.

IV. Emanation and Return: Procession, Contemplation, Ascent

How does anything come from the One? Plotinus’s answer, developed across V.4, V.1, and III.8 and paraphrased here, refuses the two obvious models. Not making: the One does not deliberate, choose, or act in time, and a first principle that decided to create would already be two things — a decider and a decision. Not diminution: the One is not divided among its products like a loaf among guests, and it loses nothing by their existence. The model Plotinus reaches for, in image after image, is overflow from perfection: as the sun gives light without being spent, as a spring flows without deciding to, as heat radiates from fire — what is perfect produces, necessarily and eternally, an external activity that is its image, while its internal activity remains itself, undiminished. Modern scholarship calls the doctrine emanation; Plotinus’s own preferred vocabulary is procession (proodos) and the double activity — each reality has an activity that constitutes it and an activity that flows from it, and the second is the next level of the world.

The mechanism, stated as precisely as paraphrase allows, is contemplation (theōria). What flows from the One is at first indefinite — a kind of unformed seeing, an intelligible matter — and it becomes Intellect by turning back toward the One and contemplating it; the turning (the return, epistrophē) is what gives it form, limit, and being. The pattern then repeats at every level: Intellect, contemplating the One, produces Soul as its external activity; Soul, contemplating Intellect, produces the ordered cosmos; and in the audacious generalisation of III.8, everything produces by contemplating — even nature, even plants, even the earth itself, in a quiet, dreamlike contemplation that issues in form. Production and return are not two events but one structure seen from two ends: the world is the hypostases’ contemplation, externalised; salvation is the same contemplation, recovered by the one part of the world that can recover it deliberately — the human soul.

The Return: Philosophy as the Way Back

For Plotinus the soul’s return is an inward journey with a strict itinerary, and its vehicle is philosophy practised as a way of life. In paraphrase of I.6 (On Beauty) and VI.9 (On the Good, or the One), the ascent runs: from beautiful bodies, which are form shining in matter, to the beauty of souls and practices; from there to Intellect, beauty itself, where the soul discovers that what it loved in fragments it is, in its own highest part; and beyond Intellect — beyond even the duality of thinker and thought — to the One, in a union (henōsis) that Plotinus describes, in the treatises’ most famous movement, as no longer seeing but being one with the seen, the centre coinciding with the centre. The treatise I.2 adds the ethical engine: the virtues purify, civic virtues first, then the higher, purificatory virtues that make the soul like god — Plato’s Theaetetus programme of homoiōsis theōi, assimilation to god, given a metaphysical staircase to climb. Dialectic (I.3) is the discipline that steadies the climb. Nothing in the itinerary requires a temple, a priest, an offering, or a name of power. It requires attention. Hold that fact: it is the exact point at which Iamblichus will dissent — not from the itinerary’s destination, but from its sufficiency for passengers as embodied as we are.

V. Matter as Privation — and the Honest Tension

Every system has a floor, and Plotinus’s is the strangest item in his philosophy. Matter (hylē) is what Soul’s productive activity finally reaches and cannot inform completely: pure receptivity without any form of its own, no quality, no quantity, no shape — in the analysis of II.4 (On Matter), a kind of non-being that is nevertheless not nothing, since bodies are manifestly of something. Plotinus’s language for it is relentlessly privative: matter is indefiniteness, unmeasuredness, the absence of limit and form; it is known, in a notorious phrase of the tradition’s paraphrase, only by a kind of bastard reasoning that thinks it by thinking everything else away. And in I.8 (On What Are and Whence Come Evils) Plotinus draws the conclusion his premises force: if the Good is the measure of all value, then matter, as the utter privation of form and measure, is evil itself — not a substance that does evil, not a rival god (Plotinus is no dualist; there is no evil principle enthroned beside the One), but evil as darkness is the absence of light: primary evil, with the soul’s vices as secondary evils, the infections a soul contracts by attending to the formless.

The doctrine is rigorous, and it is also — stated now as plainly as this article can state it — a standing problem for any philosophy of material transformation, and therefore for alchemy itself. Three tensions deserve honest statement rather than apologetic softening.

The Three Tensions

First, the tension of value. If matter is privation and primary evil, then matter is not, in itself, perfectible: there is nothing in it to perfect. Form comes to it wholly from above, as light falls on a mirror that contributes only the reflecting. An alchemy that claims to ennoble matter — to ripen lead toward gold as toward a nature matter itself is striving for — is speaking a language Plotinus’s floor does not obviously license. What the system licenses is imposition: form mastering a recalcitrant substrate, never the substrate’s own fulfilment.

Second, the tension of practice. Plotinus’s own ethics points away from the workshop. The philosopher’s task is purification — loosening the soul’s attention from the bodily — and the sensible world, however beautifully ordered as Soul’s image, is the place one learns to stop mistaking for home. A lifetime spent perfecting furnaces is, on a strict Plotinian accounting, a lifetime spent polishing the mirror. The historical alchemists knew the counter-texts inside the system — nature’s own contemplative production in III.8, the World Soul’s ungrudging government of the cosmos, the doctrine that the sage does not flee the world but ceases to be ruled by it — and they built on those. But they built against the grain of I.8, and intellectual honesty requires saying so.

Third, the tension inside Plotinus himself. The same philosopher who calls matter primary evil also insists, against the Gnostics in II.9, that the cosmos is beautiful, divine, and the best possible image of the intelligible; that the stars are gods; that to despise this world is to misunderstand its maker, who is Soul, who is good. Matter as substrate of the cosmic body is not the same topic as matter as bare privation, and Plotinus holds both analyses at once — the cosmos as splendid, its ultimate substrate as a kind of nothing that anchors evil. Later Platonists felt the strain. It is one of the pressures that pushed Iamblichus and Proclus toward a more positive account of the material vehicle: if the gods themselves sow tokens of their presence into stones and plants (Section XI), then matter, however lowly, is not mere privation — it is a receptacle prepared by divinity for divinity. Theurgy, among its other work, is Neoplatonism repairing its own floor.

It does not claim that Plotinus was an alchemist, that the Enneads teach transmutation, or that alchemy “is” applied Neoplatonism. The Alexandrian art is older than Plotinus — its roots are the subject of the hub’s Greek Alchemy: Origins article — and its practitioners drew on Stoic, Hermetic, Egyptian, and craft traditions as well as Platonic ones. The claim is the narrower, documented one: that from the third century onward the art was practised and defended inside a Neoplatonic cosmos, by men who increasingly spoke its language, and that the fit was never perfect. The seam shows, and this section has shown it on purpose.

VI. The Undescended Soul: Plotinus’s Wager

Buried in the technical treatises of Ennead IV is the single doctrine on which the whole theurgic controversy turns. In IV.8 (On the Descent of the Soul into Bodies) Plotinus confronts the obvious objection to his own system: if the soul has descended into body, fallen among sensations, passions, and forgetting, how can philosophy — the soul’s own act of thinking — possibly lift it out? His answer, in paraphrase, is the wager of his entire soteriology: the soul has not descended entirely. Something of it — its highest part, the soul insofar as it is turned toward Intellect — remains always in the intelligible world, contemplating, unfallen. We are not aware of it because awareness is monopolised by the descended part’s traffic with the body, as a man in a noisy room does not hear the music that never stopped. But the link was never cut. Ascent, therefore, is not travel to a foreign country; it is attention redirected — waking, as the tradition’s paraphrase has it, to what we already are. Contemplation suffices because the contemplator’s summit never left the place contemplation seeks.

The doctrine does enormous work. It explains how a fallen soul can know the intelligible at all (its summit is there); how virtue is possible without grace (the ladder’s top rung is already in our hands); why Plotinus can treat ritual as, at best, an irrelevance and, at worst — his reported refusal of a temple invitation, in Porphyry’s telling, on the ground that the gods ought to come to him, not he to them — a category error about where divinity is found. It also carries a cost that his successors itemised: it makes embodiment curiously unreal. If the true soul never descends, then the person who suffers, errs, and dies is a kind of shadow-administration, and the drama of salvation becomes a drama of noticing. Porphyry, who loved the doctrine, spent a career negotiating its pastoral difficulties. Iamblichus, who had been Porphyry’s own pupil, rejected it outright — and the rejection, not any love of ritual for its own sake, is the engine of the De Mysteriis. Sections VII to X follow the argument in its own order: the question, the text, the counter-doctrine, and the rite that the counter-doctrine requires.

// A DOCTRINE WITH A LONG AFTERLIFE //

The undescended soul did not die with its critics. It recurs wherever Platonism needs an unfallen point of contact between the human and the divine — in the Christian doctrine of the soul’s highest part in some medieval readers of Augustine and Dionysius, in Renaissance Platonism, and in modern depth psychology’s borrowings from Plotinus. Its rival, the fully descended soul, has an equally long posterity: every tradition that insists salvation must arrive from outside the soul’s own resources, because the soul’s resources are exactly what is damaged, is arguing Iamblichus’s side of this quarrel whether it knows his name or not.

VII. Porphyry Between Two Worlds: The Letter to Anebo

Porphyry of Tyre (c. 234–c. 305) is the hinge figure of this whole history, and the hinge creaks. Editor of Plotinus, author of the Isagoge that would introduce Aristotle’s logic to a millennium of students in Greek, Syriac, Arabic, and Latin, biographer of Pythagoras, vegetarian controversialist, and the most formidable pagan critic Christianity faced in the third century — he was also, by his own honest record, a man divided about ritual. In the Philosophy from Oracles he collected divine utterances as authorities; in On Abstinence he restricted blood sacrifice and assigned the higher gods a worship of pure intellect; in the treatise On the Return of the Soul, known through Augustine’s extensive reports, he is found — in Augustine’s paraphrase — conceding that theurgy might purify the soul’s lower, pneumatic part for a return to the aetherial regions, while denying that any rite could purify the intellectual soul or bring it to the Father; and complaining that a universal way of salvation had not been found. The concessions and the denials are both his. Later tradition, which likes its debaters consistent, has repeatedly ironed him flat; the texts will not allow it.

The document that matters here is a letter. At some point late in his career — proposals range across roughly 280–305, and the dating remains genuinely unsettled — Porphyry addressed a set of questions to an Egyptian priest named Anebo, otherwise unknown to history. The letter survives only in the use made of it by its answerer, quoted and paraphrased point by point, so that its contents are known the way a demolished building is known from the surveyor’s report on its demolition. The questions, as they can be reconstructed, are not hostile; they are worse than hostile, they are perplexed, and they come from inside the tradition. In paraphrase of the reconstructed dossier: What distinguishes gods from daimones, heroes, and souls, and how can the worshipper tell an epiphany of the one from an apparition of the other? How can the gods — impassible, incorporeal, unchanging — be invoked, adjured, even threatened, as the rites presume? How can beings who do not perceive with organs be moved by fumigations, sounds, and offerings? Why do the rites use names that mean nothing in any language — the so-called barbarous names — if the gods understand all tongues? What is the personal daimon, and how is it allotted? Either the rites’ premises are false, the letter in effect asks, or the theology is. Anebo never answered. His teacher did.

// THE GENRE OF THE EXCHANGE //

Question-and-solution (erōtapokrisis) was a standard late-antique scholarly form: a dossier of difficulties (aporiai) submitted to a master, whose solutions (lyseis) constitute the treatise. The exchange’s Egyptian staging — a Greek philosopher questioning an Egyptian priest, answered by the priest’s Egyptian master — is a transparent and, to contemporaries, legible fiction: Proclus already knew, and says, that the answering voice is Iamblichus’s. Modern scholarship, following the scholion tradition and the most recent editors (Saffrey, Segonds, and Lecerf, 2013), accepts the attribution without serious dissent. The fiction is not a forgery in any sense that mattered to its first readers; it is a claim about where the authority to answer resides — not in the Greek seminar, but in the temple tradition the questions were addressed to.

VIII. De Mysteriis as a Text: Abamon Answers

The answer is the work posterity knows as the De Mysteriis — On the Mysteries of the Egyptians, in the fuller Renaissance title De Mysteriis Aegyptiorum — and the first thing to know about it is that neither the title nor the author’s name on it is original. Its transmitted title is a small treatise in itself: “Reply of Master Abamon to Porphyry’s Letter to Anebo, and Solutions to the Difficulties Contained Therein.” The work is cast as the response of Abamon, an Egyptian prophet and Anebo’s teacher, taking up his pupil’s correspondence. That the author is in fact Iamblichus of Chalcis (c. 245–c. 325) — Syrian-born, trained under Anatolius and then under Porphyry himself, founder of a school at Apamea, and the most consequential Platonist between Plotinus and Proclus — was stated already by Proclus, is preserved in a scholion ascribed to the Byzantine scholar Michael Psellos, and is the settled position of modern scholarship. The pseudonym is Egyptian dress of a deliberate kind: the Syrian philosopher answers the Greek philosopher in the person of an Egyptian priest, because the matter in dispute is precisely whether Egyptian temple practice contains a wisdom Greek philosophy must receive rather than judge.

The work runs to ten books. Their architecture is worth setting out, because the De Mysteriis is often described as a defence of magic when it is in fact a systematic theology that happens to have ritual as its test case. The standard modern English version is the Clarke–Dillon–Hershbell translation of 2003 (Iamblichus: On the Mysteries, Society of Biblical Literature), made from des Places’s Budé Greek text; the paraphrases in Sections VIII–XII follow it.

Read as a whole, the treatise performs a single manoeuvre ten times. Each of Porphyry’s difficulties assumes that the rites must work, if at all, by human power operating on divine passibility — prayer as persuasion, offering as bribery, invocation as pressure. Abamon’s solutions relocate the agency: the rites work, when they work, because the gods have established them, sown their tokens through the cosmos, and act in them; the human participant contributes not power but receptivity — a soul and a material vehicle made fit (epitēdeiotēs, fitness or suitability, is the treatise’s master technical term) to receive a divine activity that descends because giving is what divinity does. The impassibility of the gods is not the rites’ problem; it is their guarantee. Nothing in the worshipper can compel, flatter, or alter a god — and therefore whatever happens in a true rite is the god’s own free self-giving, prepared for by purification and enacted through the god’s own signatures in matter. That relocation of agency is the whole doctrine of theurgy in embryo. Section X states it in its developed form; first, the premise that makes it necessary.

IX. The Fully Descended Soul: Why Contemplation Cannot Save It

Against Plotinus’s undescended soul (Section VI), Iamblichus sets the counter-doctrine on which everything else in the De Mysteriis depends: the soul descends entire. There is no part of the individual human soul that remains above, contemplating in undisturbed blessedness, while its shadow blunders below. The soul that enters generation enters it wholly — its essence itself is changed by the descent, bound to the body’s rhythms, subject to the compound’s affections, genuinely ignorant, genuinely at risk. The position is developed across Iamblichus’s surviving and fragmentary works (the treatise On the Soul, preserved in Stobaeus, is the other main witness) and is presupposed on every page of the De Mysteriis; Gregory Shaw’s Theurgy and the Soul: The Neoplatonism of Iamblichus (1995; revised edition 2014) is the modern study that made the doctrine impossible to ignore, arguing — in paraphrase — that Iamblichus’s whole ritual system is the rigorous consequence of taking embodiment seriously: a soul that is really here cannot be saved by a part of it that never left there.

The consequence for practice follows at once, and Iamblichus draws it without flinching. Contemplation alone cannot save a fully descended soul, for three connected reasons. First, the organ of contemplation is itself descended: the soul’s thinking in the body is discursive, image-bound, and intermittent — it cannot sustain the Intellect’s timeless act, and what it cannot sustain it cannot be saved by. Second, the soul’s self-knowledge is compromised at the root: embodied, it identifies itself with the compound, mistakes the body’s appetites and the ego’s fixations for its own nature, and no act of that misidentified self can lift it out of the misidentification — the patient cannot be his own surgeon at the exact point where the disease is selfhood. Third, salvation in the Platonic sense is not information: to know about the gods, however truly, is a relation of the soul to its own thoughts, not a union of the soul with the gods themselves. Hence the treatise’s programmatic insistence, at II.11 and throughout, in paraphrase: it is not thought that joins the theurgist to the gods — for then the thinker would be joined whenever he thought, and the rite would be decoration — but the performance of acts that exceed understanding, and the power of symbols that the gods alone understand. Thought prepares, purifies, interprets, and consents. It does not, of itself, unite.

PLOTINUS: THE SOUL PARTLY ABOVE

The highest part of the soul does not descend; it contemplates Intellect eternally. Embodiment is an attention-problem. Remedy: philosophy — purification, dialectic, contemplation — recovering awareness of the undescended summit. Ritual: unnecessary for the intellectual soul, at best a concession to the multitude.

IAMBLICHUS: THE SOUL WHOLLY BELOW

The soul descends entire; its essence is altered by embodiment; no unfallen reserve remains. Embodiment is a condition-problem. Remedy: theurgy — divinely instituted rite, working by divine power through material tokens — because only a power greater than the descended soul can raise the descended soul. Philosophy remains necessary: as preparation, interpretation, and the soul’s consent.

Two clarifications, without which the doctrine is routinely caricatured. Iamblichus does not hold that the soul is material, or damned, or cut off from divinity by nature: the soul remains an incorporeal essence of divine lineage, and it carries — Shaw’s reading stresses this — a “one of the soul”, a supra-intellectual point of unity by which it can receive what it cannot achieve. Nor does he hold that philosophy is abolished: the theurgist of the De Mysteriis is a philosopher who has added rite to study, and the treatise itself — ten books of tight metaphysical argument — is the proof that its author expected the addition to be argued for, not merely undergone. What is denied is a single sufficiency: that thinking, unaided, is enough for beings in our condition. The dispute with Plotinus is therefore narrow, technical, and total: narrow, because both men agree on the One, the hierarchy, the goal of union, and the authority of Plato; technical, because it turns on the metaphysics of the soul’s descent; total, because everything about practice — temple or study, altar or book — follows from which side of it you take.

X. Theurgy: God-Worked Rite, Not God-Compelling Magic

The word theurgy (theourgia) is older than Iamblichus. It appears first in connection with the Chaldaean Oracles (Section XIII) and their reputed authors, the Juliani — the younger of whom bears “the Theurgist” as his very surname — and it means, built from theos and ergon, god-work: a working of the gods, or a work performed upon, with, or by them. The ambiguity of the genitive is the doctrine in miniature. In the usage Iamblichus fixes, theurgy is the complex of divinely instituted rites — sacrifice, invocation and hymn, divination in its inspired forms, the consecration and animation of statues, the reception of the personal daimon — by which the embodied soul is purified, illuminated, and finally united to the gods, by the gods’ own action in the rite. Its standing contrast, in the texts themselves, is double: against theologia, god-talk — discourse about the divine, necessary and insufficient — and against goēteia, sorcery, the human attempt to bend spirits to human purposes, which the De Mysteriis condemns in terms as severe as any church father’s. Theurgy is defined by the direction of its agency. Sorcery is man working on gods; theology is man talking about gods; theurgy is gods working — with man’s prepared cooperation — on man.

The theory, in paraphrase of the De Mysteriis I–II and V–VI, runs in four steps. (1) Divine initiative. The rites are not human inventions that happen to work; they are the gods’ own institutions, handed down in the sacred traditions of Egypt and Chaldaea, and their efficacy is the gods’ fidelity to their own gifts. (2) Tokens in matter. The gods have sown through the cosmos the symbola and synthēmata — signatures, passwords, tokens — of their presence: particular stones, plants, animals, scents, sounds, figures, and names that belong to each god’s series (Section XI) and resonate with it. (3) Fitness. The rite assembles the tokens and purifies the recipient until the composite — place, statue, medium, worshipper — becomes fit (epitēdēios) to receive the divine illumination, as a cleaned mirror is fit to receive an image it does not create. (4) Divine action. The god then acts: illuminating, possessing, speaking, healing, elevating — freely, impassibly, and without being altered by the transaction, as light is not altered by the mirror. The worshipper’s part is real but receptive: preparation, purity, attention, and a consent that the tradition describes, in its boldest extension, as the soul’s becoming a vehicle — a receptacle (hypodochē) — of the god.

// THE THREEFOLD GRADE OF THEURGY //

Later sources within the tradition — the summary is associated above all with the emperor Julian’s circle and with Proclus’s school, and modern accounts (Shaw; Tanaseanu-Döbler) treat its exact schematisation with care — distinguish grades of theurgic work by the kind of vehicle it perfects: a material grade, working through bodies, statues, and the pneumatic vehicle, concerned with divination, protection, and the ordering of embodied life; a middle grade, working through the soul’s discursive and imaginative powers; and a highest, intellective grade, in which the soul is united to the gods beyond image and rite alike. The grades are a ladder, not a menu: the lowest is not despised but founded upon, and the highest does not abolish the rites that carried the soul to it. Readers should treat any presentation of the three grades as a fixed ancient syllabus with caution — the sources give a trajectory more often than a table.

What theurgy is for, finally, is stated by the treatise’s own conclusion (Book X, in paraphrase): happiness is not the soul’s successful self-management but its union with the gods, and the theurgic path — worship, purification, illumination, union — is presented as the complete form of the Platonic life for embodied beings. Philosophy tells the soul where home is. Theurgy, on Iamblichus’s account, is the road the gods built for souls who cannot fly there on a thought. Whether the road exists is a religious question this archive does not adjudicate; that Iamblichus built a complete philosophy around its existence, and that Proclus’s Athens ran on it for a century, are historical facts the next sections document.

XI. Sympatheia and the Chain: Each God’s Seira

Theurgy’s physics — the account of why a stone, a herb, a name can carry a god’s power — is the doctrine of sympathy (sympatheia) organised by the doctrine of the chain (seira). Sympathy first. The idea that distant things act on one another because the cosmos is one living whole, pervaded by a single breath and life, is not a Neoplatonic invention: it is the physical root of the doctrine in Stoic cosmology, where the pneuma binding the universe makes every part co-affected (sympaschein) with every other. That physical root is treated in this hub’s companion Foundations article on Stoic Physics, and the reader who wants sympathy as a theory of nature should go there; what concerns this article is what the Platonists built on the Stoic foundation. Plotinus already gives sympathy a metaphysical ground in IV.4: the cosmos is one animal, ensouled by one Soul, and its parts respond to one another not by mechanical impulse across a void but because they are organs of a single living thing — which is why, in his analysis, prayer and even magic can have real effects within the cosmic body, effects he is careful to confine to the irrational, pneumatic level of soul. Iamblichus takes the confined effect and gives it a theology.

The theology is the chain. In the developed system — stated in the De Mysteriis and brought to full articulation by Proclus — reality proceeds from the One not as an undifferentiated flood but in series: each god heads a seira, a chain or cord of beings that share that god’s characteristic power, running unbroken from the divine level down through angels, daimones, heroes, and souls, and continuing — this is the decisive extension — into the material world itself: into animals, plants, stones, places, sounds, and names. The lion and the sunflower and gold and the bay belong, in Proclus’s famous example in his short work On the Hieratic Art (in paraphrase), to the solar series: the power of the Sun runs down its chain from the god through every link, so that the heliotrope turning on its stem is already, in its small way, performing the solar liturgy, and the theurgist who assembles solar things is not inventing a symbolism but gathering a family. Every god’s chain is a thread of the one cosmic weaving; the totality of chains, interwoven, is the sensible world as the Platonists understood it — a theophany in inventory form.

SYMBOLA

Tokens: material things, figures, and acts that bear a god’s signature by membership in the god’s chain — a stone, a plant, an animal, a scent, a diagram. Their power is ontological before it is meaningful: they do not stand for the god the way a word stands for a thing; they participate in the god’s power the way a ray participates in the sun.

SYNTHĒMATA

Watchwords or passwords: the verbal and ritual counterparts of the material tokens — divine names, including the untranslated “barbarous names” of Book VII, formulas, and consecrated phrases. In the Oracles’ image (fr. 108 in Majercik’s numbering, in paraphrase), the Father sowed the symbols through the cosmos; the theurgist’s knowledge is knowing where they fell.

The consequences for the neighbouring arts should be drawn explicitly, because they are this article’s reason for existing. A cosmos of chains is a cosmos in which natural knowledge is theological knowledge: to learn which stone belongs to which series is to learn the map of divine presence in matter, and the lapidaries, herbals, and catalogues of sympathies and antipathies that fill the technical literature — the Cyranides tradition, the Hermetic iatromathematical texts, the alchemical lists of planetary metals — are, whatever their individual merits, attempts to chart the chains. It is also a cosmos in which the alchemist’s central intuition receives a metaphysical charter: if gold is the solar metal not by convention but by membership — if the Sun’s power literally runs down its series into the metal in the crucible — then work on gold is work at one end of a chain whose other end is a god, and the distance between metallurgy and liturgy becomes a difference of emphasis. The Platonists did not write the alchemical corpus; but they built the only cosmos in which that corpus’s claims about matter could be, in the strictest sense, theories.

// SYMPATHY IS NOT A METAPHOR, HERE //

Modern readers instinctively demote sympathy to a poetic figure — things “corresponding” the way verses correspond. In the sources it is a causal doctrine with a mechanism (one Soul, one living cosmos, series of shared power) and testable-looking consequences (this herb for this god’s rite; this stone against this affliction). The mechanism is not ours, and this article does not recommend it as physics. But arguments about theurgy, telestikē, and alchemy cannot even be stated until sympathy is granted its ancient force: a real transmission of power along lines of real kinship, in a world where kinship between a flower and a god was a finding, not a figure of speech.

XII. Telestikē: The Animation of Statues

Of all theurgic practices, one has attracted disproportionate fascination and disproportionate embarrassment, and it is treated here soberly, as what the sources present it as: a documented rite with a theory, a technique, and a literature. Telestikē — the “perfecting” or consecrating art, from telein, to complete, to initiate — names in theurgic usage two connected operations: the perfecting of the theurgist’s own soul, and the perfecting of a statue so that it becomes a fit receptacle for a god’s presence and can manifest that presence — by movement, by voice, by oracular response, by the signs the sources call epiphanies. The second operation is this section’s subject. It is the practice Augustine had already singled out for condemnation in the Hermetic Asclepius (see the hub’s Hermetica article, Section VIII) under the description of gods made by men; the theurgic Platonists inherited the practice, supplied it with a metaphysics, and — unlike the Hermetic dialogue — described its rationale in systematic prose.

The rationale follows directly from Sections X and XI. A statue is a body: shaped matter, form imposed on stone or wood or metal by a human craftsman — and as mere craftsmanship it is exactly as divine as a chair. Telestikē perfects it by making it a receptacle (hypodochē, the term Plato’s Timaeus uses for the receptacle of becoming). In the accounts the later sources give — the fullest discussions are in Iamblichus’s treatment of epiphany and sacrifice, in Proclus’s references across the commentaries and the Hieratic Art, and in the descriptions collected and analysed by modern scholarship (Johnston on the animation of statues; Shaw; Tanaseanu-Döbler) — the consecration assembles the god’s symbola: materials from every level of the god’s chain — stones, herbs, aromatics, animal products, engraved characters and names — placed within or beneath the image, accompanied by invocation, hymn, and fumigation, until the composite object is no longer a portrait of the god but a node in the god’s series: a place where the chain surfaces in the material world in concentrated form. The god’s illumination then manifests in the receptacle as light manifests in a mirror — really present, really active, and no more contained by the statue than the sun is contained by the heliotrope. The statue is not the god. It is the god’s address.

Three sobering observations belong with the description. First, the sources are philosophical, not liturgical manuals. No complete telestic rite survives with rubrics; what survives is the theory, scattered descriptions, the Asclepius passage, the Oracles’ fragments on receptacles and symbols, and hostile or fascinated reports. Reconstructions that present a step-by-step ancient ceremony go beyond the evidence, and this article does not offer one. Second, the practice had critics inside paganism. Porphyry’s questions (Section VII) press exactly its weak points — how can the impassible be housed, and how does the worshipper distinguish a god’s presence from a daimon’s imposture or a fraud’s mechanism? — and Book II of the De Mysteriis, with its elaborate diagnostics of true and false epiphany, is the tradition’s own admission that the question was serious. Third, the rite is the limiting case of the whole system. If the fully descended soul needs material vehicles, and if the chains really run into stones, then a consecrated stone that mediates a god is not an aberration of the philosophy but its most consistent application — which is why telestikē, embarrassing as later ages found it, could not be amputated from theurgy without the system bleeding out. Proclus’s Athens kept statues; Marinus’s Life of Proclus reports the philosopher’s own devout practice in these terms. The last pagan centuries did not hide the rite. They theorised it.

// A WORD ON THE EVIDENCE //

Telestikē sits at the junction of three evidentiary streams that must not be conflated: the Hermetic stream (the Asclepius god-making passage and its patristic quotation), the Oracular-theurgic stream (fragments and Neoplatonic commentary), and the Egyptian temple stream (the animation and mouth-opening of cult images, an Egyptian practice of immense antiquity, treated from the Egyptian side in the Egyptology division — see its Neoplatonism article and its temple-ritual articles). The streams converge in late antiquity and the theurgists themselves drew on all three; but a rite attested in one stream is not thereby attested in the others, and the careful reader will keep the labels attached.

XIII. The Chaldaean Oracles: The Tradition’s Scripture

Behind Iamblichus stands a text he did not write and could not have argued without: the Chaldaean Oracles (ta logia, “the oracles” — in the tradition’s reverent usage, simply the Oracles). The Oracles are a body of Greek hexameter verse — oracular utterances, metaphysical pronouncements, and ritual instructions interwoven — composed, on the standard scholarly account, in the late second century CE, in the reign of Marcus Aurelius, and ascribed to two figures, father and son: Julian the Chaldaean and Julian the Theurgist, the son remembered in the tradition also for the rain miracle of Marcus’s Danubian campaign, when a storm was credited — by several competing claimants, the historians note — with saving a Roman army. Whether the son composed the verses in mediumistic trance, compiled them, or received them, in the poem’s own fiction, from the gods and from the soul of Plato, the ancient sources do not let us decide; what they establish is the product: a mystery-poem of perhaps a few hundred lines, in the oracular hexameter of Delphi and of Homer, presenting itself as the wisdom of Chaldaea — Babylon as the Greek imagination revered it, the immemorial East.

The poem does not survive. What survives is its reception: some two hundred fragments — individual lines and short runs of verse — preserved because later Platonists quoted them, in works that argue with, expound, and build upon the verses the way theologians quote scripture. Porphyry wrote on them (his Philosophy from Oracles draws chiefly on other oracles, but the Chaldaean collection already stands high in his world); Iamblichus is said to have written a commentary running to at least twenty-eight books, lost; Proclus’s commentary, also lost, was reputed enormous; Damascius quotes them at the foundations of his system; and the fullest surviving ancient exposition is, by the usual irony, a Christian one — the eleventh-century Byzantine philosopher Michael Psellos’s. The modern standard edition is Ruth Majercik’s The Chaldaean Oracles: Text, Translation, and Commentary (1989), resting on the foundational study of Hans Lewy, Chaldaean Oracles and Theurgy (1956; third edition 2011). Every statement about the Oracles’ contents in this section is a statement about the fragments as Majercik edits them, and is paraphrase throughout.

What the Fragments Teach, in Paraphrase

A transcendent Father, primal Fire, beyond reach of the intellect that strains toward him; with him, Power (Dynamis) and the Paternal Intellect, which conceives the Forms in a flash the fragments figure as lightning; a second, demiurgic Intellect that fashions the cosmos; and between the intelligible fire and the material world, the great mediating figure of Hecate — in the Oracles not chiefly the witch-goddess of the crossroads but the cosmic Soul and membrane, the boundary-power through whom life passes from the Father’s realm into generation. Below and between: the iynges, the Father’s thoughts figured as whirling powers that carry his conceptions downward; the connectors and teletarchs who govern the cosmic zones; angels, daimones, and the material daimones of the sublunary deep, against whom the fragments warn in tones of real dread. And for the human soul — a spark of the Father’s fire, descended into the body’s darkness — a way back: by virtue, by the rites, by the symbols the Father sowed, by refusing the daimones of fate and nature, the soul is to gather its flower of intellect and ascend through the spheres to the fire from which it came. The metaphysics is Middle Platonic in its furniture (Section III’s caution applies with double force: the Oracles and Plotinus are cousins, both drinking from Numenius’s century); the register is what is new. This is philosophy as revelation — verse that does not argue but commands, prohibits, promises — and it was received exactly so.

SCRIPTURE, FUNCTIONALLY

For the later Platonists the Oracles stood beside Plato’s Timaeus as the tradition’s two supreme authorities — the one a revelation in verse, the other philosophy’s own cosmology — and were expounded with the same commentary techniques. Proclus is reported to have said that, if he had the power, he would keep in circulation only the Oracles and the Timaeus and withdraw every other book: the anecdote, preserved by Marinus, is the tradition’s own measure of the text’s rank.

THEURGY’S NAME AND WARRANT

The word theourgia enters history with the Juliani: the younger Julian is “the Theurgist”. The rites the Oracles prescribe and presuppose — the symbols, the receptacles, the ascent through the zones — are the practices Iamblichus’s De Mysteriis later defends in prose. Tanaseanu-Döbler’s caution belongs here: in the fragments themselves, metaphysics predominates and explicit ritual instruction is comparatively sparse; the fully elaborated “Chaldaean ritual system” is in large part the work of the commentators — an invented tradition, in her subtitle’s phrase, though invented the way cathedrals are: on a real foundation, by believers.

XIV. Proclus and the Athenian School: System, Commentary, 529

Theurgy might have remained one Syrian schoolmaster’s controversial synthesis. It became the operating system of late pagan philosophy because of Proclus (412–485) and the school he led at Athens. Born at Constantinople, raised at Xanthus in Lycia, educated at Alexandria, Proclus came to Athens about 430 and succeeded, through Plutarch of Athens and his own teacher Syrianus, to the headship of the Platonic school there — the diadochē, the succession, which the Athenian Platonists traced, by institutional courtesy, to Plato’s own Academy. He held the chair for nearly fifty years, taught the curriculum entire — Aristotle as propaedeutic, Plato as the mysteries, the Orphic and Chaldaean theologies as the crown — practised the theurgic life his biographer Marinus describes (prayers, hymns, fasts, the observation of Egyptian and Chaldaean holy days alongside Greek ones), and wrote with a productivity that still staggers editors: commentaries on the Timaeus, Republic, Parmenides, Alcibiades, and Cratylus; the six books of the Platonic Theology; the hymns; and the work by which every student still meets him, the Elements of Theology — two hundred and eleven propositions, each proved from the ones before, in which the whole Iamblichean universe is set out with Euclidean rigour.

Three Proclean contributions matter to this article. The system completed. Between the One and the world Proclus interposes the orders Iamblichus had sketched — the divine henads, the intelligible, intellectual, and hypercosmic orders of gods, the chains of Section XI formalised into a complete doctrine of series — until the universe is a single articulated procession in which every being has an address. The commentary enthroned. The characteristic literary form of the school — the line-by-line commentary on Plato, on Aristotle, and in intention on the Oracles — became, in Proclus’s hands, the vehicle of original philosophy itself: to expound the master’s text is to do metaphysics, because the text, rightly read, contains the system. That form is the subject, for its alchemical branch, of this hub’s Commentators article, where Olympiodorus, Christianus, and Stephanus do for Zosimos and pseudo-Democritus what Proclus did for Plato; the reader should hold the two articles together, since the lecture hall is one institution with two syllabi. Theurgy normalised. In Proclus there is no longer a controversy to win: the Hieratic Art treats the chains and the consecration of receptacles as applied metaphysics, the hymns are liturgy, and the philosopher’s day is ordered by prayer as naturally as by lecture. What Iamblichus argued, Athens assumed.

529, Stated Carefully

The hub’s timeline closes the ancient schools’ story in 529 CE, and the entry stands — but the event deserves the precision this hub applies elsewhere. In 529 the emperor Justinian promulgated measures against pagan teaching — the legal tradition (the Codex Justinianus and the chronicler Malalas’s report that teaching and publicly supported salaries for pagan teachers at Athens were ended) supports the conclusion that the Athenian school, under its last head Damascius, ceased to function as a public, endowed institution. There was no single edict that says, in so many words, “the Academy is closed” — the famous “closure of the Academy” is a modern summary of a legal and financial strangulation — and the school was in any case not Plato’s Academy in unbroken corporate survival but its late-antique successor institution, refounded in the early fifth century. What happened next is known from the historian Agathias: Damascius, Simplicius, and five colleagues — Eulamius, Priscian, Hermias, Diogenes, and Isidore — emigrated to the court of the Persian king Chosroes I, hoping, Agathias says with gentle irony, to find the philosopher-king of the Republic; found Persia uncongenial; and returned under a clause of the peace treaty of 532 that guaranteed them the right to live unmolested in their own convictions — a clause sometimes cited as an early landmark in the history of religious toleration. Where they then lived and wrote — Simplicius’s immense Aristotle commentaries postdate the emigration — remains debated, with Harran (Carrhae) the most discussed candidate. Meanwhile Alexandria, where philosophy had made its peace with a Christian environment a generation earlier, taught on: Olympiodorus was still lecturing after 565, and the Alexandrian line runs forward to Stephanus (Section XVI). 529 is therefore best read as this article reads it: the end of the Athenian school and of publicly endowed pagan philosophy, a real and symbolic terminus — not the instant extinction of the tradition, whose books, methods, and chains passed to Syriac, Arabic, and Byzantine keepers, and, by routes the hub’s Transmission article follows, to the alchemists among them.

XV. The Dispute: “Irrational Turn” or Coherent Philosophy?

Every account of this material takes a side in a historiographical quarrel, usually without declaring it. This section declares it. The older reading — call it the decline narrative — was given classic form by E. R. Dodds, whose The Greeks and the Irrational (1951) and whose edition of Proclus’s Elements framed the later Platonists as the exhausted heirs of a rational tradition lapsing, under the anxiety of a collapsing world, into ritual, revelation, and system-spinning: on this reading, Plotinus is the last great Greek philosopher, Porphyry his ambivalent editor, Iamblichus the point of surrender, and Proclus a magnificent scholastic of a dead cause. The narrative had evidence: the schools did multiply entities; the Oracles are not the Republic; statues were animated. And it had a confessional undertow its holders did not always notice — a definition of philosophy, drawn from a particular modern rationalism, by which rite is philosophy’s opposite by stipulation, so that finding rite in Iamblichus proved decline without further argument.

The revisionist reading, built across the last fifty years, answers on the texts. John Dillon — whose The Middle Platonists (1977) re-mapped the tradition’s prehistory, whose studies and whose share in the Clarke–Dillon–Hershbell De Mysteriis re-established Iamblichus as a philosopher to be argued with rather than diagnosed — showed the theurgic doctrines growing from Middle Platonic roots and debated with technical precision. Gregory Shaw’s Theurgy and the Soul (1995) made the systematic case summarised in Section IX: theurgy is the consequence of a metaphysical correction — the fully descended soul — and a philosophy that takes embodiment seriously must find its soteriology in the material vehicles the gods provide, or confess that salvation is for the disembodied only. Polymnia Athanassiadi’s and Sarah Iles Johnston’s work restored the social and religious world in which the arguments were live options rather than symptoms. And Ilinca Tanaseanu-Döbler’s Theurgy in Late Antiquity (2013) complicated the revision from within: examining the discourse rather than assuming the practice, she argues — in paraphrase — that “theurgy” as a single, continuous ritual tradition is substantially a construction of the texts that discuss it, that the Oracles themselves are metaphysics-heavy and ritual-light, and that each generation of Platonists reinvented the rite it claimed to inherit. The construction is not thereby a fraud; but the historian’s object becomes the discourse on theurgy, and claims about what theurgists actually did, beyond what the texts perform, must be correspondingly modest.

// THIS ARTICLE’S ADJUDICATION //

On the philosophical question, the revisionists have the better of the argument, and it is not close: Iamblichus’s premises are stated, his inferences are valid against them, his disagreement with Plotinus is located at one exact doctrinal point, and the “irrational turn” reading survives mainly by declining to follow the argument it judges. On the historical question — what rites were actually performed, how often, by how many — Tanaseanu-Döbler’s modesty governs: the evidence is textual, programmatic, and proud, and this article has written of practices throughout as documented in the sources’ own accounts, which is a different, weaker, and honest claim. Both halves of that adjudication are the archive’s standing method, applied here to philosophy as elsewhere to furnaces: take the argument seriously; take the performance reports at their evidentiary weight; never confuse the two.

One further dispute, internal to the revision, should not be smoothed over: how far Iamblichus departs from Plotinus. The maximum reading (Shaw at his boldest) makes the two men antipodes — contemplation against rite, escape from matter against its consecration. The minimum reading notes that Plotinus himself allows prayer and cosmic sympathy real effects at the pneumatic level (IV.4), that Porphyry — the supposed contemplative party — collected oracles and conceded theurgy’s lower efficacy, and that Iamblichus presents himself throughout as Plotinus’s faithful heir correcting a single error. The truth this article adopts is the documented middle: the departure is real, it is exactly located (the soul’s descent, and therewith the sufficiency of contemplation and the status of rite), and it is a departure within a shared system whose other commitments — the One, the hierarchy, the chains, the goal of union — neither party questioned. Revolutions in philosophy are usually like that: one premise changed, everything downstream rearranged, the flag unchanged.

XVI. Closing the Loop: Zosimos, Stephanus, and the Hermetica

Return, finally, to the workshop. Zosimos of Panopolis (fl. c. 300) — the hub’s Zosimos of Panopolis article treats his texts in full — wrote in the very decades when Porphyry’s questions and Abamon’s answers were circulating, in the same Egypt, in the same Greek, for readers formed by the same Platonism. Read his Visions against this article’s background and their colouring resolves into doctrine. The dream-altars on which a priest is dismembered, boiled, and reconstituted; the brazen and leaden men who suffer transformation; the daimones who must be propitiated or refused; the insistence that the art’s operations succeed only for the operator whose own nature has been purified and aligned — these are not free-floating surrealism. They are the fully descended soul’s world, in which material processes and spiritual states are one continuous fabric, daimonic powers are real inhabitants of the cosmic middle regions, and a rite-like operation on matter is the natural idiom for a transformation that is simultaneously metallurgical and personal. Zosimos does not cite Iamblichus, and the chronology forbids making him the De Mysteriis’s pupil; the claim is the reverse and the weaker, stronger one — that both men breathe the air this article has described, and that Zosimos’s ritual colouring, opaque without the background, is legible with it. The alchemist’s daimones have an address in the Neoplatonic hierarchy; his purifications have a theory in the doctrine of fitness; his sympathies have a map in the chains.

The loop closes at the other end of the tradition with Stephanus of Alexandria (fl. early seventh century), whose nine lectures On the Great and Sacred Art are treated in the hub’s Commentators article. Stephanus is the two halves of this hub in one person: a Platonist of the Alexandrian school — heir, through Olympiodorus and the Christianising lecture tradition, of Proclus’s commentary form — lecturing on alchemy as a sacred science under a Christian emperor, in a city where the Neoplatonic curriculum had survived 529 by learning to speak carefully. His lectures open with prayer, proceed by commentary on the ancient alchemical authorities, and frame the art’s operations inside a cosmos of divine powers and correspondences that Iamblichus would have recognised entire. When Stephanus calls the art sacred and means it as a curriculum classification rather than a compliment, the long argument of Sections IX–X has become an institution: the descended soul’s science, taught from a professor’s chair, in the city where Plotinus had found Ammonius four centuries before.

And the third member of the closing triangle is the family with which this Foundations cluster began. The Hermetica — the subject of the hub’s Hermetica article — are theurgy’s cousins, not its children and not its parents: a parallel devotional Platonism of the same Roman Egypt, sharing the furniture (the divine Mind, the mediating Word, fate in the spheres, the soul’s kinship with God and its staged return), sharing readers (Zosimos stands in both families’ doorway), and differing in the precise place this article would lead a reader to expect — the Hermetic path runs through gnōsis, rebirth, and hymn, a contemplative soteriology nearer to Plotinus’s temper, while its one great operative passage, the Asclepius’s animation of statues, stands in the Hermetic corpus exactly where telestikē stands in the theurgic one: at the point where the philosophy of receptacles touches matter and a god is given an address. Cousins, then — raised in the same city, recognisable at a glance as kin, and distinguishable, on the evidence, by whether the way home is walked by knowing or by rite. Late antiquity, characteristically, kept both doors open, and its alchemists used whichever the work required. The air was one air. This article has been its weather report.
