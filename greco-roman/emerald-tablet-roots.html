I. The Problem: A Famous Text with No Ancient Manuscript

Begin with the embarrassment, because the embarrassment is the subject. The Emerald Tablet — in Latin the Tabula Smaragdina, the Smaragdine Table — is the most quoted document in the whole history of Western alchemy. Medieval commentators treated it as the art’s foundational charter; Renaissance printers set it at the head of collections; Isaac Newton translated it with his own hand and commented on it. It speaks in the voice of Hermes Trismegistus, and it was read for centuries as a survival from the deepest antiquity of Egypt — a text older than Plato, older than Moses on some reckonings, cut into green stone by the thrice-greatest sage himself. And yet: no ancient Greek manuscript of the Tablet exists, and no ancient Latin one either. No Greek author of antiquity quotes it. No papyrus carries it. The philosophical Hermetica gathered in the Corpus Hermeticum — the subject of this hub’s Hermetica article — do not contain it, and Brian Copenhaver’s standard modern edition of those treatises does not print it, because it does not belong to that Greek family at all. The Tablet’s trail, followed honestly backward, does not end in a temple library in pharaonic or even Roman Egypt. It ends in an Arabic book.

The book is the Kitāb sirr al-khalīqa wa-ṣanʿat al-ṭabīʿa — the Book of the Secret of Creation and the Craft of Nature, known also as the Kitāb al-ʿilal, the Book of Causes — an encyclopaedic Arabic treatise on natural philosophy, compiled in the late eighth or early ninth century CE, which presents itself as the work of Balīnūs, or Balīnās: the Arabic form of the name of Apollonius of Tyana, the first-century Greek sage and wonder-worker. At the end of that book, after its formal close, stands the Tablet: a short sequence of aphoristic sentences in the voice of Hermes, prefaced by a narrative of how Balinas found them — in a subterranean vault, beneath a statue of Hermes, on a tablet of emerald held by an old man seated on a golden throne. That narrative, that book, and that Arabic text are the earliest witnesses the Tablet has. Everything else — the Jabirian quotation, the Secret of Secrets recension, the twelfth-century Latin translations, the vulgate text that conquered Europe, Newton’s English — is downstream of the Arabic centuries this article covers.

This is not a marginal scholarly opinion. It is the settled result of the twentieth-century rediscovery of the Arabic sources, begun by E. J. Holmyard in 1923 and established by Julius Ruska’s Tabula Smaragdina (Heidelberg, 1926), the foundational modern study, which demonstrated that the Arabic versions precede the Latin by some three centuries and that the Latin wording cannot be projected backward onto an imagined Greek original. Ursula Weisser’s critical edition of the Sirr al-khalīqa (Aleppo, 1979) and her monograph on the pseudo-Apollonian book (Berlin, 1980) gave the earliest witness its standard text. Paul Kraus, in his great study of the Jabir corpus (Cairo, 1942–43), fixed the book’s place in the intellectual world of early Abbasid science. Modern syntheses — Lawrence Principe’s The Secrets of Alchemy (2013) among them — present the Arabic provenance as the starting point of any responsible account. This article follows that scholarship, and states its limits where the scholarship states them.

WHAT SURVIVES

Four Arabic recensions of the Tablet, preserved in treatises of the 8th–10th centuries — chiefly the Sirr al-khalīqa and the pseudo-Aristotelian Sirr al-asrār (Secret of Secrets) — and Latin translations from the 12th century onward.

WHAT DOES NOT SURVIVE

Any Greek or Latin manuscript of the Tablet from antiquity; any quotation of it by an ancient author; and the emerald object itself, which no writer outside the frame story has ever claimed to have seen.

THE EARLIEST WITNESS

The Tablet as an appendix to the Kitāb sirr al-khalīqa of pseudo-Apollonius (Balinas), compiled in Arabic in the late 8th or early 9th century and certainly complete by the caliphate of al-Maʾmūn (813–833).

THE COUNT

The Tablet’s text runs to thirteen sentences in the usual segmentation — fourteen in editions that divide the closing sentences. That is the archive’s agreed formulation, stated here and kept throughout; see the Egyptology division’s Alchemy.

The vault narrative is the Sirr al-khalīqa’s own account of where its Tablet came from; Section III tells it as the text tells it, and neither endorses it as an excavation report nor apologises for its literary power. II. Balinas: Apollonius of Tyana in His Arabic Afterlife

The name on the Tablet’s first book is a borrowed one, and the borrowing is the first fact to get straight. Apollonius of Tyana (c. 15 – c. 100 CE) was a historical figure: a Greek Neopythagorean sage from Tyana in Cappadocia, remembered as a travelling teacher, ascetic, and worker of wonders, whose life the sophist Philostratus wrote up in the early third century at imperial commission. In the Greek and Roman world his reputation was contested — philosopher to his admirers, charlatan and magus to his detractors — but it was as a master of hidden powers that he passed into the literatures of the Near East. In Syriac and then in Arabic he became Balīnūs or Balīnās, and the Arabic Balinas is a larger figure than the Greek Apollonius ever was: in the formulation of Mohammad Karimi Zanjani Asl’s study of the Sirr al-khalīqa’s influence (2016), Apollonius was known in the Islamic period both as “Lord of the Talismans” (ṣāḥib al-ṭilasmāt) and as a Neo-Pythagorean-Hermetic philosopher. Arabic tradition credited him with founding talismanic images that protected cities, with books on the talismanic art, and with a body of wisdom reaching back behind Islam to the sages of the ancient world. A name like that is an author-function: to sign a book “Balinas” was to claim for it the authority of the ancient talisman-master himself.

The author of the Sirr al-khalīqa was not Apollonius. The book is an Arabic composition of the late eighth or early ninth century — some seven centuries after its supposed author’s death — and modern scholarship accordingly calls its writer pseudo-Apollonius: Weisser’s 1980 monograph is titled, precisely, Das “Buch über das Geheimnis der Schöpfung” von Pseudo-Apollonios von Tyana, “The ‘Book of the Secret of Creation’ of Pseudo-Apollonius of Tyana”. The pseudepigraphy is not a fraud in the modern sense so much as a convention of the literature: the Arabic Hermetic and occult-scientific corpus is thick with works signed by Hermes, by Aristotle, by Plato, by Apollonius, in which the ancient name certifies the teaching’s pedigree. But the convention has a consequence the reader must hold firmly. When the Sirr al-khalīqa says “Balinas found” or “Balinas said”, the speaker is an anonymous Arabic author of the early Abbasid centuries writing in character — and the character he is writing is himself presented, inside the book, as a finder and transmitter rather than an inventor. The Tablet thus arrives doubly framed: a text by Hermes, found by Balinas, written down by an author who is neither. Section III unpacks that frame; this hub’s article on the legendary lives of the sages treats the Balinas persona and its congeners at length — see Legendary Lives.

One more strand of the Arabic Apollonius matters for the Tablet’s story, because it explains why this sage, of all the ancients, is the one made to find Hermes’ book. In the Arabic tradition Balinas is not merely a sage who might plausibly enter a vault; he is the sage of talismans — of made objects in which celestial powers are lodged — and the Tablet, in its Arabic form, calls the One Thing it describes the father of the talismans (abū al-ṭilasmāt). The discoverer and the discovery match. The book that bears Balinas’s name is a cosmology of how powers descend into things and are bound there; the short text at its end names the principle of that binding. Whoever assembled the Sirr al-khalīqa — whether he composed the Tablet, inherited it, or received it from the Greek or Syriac materials the book elsewhere draws on — set it in the keeping of the one ancient name under which such a text most naturally travelled in Arabic. The attribution is literary. It is also, as construction, exact.

// NAMES, KEPT STRAIGHT //

Apollonius of Tyana: the historical 1st-century Greek sage. Balīnūs / Balīnās (Balinas): his Arabic name and persona, Lord of the Talismans. Pseudo-Apollonius: the modern scholarly label for the unknown Arabic author who wrote under that name. Hermes Trismegistus: the voice of the Tablet itself, and — inside the frame story — the enthroned old man who holds it. Four figures, three of them textual functions; the article keeps them distinct throughout.

The Arabic Balinas had a readership, and it was not a fringe one. Asl’s 2016 survey traces citations of Apollonius across Islamic sources in field after field, notes that Jābir ibn Ḥayyān himself refers in the Kitāb al-Aḥjār to “the Muslim advocates of Balinas” (aṣḥāb Balīnās al-islāmīyūn) — evidence, Asl argues, that the Sirr al-khalīqa was already famous in the Arabic-speaking world very early — and follows the book’s reception into the Ismāʿīlī tradition and into a fourth/tenth-century Arabic commentary by ʿAwn b. al-Mundhir, preserved in a unique manuscript and in a Persian translation that preserves parts of the commentary the Arabic excerpt has lost (Section XI). A pseudepigraph that attracts commentators within two centuries is not a curiosity. It is a classic in the process of becoming one, and the Tablet rode inside it.

III. The Frame in Full: The Vault Beneath the Statue of Hermes

The Sirr al-khalīqa does not simply contain the Tablet. It stages its discovery, and the staging deserves to be told in full — as the text’s own frame story, labelled as such — because for a thousand years the staging was the Tablet’s provenance: this is the account every later reader, Arabic, Latin, and Renaissance, received along with the text, and the account that fixed the Tablet’s imagined object-form (a slab of emerald, in a tomb, in dead hands) in the tradition’s imagination.

The book’s introduction presents the account as transmitted teaching. What the priest Sājiyūs of Nablus set down — the frame is his dictation, treated in Section IV — concerns the entrance of Balinas into a hidden chamber. In the narrative as the Arabic text tells it, Balinas enters a vault or crypt situated beneath a statue of Hermes — in the recensions’ geography, at Tyana, the sage’s own city. The chamber is a place where a talisman has been set up: Balinas has come, in the logic of the story, to the guarded heart of an ancient installation of power. Passing into the darkness, he comes upon an old man seated on a throne of gold. The seated figure is Hermes himself, long dead and enthroned in his vault. In his hands the old man holds a tablet of emerald — a slab of green stone, the lawḥ from which the text’s later Latin name, Tabula, descends — and before him rests a book. Balinas takes up what he has found: the tablet, and with it the book. The wisdom passes, in the story’s terms, from the keeping of the dead sage into the hands of the living one.

The introduction, as Kraus read it and as Asl’s translation of Weisser’s edition (p. 7) presents it, ties the two found objects to the two halves of the book’s own full title — a detail of construction too neat to be accidental. On the emerald tablet, in the frame’s account, stand the words “this is the art of nature”; on the book found with it, “this is the secret of creation and the science of the causes of things”. The Sirr al-khalīqa wa-ṣanʿat al-ṭabīʿa — the Secret of Creation and the Craft of Nature — thus presents itself as the book from the vault, and the Tablet as its companion piece: the book expounds the causes of things openly, at encyclopaedic length, while the tablet compresses the art of nature into a page of aphorisms. Kraus’s celebrated reading builds on exactly this division of labour, and states it as his interpretation, not the text’s: the Tablet, he argued, says in veiled terms what the book never spells out — the making of the elixir — and its few lines are, in his phrase, the key to the book of Balinas (Kraus 1942, pp. 302–303; Asl 2016, pp. 443–444). Whether or not the elixir reading is accepted, the structural point stands on the text itself: the Tablet stands after the formal close of the Sirr al-khalīqa (Kraus 1942, p. 302), as an appendix — the vault’s second object, appended to the first.

An old man on a golden throne, a tablet of emerald in his hands, and a book before him: the frame gives the Tablet a finder, a place, and a material — everything, that is, except a date. — the frame story of the Sirr al-khalīqa, in paraphrase

Two features of the frame repay attention before its literary character is assessed. The first is the language claim. In the recension Ruska printed, the words on the tablet are introduced as written in Syriac, in the first — the primordial — language: Azize’s translation of Ruska’s Arabic gives the preamble’s sense as a tablet of emerald “written in Syriac, in the first language”, in the keeping of the enthroned shaykh. The claim does double work inside the fiction — it makes the text older than Arabic, older than Greek, coeval with the first speech of mankind — and it has done double work in the scholarship ever since, since a Syriac intermediary behind the Arabic is one of the live hypotheses about the book’s sources (Section VI). But a claim inside a frame story is evidence of what the framer wanted believed, not of what happened; this article reports it as the text’s claim, at exactly that strength. The second feature is the throne. The old man is not lying in a sarcophagus; he is seated, enthroned, holding the tablet as a king or a god holds an emblem of office. The iconography is that of the sage as ruler of hidden things — the same enthroned Hermes who presides, in the parallel Arabic discovery narratives of Section XI, over the Book of Crates’ temple vision. The frame is composed, in other words, out of the tradition’s own furniture.

And that is the assessment. The vault narrative is a discovery narrative — a recognised literary device of Hermetic and alchemical writing, in which a new or newly presented text is authorised by being found: hidden by an ancient sage against a corrupt age, preserved in a tomb, a vault, a pyramid, a statue’s hollow, and recovered by a worthy finder. The device is older than Islam — late-antique Hermetica already deposit their teachings in temples and stelae — and the Arabic literature of the ninth and tenth centuries is full of it. To call the frame a device is not to call it a lie in any interesting sense; it is to locate it in its genre. What the frame establishes historically is not that Balinas entered a vault — the historical Apollonius had been dead seven centuries when the book was compiled — but that the book’s compiler understood the Tablet as a thing received: older than its finder, hidden rather than composed, and written in a language older than the one in which he presented it. Every element of that self-understanding is a claim, and Section XIV weighs the claims one by one. The narrative itself, told as the text tells it, is this section’s whole duty.

// NO EMERALD HAS EVER BEEN PRODUCED //

No emerald slab answering to the frame’s description survives in any museum, treasury, or inventory; no fragment, rubbing, or independent eyewitness account exists. “Emerald” itself, in ancient and medieval usage, named a family of green stones — true emerald, green jasper, even green granite among them — but the point is prior to mineralogy: the object belongs to the frame story. The historical Emerald Tablet is a text, and its earliest witnesses are Arabic manuscripts, two of which the scholarship cites by shelfmark: the Leipzig manuscript Vollers 832, carrying the oldest recension (A) of the Sirr al-khalīqa, and Paris, Arabe 2300, carrying the younger recension (B).

IV. Sājiyūs of Nablus: The Priest Who Transmits the Account

Between Balinas and the reader stands a third figure, easy to overlook and structurally indispensable: the transmitter. The Sirr al-khalīqa presents itself not as Balinas’s autograph but as an account dictated and explained by a priest of Nablus — the ancient Shechem, in Palestine — who comments on the words and the discovery of Balinas. The frame’s chain of custody is therefore three links long before the text even begins: Hermes writes; Balinas finds; the priest of Nablus transmits and expounds. It is the Arabic book-culture’s instinct for the isnād, the chain of transmission, applied to a Hermetic discovery: a teaching is worth what its chain is worth, and so the book supplies one.

The priest’s name is itself a small lesson in the state of the evidence. The manuscripts do not give it cleanly. Kraus read it, with a question mark, as Sājiyūs (Sagijus in the older Latinising literature), and also considered a reading as [He]sychius; older scholars took the figure for a Sergius (Kraus 1942, p. 272 n. and pp. 273, 280; Asl 2016, p. 442). The older secondary literature — the summaries that descend from Ruska through Holmyard’s generation — accordingly speaks of a recension “dictated by Sergius of Nablus”, and that form of the name still circulates; this article uses Sājiyūs, following Kraus and Asl, and records the alternatives because the instability is the fact. Martin Plessner and Julius Ruska took the priest for an invention of the author — one more layer of the frame. Kraus, by contrast, found the detail plausible enough to look for the book’s roots in the Hellenising circles of Syria: a Christian priest of a Palestinian town, expounding a pagan sage’s book, in a milieu where Greek learning survived in Syriac dress. Both judgements are inferences from the same damaged name. Neither upgrades the frame into a report.

The Christian colouring of the transmitted account is, however, a datum of the text and not only of its modern readers. Azize, translating Ruska’s Arabic preamble in full, notes that the relevant passage opens with a Trinitarian invocation — in the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, one God — which authenticates, in literary terms, the transmitter’s priesthood, and that the tablet’s claimed Syriac wording places the whole narrative within a Christian frame of reference: Syriac was, by the eighth and ninth centuries, the prestige language of the Christians of the Islamic world. His inference — offered as inference — is that the account as we have it passed through Christian hands, whatever the religion of the Tablet’s first composer. The observation aligns with what the Sirr al-khalīqa’s modern editor concluded from a different direction: Weisser, following Fuat Sezgin’s judgement, held that the Arabic book rests on a translation made by this same Nablus priest from a Greek original — a position Section VI states with its rivals. For the present section the point is narrower. The book’s own presentation makes its transmitter a Syriac-reading Christian priest of Palestine. In a transmission history, that is the receiving culture signing its work.

// THE CHAIN, AS THE BOOK GIVES IT //

Hermes, enthroned in the vault, holds the tablet and the book. Balinas enters, finds, and takes them up. Sājiyūs of Nablus dictates and comments. The anonymous compiler of the Sirr al-khalīqa sets the account at the head of his cosmology and the Tablet at its end. Four stations, of which only the last is historical without qualification — and the book is honest, in its way, about the arrangement: it never claims Balinas wrote the Tablet. It claims he found it. Finding is the frame’s whole thesis.

V. The Sirr al-khalīqa: A Cosmology of Causes

What kind of book carries the Tablet at its end? The Sirr al-khalīqa is an encyclopaedic treatise on natural philosophy cast as a cosmogony: an account of how the world is made, ordered, and caused, from its first principle down through the heavens to the sublunary world of minerals, plants, animals, and man. Its alternative title, Kitāb al-ʿilal — the Book of Causes — is the accurate one. After a prologue on the unity of God and on creation, the book lays out a cosmology that descends the ladder of being: the heavenly spheres and their motions; the elements and the four natures — hot, cold, moist, and dry — of which all lower things are mixtures; the generation of minerals in the earth, of plants, of animals; and finally the human being, in whom the whole system is recapitulated. Every phenomenon, in the book’s method, is traced to its material cause: a fitting mixture of the natures, in due proportion, under the influences of the world above. The relations of sympathy and antipathy between beings — why this substance acts on that one, why this talisman binds that power — are explained by the same mixtures: things agree or quarrel because their natures do (Kraus 1942, pp. 272, 282 n., 303; Asl 2016, pp. 442–443).

The architecture matters for the Tablet’s reading. A short text that says the upper is from the lower and the lower from the upper, that all things come from one thing by one ordering, and that a single power ascends from earth to heaven and descends again, is — inside this book — not a riddle pasted onto an unrelated cosmology. It is the cosmology’s own grammar, compressed to aphorism. The spheres act on the mixtures below; the mixtures, rightly prepared, receive and return the action; the whole is one ordered system of causes, and the book’s six treatises (the maqālāt into which Weisser’s edition divides the work) unfold at length what the Tablet’s sentences state in shorthand. This is why the question that has exercised the scholarship — whether the Tablet is an extraneous appendix to the Sirr al-khalīqa or an integral part of it, cosmogonic in sense or alchemical from the outset — is a real question with evidence on both sides. The Tablet stands after the book’s formal close, which suggests attachment; its matter is the book’s matter, which suggests belonging. Kraus’s key-to-the-book reading (Section III) is the strongest form of the belonging case. The prudent statement, and this article’s, is that the Tablet’s earliest surviving context is this cosmology, that it reads coherently as the cosmology’s epitome, and that its later career as a free-standing alchemical charter began when readers detached it from the book that framed it — a detachment the Jabirian quotation (Section X) already performs.

THE PROLOGUE

The unity of God and the act of creation: the book opens as theology before it proceeds as physics, in the manner of the early Abbasid cosmological treatise.

THE SPHERES

The heavenly order and its influences — the “above” whose powers descend into every mixture below, and the frame within which talismans, the book’s patron art, are intelligible.

THE NATURES

Hot, cold, moist, and dry — the book calls the elemental qualities “natures” — whose mixtures and proportions cause the properties of minerals, plants, animals, and men.

THE APPENDIX

After the formal close: the Tablet, presented as the vault’s second object — the art of nature in aphorisms, where the book has given the secret of creation in treatises.

The book’s sources are as layered as its frame. Weisser’s edition — established from four principal manuscripts out of sixteen she appraised — prints, among its supplements, extracts from the Greek bishop Nemesius of Emesa’s work on the nature of man (c. 400 CE), and the scholarship has long noted the Sirr al-khalīqa’s kinship with Syriac cosmological writing of the ninth century, the Book of Treasures of Job of Edessa among the comparanda named in the older literature. A cosmology compiled in Arabic around 800 that digests a fourth-century Greek bishop, breathes the air of Syriac school culture, and signs itself with a first-century pagan sage’s Arabic name is not an anomaly of the Abbasid translation age. It is a typical product of it — and it is precisely the kind of product in which a short Hermetic text of uncertain, probably late-antique, ancestry would come to rest. The book is the Tablet’s earliest home. It is also the best evidence for the milieu that made the Tablet, as we have it, possible.

VI. Dating the Book: Ruska, Kraus, Weisser — and an Early Doubter

The Sirr al-khalīqa is conventionally placed in the eighth to ninth centuries CE, in the Arabic world of the early Abbasid caliphate. Behind that bland range stand three landmark datings, and the differences among them are instructive, because each scholar was dating a slightly different thing. Ruska (1926), who first established the book as the Tablet’s earliest witness, allowed that its underlying material might reach back to the sixth–eighth centuries — a judgement about the sources the compilation digests, Greek and Syriac materials of late antiquity. Kraus (1942–43) placed the compilation itself under the caliph al-Maʾmūn (reigned 813–833). Weisser (1980), in the standard critical study, settled on a compilation around 750–800. The formulation the evidence supports, and the one this article uses throughout, is the composite one: compiled in Arabic in the late eighth or early ninth century, and certainly complete by the end of al-Maʾmūn’s caliphate in 833. The archive’s Egyptology division dates it the same way — “before 833” — in its Alchemy article.

Was the book composed in Arabic, or translated into it? Here the scholarship divides, and the division must be reported rather than resolved. Weisser, agreeing with Sezgin’s judgement in the Geschichte des arabischen Schrifttums, held that the Arabic text is a translation — made by the Nablus priest of Section IV — of a Greek original of the sixth century, now lost; on her dating, the Arabic version belongs around 815–816, in al-Maʾmūn’s reign. Reviewers of her edition already registered the alternative: that the book was composed in Arabic from older materials, its Greek and Syriac ingredients worked into an Arabic original — a position argued on grounds of style, and placed by one early reviewer as late as the middle of the ninth century. The current reference position keeps both doors open: the Secret of Creation, the Tablet’s earliest source and its likely original context, was either wholly or at least partly compiled from earlier Greek or Syriac materials. What no party to the debate claims is a surviving Greek or Syriac manuscript of the book — still less of the Tablet. The lost original, if it existed, is an inference from the Arabic text’s character, and Section XIV keeps it at that strength.

The most striking witness to the dating debate is also the oldest — older than Ruska by a thousand years. The Ismāʿīlī writer Abū Ḥātim al-Rāzī (d. 933/934) records that his contemporary and opponent, the philosopher-physician Abū Bakr al-Rāzī — Rhazes, the greatest clinician of the Arabic Middle Ages — told him the book was a recent work, by an author who lived under al-Maʾmūn (813–833) and merely took the name of Balinas. The report survives at second hand, in an adversary’s record of a debate, and this article gives it at that strength. But its existence matters beyond its precision: by the early tenth century, a leading Arabic man of science was already making, in public controversy, exactly the judgement modern scholarship makes — that the attribution to Apollonius is a literary device and the book a product of al-Maʾmūn’s age. The pseudepigraphy fooled the tradition’s devotees for a millennium. It did not fool its first critical readers, because it was never really designed to: it was designed to place the book, and placing is a claim a contemporary could check against the book-trade of his own Baghdad. The modern dating debate — 750–800 against 813–833 — is, in the end, an argument over a single generation, conducted within the frame Abū Bakr al-Rāzī already drew.

// HOW TO DATE A PSEUDEPIGRAPH //

Date the compilation, not the name on it, and not the oldest ingredient in it. The Sirr al-khalīqa contains material with late-antique roots (Ruska’s sixth–eighth centuries), was compiled as the book we have in the late eighth or early ninth (Weisser; Kraus), and is quoted by the Jabir corpus of c. 850–950 — three different dates for three different objects, all of them true, none of them interchangeable. Most popular datings of the Tablet go wrong by silently substituting one for another.

VII. The Book’s Teaching: Natures, Mixtures, and the First Sulphur–Mercury

The Sirr al-khalīqa’s cosmology is a physics of mixture. All things below the moon are composed of the four elemental principles — in the book’s own idiom, the four natures: heat, cold, moisture, and dryness — combined in differing proportions, and their combinations account for the properties of things and for the relations of sympathy and antipathy between them. A substance is what its mixture is; an operation is an adjustment of a mixture; a talisman is a mixture prepared, under the right celestial configuration, to receive and hold a power from above. Creation itself, in the book’s cosmogony, is the first and greatest act of ordering: the one God disposes the natures into the spheres, the elements, and the generated world, so that the whole of nature is a single system of balances — proportions held, disturbed, and restored. Later Arabic science would give that intuition a technical name and an arithmetic, the mīzān, the Balance, of the Jabir corpus (Section X). In the Sirr al-khalīqa it is still cosmology rather than calculus — but the direction is set: the world is intelligible because it is proportioned, and it is workable because its proportions can be known.

Within that physics sits the book’s most consequential single doctrine. Its mineralogy holds — in Kraus’s identification (1942, p. 1 n. 1 and p. 283) — the oldest known statement of the theory that the metals are formed in the earth from sulphur and mercury mixed in due proportion: the two principles whose varying purity and balance make lead lead and gold gold. The Jabirian corpus took the theory over from the Balinas book, built its metallurgy on it, and through Arabic sources it passed into the alchemy of the Latin Middle Ages, where it stands at the foundation of the Summa ascribed to Geber. The lineage is worth pausing over, because it reverses the usual picture of the Tablet’s book as a passive vessel. The Sirr al-khalīqa did not merely preserve a Hermetic aphorism at its end; it originated, so far as surviving texts show, the central matter-theory of medieval alchemy, and the corpus that systematised that theory is the same corpus that quotes its Tablet. Container and contained were read together, by the same readers, as one teaching — which is how the Tablet acquired, long before Latin, its alchemical sense.

A word, finally, on letters. The brief of the Arabic cosmological tradition to which the Sirr al-khalīqa belongs treats the created order as a text as well as a mixture: the world is disposed by a single ordering act, and in the Jabirian development of the book’s physics the letters of a substance’s Arabic name become the very instrument by which its hidden proportions are computed — the arithmology of the Balance, in which the natures concealed in a name fall in the proportion 1:3:5:8 (the archive’s Egyptology division sets out the system in its Jabir section). That full letter-calculus is Jabir’s, not Balinas’s, and this article does not antedate it. What the Sirr al-khalīqa itself contributes is the prior conviction the calculus presupposes: that creation is one act with one ordering — the Tablet’s own phrase for it, in the Arabic, is that all things were from One by one plan, one considered act of ordering (bi-tadbīr wāḥid) — and that the same ordering runs, without break, from the spheres to the metals in the earth. Letters, natures, and balances are three notations for that single conviction, and the Arabic centuries developed all three. The Tablet is the conviction’s shortest statement.

// WHY THE TABLET READS AS COSMOGONY FIRST //

Modern readers meet the Tablet as an alchemical recipe in code, because the Latin Middle Ages — Hortulanus above all — read it so. In its earliest home it closes a cosmology, and its sentences are about the making of the world: one source, two parents, a bearer, a nurse, a separation, an ascent and return. Whether it was composed as cosmogony and only later read as alchemy — the talismanic-origin suggestion in the recent literature — or was alchemical from the outset, as Kraus’s key-reading holds, is a genuine open question (Section XIV). What is not open is the order of the evidence: cosmogony is the context of the earliest witness, and the recipe-reading is the reception.

VIII. The Tablet’s Text as the Arabic Gives It

The Tablet, in the earliest recension — the text Weisser prints at the close of her edition (1979, pp. 524–525) from the Sirr al-khalīqa manuscripts — is a sequence of short declarative sentences in the voice of Hermes. It runs to thirteen sentences in the usual segmentation, fourteen in editions that divide the closing sentences: the archive’s agreed count, stated in the Egyptology division’s Alchemy article in the same words, and kept here. What follows walks the text sentence by sentence, in paraphrase, following the Arabic as the modern literal translations render it; the few phrases given in quotation marks are from the published translations named in place. The paraphrase deliberately keeps the Arabic’s own turns of phrase where the Latin tradition smoothed them away — the differences are Section IX’s subject, and they begin in the first line.

The Opening and the Correspondence

The text opens with an asseveration: it is truth, no doubt in it, true — the Arabic piles up certitude in a formula whose cadence, Azize notes, is redolent of the Qurʾānic “the Book in which there is no doubt”, framing the Tablet with scriptural authority. Then the correspondence, in the Arabic’s own direction: the uppermost is from the lowermost, and the lowermost is from the uppermost. Not, in the first instance, like — from. The Arabic states a derivation, a traffic of origin between the levels of the world, where the famous Latin will state a resemblance (Section IX). The sentence continues: it — the One Thing of the sentences that follow — worked the wonders from one, just as all things came from one by one act of ordering. The correspondence is thus grounded in a monism: above and below exchange natures because both proceed from a single source by a single plan.

The One Thing and Its Parents

The text then gives its subject a parentage, in the idiom of generation that the whole Arabic cosmological tradition speaks. Its father is the sun, its mother is the moon. The wind carried it in her womb; the earth fed it — nourished it, is its nurse. Four cosmic powers dispose of the One Thing between them: sun and moon beget it, wind gestates it, earth rears it. In the Jabirian version the middle terms trade places — the earth carries it in her belly and the wind nourishes it there (Section X) — a variant worth recording precisely because it shows the text already fluid in its earliest transmissions. The subject itself is never named. It is characterised instead by titles: father of the talismans (abū al-ṭilasmāt), keeper of wonders, perfect in power — a litany of epithets in the Arabic’s manner, identifying the One Thing by its office: it is the principle by which powers are bound into things, which is to say, in the Sirr al-khalīqa’s own physics, the principle of the talismanic art whose lord its finder was.

The Separation, and the Ascent and Descent

The text’s one imperative follows: fire became earth — separate the earth from the fire; the subtle is nobler than the gross. Here, and only here, the Tablet speaks in the grammar of an operation: a separation of the refined from the coarse, to be performed — the next sentence adds the manner — with gentleness and wisdom. Then the movement that completes the circuit: it ascends from the earth to the heaven and descends to the earth from the heaven, and in it is the power of the uppermost and the lowermost, because with it is the light of lights, and therefore the darkness flees from it. The One Thing is a traveller between the levels whose correspondence the opening asserted: it goes up, it comes down, and it carries the force of both ends of the journey in itself. In the book that frames it, this is the cosmos’s own circulation — influence descending, prepared matter ascending — stated as a biography.

The Close: Power of Powers, and the Naming of Hermes

The closing sentences gather the claims. It is the power of powers; it prevails over everything subtle and enters into everything gross — nothing is too fine for it to master or too dense for it to penetrate. And the work, the text concludes, was formed after the pattern of the making of the greater world: the operation, whatever it is, recapitulates the creation of the macrocosm. Then the voice names itself, in the sentence whose division is the source of the count’s variation: this is my renown, and therefore I am named Hermes, the threefold in wisdom. Whether an edition prints that self-naming as one sentence or divides the closing sentences is the whole difference between the archive’s thirteen and its fourteen — thirteen sentences in the usual segmentation, fourteen in editions that divide the closing sentences. The text ends, in the Arabic, not with a recipe’s colophon but with an author’s signature: the sage names himself, and the book from the vault is complete.

“Truth! Certainty! That in which there is no doubt! That which is above is from that which is below, and that which is below is from that which is above, working the miracles of one.”

— THE JABIRIAN VERSION, TRANS. E. J. HOLMYARD, NATURE 112 (1923)

Two cautions close the walk-through. The first concerns what the text does not contain. It names no substance that can be weighed, no vessel, no quantity, no fire-regime, no sequence of laboratory operations. It sets out no steps — the Egyptology division’s Alchemy article makes the same point in its own terms: the Tablet is not a recipe, and its power lies in its compressed statement of cosmic correspondence, not in a practical protocol. Every procedural reading of it — and the Latin Middle Ages produced centuries of them — is commentary supplying what the text withholds. The second caution concerns the subject. Generations of readers have named the One Thing: the philosophers’ stone, the elixir, philosophical mercury, the world-soul, the talismanic power itself. The Arabic text names none of these. It gives epithets — father of talismans, keeper of wonders, power of powers — and a biography — begotten of sun and moon, borne by wind, nursed by earth, ascending and descending. The reticence is not a gap in transmission. It is the text’s form: a creed, not a manual, whose subject is identified by what it does in the cosmos the Sirr al-khalīqa describes.

IX. Reading the Arabic Against the Latin: Ṭilasmāt, Telesmus, and a Paraphrase That Became a Motto

The surest way to feel the distance between the Tablet’s Arabic life and its Latin one is to set three of its phrases side by side with their famous Latin descendants — not to correct the Latin, which has its own long legitimacy, but to show what the Latin tradition was actually working from, and what it changed. The comparison is possible only because the Arabic was recovered: until Holmyard and Ruska, the Latin wording was the only wording there was, and it was naturally read as the sage’s own.

The motto deserves its own sentence of record, because it is the Tablet’s most famous phrase and it is not, strictly, a phrase of the Tablet. “As above, so below” is a modern paraphrase — of the Latin vulgate’s resemblance-clause, which is itself a rendering of the Arabic’s derivation-clause. Azize’s translation of the older Arabic recension gives the underlying sense with its direction intact: the highest is from the lowest, and the lowest from the highest, and it worked all wonders from one. Three stages — Arabic derivation, Latin resemblance, modern motto — each a fair translation of its predecessor and none identical with the first. A transmission history that cannot keep those stages apart will mistake the motto for the text, and the text for the stone. This article has kept them apart, and quotes the motto only as a motto.

The ṭilasmāt case carries the deeper lesson, and Jean-Marc Mandosio’s study of the vulgate’s Latinity (2005) is the modern authority for it. The word the Arabic uses is transparent to any Arabic reader: talismans, the made objects of Balinas’s own art. The vulgate’s translator, meeting a word he did not recognise, did the honest scribal thing and transcribed its sound — and thereby manufactured an enigma that centuries of commentary then laboured to solve, glossing telesmus as secret, as treasure, as the whole work of wonder. The obscurity of the Latin Tablet, so often celebrated as Hermetic depth, is at this point simply a translation accident, visible as such only from the Arabic side. That is the practical yield of the roots this article traces: not a purer text to replace the Latin one, but a vantage from which the Latin text’s own history — its choices, its expansions, its accidents — can at last be seen.

X. The Jabir Corpus and the Tablet: The Balance as an Intellectual Home

The Tablet’s second Arabic home is the most important alchemical corpus of the Arabic world: the writings attributed to Jābir ibn Ḥayyān. In the Kitāb usṭuqus al-uss al-thānī — the Second Book of the Element of the Foundation — the corpus quotes the Tablet, ascribes its knowledge of the text to Balinas, and comments on it as a received authority. The quotation is the version Holmyard published in Nature in 1923 — “The Emerald Table”, Nature 112, pp. 525–526 — the article with which the modern rediscovery of the Arabic Tablet began, and the source of this article’s one block quotation (Section VIII). Holmyard went on to edit the text in his Arabic Works of Jābir ibn Ḥayyān (Paris, 1928, p. 90). The Jabirian Tablet is shorter than the Sirr al-khalīqa text: measured against the fuller recension, several of its sentences are missing — among them the father-of-talismans epithet and much of the close — other parts read as corrupt, and the wind and earth exchange their offices in the parentage sentence (Section VIII). It is, in short, a quotation, made by a working author for his own purposes, not a careful copy of the book it came from.

The direction of the borrowing was contested, and the contest is a model of how this field’s dates get settled. Ruska and Plessner had inclined to treat the Jabirian version as the older — a natural inference while “Jābir” was still dated by his traditional eighth-century lifetime. Kraus argued the opposite, on the corpus’s internal evidence: the Jabirian Tablet is not older than the version in the Sirr al-khalīqa, and Jābir, by his own account, knew the text from Balinas’s book (Kraus, vol. I, 1943, pp. lvii–lviii; vol. II, 1942, pp. 280–281 n.; Asl 2016, pp. 444–445). Kraus’s redating of the whole Jabir corpus — to c. 850–950, as the work of an anonymous school writing under the master’s name rather than of a single eighth-century author — is the dating this archive follows everywhere, stated in the Egyptology division’s Jabir section and in this hub’s Transmission article, and it makes the sequence coherent: the Balinas book first (before 833), the Jabirian quotation second (ninth–tenth century), each at its own strength. The older habit of dating the Tablet to the eighth century “because Jābir lived then” dates a quotation by a lifetime its own corpus does not securely have.

What the Jabir corpus gave the Tablet was not only a second witness but an intellectual home — the system inside which its aphorisms could be read as exact science. That system is the science of the balance (ʿilm al-mīzān): the corpus’s attempt to reduce all phenomena — substances, elements, and their operations — to measures and quantitative proportions, assigning quantities to the four natures (hot, cold, wet, dry) and computing with them through the numerical values of the letters of a thing’s Arabic name, the hidden natures falling in the proportion 1:3:5:8. Read inside the mīzān, the Tablet’s sentences stop being oracles and become theorems of the system: the correspondence of above and below is the balance’s presupposition; the One Thing from which all things come by one ordering is the proportioned substrate the balance measures; the ascent and descent are the circulation the balance governs. The corpus likewise took over the Balinas book’s sulphur–mercury theory of the metals (Section VII) and built its metallurgy on it. The pattern is the pattern of Transmission §XIII in this hub: the Jabir corpus’s debts to the Greek and pseudo-Apollonian inheritance are real, and what it made of them is an Arabic creation, not a translation of anything. The Tablet, quoted inside that creation, became something it had not been in the Sirr al-khalīqa alone: a proof-text of a working science.

Asl’s survey (2016) adds the reception’s social texture. The Sirr al-khalīqa was, on Jābir’s own evidence in the Kitāb al-Aḥjār, famous early enough to have partisans — the “Muslim advocates of Balinas” — and its influence runs through the Jābirian works and into the Ismāʿīlī tradition, where a cosmology of one ordering disposing all levels of being found readers prepared for it. The Tablet’s Arabic centuries were not a dormancy between an imagined Greek birth and a Latin rebirth. They were a career: quoted by the age’s greatest alchemical corpus, systematised by its boldest theory, commented by its scholars (Section XI), and carried — still inside Arabic books — to the very threshold of Spain.

XI. Other Arabic Witnesses: The Tablet’s Life in Arabic Before Latin

The Sirr al-khalīqa and the Jabir corpus are the Tablet’s earliest witnesses, not its only Arabic ones. The modern count speaks of four Arabic recensions of the text, preserved in mystical and alchemical treatises between the eighth and tenth centuries. This section walks the remaining witnesses at the strength the scholarship supports for each — which varies, and is stated as it varies.

The Sirr al-asrār: Aristotle’s Letter to Alexander

The third great Arabic carrier is the Sirr al-asrār, the Secret of Secrets: the pseudo-Aristotelian mirror for princes, framed as Aristotle’s letter of counsel to Alexander the Great, and usually dated to the tenth century. Towards its end, among its teachings on alchemy and the occult properties of things, stands a full text of the Tablet — taken over, on Kraus’s judgement (1942, p. 272 n.), probably from the Sirr al-khalīqa. Its wording is close to the Balinas recension with small, telling differences: the earth nourishes the One Thing “with her milk”, and Hermes is named, at the close, threefold in innate wisdom. The recension’s importance is its vehicle. The Secret of Secrets became one of the most widely read books of the Arabic and then the Latin Middle Ages; the Tablet that rode in it reached audiences no alchemical treatise could touch — and it is this recension, through Philip of Tripoli’s thirteenth-century Latin Secretum secretorum, that carried a second, distinct Latin Tablet into Europe alongside Hugo’s and the vulgate (Section XIII). The Arabic text is edited in ʿAbd al-Raḥmān Badawī’s collection of the Greek sources of Islamic political theory (Cairo, 1954); the dating rests on Mahmoud Manzalaoui’s study of the pseudo-Aristotelian corpus (1974).

Ibn Umayl and the Silvery Water

The fourth recension belongs to Ibn Umayl (Muḥammad ibn Umayl al-Tamīmī, tenth century), the Egyptian alchemist whose Book of the Silvery Water and the Starry Earth reproduces a version of the Tablet inside an alchemical commentary — the recension through which the text passed, in Latin dress as the Tabula chemica, to a further Latin readership. Ibn Umayl’s frame deserves note beside Balinas’s: in his discovery story a stone table rests on the knees of Hermes in the secret chamber of a pyramid — the vault become Egypt’s monument — though the table in the picture is inscribed with pictured, “hieroglyphic” writing rather than with the Tablet’s text, which the treatise quotes separately. The migration of the frame’s furniture — vault to pyramid, text to picture — shows the discovery narrative developing a life of its own in the Arabic tradition, a generation before any Latin reader saw it.

The Book of Crates, and the Shape of the Topos

The parallel that best displays the topos is the Book of Crates, another Arabic alchemical work of around the tenth century. Its introduction tells how the philosopher Crates, praying in the temple called the Sarapieion, is granted a vision: an old man, the most beautiful of men, seated on a chair, dressed in white, a board before him on which rests a book, with wondrous vessels at hand — and the answer to his question: the old man is Hermes Trismegistus, and the book before him is one of those containing the explanation of the secrets he concealed from humankind. Enthroned sage, book, vessels, revealed secret: the same furniture as the Balinas vault, differently arranged. Set beside the Sirr al-khalīqa’s frame, the Crates vision confirms what Section III argued from the frame alone: the discovery of Hermes’ hidden book was a shared literary form of the Arabic Hermetic centuries, not one author’s report of an event. The tradition is telling the truth about itself in the only way the form allows: these books present themselves as found because found is what, in their world, a book of Hermes was.

Commentators, Citations, and the Kitāb al-ḥabīb

Around the recensions grew the apparatus of a classic. ʿAwn b. al-Mundhir’s fourth/tenth-century Arabic commentary on the Sirr al-khalīqa — the Tablet’s host book — survives in a unique manuscript, and its hitherto unknown Persian translation, identified by Asl (2016), preserves parts of the commentary the Arabic excerpt has lost: the Balinas book was being commented and translated within the Islamic world two centuries before Latin saw it. Citations of Apollonius-Balinas run, on Asl’s survey, across Islamic sources in field after field. And the text continued to be copied into further Arabic carriers — among them the work the literature cites as the Kitāb al-ḥabīb, the Book of the Friend — at a strength this article states carefully: the recension is registered in the scholarship on the Tablet’s Arabic transmission, but its details are less fully published than those of the four principal witnesses, and it is named here for completeness, not built upon. That distribution of confidence — four recensions in edited texts, a commentary tradition in manuscript and Persian, further carriers at citation strength — is the honest map of the Tablet’s Arabic life. It was not a single manuscript sleeping in a single library. It was a text in circulation, in more than one book, in more than one language of the Islamic world, for three centuries before 1144.

XII. The Rediscovery: Holmyard, Ruska’s Tabula Smaragdina, and Weisser’s Edition

The Arabic Tablet was not lost; it was unread — by the Latin-reading world, which for eight centuries knew the text only in Latin and assumed the Latin was the text. Its recovery is a dated episode of twentieth-century scholarship, and it has three names. Eric John Holmyard (1891–1959), the English historian of alchemy, opened it: his short paper “The Emerald Table” in Nature 112 (6 October 1923), pp. 525–526, announced the Jabirian Arabic version to the scientific world, and his edition of the Arabic Works of Jābir ibn Ḥayyān (Paris, 1928) gave it a printed text. Julius Ruska (1867–1949), the German orientalist, established it: his Tabula Smaragdina: Ein Beitrag zur Geschichte der hermetischen Literatur (Heidelberg, 1926) — “A Contribution to the History of Hermetic Literature” — assembled the Arabic and Latin versions, printed the Sirr al-khalīqa recension with the Sājiyūs frame, and demonstrated the priority of the Arabic tradition. Ruska’s book remains the foundational study: every modern account of the Tablet’s textual history, this article included, works in the space it opened. His companion volume Arabische Alchemisten I (1924) had already mapped the Arabic alchemical literature into which the Tablet fits.

Ursula Weisser completed the recovery’s editorial work. Her edition of the Kitāb sirr al-khalīqa wa-ṣanʿat al-ṭabīʿa (Aleppo: Institute for the History of Arabic Science, 1979) — the fruit of her 1974 Sezgin-supervised dissertation — appraised sixteen Arabic manuscripts of the book and established the text from four of them, with the Tablet at pp. 524–525; her monograph Das “Buch über das Geheimnis der Schöpfung” von Pseudo-Apollonios von Tyana (Berlin: De Gruyter, 1980) is the standard critical study of the pseudo-Apollonian book, its authorship, its sources, and its date. Between them, Holmyard, Ruska, and Weisser changed the Tablet from a Latin relic with a legend into an Arabic text with a bibliography. Kraus’s Jabir volumes (1942–43) supplied the intellectual history; the mid-century syntheses of Holmyard’s Alchemy (1957) carried the new account to general readers; and the modern literature — Principe’s transmission chapters; Kevin van Bladel’s The Arabic Hermes (2009) for the wider Arabic Hermetic corpus; Asl’s 2016 reception study; Azize’s full English translation of Ruska’s Arabic recension, with its Syriac-facing notes — continues to work the seam they opened.

// WHAT THE REDISCOVERY DID NOT FIND //

No Greek Tablet. No Syriac Tablet manuscript either: the Syriac stage of the transmission rests on the frame’s own claim (Section III), on the book’s Syriac-facing character, and on readers like Azize who find the Arabic construing better as Syriac at several hard places — an argument, not a codex. A century of looking, by scholars who would have been delighted to find an older witness, has produced none. In a field this thoroughly searched, the absence is itself a finding, and Section XIV states it as one.

XIII. The Threshold: Hugo of Santalla and the Latin Door

This article ends where this hub’s Transmission article opens its door — and the door, for the Tablet, has a translator’s name on it. In the first half of the twelfth century, at Tarazona in the Ebro valley, Hugo of Santalla translated the Sirr al-khalīqa into Latin as the Liber de secretis naturae — On the Secrets of Nature — working, on the edition’s evidence, from the abridged Arabic recension of the Balinas book. The translation is dated to about 1145–1151 — the dating the archive’s Egyptology division uses in its Hermeticism article — and it is the Tablet’s first Latin home: the sentences of Hermes, vault frame and all, in Latin dress, within a generation of Robert of Chester’s dated 1144 translation that opened Latin alchemy (Transmission §XVI). Hugo’s version did not conquer Europe. It survives in essentially one manuscript tradition — Paris, Bibliothèque nationale, lat. 13951, of the first half of the twelfth century, with a seventeenth-century copy, lat. 13952 — and it circulated narrowly; its modern edition is Françoise Hudry’s, in Chrysopoeia 6 (1997–1999, printed 2000), pp. 1–154. But priority is not popularity, and the fact stands: the first Latin a reader could give the Tablet was Hugo’s, and it was a translation of this book — the Balinas book, the Tablet’s Arabic cradle.

Hugo’s rendering shows, in miniature, the whole process Section IX described. Where the Arabic derives the upper from the lower, Hugo’s Latin already levels the traffic into mutual relation — superiora de inferioribus, inferiora de superioribus, the upper things from the lower, the lower from the upper, the working of wonders from one — and where the Arabic calls the One Thing father of the talismans, Hugo addresses its readers instead as sons of the talismanic art, perfected in discretion. The translation is free, intelligent, and unmistakably a twelfth-century Latin mind at work on a ninth-century Arabic text. It is quoted here, in paraphrase and in a single short phrase, as what it is: the threshold document, not the original.

The Tablet did not cross only once, and the crossings should be distinguished before this article hands the story over. Herman of Carinthia, a friend of Robert of Chester working in the same Spanish translation world, already cited the Tablet in his treatise On Essences in 1143 — recalling, with it, the frame story of the discovery under the statue of Hermes, from the Book of the Secret of Creation: the vault crossed into Latin alongside the text, a year before the conventional door-date. The vulgate — the version that actually conquered Europe, carried in the anonymous Liber Hermetis de alchimia, translated from a lost Arabic exemplar in the twelfth century by a translator still unidentified (the attribution to Plato of Tivoli is conjecture, and is given as such) — is a separate Latin Tablet from Hugo’s, with the telesmus enigma and the operation-of-the-Sun colophon of Section IX. And the Sirr al-asrār recension crossed a third time, in the thirteenth century, inside Philip of Tripoli’s Secretum secretorum, with Hermes renamed Hermogenes and the worker of miracles become “one God alone”. Three Latin Tablets, from three Arabic carriers, within a century: the door, when it opened, opened onto a text that arrived in plurality. The full Latin and Renaissance career — Hortulanus’s commentary, the printed Tablets of 1541, the emblem books, and Newton’s English rendering among them (see the Egyptology division’s Newton article) — belongs to the archive’s later wings and is deliberately not told here. This article’s scope is the roots, and the roots end at the threshold.

XIV. What the Roots Establish — and What They Do Not

The evidence assembled in this article supports a positive statement, and it is worth making it in one breath. The Emerald Tablet, as a document, is an Arabic text of the early medieval period: its earliest surviving witness is the appendix of an Arabic cosmology compiled before 833; its language, its frame, its transmitter, its first commentators, and its first systematising readers are all of the Arabic world of the eighth to tenth centuries; it lived a full Arabic life — quoted, abridged, commented, translated into Persian, carried into the age’s most popular book of counsel — for some three centuries before its first Latin rendering; and the Latin tradition that made it famous received it, in plurality, from Arabic carriers in twelfth-century Spain. That much is not conjecture. It is bibliography.

The second statement concerns the core the Arabic text carries. The Sirr al-khalīqa demonstrably digests late-antique Greek material — Nemesius of Emesa stands in its own supplements — breathes Syriac school air, and presents its Tablet as translated from a Syriac “first language”. The Hermetic matter of the Tablet itself — the thrice-wise Hermes, the correspondence of the worlds, the one source of all things — is continuous with the late-antique Hermetic world treated in this hub’s Hermetica article, and nothing in the Arabic text reads as an Islamic invention ex nihilo. The measured conclusion the scholarship supports, and the one this hub adopts, is that the Tablet is an Arabic text carrying a late-antique Hermetic core: composed or compiled in the early Islamic centuries out of materials whose ancestry runs back, by routes partly recoverable and partly not, to the Greek and Syriac Hermetica of late antiquity. The Egyptology division’s Islamic Egyptology article makes the companion point for the wider corpus: the Arabic tradition’s recipe and workshop material is of the same Greek workshop kind as the late-antique papyri — kinship of kind, established by comparison — and this article claims no more for the Tablet’s ancestry than that same, careful strength.

ESTABLISHED

Earliest witness: Arabic, before 833, in the Sirr al-khalīqa. Four Arabic recensions by the 10th century. First Latin crossings: 1143 citation; Hugo of Santalla, c. 1145–1151; the vulgate; Philip of Tripoli. Sentence count: thirteen in the usual segmentation, fourteen in editions that divide the closing sentences.

REASONABLE INFERENCE

A Greek or Syriac ancestry for parts of the Sirr al-khalīqa’s material, and a late-antique Hermetic core behind the Tablet — inferred from the Arabic text’s character, its digested Greek sources, and its own Syriac claim. Inference, held as inference.

NOT ESTABLISHED

A Greek original of the Tablet: none survives, and none is attested by any ancient author. A Syriac manuscript of the Tablet: none is known. The vault event: a frame story. The emerald object: unattested outside the frame. The first-century Apollonius as author: a pseudepigraphic attribution, doubted already in the 10th century.

NOT THE TABLET AT ALL

The chiselled “Emerald Tablets of Thoth the Atlantean” of twentieth-century channelled literature are a modern work sharing a name and nothing else; no Egyptian source attests them, and the archive treats them, in the Egyptology division’s words for the same material, as modern invention, not as witnesses.

Say it plainly, then, as this hub’s rule requires: no Greek original of the Tablet survives, and none is attested. A Greek original is a reasonable inference — from the book’s Greek ingredients, from the tradition’s own claims, from the Hermetic matter’s evident antiquity of kind — and reasonable inference is where the matter must rest. The scholar who asserts a lost Greek Tabula as a fact has left the evidence; the enthusiast who asserts a pharaonic emerald has left it further. What the roots actually give the Tablet is, in the end, more interesting than either fiction: a datable birth in the most intellectually ambitious civilisation of its age, a named finder who is himself a fiction honestly worn, a priest-transmitter whose damaged name still shows the Syriac Christian hands the text passed through, a cosmology that made its aphorisms intelligible, a balance-science that made them exact, and a three-century Arabic career that made them, by the time Latin took them up, already classics. The West’s most famous alchemical text is an Arabic book’s appendix. The appendix outlasted the book — as appendices, given three centuries of readers, sometimes do.

The story continues through the Latin door, in this hub’s Transmission and in the wings that follow it. The next action for the reader of the roots is the threshold itself: Hugo’s Liber de secretis naturae beside Weisser’s Arabic, the one short phrase of Section XIII against the sentences of Section VIII — the moment, visible on two facing pages, at which the Tablet’s Arabic centuries end and its European millennium begins.
