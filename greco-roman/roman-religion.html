I. Not Greek Myth in Latin Dress: Religion as Doing

Begin with the sentence this hub requires every reader of Roman religion to hold onto, because almost every popular account violates it: Roman religion is not Greek mythology retold in Latin. The companion article on the Olympians shows what Rome did with the Greek gods — it matched them, name by name, to its own older Italic and Etruscan powers, in the process modern scholarship calls interpretatio romana, and then let Greek stories flow into the Roman names. That matching is real, and Sections XII–XIII of this article return to it. But the matching happened inside a religious system that was already Roman in its structure, its personnel, and its purposes before the Greek myths arrived in bulk — and that system, not the stories, is the subject here. A reader who knows the Aeneid and the Metamorphoses knows what educated Romans read. This article is about what Romans did: who sacrificed, on which days, in which words, under whose authority, with what consequence if a word was missed.

The modern account on which this article principally rests — Mary Beard, John North, and Simon Price, Religions of Rome (Cambridge, 1998), in two volumes, a history and a sourcebook of documents in translation — opens with a deliberate provocation of exactly this kind. Its history runs for more than a thousand years, from the eighth-century BCE hamlet on the Tiber to the Christian capital of the fourth and fifth centuries CE, and it insists throughout that the story is one of change: new gods admitted, old rites revived or reinvented, priesthoods opened, closed, and politicised. John Scheid’s An Introduction to Roman Religion (French original 1998; English translation by Janet Lloyd, 2003), the second backbone of this article, makes the complementary point from the ritual side: Roman religion is best approached through its acts — sacrifices, vows, auspices, processions — and through the documents those acts generated, rather than through any supposed Roman creed, because there was no creed to reconstruct. Neither work treats “Roman religion” as a set of beliefs Romans held in their heads. Both treat it as something Romans performed, in public and at home, correctly or incorrectly, with the correctness open to inspection.

That is the sense of the word orthopraxic, which this article uses in its precise, limited meaning. Greek cult, as the Olympians article shows, was already various — city by city, altar by altar, epithet by epithet, with no central authority to standardise it. Roman public cult, by contrast, developed a single civic centre, a small number of priestly colleges charged with advising the Senate, an inscribed calendar that told the whole community which days allowed which business, and a body of sacred law (ius divinum) administered like other law. To call Roman religion orthopraxic is not to say Romans felt nothing, believed nothing, or cared only for form — Section III takes up that caricature directly. It is to say that the system’s test of a religious act was whether it had been done rightly: the right person, the right victim, the right words, the right day, the right place. Belief was not examined. The performance was.

THE GREEK STORY-WORLD

Poets — Homer, Hesiod, the tragedians — carry the theology. Gods have biographies, quarrels, loves, and childhoods. Cult is local and plural, and no single city’s practice binds another’s. See the hub’s Theogony and Olympians articles.

THE ROMAN RITUAL SYSTEM

Priestly colleges, magistrates, and the Senate carry the practice. Gods have functions, temples, festival dates, and priesthoods. Public cult is civic and documented, and a flawed performance is a defective act to be repeated, not a private failure of faith.

INTERPRETATIO ROMANA

The naming of a foreign god by a Roman name — Jupiter for Zeus, Mars for Ares, Isis kept as Isis — is treated in this hub as a Roman administrative-religious act: an identification made by priests, generals, and the Senate, with legal and cultic consequences, not as the translation of a myth from one language to another.

WHERE THE EVIDENCE LIVES

Less in narrative poetry than in calendars on stone and plaster, priestly records, senatorial decrees preserved on bronze, dedications, and antiquarian writers — Varro, Livy, Ovid’s Fasti, Plutarch’s Roman Questions — who describe rites as rites. Section XVI lists what was actually used.

A last framing fact, because it explains the article’s shape. Rome had no word that maps cleanly onto the modern English word “religion.” The Latin religio named, in its core uses, scrupulous obligation — the care owed to the gods, the binding force of an oath or a rite, sometimes the anxiety that a duty had been neglected. Superstitio, by contrast, named excess: religion done too much, too fearfully, or by the wrong people in the wrong way — a social judgement as much as a theological one, and one Romans applied freely to foreign cults and to their neighbours. Pietas named dutifulness toward gods, parents, and fatherland in a single breath, which is why Virgil’s hero is pius Aeneas in all three relations at once. A vocabulary in which duty to the gods and duty to the family and the state share one word is not a vocabulary that separates “religion” from the rest of life. Roman religion had no outside. That is precisely why its priests were senators, its calendar was the civic calendar, and its most characteristic documents are legal ones.

II. Numen, Function, and the Gods Without Stories

A Roman god, in the oldest stratum of the system, is a numen: a divine power, a capacity to act, attached to a function, a place, or a moment — and very often to nothing else. The word matters. Numen (from nuere, to nod) is in origin the nod itself, the sign of assent and power, before it is a person. Greek gods, even in cult, keep the gravitational pull of their epic biographies: Zeus who sacrificed at Olympia is still the Zeus who quarrelled on Olympus. A large class of Roman divine powers never acquired a biography at all, and the absence is not a gap in the record. It is the point of this section, and in a sense of the whole article: a Roman power could be fully real in cult — with a temple, a festival, a priest, and a date — and have no myth whatsoever.

Janus: The God of the Door Has No Greek Double

Janus (Iānus) is the standing example. God of beginnings, doorways, passages, and the hinge-moment between states, he is depicted with two faces looking opposite ways — the image is a function drawn, not an episode from a life. He has no Greek equivalent: the interpretatio, which found a Roman partner for nearly every Olympian, found none for Janus, and he remained himself. His month, Iānuārius, opens the year; his shrine in the Forum, the Janus Geminus, stood with its doors open in wartime and closed in peace — a condition so rare that its occurrences were remembered as events, under Numa by tradition, in 235 BCE, and three times under Augustus, who advertises the closures in his own account of his reign (paraphrased). Invocations of the gods in formal prayer regularly began with Janus, as Ovid’s Fasti Book 1 stages at length, because beginnings belonged to him by office. Of a childhood, a marriage, a quarrel, a journey — the normal furniture of a Greek god — Janus has effectively none. He is a door that is also a god, and Rome found nothing missing in that.

Terminus, Fides, and the Powers of Single Acts

Terminus is a boundary stone that is a god. His festival, the Terminalia (23 February), was kept by neighbouring landholders at the boundary itself, with offerings at the stone; the Capitoline temple’s foundation legend, told by Livy and others (paraphrased), made the point into theology: when the site was cleared and the older shrines were formally deconsecrated, Terminus alone — in some versions with Juventas, Youth — refused to move, and his shrine was therefore built into Jupiter’s temple, under an opening in the roof, because Terminus would not be housed under cover. Rome’s boundaries, the story says in ritual form, do not yield — not even to Jupiter. Around Janus and Terminus stands a whole population of such powers: Fides, Good Faith, with a temple on the Capitol; Concordia, Concord, whose temple in the Forum was rebuilt at successive political crises as an argument in stone; Spes, Hope; Salus, Safety; Virtus and Honos, Valour and Honour, worshipped in paired temples so that the worshipper passed through Honour to reach Valour — the architecture itself preaching. The priestly lists known as the indigitamenta carried the principle to its limit: minute powers for the stages of a child’s life, a crop’s growth, a door’s parts — hinge, panel, and threshold each with its own name. Augustine, who preserves much of this material from Varro in order to mock it in the City of God, is thus, unwillingly, one of its chief witnesses; the mockery is Christian polemic, the lists are Roman priestly practice as Varro recorded it, and Section XVI keeps the two apart.

The systematiser who lets us see this world whole is also its great loss. Marcus Terentius Varro (116–27 BCE), the most learned Roman of his age, devoted the sixteen books on divine affairs of his Antiquitates rerum humanarum et divinarum — Antiquities of Human and Divine Things — to the Roman system as a system: who performs the rites, where, when, what is performed, and finally to which gods — the order Augustine reports in City of God Book 6, in the course of refuting it. The work is lost. It survives in fragments and, above all, inside Augustine’s hostile summary, which preserves Varro’s famous threefold division of theology (theologia tripertita): the mythical theology of the poets, the natural theology of the philosophers, and the civil theology of the cities — what the people, and above all the priests, must know and perform. Varro’s own allegiance, on Augustine’s evidence (paraphrased), was plain: the civil theology is the one a citizen lives under, and Varro wrote, in effect, its handbook. Every modern account in Section XVI works in Varro’s shadow and through his loss: Rome produced exactly one great systematiser of its own religion, and his book reached us dismembered inside the work of the man who set out to destroy its world. The archive notes the pattern because it recurs — the Arval records of Section IV and the calendar inscriptions of Section VIII are the compensating evidence, documents the system made about itself, in stone, without an author to lose.

III. Pax Deorum, Pietas, and Exact Performance

The condition Roman public religion existed to maintain had a name: pāx deōrum, the peace of the gods — the state of right relations between the Roman community and its divine powers, in which the gods were neither angered nor neglected and the community could expect their favour, or at minimum their neutrality. Its breach had names too: ira deum, the anger of the gods, inferred after the fact from defeat, plague, famine, or prodigy; and the work of restoring the peace — expiation (piaculum), vows, games, processions, new temples, imported gods — fills the annalistic record that Livy preserves. The logic is sometimes described as a contract, and the description is fair if its limits are kept. The pax was not negotiated once; it was a standing condition, maintained by a calendar of owed performances, monitored by signs, and repaired, when it broke, by further performances whose adequacy the event itself would judge. Rome’s empire, in the Romans’ own explanation, was the longest-running evidence that the maintenance worked.

Instauratio: When a Rite Fails, Begin Again

Exactness had a technical consequence that displays the system better than any definition. If a public rite was flawed — a word misspoken, a victim that bolted or struggled, an ill-omened sound interrupting the prayer, a magistrate who stumbled in the formula — the act was not a prayer that had failed to persuade. It was a defective performance, a vitium, and the remedy was instauratio: the rite, festival, or games were performed again, entire, from the beginning. Livy’s history is studded with instaurations of the great games for faults no modern reader would notice — a dancer who stopped, a chariot driver who lost a rein, a rope that broke — each recorded because each cost the state a full repetition at public expense (paraphrased throughout). The presiding magistrate sacrificed with covered head (capite velato), a flute-player played continuously to drown out ill-omened sounds, and an attendant prompted the formula word by word from a written text so that nothing depended on memory: Plutarch, in the Roman Questions (Question 10, in Frank Cole Babbitt’s Loeb translation, paraphrased here), explains the covered head in part as a precaution lest any ill-omened sound reach the worshipper’s ears while praying — the anxiety is procedural before it is anything else. A religion that stations a man with a book beside its chief officiant, to catch a mispronunciation, has told you where it locates the sacred: in the performance, done rightly.

Pietas is the virtue that operated the system, and its range is the system’s range: dutifulness to the gods, to parents and ancestors, and to the fatherland, felt as a single obligation with three objects. Its opposite was not unbelief — Rome had no instrument for detecting unbelief and no procedure that required it — but neglegentia, neglect: the festival not kept, the vow not paid, the auspices not taken, the ancestral rite allowed to lapse. Scheid’s introduction is particularly good on this point (paraphrased): impiety at Rome, in the cases that produced action, is almost always a failure of performance or a violation of sacred property and persons — theft from a temple, injury to a priest or a Vestal, a rite performed by someone not entitled — not a wrong opinion about the gods’ nature. Philosophers could argue that the gods did not exist, or did not care, in books that other senators read; what no one could do with impunity was stop the city’s rites or botch them in office. The boundary is worth stating exactly, because Section XIII’s suppressions and Section XV’s bans both turn on it: Rome policed acts.

Sacrifice stood at the centre of the acts, and its Roman form should be pictured concretely. The public victim — ox, sheep, or pig, according to the god and the occasion, unblemished and garlanded — was led in procession to an outdoor altar before the temple (the temple itself was the god’s house and the treasury of the cult, not a congregational hall; the rite happened outside it), sprinkled with salted meal (mola salsa, prepared under the Vestals’ charge) and wine, and stunned and slaughtered by attendants while the officiant, head covered, pronounced the prayer naming the god and the purpose. The entrails (exta) were inspected — a failed inspection, like a flawed formula, voided the act and required repetition — then cooked and offered; the meat was eaten by the participants or distributed, so that a great public sacrifice was also a distribution of meat through the city. Scheid’s account of public sacrifice, and the Arval protocols of Section IV which record sacrifices victim by victim and god by god, agree on the essential impression: this was skilled, staffed, expensive public work, closer in its organisation to a civic ceremony than to a congregational service, and it was done, on the great days, by the same men who governed.

IV. The Priesthoods as Civic Offices: Pontifices and the Pontifex Maximus

Rome’s public priests were not a clergy in the later sense: no separate caste, no pastoral charge, no doctrine to teach, no congregation to lead in weekly worship. They were civic office-holders — overwhelmingly senators, holding priesthood alongside consulships, commands, and governorships, often several priesthoods across a career — whose religious function was expertise: to know the sacred law, to advise the Senate and magistrates on it, to keep the calendar and the records, and to perform or supervise the rites assigned to their college. The arrangement is unintelligible if priesthood is imagined as a vocation set apart from politics. At Rome it was one of the forms politics took. Julius Caesar’s election as pontifex maximus in 63 BCE was a major political contest, fought with heavy bribery on all sides, because the office was worth a career; Augustus, who waited politely for the incumbent to die rather than depose him, made the chief priesthood thereafter a permanent attribute of the emperor. The gods’ business and the state’s business had the same personnel because, in the Roman arrangement, they were the same business conducted in two registers.

The College of Pontifices

The pontifices formed the senior college. Under their head, the pontifex maximus, they held a general superintendence of sacred law: the calendar and its intercalations, the formulae of vows, dedications, and devotions, adoptions and wills insofar as they touched ancestral rites, the discipline of the Vestals and the appointment of the flamines, and the keeping of records — the chief pontiff’s annual notice-board of events, the annales maximi, stands behind Rome’s earliest historical writing. Formally attached to the college’s world were the rex sacrorum, the “king of the sacred rites” who inherited the deposed kings’ ritual duties and ranked first in ceremonial precedence while holding almost no power; the fifteen flamines (Section VI); and the Vestal Virgins (Section VI). The college’s membership tells the political story in miniature: originally patrician, it was opened to plebeians by the lex Ogulnia of 300 BCE — one of the landmark concessions of the Conflict of the Orders — enlarged by Sulla, and refilled by Caesar and the emperors. A priesthood that a rising order fights for two centuries to enter is not a retreat from public life. It is one of public life’s prizes, and the fight over it is evidence for how much the expertise was worth.

The Arval Brothers: A Priesthood That Kept Minutes

One priesthood lets the modern reader watch the system at work day by day, because it carved its minutes on marble. The Fratres Arvales, the Arval Brothers — twelve priests of senatorial rank, serving for life, whose grove and temple of the goddess Dea Dia lay on the Via Campana outside the city — inscribed their Acta, their protocols, on tablets fixed to the temple walls. The surviving records, recovered in bulk in the nineteenth century, span in their datable entries from 21 BCE to 304 CE and document, in formulaic detail, what a Roman priesthood actually did across three centuries: the election of officers, vows undertaken and discharged for the emperor’s safety, sacrifices specified by date, place, god, and victim, expiations for such incidents as a tree struck by lightning in the grove or iron brought into it against the rule — the expiation itself requiring named substitute victims and the removal of the offending iron — and, in the record for 218 CE, the text of the Carmen Arvale, the Brothers’ archaic hymn to the Lares and Mars, already so old that its Latin was barely intelligible to the men who sang it, preserved because the protocol of that year happened to write it down.

V. Signs and Permission: Augures, Auspicia, Haruspices, and Prodigies

No Roman public act of consequence was supposed to begin until the gods had been asked — not what would happen, but whether they assented to the act proposed, on that day, by that magistrate. The asking was the auspicia, the auspices; the discipline that governed it was the augural law; and its experts were the augures. The distinction between the magistrate and the augur is the institution in miniature. The auspices belonged to the magistrate by virtue of his office — a consul, praetor, or general held the auspicia as he held command (imperium), and took them himself, at dawn, in a ritually delimited space (templum in its original sense: a zone marked out in the sky and on the ground for observation). The augur was the consultant beside him: keeper of the discipline’s precedents, entitled to declare a flaw (vitium) in the proceedings, and possessed of the formidable right of obnuntiatio — announcing unfavourable signs and thereby halting a public assembly. Elections, legislation, the departure of an army, the founding of a colony, the dedication of a temple: all ran under auspices, and an act done against them, or after a declared flaw, was religiously void in a way that Roman politics repeatedly exploited and Roman courts repeatedly had to judge.

Impetrative and Oblative: Signs Asked For and Signs Sent

Roman theory, as Scheid sets it out (paraphrased), divided signs into two kinds. Auspicia impetrativa were sought: the magistrate defined the question and the field of observation and watched for the specified birds — their species, number, direction, flight, and cries — or, in the army’s practical shorthand, fed the sacred chickens (pulli) and read their appetite. The answer was narrow by design: assent or refusal, for that act, that day; the auspices expired at nightfall, at the crossing of a boundary, at any flaw in the taking. Auspicia oblativa were unsought: signs that presented themselves — lightning, an eclipse, a stumble, a sneeze, a chance word — which the magistrate could accept as significant or formally decline to notice, a discretion without which government would have seized up daily. The system was thus neither a fortune-telling machine nor a fraud, in its own terms: it was a procedure for obtaining divine permission, with rules for what counted, who could ask, and how long the answer lasted — permission, notably, not prediction. What the gods thought of the outcome was a question the auspices did not put.

The Etruscan Discipline: Haruspices, Livers, and Lightning

Beside the Roman auspices stood a second, foreign science that Rome adopted without ever quite naturalising: the disciplina Etrusca, the Etruscan discipline, practised by haruspices. Its three fields were the inspection of victims’ entrails, above all the liver, whose lobes and markings were read against a model of the divided sky; the interpretation of lightning, classified by quarter, colour, and consequence in a doctrine of great elaboration; and the expiation of prodigies. The discipline’s prestige was proverbial — Rome’s first kings are Etruscan in the tradition, and the city’s early sacred architecture and regalia carry Etruscan workmanship — and its practitioners were summoned from Etruria by the Senate well into the Republic. Yet the haruspices long remained consultants without a college: the Senate took their responses (responsa) and ordered the expiations they prescribed, but the experts themselves stood outside the collegiate priesthoods, organised as an ordo of sixty only under the Empire — Claudius, Tacitus reports (paraphrased), proposed formal state support for the college of haruspices lest the ancient Italian discipline decay through neglect. The pattern recurs in Section XIII: Rome uses a foreign religious expertise, honours it, pays for it — and keeps the authority to decide in Roman hands.

Prodigies: The Senate as Clearing-House of the Uncanny

A prodigy (prodigium) was an event out of nature’s order reported to the Senate as a sign that the pax deorum was broken: rains of stones or blood, speaking oxen, statues sweating, two-headed calves, lightning striking a temple, a wolf in the Forum. The procedure that followed is the Roman system at its most characteristic, and Livy’s annalistic lists — year after year of prodigies and their expiations — are its archive. The Senate decided which reports to accept as public prodigies (private wonders were the owner’s problem); it referred the accepted ones to the appropriate experts — pontifices, haruspices, or the keepers of the Sibylline Books (Section VII); it decreed the prescribed remedy — a procession, a hymn sung by twenty-seven virgins through the city, nine days of rites (novemdiale sacrum), games, a temple vowed, a foreign god fetched; and it had the remedy performed at public expense and entered in the record. Modern readers meet these lists as curiosities. In their own setting they are a government’s incident log: the uncanny, routed through committee, answered with budgeted ritual, and filed. Beard, North, and Price’s treatment stresses what the lists also show — that the system contracted in the late Republic, as prodigy-reporting from an Italy-wide network thinned and elite writers grew ironic about the machinery their grandfathers had operated in earnest. The machinery’s decline is part of its history, and Section XV returns to it.

VI. Bound by Taboo: Flamines, the Flamen Dialis, and the Vestal Virgins

If the colleges show Roman priesthood as civic expertise, the flamines show its other pole: priesthood as bondage to a single god and a single ritual programme. A flamen was the priest of one deity — the word is traditionally connected with kindling the sacrificial flame — and the fifteen flamines, three major and twelve minor, served Jupiter, Mars, and Quirinus at the head of a list that reaches down to gods like Pomona of the orchards and Volturnus of the river, several of whom had little cult beyond their flamen’s rites. The three major flamines belonged to the archaic stratum of the system — Jupiter, Mars, Quirinus is the old triad that precedes the Capitoline arrangement of Section XII — and they were required to be patricians married by the archaic rite of confarreatio, because the office was held, in a real sense, by the couple: the flamen’s wife, the flaminica, had her own ritual duties, her own dress, and her own taboos, and for the greatest of them her death ended his tenure.

The Flamen Dialis: Rigour Made Visible

The flamen Dialis, priest of Jupiter (Diespiter — hence Dialis), lived under the most famous set of taboos in Roman religion, preserved in Aulus Gellius’s Attic Nights (10.15), drawing on earlier antiquarian and juristic authorities — the list is paraphrased here from the tradition Gellius reports. He might not ride a horse, nor look upon an army drawn up, nor swear an oath, nor wear a ring except one broken in form, nor have a knot anywhere on his person, nor touch or name a goat, raw meat, ivy, or beans; his hair might be cut only by a free man and with a bronze blade; the clippings of his hair and nails were buried beneath a fruitful tree; he might not sleep away from his own bed for more than a fixed span of nights; his bed’s legs were smeared with clay and no one else might sleep in it; he wore his pointed cap (apex) and his festival dress — a heavy woollen cloak of prescribed manufacture, woven by his wife — not on feast days only but as his constant public condition, a man permanently dressed for a rite. He was married by confarreatio, could not divorce, and if his wife died he resigned: the priesthood was a marriage’s office. Modern accounts sometimes present the taboos as picturesque survivals. In the system’s own terms they are better read as the flamen’s job description written on his body: Jupiter’s priest was kept, by rule, in a state of continuous ritual readiness and continuous separation from the contaminations of war, death, and the ordinary — the system’s rigour, made visible in one encumbered senator.

The rigour had a price the record does not hide. The office stood vacant from 87 BCE to 11 BCE — seventy-five years in which no flamen Dialis served — because, after the suicide of the incumbent Cornelius Merula in the Marian troubles, no eligible patrician could be found, or would accept, a life so constrained and a career so limited: the flamen was barred by his taboos from much of the military and provincial service on which senatorial careers were built. Augustus, in his general restoration of the priesthoods, refilled the office in 11 BCE and adjusted its conditions by decree — the antiquarian tradition behind Gellius’s list itself reflects, as Scheid notes, a dossier assembled from specific rulings rather than a single priestly rule-book. The vacancy is the honest counterweight to any romance about Roman piety: the most sacred priesthood in Rome was, for three generations, a post the aristocracy declined to take, and the system ran on without it. What revived it was not a surge of devotion but an emperor’s programme of restoration — religion, again, administered.

The Vestal Virgins: The Hearth of the State

The Vestal Virgins (virgines Vestales) were six in number, chosen as girls between six and ten years old from families free of defect and with both parents living, taken — the ritual word is captio, a “taking” — by the pontifex maximus, and bound to thirty years of service: ten learning, ten performing, ten teaching, by the conventional scheme the sources report. Their charge was the cult of Vesta, the hearth: the undying fire in her round temple in the Forum, which might never go out — if it did, the Vestal responsible was scourged by the chief pontiff and the fire rekindled by friction from wood, as a new fire — the sacred storehouse (penus Vestae) whose contents no outsider might see, the preparation of the salted meal for public sacrifice, and the keeping of objects on which the state’s safety was believed to ride, among them, by tradition, the Palladium, the ancient image said to have come from Troy. The Vestals’ legal position was unique in Rome: removed from their father’s power without entering a husband’s, they could own property, make wills, and give evidence, were preceded by a lictor, and carried a sanctity that made injury to their persons a capital sacrilege and an accidental meeting with them, on the road, a reprieve for a condemned man being led to execution.

The other face of that sanctity was its penalty. A Vestal who broke her vow of chastity committed incestum — a pollution of the state’s hearth, treated as a prodigy in a person — and was buried alive in an underground chamber near the Colline Gate with a lamp and small provisions, a rite carefully framed so that the city did not, in form, kill her; her lover was beaten to death in the Forum. The recorded cases are few across a thousand years and cluster at moments of public crisis — after the disaster at Cannae in 216 BCE two Vestals were condemned, in a season that also saw expiations of a far darker kind recorded by Livy with visible discomfort — and modern historians read the prosecutions as the system’s pressure-valve: when the pax deorum was felt to be broken beyond ordinary repair, suspicion fell on the guardians of the fire. The Vestals embody, in one institution, the household and the state fused: Vesta’s fire is the city’s hearth, as the fire of Section X is the family’s, and its keepers are daughters of no household because they are daughters of the city’s. Their end dates the old system’s end as precisely as any event can: the fire was extinguished and the order dissolved under Theodosius I in 394 CE, in the legislation treated in Section XV.

VII. War Made Lawful: Fetiales, Vows, and the Sibylline Books

Rome’s most distinctive theological claim was that its wars were just — not merely successful, but iusta, lawful in the sight of gods and men — and it maintained a priesthood whose whole charge was to make that claim procedurally true. The fetiales were twenty priests, keepers of the ius fetiale, the sacred law between peoples: they advised on treaties and their violation, demanded restitution for injuries, and performed the rites by which war was declared and peace sworn. Their procedures, which Livy sets out in Book 1 as archaic inheritances from the kings, are the clearest surviving specimen of Roman religion as law: a sequence of prescribed speeches, at prescribed stations, before prescribed witnesses, after which — and only after which — violence became legitimate.

The Demand, the Thirty-Three Days, and the Spear

Livy’s account (1.32) is given here from the translation actually read for this article — the Loeb translation of B. O. Foster, as presented on the Perseus Digital Library — and the one sentence quoted is quoted from it. When Rome claimed restitution (rerum repetitio) from a people who had injured it, the fetial envoy, the pater patratus, his head covered with a woollen fillet and accompanied by a colleague carrying sacred herbs (sagmina) uprooted from the Capitol, proceeded to the enemy’s frontier and spoke his formula — in Foster’s translation: “Hear, Jupiter; hear, ye boundaries of” the people named; “let righteousness hear! I am the public herald of the Roman People; I come duly and religiously commissioned; let my words be credited.” He repeated the demand, with its oath — that if he demanded unjustly he might never enjoy his native land — at the frontier, to the first man he met, at the city gate, and in the forum to the magistrates. If restitution was not made within thirty-three days, the conventional number Livy specifies, he pronounced the enemy unjust before Jupiter, Janus Quirinus, and all the gods of heaven, earth, and the underworld, and returned to Rome. The king — in the Republic, the Senate and people — deliberated in set form; if war was voted, the fetial returned to the frontier and, before at least three adult witnesses, hurled a spear — iron-tipped or fire-hardened, of cornel wood — into the enemy’s territory with the declaration of war. War so begun was a bellum iustum: the gods had been notified, the forms observed, the enemy given his interval.

The Sibylline Books: A Greek Oracle as a Roman State Archive

The Sibylline Books were Rome’s strangest religious possession: a collection of Greek hexameter oracles, acquired — in the famous legend (paraphrased) — by the last king, Tarquin, from the Sibyl of Cumae, who burned half her nine books and then a third of the rest before he would pay her original price for the remainder. Whatever the legend’s worth, the institution is documentary. The books were kept in a stone chest in the vaults of the Capitoline temple, later in two gilded cases in Augustus’s temple of Apollo Palatinus, in the custody of the priestly board that grew with the collection’s importance: two men (duumviri), then ten (decemviri, from 367 BCE, half plebeian), then fifteen (quindecimviri sacris faciundis). They were consulted only on the Senate’s order, and only at crisis: prodigies of the gravest kind, plague, famine, military disaster, the portents that attended civil breakdown. And what the consultation produced was never, in the Roman use, a prediction. The quindecimviri searched the verses for the remedy the text prescribed — a procession, a lectisternium, a hymn, a temple, a foreign god to be fetched — and reported the prescription, not the prophecy, to the Senate. A Greek oracle, written in Greek verse by Greek Sibyls, was thus converted into a Roman administrative instrument: an archive of remedies, kept under lock, opened by decree, its answers implemented as public works of religion. Nearly every great importation of Section XIII entered Rome by this door.

The books’ history is a parable of the archive itself. The original collection burned with the Capitoline temple in 83 BCE; the Senate sent envoys through the Greek world to reassemble a corpus from the Sibylline verses held at Erythrae and elsewhere, and the quindecimviri edited the harvest — admitting, rejecting, and, inevitably, shaping what would count as Sibylline for the next four centuries. Augustus, Suetonius reports (paraphrased), burned some two thousand unofficial prophetic scrolls in circulation and deposited the purged official books in their new home: the state’s monopoly on crisis-oracles was a monopoly on a text the state itself had recompiled. The books were still being consulted in the fourth century CE, and were burned — the tradition ascribes the act to the general Stilicho, around the turn of the fifth century — as the world that had kept them ended. No line of the official Roman collection survives as such; the “Sibylline Oracles” read today are Jewish and Christian compositions in the same form, a different corpus wearing the name, and the archive files them apart.

VIII. The Calendar as Religion’s Skeleton: Fasti, Nefasti, and the Inscribed Year

If Roman religion has a skeleton — the structure on which everything else hangs and by which everything else is dated — it is the calendar. The Roman year was not a neutral grid on which festivals happened to fall. It was a religious instrument, administered by the pontifices, that classified every day by what might lawfully be done on it, and the classification was the community’s daily experience of the sacred. The word for the calendar itself gives the game away: fasti, from fas, divine law — the days on which it was fas, religiously lawful, to conduct legal business. A Roman did not need to attend a rite to live inside Roman religion. He needed only to discover, before suing his neighbour, what kind of day it was.

The Letters of the Days

The day-marks, as the surviving inscribed calendars display them, are these. A day marked F (fastus) allowed the praetor’s court to sit and legal formulae to be pronounced. A day marked N (nefastus) forbade it: the courts closed, assemblies did not meet, and public business of the secular kind stopped — not because the day was a festival in every case, but because the day belonged, in the classification, to the gods. A day marked C (comitialis) allowed the people to meet in assembly and vote, when it was not otherwise barred. Days marked NP — the abbreviation’s expansion is debated, conventionally read as marking a public festival day — carried the great fixed festivals in large letters. Split days (endotercisi, EN) were nefasti in the morning and evening, fasti between. Across the columns ran the eight-day market cycle, the nundinae, lettered A to H, so that the calendar is simultaneously a liturgical document and a commercial one. And through the months ran the three fixed points by which Romans named their days: the Kalends (Kalendae, the 1st), the Nones (the 5th, or 7th in March, May, July, and October), and the Ides (the 13th, or 15th in those same months) — every other day counted backwards, inclusively, from the next of these points, so that a Roman date is a countdown to a sacred landmark. The pontifex had originally proclaimed the month’s shape at the Kalends — calare, to call out, gives the Kalends their name — and the calendar’s custody was pontifical long before it was public.

The Calendar Inscriptions as Documents

The calendars survive as objects, and the objects deserve a paragraph of their own, because they are this article’s favourite kind of evidence: the system describing itself, without an author. More than forty Roman calendars or calendar fragments survive from antiquity, in marble and in paint, from Rome and from Italian towns. The oldest and most precious is the Fasti Antiates Maiores, a painted wall-calendar from Antium (modern Anzio), dated to between 84 and 55 BCE — the only surviving calendar from before Caesar’s reform, and therefore the one witness to the Republic’s own year: thirteen columns (the twelve months plus Intercalaris), the days running down each column with their nundinal letters and day-marks, the festivals in red and black, the temple-foundation days (dies natales templorum) entered beside the festivals they belonged to, and, tellingly, only two historical anniversaries in the whole year — the birthday of Rome on 21 April and the black day of the Allia defeat on 18 July. The great marble Fasti Praenestini, set up at Praeneste in the years around the turn of the era and associated with the grammarian Verrius Flaccus, added antiquarian notes to the bare letters — why this festival, why this name — making the calendar a commentary on itself, and it is from such notes, and from Ovid’s poetic expansion of the same material, that much of Section IX’s detail ultimately derives. A calendar inscription is not an illustration of Roman religion. It is a working part of it, posted where business was done.

IX. The Great Festivals in Their Seasons

The fixed public festivals (feriae publicae stativae) numbered, by the late Republic, some forty-five in the oldest surviving calendar and more thereafter, not counting the games (ludi) that grew around many of them into multi-day spectacles. No article can walk the whole year; what follows takes the festivals the brief of Roman religion cannot omit, in their calendar order, and reads each for what it shows about the system — its antiquity, its agricultural root, its handling of the dead, and its genius for turning a rite into a civic institution. Ovid’s Fasti (Frazer–Goold translation, paraphrased except where a formula is given in Latin) and the calendar inscriptions are the guides; Scullard’s Festivals and Ceremonies of the Roman Republic (1981), listed in Section XVI, is the standard modern commentary the selection follows.

13–21 FEB

PARENTALIA — THE DAYS OF THE PARENTS

Nine days on which families honoured their dead at the tombs outside the city (Section XI). Temples closed, magistrates laid aside their insignia, no marriages were celebrated. The sequence closed on 21 February with the Feralia, the public offering to the Manes, and on the 22nd with the Caristia, the feast of the living family reconciled — the dead visited, the household reknit, in that order.

15 FEB

LUPERCALIA — THE WOLF-RITE OF THE PALATINE

Inside the Parentalia’s solemnity falls Rome’s most archaic and exuberant rite. At the Lupercal, the cave at the Palatine’s foot where tradition placed the suckling of Romulus and Remus, the priestly bands of the Luperci sacrificed goats and a dog; two youths of the bands had their foreheads touched with the bloody knife and wiped with wool dipped in milk, and were required to laugh; the goats’ hides were cut into thongs, and the Luperci, nearly naked, ran a course around the Palatine striking at bystanders — women, the sources report, offered their hands and backs to the blows, which were held to promote fertility and ease childbirth. Plutarch’s Roman Questions and his Romulus puzzle over the rite’s elements (paraphrased); no ancient author explains it whole. Its longevity is its own datum: the Lupercalia was still being run, and still drawing a crowd, when Bishop Gelasius protested against Christian participation in it around 494 CE — the episode with which Beard, North, and Price open their history, more than a millennium after the rite’s first attestations.

21 APR

PARILIA — SHEPHERDS’ PURIFICATION AND ROME’S BIRTHDAY

The festival of Pales, power of shepherds and flocks: in the country, sheepfolds were swept and sprinkled, fires of straw and laurel lit and the flock driven — and the shepherds themselves leaping — through the smoke and flame, with prayers, in Ovid’s detailed account (Fasti Book 4, paraphrased), for forgiveness of unwitting offences against woodland and spring. In the city the same date was kept as the dies natalis urbis, the birthday of Rome, founded by the tradition on 21 April 753 BCE — the calendar’s own fusion of the pastoral and the civic past, a shepherds’ rite and an empire’s anniversary sharing a day without embarrassment.

9, 11, 13 MAY

LEMURIA — THE UNQUIET DEAD, EXPELLED

Three non-consecutive nights on which the paterfamilias, rising at midnight, barefoot, cast black beans behind him nine times and clashed bronze to drive the restless dead from the house. The rite is household, not public, and is treated where it belongs, in Section XI — the calendar enters it here because the state calendar marked these days as it marked the great festivals: the household’s terrors had dates too.

9 JUNE

VESTALIA — THE HEARTH OPENS

Vesta’s festival, and for once her temple stood open to the matrons of Rome, who came barefoot to the hearth of the state; the festival belonged also to bakers and millers, whose donkeys — the animals of the mill — were garlanded and rested, hung with loaves. For the festival’s days the sacred storehouse was accessible, and at its close, on 15 June, the temple was swept and the sweepings carried to the Tiber — the calendar noting the day with the letters Q.STER.D.F., when the dung is carried out, it is lawful. Domestic work, animal labour, and the state’s most guarded shrine share one festival, which is the household–state fusion of Section VI seen from the calendar’s side.

17 DEC →

SATURNALIA — THE WORLD TURNED OVER, BRIEFLY

Saturn’s festival began on 17 December — a single day in the old calendar, expanding in practice across the better part of a week by the Empire — with sacrifice at his temple in the Forum, performed, unusually, with uncovered head in the Greek manner (Plutarch’s Roman Questions 11 asks why, and offers the antiquity of the rite and Saturn as father of Truth among his explanations, paraphrased), and a public banquet. Then the inversion for which the festival is famous: the woollen bonds on Saturn’s image loosed, gambling in public licensed, gifts and wax candles exchanged, the felt cap of freedom (pilleus) worn, and slaves dining — in the literary sources’ delighted summary — before, or with, their masters, waited on by them, and licensed to speak freely. The hub’s Olympians article treats the festival as Saturn’s Roman core under his Kronos-identification; here the point is calendrical: the inversion is scheduled. The order of the Roman year contains, by appointment, its own brief suspension — and contains it, which is why the order survives it. What the Saturnalia’s afterlife in European midwinter custom may and may not be claimed to be is handled, carefully, in Section XV.

X. The Household: Lares, Penates, Genius, and the Lararium

Most Roman religious life did not happen in temples, before colleges, or on the state’s great days. It happened at home, daily, in the charge of a man who held no priesthood anyone had conferred: the paterfamilias, the household’s head, who was by virtue of that headship the officiant of its cult, responsible for its rites as he was for its property and its persons — wife, children, slaves, and the gods and dead who belonged to the house. The household (familia, domus) was the state in miniature and knew it: it had its own sacred place, its own divine population, its own calendar of birthdays and observances, its own treasury of rites inherited with the estate, and its own peace to maintain with its own powers. The sections on the public cult have described the city’s half of the system. This section and the next describe the half in which every Roman actually lived.

The Powers of the House

The Lares are the household’s presiding powers: guardians of the place and of the community that inhabits it. The Lar familiaris watched over the house and its land and everyone in it, slave and free — Plautus opens his Aulularia (The Pot of Gold) with the Lar himself speaking the prologue (paraphrased), introducing himself as the family’s guardian across generations, rewarding the household’s devotion and neglecting the neglectful, which is the pax of Section III rewritten at domestic scale. In the standard iconography the Lares are a pair of dancing youths in short tunics, one with a drinking-horn, one with a libation bowl — figures of a prosperity that moves. Beyond the house stood the Lares compitales of the crossroads, where the neighbourhood’s shrine (compitum) gathered the households of a district and where slaves had a recognised ritual role and, at the Compitalia festival, a holiday of their own; and beyond them, the Lares of the city and, under Augustus, the Lares Augusti of Section XIV. The principle scales without changing: a Lar belongs to a place and protects whoever is properly within it.

The Penates (Di Penates) are the gods of the store-chamber — from penus, the household’s stock of food, and penitus, innermost — the powers of the house’s substance and continuity, honoured with Vesta at the hearth, offered a portion of the family’s meals, and carried with the family when it moved, as the Lares of a place could not be. Rome had public Penates too, among the pledges of empire kept in Vesta’s custody, said by the tradition to have come from Troy with Aeneas (paraphrased) — the state, once again, keeping a household cult of its own at larger scale. The Genius completes the domestic triad: the generative, life-carrying power of a man, born with him and dying with him, honoured above all on his birthday with wine, incense, and cake — offerings without blood, as befitted a power of life — and sworn by, in oaths, as a man’s innermost self made divine. A woman’s corresponding power was her Juno. In the household the Genius that mattered publicly was the paterfamilias’s own: his birthday was the house’s festival, his Genius the recipient of its vows, and dependents and slaves swore by it and kept its day — a domestic fact whose political scaling, when the paterfamilias in question was the emperor, is the whole subject of Section XIV.

The Lararium and the Day’s Rites

The cult’s furniture was the lararium: the household shrine, ranging from a painted niche in a Pompeian corridor — the Genius in his toga, head covered, sacrificing between the two dancing Lares, a serpent below approaching an altar, in the famous composition of the House of the Vettii and dozens of humbler copies — to a small temple-fronted aedicula with bronze figures of Lares, Penates, Genius, and the family’s chosen gods together. Before it the day’s religion was done: morning salutation of the Lares, offerings of meal, wine, incense, and garlands on the Kalends, Nones, and Ides of each month and on the house’s own days — birthdays, comings of age, marriages, returns from journeys — and the placing of a portion for the Penates at meals. Cato’s On Agriculture, the oldest prose manual Rome produced, gives the farm version with a manager’s precision (paraphrased): the farm’s mistress (vilica) is to keep the hearth clean and garlanded on Kalends, Nones, and Ides and offer prayer to the Lar familiaris for abundance — and is forbidden, in the same breath, to consult any diviner or enchantress on her own account. Domestic religion in Cato is not an atmosphere. It is a roster of duties, assigned, dated, and supervised, exactly as Section III’s system predicts.

XI. Birth, Marriage, Death: The Di Manes and the Family Dead

The household’s gods included its dead. The di manes — the divine Manes, the honoured dead of the family taken together — received cult at the tomb and at home, and the standard dedication of Roman tombstones across the empire, Dis Manibus, “to the divine Manes” of the named dead, is among the most repeated religious formulae in Latin epigraphy. The conception is collective and dutiful rather than speculative: the Manes are the family’s dead as a body, owed remembrance, offerings, and the observance of their days, and dangerous — as all owed and unpaid powers are dangerous in this system — chiefly when neglected. Beside them the tradition distinguished the unquiet dead: lemures or larvae, the restless, unfed, or unburied shades who moved abroad on their nights and had to be expelled rather than entertained. Roman religion’s dead, like its gods, divide by function and by the state of their accounts with the living.

The Rites of Passage

A Roman life was punctuated by household rites at its thresholds, and the antiquarian tradition — Plutarch’s Roman Questions opens with a run of them — preserves their texture. On the ninth day after a boy’s birth (the eighth for a girl), the dies lustricus, the child was purified and named, and received the bulla, the amulet worn through childhood. The boy’s coming of age was marked by the laying aside of the bulla and the striped toga of childhood (toga praetexta) and the assumption, with procession to the Forum and sacrifice on the Capitol, of the plain white toga virilis — the Liberalia of 17 March being the favoured date, which is why that festival of Liber belongs in this section as much as in Section IX. Marriage, in its most archaic and religiously weighty form, confarreatio — required for the great flamines’ marriages and for certain priesthoods’ eligibility — was celebrated before the pontifex maximus and the flamen Dialis with a cake of spelt (far) offered to Jupiter Farreus, amid a fixed liturgy of auspices, sacrifice, and formulae; Plutarch’s first Roman Questions ask why the bride touches fire and water (Question 1) and why five torches, neither more nor less, are lit at the wedding (Question 2), and offer, for each, a bouquet of rival explanations — elemental, purificatory, numerological — which this article reports as Plutarch’s enquiries, not as the rite’s meaning (paraphrased throughout). The bride was received into her new household’s cult as the child had been into the old: Roman marriage, in its sacred form, is an adoption into a set of rites.

Death, the Funeral, and the Parentalia

Death made the household ritually unclean (familia funesta) until the funeral and its purifications were complete. The funeral of a person of standing was a public procession — the pompa funebris — in which the family’s ancestors themselves attended, their wax portrait-masks (imagines) carried by actors in the procession, so that the dead man joined, before the city’s eyes, the company he was about to enter; Polybius’s famous description (paraphrased) treats the custom as a school of Roman virtue, and the masks, kept in cupboards in the atrium with their labels of office, were the household’s archive of itself. Burial or cremation took place outside the sacred boundary, in the tomb-lined roads every traveller into Rome still walks; a period of mourning and a ninth-day rite and meal (cena novendialis) closed the sequence, and a sow sacrificed to Ceres and the Lares of the dead purified the household and the heir. Thereafter the dead person’s claim was calendrical: the Parentalia of Section IX, 13–21 February, when families carried to the tombs offerings whose modesty Ovid insists on in Fasti Book 2 (paraphrased) — a tile wreathed with garlands, scattered grain, a little salt, bread soaked in wine, loose violets; the Manes, the passage’s burden is, ask for devotion, not expense — and the Feralia of 21 February closed the season with the public offering. A Roman’s ancestors were not memories. They were creditors of small, regular, dated payments, and the payments were the relationship.

The Lemuria: The Other Side of the Dead

Against the Parentalia’s February tenderness stands the Lemuria of 9, 11, and 13 May, and the contrast is the household system’s whole doctrine of the dead in two festivals. The Lemuria’s rite, as Ovid describes it, belongs to the paterfamilias alone, at midnight, in a house asleep. He rises, making with thumb and fingers the sign against ghosts, walks barefoot, washes his hands in spring water, and takes black beans into his mouth; then, without looking back, he throws the beans behind him, saying nine times the formula Ovid preserves in Latin: haec ego mitto; his redimo meque meosque fabis — in paraphrase, “these I send; with these beans I redeem me and mine” — the shades, it is understood, gathering the beans behind his averted back. He touches water again, clashes bronze vessels together, and nine times bids the powers leave, in the second formula: manes exite paterni — in paraphrase, “ancestral shades, depart.” Only then may he look round. Beans, bronze, bare feet, averted eyes, nines: the rite is archaic in texture in a way Ovid himself remarks, and its logic is Section III’s logic inverted — not the maintenance of a peace but the termination of a visitation, performed exactly, by the one person entitled to perform it, on the nights the calendar appoints. That the state calendar marks the Lemuria days, and that temples closed and no marriages were made during them, shows the household’s rite and the city’s system interlocking at the level of the date itself.

XII. The Temple System: The Capitoline Triad, Vows, Triumphs, and Evocatio

The Capitoline Triad: The State’s Address

At the system’s summit stood the temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus Capitolinus — Jupiter Best and Greatest — on the Capitoline Hill, shared in three parallel cellae with Juno Regina and Minerva: the Capitoline Triad. Vowed by the kings, in the tradition, and dedicated in the first year of the Republic — 509 BCE by the conventional chronology — the temple was the ceremonial centre of the Roman state for a thousand years: consuls took office and made their vows there, triumphs ended there (below), the Sibylline Books lay in its vaults (Section VII), Terminus kept his unroofed shrine within its precinct (Section II), and its pediment and cult statues — the great Jupiter in terracotta by the Etruscan craftsman Vulca of Veii, in the tradition’s account (paraphrased) — embodied the young Republic’s debt to Etruscan workmanship. The triad itself is Rome’s characteristic renovation: behind Jupiter–Juno–Minerva stands the Etruscan grouping of Tinia, Uni, and Menrva, and behind that, the older Italic triad of Jupiter, Mars, and Quirinus whom the major flamines served (Section VI) — three arrangements, three eras of the city, superimposed on one hill. Colonies and municipalities across the empire reproduced the Capitoline temple as a Capitolium: to plant a Capitolium was to plant Rome’s religious address in provincial soil, a fact Section XIV’s provincial altars extend.

Vows and Dedications: The System’s Engine

The vow (votum) is the mechanism that built the temple system, and its form is contractual in the strict sense. A commander in battle, a magistrate in plague or crisis, the Senate on the state’s behalf, undertook a conditional obligation in set words before witnesses: if the god granted the thing asked — victory, deliverance, the city’s safety — the vow-maker would render the thing promised: a temple, games, an altar, a tithe of the spoil, a golden offering of stated weight. The discharge (voti solutio) was as formal as the undertaking, and dedications across the empire close with the abbreviation that is Roman piety’s signature: v.s.l.m. — votum solvit libens merito, in paraphrase, “paid the vow, gladly, deservedly.” Public vows were renewed annually for the state’s and later the emperor’s safety (vota publica, solemnly undertaken on 3 January under the Empire, when the Arval protocols of Section IV duly record them); great quinquennial and decennial vows punctuated reigns. The vow’s theology is exact and unembarrassed: it creates a debt, the god’s performance is inferred from the event, and the debt is paid in stone, spectacle, or gold, publicly, so that the payment itself advertises the god’s credit and the payer’s pietas in one act. Temples founded in battle were routinely built from the spoil (ex manubiis) — the defeated enemy, by a neat theological accounting, paid for the shrine of the god who had defeated him.

The Triumph: A Religious Act in Armour

The triumph was a procession, a spectacle, and — in its own understanding — a rite: the victorious general, granted the honour by the Senate, entered the city in procession with his spoil, his captives, and his army, and ascended the Capitol to lay his laurel in the lap of Jupiter Optimus Maximus and sacrifice white oxen in discharge of the vows of his campaign. For that day the triumphator wore the regalia kept in Jupiter’s temple — the embroidered tunic and purple toga of the god’s own statue — rode in a four-horse chariot, had his face, in the older accounts (paraphrased), painted red like the terracotta Jupiter’s, and heard, by the famous tradition, a slave behind him repeating the reminder of his mortality while the crowd shouted its acclaim. Modern scholarship debates the details and the degree to which the triumphator was, for the day, Jupiter’s living image; Beard’s study of the triumph, listed in Section XVI, is the essential modern treatment and is properly sceptical of the tradition’s neater claims. What is not in doubt is the procession’s destination and grammar: a Roman victory was not complete until it had been carried, in order, through the city to the god who had granted it, and rendered to him in sacrifice and dedication. The triumph is the vow of the section above, discharged at the scale of the state, with an army for a choir.

Evocatio: Calling the Enemy’s God Out

The most purely Roman rite in this article is evocatio, the “calling out” of an enemy city’s tutelary god before the assault. The Roman commander, before a besieged city, formally invited its god to abandon it and come to Rome, promising a temple and cult there equal or greater than the god’s present honours; the city, deprived of its divine inhabitant, could then be taken without sacrilege, and its god arrived at Rome not as spoil but as an immigrant of rank. The classic case is Juno Regina from Veii in 396 BCE. Livy’s account (5.21–22, paraphrased) has the dictator Camillus, having taken the auspices before the final assault, pray to Juno Regina, who then dwelt at Veii, asking her to follow the victors to Rome, where a temple worthy of her greatness would receive her; after the city fell, chosen young soldiers, washed and in white, entered her temple to move the statue and hesitated to touch it, until one of them — inspired, or joking, Livy leaves both possibilities open — asked the goddess whether she wished to go to Rome, and the statue was understood to nod assent, and in a later version of the tale, to speak it. The image was carried to the Aventine, where Camillus dedicated her temple in 392 BCE. The story’s embroidery is Livy’s and later tradition’s; the institution is documentary, with the Aventine temple and its dedication-day in the calendar to attest it, and a later evocatio formula for Carthage preserved, centuries on, by Macrobius (Saturnalia Book 3, paraphrased) — addressed, prudently, to “whatever god or goddess” protected the place, since the tutelary name was itself a secret Rome guarded for its own city with the same care. Evocatio states, in ritual form, the premise of the whole of Section XIII: gods can move, cults can be transferred, and Rome’s part in the transaction is to make the offer, keep the promise, and keep the records.

XIII. Imported Gods and the State’s Management: Magna Mater, the Bacchanalia, Isis

Rome’s reputation for absorbing every god it met is deserved and, as stated, misleading. Rome absorbed gods the way it absorbed peoples: by grant, on terms, with the grant recorded and the terms enforceable. A foreign cult could arrive by invitation of the state, on the prescription of the Sibylline Books, and take its place inside the system with a temple, a festival in the calendar, and a priesthood under Roman supervision; or it could arrive uninvited, spread through private association, and meet the system’s other face — investigation, decree, and demolition. The three cases of this section, taken in chronological order, are the pattern’s three positions: admission with containment (Magna Mater, 204 BCE), suppression with licensing (the Bacchanalia, 186 BCE), and a century of expulsions ending in a temple (Isis). The hub’s Mysteries article treats these cults’ Greek side — their rites, secrecy, and promises to initiates. This section treats their Roman civic handling: what the Senate did about them, and what the doing reveals.

204 BCE: The Great Mother Arrives by Decree

In 205 BCE, in the darkest years of the Hannibalic war, a shower of stones — a prodigy of the classic kind — sent the Senate to the Sibylline Books, and the Books, as Livy reports (29.10–14, paraphrased), answered with a verse to the effect that a foreign invader could be driven from Italy if the Idaean Mother of the Gods were brought to Rome from Pessinus in Asia Minor. Delphi, consulted in parallel, concurred. An embassy fetched the goddess — whose presence travelled in a sacred black stone, unworked, the size to be held in a hand, by the later descriptions — and in April 204 BCE she was received at Ostia by a delegation the sources turn into a set-piece of Roman self-presentation: the Senate’s chosen “best man” of the state, Publius Cornelius Scipio Nasica, met the ship, and the matrons of Rome passed the goddess hand to hand up the Tiber road into the city, to be lodged in the Temple of Victory on the Palatine until her own temple beside it was dedicated in 191 BCE. Her festival, the Megalensia (4–10 April), entered the calendar with games.

Then the containment, which is the section’s real subject. The goddess — Magna Mater deorum Idaea, the Great Idaean Mother of the Gods — was a state cult from her first Roman day, and her cult’s exotic personnel were kept at a juridical distance from the citizen body: her Phrygian priests, the galli, self-castrated ecstatics who processed with her image, begged, and danced to drum and pipe, were foreigners; a Roman citizen might not become a gallus, might not process in the Phrygian manner, and the goddess’s Roman priesthood proper was held by a quindecimvir-appointed sacerdos of citizen standing, while her April games were given by Roman magistrates in Roman form. Dionysius of Halicarnassus, describing the arrangement a century and a half later (paraphrased), remarks on it with an administrator’s approval: the Romans, he says in effect, took the goddess and her mythic honours into the state, and kept the ecstatic apparatus foreign, at arm’s length, by law. Both halves of the sentence are the policy. Rome did not dilute the import; it licensed the import and quarantined its priesthood — the first fully documented instance of a technique Section XIV will show the Empire using at continental scale.

186 BCE: The Bacchanalia — Suppression as Licensing

Eighteen years later the same Senate met the opposite case: a cult of Bacchus — the Roman form of the Dionysian worship whose Greek career the Mysteries article follows — that had spread through Italy without invitation, organised in private associations (coniurationes in the hostile vocabulary), meeting at night, initiating men and women together across the lines of age, class, and household authority, bound by oath, holding common funds, and answerable to its own priests and masters rather than to any magistrate. Livy’s account (Book 39.8–19) — written two centuries later, moralising, novelistic in its set-pieces of the courtesan-informant Hispala and the young Aebutius, and reporting crimes from forgery and perjury to ritual murder — must be read, as the scholarship in Section XVI insists, with its genre in view: it is a senatorial morality tale about a real senatorial act. The act itself does not depend on Livy. It survives on bronze.

The senatus consultum de Bacchanalibus, the Senate’s decree on the Bacchanalia, was issued in October 186 BCE and circulated to Rome’s Italian allies; the copy that survives — a bronze tablet found at Tiriolo in Calabria in 1640, now in Vienna — is the text as posted in the allied district of the ager Teuranus. Its terms, in the standard translation of the inscription (the version of Nina E. Weston, as reproduced in the scholarly literature and paraphrased here except for the one clause quoted), are a licensing regime, not a prohibition of Bacchus. In Weston’s translation the decree opens its operative part by resolving, in paraphrase of the surrounding clauses and in her wording for the central one, that “no one of them is to possess a place where the festivals of Bacchus are celebrated” without authorisation — and then specifies the authorisation: anyone claiming a religious necessity must apply in Rome to the urban praetor, and the Senate, with not fewer than a hundred members present, must decide. No man might be a priest of the cult; no one, man or woman, might be its master or officer; no common fund might be held; no mutual oaths, vows, pledges, or promises might be exchanged; no rites might be performed in secret, nor in public or private or outside the city, without the same authorisation; and no gathering for the rites might exceed five persons in all — no more than two men and three women — without it. Existing Bacchic shrines were to be dismantled within ten days of the tablet’s receipt, saving anything genuinely sacred in them. Violation was a capital matter. The decree was to be proclaimed in assembly on three successive market days and engraved on bronze and fixed where it could most easily be read.

// WHAT THE DECREE DOES AND DOES NOT SAY //

It does not ban the worship of Bacchus, dissolve a religion, or name a heresy. It abolishes an organisation — officers, funds, oaths, secrecy, size — and subjects the residue to case-by-case senatorial licence. Livy’s seven thousand arrests and his catalogue of crimes are Livy’s, two centuries after the event, and modern scholarship (Gruen; North; Beard, North, and Price, in Section XVI) reads the affair primarily as the Senate asserting, after the Hannibalic war, its authority over Italy’s associations and over any allegiance that bypassed household and magistrate. The bronze, not the narrative, is the evidence for the terms; the narrative is the evidence for how Rome preferred to remember them.

The pattern’s third case ran for a century. Isis — whose Egyptian substance the archive treats in its Isis article and whose Roman-era mystery form the Mysteries article closes with, in Apuleius — reached Italy through the ports in the second and first centuries BCE and built, by private devotion, altars and temples in Rome itself. The Senate’s response, across two generations, was demolition by decree: her altars on the Capitol destroyed in 59 or 58 BCE (the tradition, preserved through Varro in Tertullian’s hostile summary, itself reports a popular protest and a restoration); a decree of 53 BCE against privately built temples of Isis and Serapis, with the cult confined outside the pomerium; the consul Lucius Aemilius Paullus, in 50 BCE, taking the axe to a temple door himself when the workmen hung back, in Valerius Maximus’s anecdote (paraphrased); a further demolition after an ill-omened incident in 48 BCE. And then the reversal that proves the rule: in 43 BCE the triumvirs — Antony, Octavian, and Lepidus, the state’s masters of the hour — themselves voted a temple to Isis and Serapis. Augustus, after his war with Egypt’s queen, redrew the line rather than renewing the bans: Egyptian rites were excluded from within the pomerium in 28 BCE, and later from a wider radius, while the temples outside it were maintained. Under Tiberius in 19 CE a scandal in the Roman temple — Josephus’s story (paraphrased) of the matron Paulina, deceived by priests bribed by the equestrian Decius Mundus, who posed as the god Anubis — brought the regime’s full violence down on the cult in the city: priests crucified, the temple demolished, the cult image thrown into the Tiber, and, in Tacitus’s and Suetonius’s notices of the same measures, four thousand freedmen of the Egyptian and Jewish rites deported to Sardinia. Josephus is the sole source for the Paulina story’s details, and they are flagged here as his; the expulsion itself is multiply attested. Within a generation the cult was back; Caligula built Isis a great temple in the Campus Martius — the Iseum Campense, inside the city the goddess had been expelled from for a hundred years — and the Flavian emperors, whose dynasty’s founder had spent the night before his triumph, in Suetonius’s account (paraphrased), in the temple of Isis, made her cult a fixture of the capital. Expulsion, repeatedly, was not the opposite of admission. It was the long negotiation that preceded it, on the state’s terms, at the state’s pace.

XIV. The Imperial Cult: Genius Augusti, Divus Julius, and the Altars of the Provinces

The worship of emperors is the part of Roman religion most often described in a vocabulary — flattery, propaganda, megalomania — that prevents it being understood. This section states it in the system’s own terms, without anachronism in either direction: the imperial cult was neither a cynical fraud upon the credulous nor a spontaneous theology of the ruler’s godhead. It was the extension, by the ordinary instruments of Sections X–XIII — genius, vow, altar, priesthood, festival, provincial decree — of Roman cult to the one household that now contained the state. Every element of it existed before it. What was new was the address.

Divus Julius: The Precedent, Carefully Made

The precedent was made, characteristically, by procedure. Julius Caesar received in his lifetime honours that approached the divine — a temple voted to his Clemency, a flamen designated for his cult, his image carried among the gods in procession — and after his assassination the comet that appeared during the games of July 44 BCE was read, and was allowed by his heir to be read, as his soul received among the gods: the sidus Iulium, the Julian star, set on his statues’ brows. In 42 BCE the triumvirs and Senate formally consecrated him Divus Julius, a deified dead man by act of state, and his temple in the Forum — vowed at Philippi, dedicated by Augustus in 29 BCE on the site of his cremation — gave the new category an address. The category is the point: divus is not deus. A divus is a mortal who has been, by senatorial decree after death and by the testimony, under the developed rite, of a witness to his ascent, enrolled among the divine — an honour Rome had always possessed in germ for its founders (Romulus as Quirinus is its archaic type) and now routinised for its rulers. Augustus, in his lifetime, was not a god at Rome. He was the son of a god — divi filius, the title on his coinage from the start of his career — which is a different, and carefully lesser, and politically far more useful, thing.

Genius Augusti: The Household Form of Loyalty

What was worshipped at Rome in Augustus’s lifetime was his Genius — and Section X has already supplied the grammar. Every paterfamilias’s Genius received his household’s vows and oaths; the emperor was paterfamilias of the Roman household writ largest — pater patriae, father of the fatherland, by senatorial grant in 2 BCE — and the cult of his Genius was the domestic rite at imperial scale. From 30 BCE, by senatorial decree, a libation to his Genius belonged at public and private banquets; oaths were sworn by it; and in 7 BCE, when Augustus reorganised the city’s neighbourhoods (vici), the crossroads shrines of the Lares Compitales were refounded as shrines of the Lares Augusti, with the Genius Augusti set between the Lares in the standard image, and the neighbourhood officers (vicomagistri), largely freedmen, as their priests — men for whom the imperial crossroads cult was a genuine, and genuinely honourable, civic career open nowhere else to their order. At the hearths of the empire’s capital, loyalty to the emperor was not an opinion to be held. It was a libation to be poured, by neighbours, at the corner shrine, on the festival day — Section III’s orthopraxy in its most successful political form. To ask whether the pourers “believed” Augustus divine is to import Section I’s rejected question; the rite asked for the pouring.

Rome and Augustus: The Provincial Settlement

In the provinces the settlement was more direct, and its forms were set, with characteristic precision, by permission. In 29 BCE, Cassius Dio reports (51.20, paraphrased), Augustus authorised the provincials of Asia and Bithynia — Greek cities with a long Hellenistic tradition of ruler cult behind them — to consecrate precincts to Rome and Augustus at Pergamum and Nicomedia, while Roman citizens resident in those provinces were directed to precincts of Rome and Divus Julius at Ephesus and Nicaea: the living emperor worshipped, in the East, by non-citizens, in company with the personified Rome, and not alone; citizens worshipped the deified predecessor instead. The distinction — citizen and provincial, living and deified, alone and paired with Roma — was maintained with legal care because it was the theology: a graded system in which the emperor’s divinity was real in the forms in which it was offered, and the forms differed by the worshipper’s status and place. Simon Price’s Rituals and Power (1984), the standard study of the cult in Asia Minor listed in Section XVI, established the reading this article follows: the imperial cult was the language in which Greek cities represented the emperor’s power to themselves, negotiated with it, and competed for his favour — a cognitive system, not a loyalty test imposed on the unwilling, and its festivals, priesthoods, and temples were the cities’ own most prestigious civic honours, fought over accordingly.

LUGDUNUM: THE ALTAR OF THE THREE GAULS

Drusus dedicates the great altar of Rome and Augustus at Lugdunum (Lyon), at the confluence of Rhône and Saône, for the sixty peoples of the Three Gauls — see the text below. The altar’s dedication day, 1 August, was chosen, the tradition notes, with the calendar in mind: it was also the day of the dedication, in 30 BCE, of an altar to Augustus at Alexandria’s taking — the imperial year acquiring its feasts.

14 CE

DIVUS AUGUSTUS

Augustus dies at Nola, 19 August 14 CE; the Senate consecrates him Divus Augustus, a senator attesting his ascent; his temple in Rome is vowed, his flamen and his priestly sodales Augustales created. Tiberius, succeeding, is divi filius in his turn.

The western provinces received the cult in the form Lugdunum set. At the confluence of the Rhône and the Saône, where Augustus’s stepson Drusus dedicated the altar of Rome and Augustus in 12 BCE (some accounts give the formal dedication to 10 BCE, the year of Claudius’s birth at Lugdunum, and the discrepancy is noted here as the sources leave it), the sixty peoples of the Three Gauls — Aquitania, Lugdunensis, and Belgica — met annually in a federal council (concilium), elected the altar’s high priest (sacerdos Romae et Augusti) from among their own leading men — the first was the Aeduan Gaius Julius Vercondaridubnus, a Gallic noble with a Roman name, which is the settlement in a single person — sacrificed at the great altar whose image, flanked by winged Victories on tall columns and inscribed with the peoples’ names, the Lugdunum mint’s coinage spread across the West, and transacted the provinces’ business with the emperor in the cult’s frame: embassies of praise and complaint alike went out from the council that sacrificed together. Strabo’s description (paraphrased) of the precinct, the altar, and the inscribed sixty names is the geographer’s witness to the institution in its first generation. Lugdunum’s pattern — a provincial altar, an annual council, a priesthood that crowned a local career — was reproduced from Britain to Arabia, with municipal flamines and seviri Augustales (the freedmen’s board of Section X’s crossroads writ municipal) filling its lower storeys. Duncan Fishwick’s multi-volume The Imperial Cult in the Latin West, listed in Section XVI, is the documentary foundation for the western material.

The cult’s later career — Claudius deified by a Senate his successor’s circle mocked in Seneca’s pumpkin-satire, the Apocolocyntosis (paraphrased); Vespasian, on Suetonius’s deathbed report (paraphrased), joking that he thought he was becoming a god; the good emperors consecrated in sequence, the bad refused or rescinded; the temple of the Deified Augustus restored, the sodales serving new divi as they accrued — belongs to the Empire’s general history and is followed here only to its consequence for the system. By the third century the imperial cult was the empire’s one universal cult: the single rite in which every community of the Roman world participated in the same form, and therefore — the hinge on which Section XV turns — the rite whose refusal could identify, as sacrifice to the gods of Rome generally could, those who stood outside the Roman peace. When Decius in 250 CE required sacrifice of all inhabitants, with certificates (libelli) issued to those who complied, the machinery was the imperial cult’s machinery turned universal and compulsory — and the Christians who refused it, and the Christians who lapsed under it and then disputed their readmission, discovered what Section III stated: Rome policed acts, and the act, now, was the empire’s definition of belonging. The persecution’s logic is unintelligible without this section’s cult. So is its failure.

XV. Bans, Survivals, and the Afterlife in the European Year

Roman religion did not end on a date, and this section will not pretend one. It contracted, over the fourth and fifth centuries CE, under a sequence of imperial laws whose cumulative effect was to dismantle the public system piece by piece — funding first, then sacrifice, then the temples, then the priesthoods — while private and rural practice, and the calendar that carried the old festivals, persisted well beyond the laws that abolished their cult. The hub’s timeline on the Greco-Roman index enters the process at its most famous single moment, 391 CE, and the entry should be read with this section’s longer sequence around it.

The Dismantling, 341–435 CE

The sequence, in its load-bearing dates, is this. Constantius II’s laws of 341 and 356 CE forbade sacrifice and ordered temples closed — measures whose enforcement the repeated re-enactments themselves call in question, as Section XIII’s Isis demolitions were called in question by their repetition. Julian’s brief reign (361–363) attempted a restoration — temples reopened, sacrifices resumed, a pagan priestly hierarchy organised in conscious parallel to the Church’s — and demonstrated, by its failure to outlive him, how far the system’s social base had already shifted. In 382 Gratian struck at the funding: the state subsidies of Rome’s public cults were withdrawn, the revenues of the priestly colleges and the Vestals confiscated, the Altar of Victory removed from the Senate house — provoking Symmachus’s famous plea for its restoration in 384, and Ambrose’s victorious refusal — and Gratian declined, the tradition reports, the robe and title of pontifex maximus as unlawful for a Christian, breaking the line from Augustus. Then, in 391–392, the laws of Theodosius I (preserved in the Theodosian Code, Book 16, title 10, paraphrased) forbade sacrifice in public and in private, forbade entry to temples for worship, and attached penalties to officials who connived; the Serapeum of Alexandria was destroyed in the same season, in the violence the hub’s timeline records under 391 CE. In 394 the Vestals were dissolved and Vesta’s fire extinguished (Section VI). In 435 Theodosius II ordered the destruction or conversion of the temples that remained. A public religion whose rites were public acts, funded by the state and performed by its magistrates, could be — and was — abolished by the state’s changing its mind: the system’s civic nature, its strength for a thousand years, was the precise mechanism of its end.

// LAW IS NOT PRACTICE //

The bans’ repetition is the evidence of their limits. Sacrifice was forbidden in 341, 356, 391, and again thereafter because it continued; the Lupercalia was still run at Rome, with Christian spectators, in the 490s (Section IX); Libanius’s plea For the Temples (paraphrased) describes rural shrines still standing, and monks destroying them, in the 380s; and Augustine’s sermons complain of Kalends revels and festival customs among his own baptised congregations a generation after Theodosius. What ended on schedule was the public system — colleges, subsidies, temples, the calendar’s official observance. What ended slowly, unevenly, and in places never entirely was practice. The archive states both halves, because each half alone is a falsification.

What Survives, Stated Carefully

Survival claims about Roman religion are a genre of their own, and most of them are too strong. This article makes three, each at the strength its evidence supports. First, the calendar. The Julian year — Caesar’s reform of 46–45 BCE, corrected under Gregory XIII in 1582 in its leap-year rule and its accumulated drift — is the year Europe and then the world kept: its month-lengths, its month-names (Janus’s January, Mars’s March, Juno’s June among them, whatever the debated etymologies of others), its leap day, and its numbering of days are Roman administrative survivals of the most literal kind. This is not an influence. It is the same instrument, repaired once, in continuous use. Second, festival dates in the Christian calendar. That the Church’s year settled some of its feasts in calendrical conversation with Rome’s is certain at the level of the calendar’s general shaping — a missionary church in a Roman world dated its observances by the Roman year it lived in. Specific derivations must each be argued, and the famous ones are weaker than their reputation: the date of Christmas, 25 December, coincides with the Natalis Solis Invicti instituted by Aurelian in 274 CE, and the hypothesis that the Christian feast was set to supplant the solar one is old and plausible — and contested by the rival “calculation” hypothesis, which derives the date from ancient computations of the Annunciation’s date, independent of the solar feast. This article reports the coincidence and both hypotheses, and adopts neither. The Saturnalia-to-Christmas derivation beloved of popular writing — gifts, greenery, feasting, therefore descent — is weaker still: midwinter festivity is a feature of every northern calendar, resemblance is not transmission, and no ancient source states the borrowing. It is recorded here as a modern claim, not as a finding. Third, the words and the forms. Pontifex among the Pope’s titles, the basilica’s migration from law-court to church, incense and processions and votive candles in Mediterranean Catholic practice, the genius of a place become, in folk custom, its patron — these are real continuities of form within a changed religion, of the kind Beard, North, and Price’s closing chapters trace, and they are stated here as continuities of form, not of cult: no one who lights a votive candle is keeping a vow to Vesta, and the archive does not say otherwise.

The archive’s sequence places Roman religion between two longer stories. Behind it stands the world of the Before Kemet hub and the Egyptology division, whose gods Rome’s Isis carried into the Campus Martius; ahead of it stands the transmission the hub’s Transmission article follows for alchemy — the same late-antique centuries in which the temples were closing were carrying Greek learning, through Syriac and Arabic, toward the Latin door of 1144. Roman religion’s own transmission was of a different kind: not texts through languages, but a calendar through centuries, a vocabulary through a church, and a set of inscribed stones through the spolia of later buildings into the museums where Sections VIII and XIII found them. The system that ran on exact performance is gone. The year it built is still running.
