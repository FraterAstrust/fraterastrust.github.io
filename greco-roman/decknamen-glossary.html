I. What a Deckname Is — and Is Not

A Deckname — German for “cover-name”, plural Decknamen — is a substitute name for a substance. Where the plain word would tell any reader exactly what to weigh out, the cover-name tells only the reader who already holds the key: a god's name for a metal, an animal product for a mineral, a colour, a place, a riddle of resemblance. The recipe that results is not nonsense and was never meant to be. It is a working instruction with the ingredient list encrypted — legible in the workshop that shared the code, opaque on the open market, and safe, or safer, in the hands of anyone who might use the knowledge badly or prosecute its owner for possessing it.

The word itself is modern scholarship's, not antiquity's. It entered the literature through the German study of Arabic alchemy — Julius Ruska and Eilhard Wiedemann's “Alchemistische Decknamen” (1924) catalogued the cover-name lists preserved in Arabic manuscripts, twenty-four names apiece for iron, copper, tin, lead, mercury, and sal ammoniac, a list E. J. Holmyard showed in Nature (1926) to have a near-twin in a British Museum manuscript that adds sulphur to the set. From there the term was taken up for the whole Western tradition. Lawrence Principe's account, in The Secrets of Alchemy (2013) and in his earlier work on Boyle's secrecy, is the one this archive follows: cover-names are, in the main, practical secrecy — the secrecy of the trade secret, the dangerous process, and the initiatory community — rather than mystification for its own sake. William Newman pressed the complementary caution, in “Decknamen or Pseudochemical Language?” (1996): not every strange word is a code, and some alchemical language is genuinely descriptive, metaphorical, or confused on its own terms. Sarah Lang's recent survey of the lexicography ( Ambix, 2022) states the resulting discipline exactly: Decknamen are context-sensitive cover-words, not technical terms with one fixed meaning, and no dictionary — including this one — can abolish that sensitivity. It can only record it honestly.

What a Deckname is not is equally important, and this hub draws the line in a companion article. Allegory hides a process inside a story: a king and queen marry, die, and rise; a dragon is slain; a priest is dismembered in a vessel. The narrative is the cover, and what it covers is a sequence of operations. A Deckname hides a substance inside a word: one noun stands for another noun, and what it covers is an ingredient. The two devices constantly travel together — an allegory will be stocked with cover-named substances, and a cover-name may be a miniature allegory in itself — but they fail differently and are decoded differently, and confusing them produces the worst readings in the literature. The story-side of the distinction is treated in Alchemical Allegory; this glossary treats the word-side only.

A DECKNAME

One substance, deliberately renamed: “moon foam”, “the ever-fugitive”, “human excrement”. Decoded by a gloss, a lexicon, or a context tight enough to leave one possible ingredient.

AN ALLEGORY

One process, deliberately narrated: a marriage, a death, a resurrection in the vessel. Decoded by matching the story's sequence to an operation's sequence. See Alchemical Allegory.

A PLAIN TECHNICAL TERM

One substance or operation, openly named: styptēria (alum), oxos (vinegar), leukōsis (whitening). Much of the workshop vocabulary was never covered at all — the papyri prove it.

A TRADE NAME

A name that looks covered but is commercial: a place, a grade, a preparation — “cadmia of Thrace”, “lamellose alum”, “salt of Cappadocia”. Geography here is specification, not code.

Three motives for covering a name recur in the evidence, and an entry in this glossary may owe its existence to any of them. Commerce: a process that doubles gold-coloured alloy or fixes a fast purple is worth money, and its ingredient list is the asset. Safety — the practitioner's and the public's: the covered substances of this glossary are disproportionately poisons (mercury, the arsenicals, lead compounds, corrosive vitriols), and a literature that names them in riddles restricts casual imitation, whatever else it does. Initiation: the art presented itself, from the Physika kai Mystika onward, as mystika — things disclosed to initiates — and a vocabulary that must be taught face to face is a membership test built into the text. None of the three motives requires the others, and the reader should resist the romantic inference that a covered name signals a spiritual meaning. In this corpus, a covered name usually signals a substance somebody did not want strangers to identify. That is quite interesting enough.

// HAZARD NOTE — DECODED IS NOT LICENSED //

Many referents in this glossary — mercury and its vapour, orpiment, realgar, cinnabar, white lead, litharge, the vitriols — are toxic, several of them cumulatively and severely. The glossary decodes historical names so that historical texts can be read. It is a reading tool, not a set of preparations, and no identification given here should be treated as an invitation to obtain or heat the substance named.

II. The Evidence Base: Lexica, Commentaries, and Papyri

This glossary is not a free reconstruction. The Greek alchemical corpus is unusual among ancient technical literatures in that it preserves its own dictionaries, and the entries below are drawn from those witnesses first, from workshop documents second, and from modern identification last — with the Evidence column of every entry stating which of the three is doing the work.

1. The Corpus's Own Lexica

The oldest great manuscript of the corpus, the Marcianus graecus 299 (late 10th–early 11th century, Venice), opens with lists of the signs of the science — the symbols and abbreviations of the art — and contains, at folios 131r–136r, an alphabetical Lexicon on the Making of Gold (Lexikon kata stoicheion tēs chrysopoiias) of more than two hundred entries. Matteo Martelli's study of the alchemical lexica (2021) is the current guide: the Byzantine lexicon, as he puts it in paraphrase, condenses centuries of exegetical effort, recording identifications and equivalences that can often be traced back into the surviving treatises themselves — as when it equates kadmeia with magnēsia, or aphroselēnon (“moon foam”) with kōmaris and “light stone”. The sign-lists printed by Berthelot and Ruelle in the first volume of the Collection des anciens alchimistes grecs (1887–88) belong to the same apparatus: they give the planetary names of the metals (Section III), the grades of sulphur, and glosses — krokos, for instance, as both saffron and egg yolk — on which several entries below rest. Martelli and Stefano Valente's programme for a new commented edition of a Byzantine alchemical lexicon (Eikasmós, 2013) marks how much of this material still awaits its editor; where the lexica disagree with each other, this glossary reports the disagreement rather than electing a winner.

2. The Authors Glossing Themselves

The second witness class is the tradition explaining its own words in running text. Zōsimos of Panopolis (fl. c. 300 CE) writes for his disciple Theosebeia as a commentator: he quotes the older authorities — pseudo-Democritus above all, and Maria the Jewess — and repeatedly stops to say what their covered expressions mean, because his whole project fails if she cannot follow them. Synesius, in his commentary on pseudo-Democritus addressed to Dioscorus, does the same work for the founding text: it is Synesius, as Martelli's edition shows, who preserves pseudo-Democritus's two kinds of “divine water” and who records that the water, once macerated, “is called vinegar, and Aminean wine, and the like” — an ancient author caught in the act of listing one substance's cover-names. The commentator generation as a whole is treated in The Commentators; for this glossary their importance is simple: a gloss by Zōsimos or Synesius is evidence of a different order from a guess by us, and the Evidence column says so.

3. The Workshop Papyri: The Control Group

The third witness class disciplines the other two. The Leyden Papyrus X and the Stockholm Papyrus (3rd–4th century CE, from Thebes; English translation by Earle Radcliffe Caley, 1926–27; edition by Robert Halleux, 1981) are working recipe collections that name most of their ingredients plainly — misy, alum, cadmia, cinnabar, urine, vinegar — because a bench handbook has no audience to exclude. They are therefore the control group for every claim that a word is a cover-name: if the papyri use the word openly in recipes, the word is a technical or trade term, however strange it sounds. And the papyri also preserve the single most instructive Deckname in this glossary: at the end of Stockholm recipe 51, on softening quartz, an ancient gloss explains that “human excrement” means garlic — a cover-name caught, by its own tradition, in the act of covering. Martelli notes that the gloss is probably misplaced and belonged to a recipe the collection did not include; the displacement is itself the lesson, since it shows glosses travelling separately from the texts they unlock. The papyri's full treatment is in the Egyptology division's Alchemy article; the prehistory they share with pseudo-Democritus is in this hub's origins article.

// THE EVIDENCE GRADES USED IN EVERY TABLE //

Ancient lexicon states it — a lexicon or sign-list of the corpus itself gives the equation. The text itself glosses it — an ancient author or papyrus note explains the name in place. Context makes it certain — no gloss survives, but the recipe's quantities, sequence, and result admit one ingredient only. Commentators' identification, probable — ancient commentators or the early scholarly tradition converge on an identification that fits, without proof. Modern conjecture, disputed — a modern proposal, contested by other specialists. UNKNOWN — no identification this glossary can verify; the entry is kept precisely so that the gap is on record.

One further rule governs the Greek. Headwords are given in Greek script only where the spelling is secured by a source actually consulted for this article — the Berthelot–Ruelle sign-lists, Martelli's editions and studies, Caley's papyri, or the standard lexica. Where a name is known to this glossary only through later lists or secondary report, it is given in transliteration or in English alone, and the entry says why. A glossary of cover-names that invented its own Greek would be committing the offence it exists to document.

III. The Seven Metals and Their Planetary Names

The most durable cover-names in the tradition are not riddles but equivalences: each of the seven metals known to antiquity travels under the name — and the sign — of one of the seven wandering stars. In the scheme that became canonical, gold is the Sun, silver the Moon, copper Aphrodite, iron Ares, tin Zeus, lead Kronos, and mercury Hermes. A recipe could then be written in gods alone, and a sign-list could serve as its key. The equivalences are so familiar from later alchemy that their own history is usually skipped. It should not be, because the earliest surviving key does not match the canonical scheme.

The history, in the brief form the evidence allows: the workshop papyri know only the first pair. In recipe 88 of the Leyden Papyrus, as Martelli notes, the signs for “sun” and “moon” are written in place of the words chrysos (gold) and argyros (silver) — the earliest witness to the planetary shorthand, and a witness to two pairings, not seven. The sign-list preserved in the Marcianus tradition and printed by Berthelot and Ruelle gives a full set of planetary names — but with two assignments the canonical scheme later reversed: there, Zeus is electrum and Hermes is tin. The list's logic is recoverable: quicksilver, a liquid metal drawn from cinnabar, had not yet taken its seat among the seven bodies, so the bright alloy electrum held Zeus's place and tin held Hermes's. The canonical scheme — Hermes for quicksilver, Zeus for tin — is the product of the later systematisation, carried by the Byzantine lexica and, in the Syriac and Arabic traditions Martelli surveys, by glossaries that list seven metallic bodies (gold, silver, iron, copper, tin, lead, mercury) alongside seven spirits. Even there the join shows: Martelli notes a standing hesitation in the sources about whether mercury belongs among the bodies or the spirits — it is listed, in different witnesses, as both. The pairings below are therefore graded individually: the Sun–gold and Moon–silver equations are as old as our earliest key; the rest are ancient, but younger, and two of them changed their metal on the way.

A note on the god-names themselves: the sign-list gives each planet its Greek star-name as well — Kronos the Shining One (Phainōn), Zeus the Radiant (Phaethōn), Ares the Fiery (Pyroeis), Aphrodite the Light-Bringer (Phōsphoros), Hermes the Glittering (Stilbōn). These are astronomical epithets before they are alchemical ones, and their presence in the list is a reminder that the scheme is borrowed cosmology put to workshop use. The Sun's god, likewise, is Hēlios in the early lists; his equation with Apollo, and the Moon's (Selēnē's) with Artemis, belongs to the wider Greco-Roman syncretism and is the form in which the pairings are often cited. This glossary gives the names as the sign-list gives them.

The lesson of the table is the glossary's lesson in miniature. Even the most “universal” code in alchemy — seven gods, seven metals — turns out to be a local, dated arrangement that shifted at exactly the point (quicksilver's promotion) where the art's theory was changing. The reader who memorises the canonical seven and applies them to the sign-list's period will misread Zeus and Hermes in every recipe. Section X returns to the rule this imposes.

IV. Substances of the Work I: Mercury, Sulphur, and the Arsenicals

If the metals of Section III have one cover-name each, the volatile substances have dozens. The reason is structural: the spirits — the things that rise under heat, carry colour, and flee the vessel — are the art's active agents, its most valuable and most dangerous stock, and therefore its most renamed. Mercury heads the swarm. C. A. Browne's classic survey of alchemical obscurity, summarising the Greek lexicon tradition, reports mercury described by Zōsimos as “the silvery water; the masculine-feminine; the ever-fugitive; that which hastens unto its own; and the divine water”, and lists the lexicon's further names for it — seed of the dragon, bile of the dragon, dew, milk of a black cow, sandarach, Scythian water, water of silver, water of the moon, river water, divine water, the sea and sea-water. That list is quoted here as Browne reports it, at second hand, and the entries drawn from it are graded accordingly: the swarm is real, but any single name in it carries weaker evidence than a name a papyrus glosses in place.

One name in the section requires its philology up front, because it is the most famous pun in Greek alchemy. Θεῖον (theion) is two words wearing one spelling: theion the adjective means “divine”; theion the noun means “sulphur” (brimstone — the burning stone itself, whose fumes purified Greek sanctuaries long before they tinctured metals). The phrase θεῖον ὕδωρ (theion hydōr) therefore says “divine water” and “sulphur water” at once, and in the genitive — theiou hydatos — Greek grammar itself cannot tell the reader which was meant. Matteo Martelli's study of the phrase in pseudo-Democritus (Ambix, 2009) is the definitive treatment: the alchemists, he shows, play on the polysemy deliberately, the manuscripts abbreviate the phrase with signs that deepen the ambiguity, and pseudo-Democritus in fact knows two kinds of the water, distinguished by composition — a sulphur-bearing water, and a water whose second form recalls the Leyden Papyrus's own recipe for “sulphur water” (recipe 89: lime and sulphur heated in strong vinegar or a child's urine until the liquor stands “like blood”, in Caley's translation, then decanted for use). The pun is not decoration. It is a cover-name generated by the language itself, and the tradition exploited it for a thousand years.

Note what the section's strongest entries share: a grade vocabulary (unburnt, untouched, decomposed, golden) that is openly descriptive. The workshop needed to distinguish its sulphurs and arsenics precisely, and did — in plain sight, in the same texts that call mercury by a dozen riddles. Covering, in this corpus, is selective, and the selection maps value and danger, not a general love of obscurity.

V. Substances of the Work II: Earths, Salts, and Vitriols

The second substance-family is the workshop's corrosive cupboard: alkalis, alum, and the vitriol group — the weathered copper-and-iron sulphates that antiquity mined, above all on Cyprus, and distinguished with a care modern readers must work to recover. Dioscorides and Pliny describe these minerals; Galen travelled to the Cypriot mines and collected them in layers — sōry from the lowest, chalcitis above it, misy at the top — and found his chalcitis crusted with misy twenty years later. That single observation governs the whole family's grading: these are mixtures that weather into one another, not pure compounds, and every modern chemical name offered below is a centre of gravity, not an identity. Karpenko and Norris's history of vitriol makes the complementary point: the commercial vitriol of antiquity, for all its copper associations, was in most samples iron-rich. Entries that flatten misy to one modern mineral formula are claiming more than the evidence holds; this glossary gives the formula as the leading modern candidate and grades it probable.

The corpus's whitening-water recipe (Berthelot–Ruelle II 391) opens with split alum and white gum. Context makes it certain. Leyden X passim (Caley); corpus recipe at Berthelot–Ruelle II 391.
χάλκανθος · chalkanthos “Copper-flower” Vitriol — the hydrated copper/iron sulphates (the sign-list: “copper sulfate”). The family's type-name: Strabo, citing Posidonius, singles out Cypriot copper as alone producing cadmia, chalcanth, and spodos together. Modern analysis urges the iron-rich caution of this section's introduction: much ancient chalkanthos was nearer green vitriol (iron sulphate) than blue. Ancient sign-list states it; exact sulphate composition varies by source. Berthelot–Ruelle sign-list; Strabo III 4.15 via the cadmia dossier; Karpenko & Norris 2002.
χαλκῖτις · chalkitis “The copper (ore)” A pyritic copper ore and its weathering vitriol — Pliny defines it against cadmia: chalcitis is quarried from exposed rock, is friable at once, and “is a composition of three other substances, copper, misy, and sory”, ageing in time into sory (in the Perseus translation of Natural History XXXIV 29, paraphrased here in part). The Syriac lexica gloss it simply “vitriol” (Martelli 2021). No single modern mineral covers it. Ancient authors describe it; modern identification probable in kind, not in species. Pliny, NH XXXIV 29; Berthelot–Ruelle sign-list (“pyritic copper ore”); Martelli 2021.
μίσυ · misy An opaque trade name (the sign-list's Cypriot association is a copyist's tangle, not an etymology) A golden-yellow iron–copper sulphate — the weathered crust of the vitriol family, the top layer of Galen's Cypriot mine. The leading modern candidate is copiapite (a hydrated iron sulphate), with related yellow sulphates in the mixture. The papyri use misy constantly and plainly: gold colouration and polishing (Leyden X recipes 15, 25, 69, 88), gilding (51), always as a bought mineral, graded “from the mines” (76). Commentators' identification, probable (copiapite-family iron sulphate). Pliny NH XXXIV 29–31; Galen's mine layers via Scott 2002; Leyden X (Caley).
σῶρυ · sōry An opaque trade name A dark, aged iron–copper sulphate — the bottom layer of Galen's mine and the end-state Pliny says chalcitis becomes with age; the sign-list glosses it “iron sulfate, used to make black dye and ink”. Modern candidates cluster around melanterite-family basic sulphates; the ancients distinguished it from misy by colour, age, and layer, not by composition we can now audit. Commentators' identification, probable; species disputed. Pliny NH XXXIV 29; Berthelot–Ruelle sign-list; Galen via Scott 2002.
melantheria / melanteria “The blackening (earth)” Black vitriol — the iron-sulphate blacking of the leather trade (Latin atramentum sutorium, shoemaker's black). Leyden X recipe 94 lists melantheria among the styptic agents that “serve in dyeing”, in the company of calcined copperas, alum, chalcitis, and lime — a family portrait of the vitriols at work. Commentators' identification, probable. Leyden X recipe 94 (Caley); vitriol history in Karpenko & Norris 2002.
καδμεία · kadmeia “Cadmus's (earth)” — the Theban founder's name on a Cypriot product Zinc oxide in both its ancient forms: the natural calamine ore, and the furnace product that sublimes onto the walls and upper chamber of copper-smelting furnaces — the material later ages call tutty (from Arabic ṭūtiyā). Dioscorides rates the Cypriot kind best and describes its furnace collection; the papyri spend it freely in asem and gold-doubling (Leyden X recipes 11, 16, 87). Pliny's warning stands: the same name covers ore and furnace crust, and texts do not always say which. Context makes it certain (zinc oxide); the ore/furnace distinction is text-by-text. Dioscorides and Strabo via the Cypriot kadmeia dossier; Leyden X (Caley).
pompholyx “Bubble, blister” — for its light, blistered crusts The light, white, volatile grade of furnace zinc oxide — the flower that rises furthest, distinguished in the pharmacological tradition from the heavier, sootier furnace deposit (spodos, next entry). Later pharmacy knows the same light oxide as pompholyx, “flowers of zinc”, and lana philosophica, “philosophers' wool”. Commentators' identification, probable. Dioscoridean furnace taxonomy as carried in the materia-medica tradition; the zinc-oxide name-cluster in the later pharmacopoeias.
spodos “Ash, soot” Furnace ash — the heavier oxide-and-soot deposit of the smelting chamber, third member of Strabo's Cypriot triad (cadmia, chalcanth, spodos, in Posidonius's report). A collective name for a mixed product; no purer referent is recoverable, and none should be imposed. Ancient authors state it (as a furnace product). Strabo III 4.15, citing Posidonius, via the kadmeia dossier.
μαγνησία · magnēsia “The (stone) of Magnesia” — a place-name, like kadmeia Disputed — see the full discussion in Section IX. In the papyri “stone of Magnesia” is a routine asem ingredient (Leyden X recipes 2, 9, 13, 18, 29, 83); the Byzantine Lexicon equates magnēsia with kadmeia; a Syriac gloss adds litharge. Modern candidates (manganese dioxide, a magnetic iron ore, a white earth) each fit some uses and fail others. Entered here among the earths because the papyri spend it as one; graded in Section IX. Modern conjecture, disputed. Martelli 2021: “the correct identification of which is still debated among scholars”.
hals ammōniakos — “salt of Ammon” Salt of the Ammon oasis (Siwa) — or of the god Ammon's country Sal ammoniac in the developed tradition — the volatile ammonium salt that the Syriac and Arabic glossaries list among the seven spirits, and whose cover-names fill a whole Ruska-list of twenty-four. Caley's Leyden translation has “salt of Ammon” at work doubling asem (recipe 10) and in the gold-leaf treatment (recipe 90). Whether every ancient “salt of Ammon” was ammonium chloride, or some was oasis rock-salt, remains debated in detail; the alchemical tradition's spirit is the volatile salt. Context makes the workshop sense certain; composition disputed in detail. Leyden X recipes 10, 85, 90 (Caley); Ruska–Wiedemann lists (Holmyard 1926); Martelli 2021 on the seven spirits.
στίμμι · stimmi The eye-black name — powdered antimony for the lids Stibnite — antimony sulphide, ground for kohl and spent in the workshop: the sign-list glosses it “powdered antimony”, and the pseudo-Democritean repertoire uses “Italian antimony” among the agents that fix mercury (see this hub's origins article, §X). Leyden X recipe 99 grinds a dye “as fine as stibnite in a mortar” — the cosmetic powder as the bench's standard of fineness. Ancient sign-list states it; context makes it certain. Berthelot–Ruelle sign-list; Leyden X recipe 99 (Caley); Martelli 2013 on the fixing repertoire.
// COPPERAS, IN CALEY //

Readers of Caley's translation meet “copperas” where this glossary writes vitriol: it is his period word for the green iron sulphate of the hardening and blacking recipes (Leyden X recipes 1, 24, 82, 94). The word is a grade-name in English dress, and the recipes that use it belong to the same Cypriot family as misy and sōry — bought sulphates, weathering into one another, distinguished at the bench by colour and behaviour rather than by formula.

The family's collective lesson is the opposite of the mercury swarm's. Here the names are mostly open — trade names, place-names, grade-names — and the difficulty is not secrecy but geology: pre-chemical mineralogy sorted by appearance and origin what chemistry now sorts by composition, and the two sortings cross-cut. The honest entry therefore often ends, as several above do, with a family rather than a species.

VI. Substances of the Work III: Products and Workshop Fluids

The third family is what the workshop made and what it poured: the prepared pigments and oxides that were themselves products of one process and ingredients of the next, and the humble fluids — vinegar, urine, bile, egg white — whose presence in the recipes is the best antidote to over-reading this glossary. A tradition that writes “urine of a small child” in plain words, in a recipe for colouring gold, is not encrypting everything; and a fluid that sounds like pure cover-name — “bile of a river tortoise” — turns out, on inspection, to be an ingredient a scribe could actually buy. Both directions of surprise are documented below.

The ios entry deserves its sentence of philology, because the brief of this glossary rightly makes it the polysemy lesson. The standard lexicon (LSJ, s.v. ἰός) files three homonymous words: ios (A), an arrow (Homer's word); ios (B), poison, venom (the tragedians' word, cognate with Latin virus); and ios (C), rust on iron, verdigris on copper and bronze (Plato's and Dioscorides' word — “ios of iron”, “ios of copper” are his phrases). Whether the rust-word is historically the poison-word (corrosion as the metal's venom) the lexicon leaves open. For the alchemist the three senses hum together: the metal's corrosion is its poison, its flowering, and — in iōsis — its final colour. A glossary that translated every ios as “verdigris” would be accurate and deaf in equal measure.

VII. Process and Product Names: Egg, Serpent, and the Colour Stages

The names in this section cover not ingredients but operations, vessels, and results — the borderland where the Deckname shades into the technical term and the emblem. They are included because the tradition itself files them together, and because two of them — the egg and the serpent — are the most misread words in the whole vocabulary: read as pure mysticism by one school, as pure apparatus by another, when the texts use them, characteristically, as both at once and say so.

The colour stages require the same care in the opposite direction. The Greek corpus names four transformations by their colours — blackening, whitening, yellowing, and the ios-flowering that reddens and purples — and the sequence is genuinely ancient: it is the operator's bench-diagnosis, read off the metal's surface. The familiar Latin tetrad nigredo, albedo, citrinitas, rubedo is a later systematisation of that Greek sequence, elaborated in the medieval Latin tradition into a philosophy of the Work with stages, durations, and spiritual correspondences the Greek recipes do not impose. The correspondences are real (melanōsis→nigredo, leukōsis→albedo, xanthōsis→citrinitas, iōsis→rubedo), and this glossary gives them — as a mapping forward, from the Greek bench to the Latin system. What it does not do is read the Latin system's meanings backward into the Greek words.

ὠόν · ōon — “the egg” An egg; by shape and by contents, a closed generative vessel Two referents, layered. (1) The vessel: the round, sealed container in which the matter is incubated under gentle heat — the form later tradition calls the philosophical egg, heir to the corpus's sealed reflux vessels (see Maria the Jewess on the kērotakis). (2) The matter itself, analysed as an egg is analysed — shell, white, yolk, and the life in the midst — so that “egg” can name the charge as readily as its container. H. J. οὐροβόρος · ouroboros — the tail-eating serpent “Tail-devourer” — the serpent in a ring, its tail in its mouth A process emblem, not an ingredient: the closed, self-feeding cycle of the work — vapour rising, condensing, returning — drawn as a serpent consuming itself. Its canonical alchemical form is the single leaf of the Chrysopoeia of Cleopatra, preserved in the Marcianus: the serpent ringed around the words hen to pān, “One is the All”, with the ring-inscription (in the published translation) naming the serpent whose poison is of two compositions, through which all things come to be. ἓν τὸ πᾶν · hen to pān “One (is) the All” The ouroboros's motto, and the work's monism in three words: one matter, one vessel, one operation, from which the differentiated products come. Not a substance-name; included because later readers repeatedly mistake it for one (a name of the prime matter), where the leaf itself presents it as the serpent's inscription — a claim about the process, not a label on a jar. Ancient inscription states it. Chrysopoeia of Cleopatra, as above.
μελάνωσις · melanōsis “Blackening” The first colour stage: the charge blackens — oxidised, sulphurised, “killed” — as its old form is broken down. It is not adequately translated “reddening”: its range is the violet-red-purple band, and its root is the verdigris-word of Section VI, with the poison-sense humming beneath. A product-name as much as a process-name: the “doubled” metal is what the customer bought. Ancient technical terms, stated as recipe titles. Leyden X (Caley); pseudo-Democritus tradition.
ξήριον · xērion “A dry powder” — in ordinary Greek, a desiccative dusted on wounds The transmuting powder — the “medicine of metals” that, projected in small quantity onto a base body, “cures” it into gold or silver. Zōsimos's Cheirokmēta gives the word its alchemical career; its journey — Greek xērion → Arabic al-iksīr → Latin elixir — is the most consequential word-history in the art. The wound-powder origin matters: the elixir begins as a medicine, and metals, in this idiom, are patients. Ancient usage states it; the etymological chain is standard philology. Zōsimos tradition; word-history per the standard histories (Strohmaier 2016, as surveyed).
νεφέλη · nephelē “Cloud” The sublimate — the vapour that rises, clouds the vessel's upper wall, and settles as the collected product. The sign-list glosses it exactly: “cloud, vapor, or sublimate”. A process-product in one word: what the cloud is depends on what was heated, and the name declines to say — the mildest cover-name in this glossary, and the most honest about its own method. Ancient sign-list states it. Berthelot–Ruelle sign-list.

VIII. Body, Soul, and Spirit: The Technical Triad

Three ordinary Greek words do the heaviest theoretical work in the corpus, and they belong in a glossary of cover-names because their alchemical senses are technical redefinitions that a reader from outside the art will systematically mistranslate. When Zōsimos defines the art — in the paraphrase the scholarship repeats from his text — as the composition of waters, movement, growth, embodying and disembodying, drawing the spirits from bodies and binding the spirits into bodies, every noun in the definition is one of this section's words used in its workshop sense. Athanasios Rinotas's analysis of conversion in Zōsimos (Religions, 2021) is the guide followed here: the pneuma is, at the bench, the sublimed tincturing vapour; its liberation from a body is aposōmatōsis, its fixation in a new body episōmatōsis; and the same vocabulary, without changing a word, carries the corpus's spiritual teaching about the human soul. The doubleness is the doctrine, not a confusion in it — but the glossary's duty is to keep the bench sense primary and labelled, so that the spiritual sense is recognised as the extension it is.

In the Syriac and Arabic glossaries the seven metals are filed as the seven bodies, against the seven spirits. A metal “killed” in melanōsis is a body whose spirit has been driven off or loosened. φάρμακον · pharmakon “Drug” — remedy, poison, or dye, by dose and use The art's generic substance-word: a preparation that does something to a body — heals it, kills it, or colours it. Pseudo-Democritus's methodological chapters compare the making of dyeing pharmaka with the compounding of medicines outright (Martelli 2021, on the Syriac transmission), and the Syriac translators rendered the word with their term for a medicinal root. IX. The Opaque Residue: Disputed and Unknown Names

Every honest glossary ends in a residue: names the tradition transmits without a key, or with several keys that open different doors. This section is the glossary's honesty exhibit, and it is deliberately not the smallest section. It opens with the one residue-name that was solved — by its own text, in antiquity — because the solution shows what a solution looks like, and how rarely the residue meets one. It closes with two names that circulate in the cover-name literature without any ancient gloss this glossary could verify. They are printed here, marked UNKNOWN, rather than omitted: an omitted unknown gets reinvented by the next compiler as a certainty; a labelled one stays a question.

ἀνθρωπίνη κόπρος · anthrōpinē kopros — “human excrement” Human dung Garlic (σκόρδον, skordon) — in the text that glosses it. The Stockholm Papyrus, closing its recipe 51 on softening quartz, adds the note (in Caley's translation, as quoted by Martelli): “human excrement is, as they say, garlic”. Both substances appear as mordants in the gem-dyeing tradition, which is how the cover could work; Martelli and Caley both note the gloss is probably misplaced, orphaned from a recipe the collection omits. The control case of this glossary: a Deckname whose key survives, attached — just — to the wrong lock. The text itself glosses it. Stockholm Papyrus, recipe 51 note (Halleux 1981; Caley 1927; Martelli 2021).
ἀφροσέληνον · aphroselēnon — “moon foam” Foam, froth, or spittle of the moon Disputed — at least three ancient referents. (1) The sign-list tradition reports Zōsimos identifying it with quicksilver (“some say selenite; Zosimos says it is quicksilver, 'moon foam'”). (2) The Byzantine Lexicon equates it with kōmaris and “light stone”. (3) The Arabic and Syriac traditions, al-Bīrūnī among them, resolve its Arabic calques (“moon saliva”, “moon stone”, “moon foam”) as ṭalq — talc, a white silicate mineral of the mica family, which Martelli judges its probable Greek sense as well. Zōsimos also wrote a treatise On komaron and 'moon foam' according to Democritus arguing the two names denote one ingredient — the debate is ancient, not a modern failure. Ancient witnesses disagree — identification disputed. Berthelot–Ruelle sign-list; Byzantine Lexicon and Syriac/Arabic witnesses per Martelli 2021.
kōmaris / komaron An opaque substance-name (spellings fluctuate: komaris, komaron, komma­ri, kōmaris) UNKNOWN, with a modern candidate. A dyeing ingredient for stones and textiles (wool especially) across the Byzantine compendium Deep Tincture of Stones, which already debates it and equates it with “moon foam” (previous entry). Modern scholarship has tentatively proposed a dyeing plant, perhaps the root of marsh cinquefoil (Comarum palustre) — Martelli's word is “tentatively”, and this glossary repeats the proposal under that word and no stronger one. Modern conjecture, disputed. Deep Tincture of Stones tradition (Berthelot–Ruelle II 350–364); Halleux 1981 s.v.; Martelli 2021.
μαγνησία · magnēsia (full entry) “The Magnesian (stone)” Disputed — the glossary's type-case of a name that will not sit still. The witnesses: the papyri spend “stone of Magnesia” as a routine flux in asem-making, in quantities (16 drachmas against a mina of copper) that suit a mineral addition, not a protagonist. The Byzantine Lexicon states flatly: kadmeia is magnēsia. A Syriac sign-list glosses magnēsia with murtak — litharge — and with kadmeia again. The wider Greek word-family covers the magnetic stone (lodestone), a black manganese earth, and a white silvery-looking mineral (the steatite/talc line), and Berthelot's tradition rendered Zōsimos's crucible-steel magnēsia as manganese dioxide. Each candidate fits a subset of uses; none fits all; Martelli's verdict — the correct identification is still debated among scholars — is the entry. Modern conjecture, disputed; ancient witnesses disagree. Leyden X (Caley); Byzantine Lexicon and Syriac lists per Martelli 2021; Shand 2006 on the word's three minerals.
oreichalkos — “mountain-copper” Copper of the mountain A prized copper alloy of uncertain composition — the sign-list glosses it “orichalcum, mountain-copper, perhaps a bronze alloy”, and its own “perhaps” is the correct grade: Plato's Atlantean metal (see the Before Kemet hub) had already made the name legendary before the workshop used it, and the Leyden Papyrus spends “orichalcum” as a stock alloy (recipe 85) without describing it. A brass-family identification is the leading modern view; it is not a settled one. Commentators' identification, probable; composition disputed. Berthelot–Ruelle sign-list; Leyden X recipe 85 (Caley).
the stone that is not a stone A paradox-name: a stone denied Disputed. Zōsimos transmits the riddle-name for the art's perfected tincture — the ancestor-figure of the later philosopher's stone. The careful modern reading (against the medieval stone's full mythology, which is later) takes the referent to be a volatile tincture — a spirit, not a mineral — the paradox being the point: the “stone” is what fixes, and is itself unfixed. No recipe in the Greek corpus weighs out an ingredient under this name, and entries that give it a composition are inventing one. Modern conjecture, disputed. Zōsimos, On Virtue tradition (the Democritus saying as he transmits it); the antecedent-not-stone caution in the current scholarship.
the spear of Ares A weapon-name: the war-god's spear UNKNOWN. A martial cover-name of the type the later lists favour — a god's weapon for a piercing, corrosive, or weapon-metal agent. This glossary could verify no ancient gloss fixing its referent: no lexicon entry, papyrus note, or commentator's equation for it was found in the witnesses consulted, and the obvious guesses (an iron instrument; an arsenical; a corrosive salt) are guesses, each with a story and none with a text. Printed as unknown, in the form in which the cover-name literature transmits it, without Greek — no secured Greek form was verified. UNKNOWN — no verified gloss. Absence recorded against the lexica, sign-lists, and papyri consulted for this glossary (Section XI).
dog's blood An animal-product name of the most covered type UNKNOWN. Blood-names are a documented family in the cover-name traditions — the lexicon's “bile of the dragon” for mercury (Section IV), the later “dragon's blood” for red resins and cinnabar — and a canine member of the family is exactly the sort of name those lists generate. But for this name, in the Greek corpus and its lexica, this glossary found no gloss and no recipe context that fixes a referent, and the candidates offered in popular compilations (a red pigment; a plant juice; an arsenical) arrive without ancient witnesses. It stands here as the residue's limit case: a name whose plausibility as a Deckname is the very reason to refuse it a referent. UNKNOWN — no verified gloss. Absence recorded as for the previous entry; the blood-name family is documented in Sections IV and VI.

Two of the section's entries teach the same lesson from opposite ends. Aphroselēnon is unknown not for lack of evidence but for surplus of it: three ancient traditions, three referents, and a lost treatise of Zōsimos in the middle trying to settle the question in the third century. Dog's blood is unknown for lack of evidence pure and simple. A glossary that cannot tell those two situations apart — that prints one flat “meaning” for both — has failed at the only task that justifies its existence.

X. How to Use This Glossary

The rule is one sentence long, and it is the archive's rule for every cover-name in every text this hub treats: a cover-name is a local fact about one text, not a dictionary key to all texts.

Everything in the preceding sections is evidence for that sentence. Zeus is electrum in the sign-list and tin in the canonical scheme. Hermes is tin before he is quicksilver. Thalassion hydōr is the sea in a dye recipe and mercury in a lexicon. Chrysokolla is a solder in the Leyden Papyrus and a green mineral in the Syriac gloss. Krokos is saffron in an ink and a yolk in a sign-list. Oxos is vinegar at the bench and the divine water in Synesius. In each case the text, its date, its genre, and its neighbours decide — and in each case a reader armed with a single flat equivalence, however well-attested somewhere, will eventually pour the sea into a crucible or weigh out a planet.

Used rightly, then, the glossary is a generator of hypotheses with their grades attached. Meet a covered name in a text: find its entry; note every referent the entry records, with the period and witness for each; test the candidates against the passage's quantities, operations, and promised result; and keep the grade of the survivor in view when the reading is cited onward. Where the entry says UNKNOWN, the correct scholarly act is to translate the name, flag it, and move on — the tradition's own exegetes, from the Stockholm glossator to Zōsimos arguing about moon foam, worked under the same discipline, and the texts that survive are the ones whose copyists preferred an honest gloss to a confident invention. The glossary asks no more of its reader than the corpus asked of its own.

// A WORKING CHECKLIST //

1 · Date the text before decoding it (the planetary scheme alone has two periods). 2 · Ask the genre: lexicon, commentary, recipe, and allegory use the same words differently. 3 · Let quantities arbitrate: a mina-against-drachmas ingredient is a bulk mineral, not a protagonist spirit. 4 · Prefer the referent the passage's result requires. 5 · Carry the evidence grade with the identification wherever you cite it. 6 · Where the glossary says UNKNOWN, write UNKNOWN.

A note on method, in the archive's standing form. Transliteration uses macrons for the long vowels (ē, ō) where the quantity is secure; Greek script is given only for headwords secured by the witnesses above, and withheld — deliberately, and with the withholding stated in the entry — for “the spear of Ares” and “dog's blood”, for which no ancient gloss was found in any witness consulted.
