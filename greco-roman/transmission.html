I. Transmission Is the Story: What a Text Has to Survive

Every other article in this wing of the archive is about something somebody wrote, made, or believed. This one is about the narrower and stranger question of how any of it is still here to be read. Greek alchemy was composed, in the main, between the first and the seventh centuries CE, in a Greek-speaking Egypt that then passed under Byzantine, Persian, and Arab rule in quick succession. No autograph survives. No papyrus codex of Zosimos of Panopolis survives. What survives is a chain: late-antique teachers who turned workshop writings into commented school texts; Byzantine compilers who gathered those texts into codices, the oldest of which was copied some seven centuries after Zosimos wrote; Syriac translators who carried a selection of the corpus into the Christian East; Arabic translators and authors in Baghdad who received it, rebuilt it, and made it the foundation of a new literature; and, at the end of the chain, a single dated Latin translation — 11 February 1144 — through which the art entered a Latin West that, by its translator’s own account, did not yet know what alchemy was.

Transmission, in this article, is therefore not a synonym for copying. Each link in the chain did something to what it passed on. The commentators systematised: they decided which ancients agreed with which, and taught the art as a doctrine with a history. The compilers selected: a codex is an argument about what belongs together, and the Greek corpus as we have it is a Byzantine editorial product, not a Roman one. The translators re-founded: the Arabic tradition did not merely keep Greek alchemy alive, it rebuilt the art’s theory, its classification of substances, and its literary forms, so that what Latin Europe received in the twelfth century was Greek alchemy as Arabic scholarship had preserved, corrected, and extended it — the formulation Lawrence Principe’s The Secrets of Alchemy (2013) argues across its transmission chapters, and the one this article follows. Preservation was never passive. It was a series of interventions, each of which can be studied, and some of which can be dated.

Three disciplines of evidence govern what follows, and they are worth stating at the front because the subject invites romance. First, shelfmarks before stories. Where a claim rests on a manuscript, the manuscript is named — Marcianus graecus 299, Parisinus graecus 2325, Parisinus graecus 2327, Cambridge Mm. 6.29 — and dated as the catalogues date it, as a range where the dating is palaeographical. Second, catalogues say only what catalogues say. Where the evidence for a transmission is a catalogue entry — William Wright’s Cambridge catalogue for the Syriac Zosimos, Ibn al-Nadim’s Fihrist for the Arabic — the article reports the entry and does not build a narrative on top of it. Third, the tradition’s own account of its transmission is labelled as tradition. The story that a Byzantine monk taught an Umayyad prince, who thereby founded Arabic alchemy, is the alchemical tradition’s account of itself. It is historically precious and historically unverified, and Section XIV treats it at exactly that strength.

THE GREEK LINK

Commentators of the 4th–7th centuries — Synesius, Olympiodorus, Christianus, Stephanus — turn the early treatises into a commented, teachable corpus; Byzantine compilers gather that corpus into codices, of which the Marcianus graecus 299 (late 10th–early 11th c.) is the oldest survivor.

THE SYRIAC LINK

A documented but narrow bridge: twelve treatises attributed to Zosimos survive in Syriac in a 15th-century Cambridge codex, alongside Democritean and workshop material of the same Greek kind as the Leyden and Stockholm papyri. The wider Greek-to-Syriac translation culture, exemplified by Sergius of Reshaina, is the context — not, on present evidence, an alchemical channel in itself.

THE ARABIC LINK

Baghdad’s translation movement (8th–9th c.) carries Greek alchemy into Arabic, substantially through Syriac. Zosimos survives in Arabic in works with no complete Greek counterpart; the Jabir corpus (c. 850–950) systematises the inheritance into an Arabic creation that is not a translation of anything.

THE LATIN LINK

Before 1144, essentially no Latin alchemical literature exists. On 11 February 1144 Robert of Chester completes the Liber de compositione alchemiae, the Morienus dialogue, from Arabic — the first dated Latin alchemical translation. Gerard of Cremona and Toledo follow within a generation.

II. The Commentators: The Art Becomes a School Text

The first transmission of Greek alchemy happened inside Greek. Between the age of the early treatises — the works circulating under the names of pseudo-Democritus, Hermes, Agathodaimon, Isis, Cleopatra, Maria, and Ostanes, composed roughly between the first and third centuries — and the age of the codices stands a generation of writers whose work was not to invent but to explain: the commentators. Scholarship on the Byzantine period of the art, notably Cristina Viano’s survey of Greco-Egyptian alchemy and the work gathered under the heading of the era of systematisation, treats this generation as the moment alchemy acquired a doctrine: a set of agreed questions, a canon of ancients whose apparent disagreements had to be reconciled, and a philosophical frame — above all the unity of matter — within which workshop operations could be discussed as theory.

The sequence, as Michèle Mertens sets it out in her study of Graeco-Egyptian alchemy in Byzantium, has three levels. The oldest level is the pseudepigraphic stratum itself: the prestigious names of the first to third centuries. The second level is Zosimos of Panopolis (active around 300 CE), in whom technical preoccupation and religious reflection are already fully mixed, and who himself cites, quotes, and argues with the first level — a transmitter inside the tradition before he is an authority of it. The third level is the exegetes: Synesius in the fourth century, commenting on pseudo-Democritus; Olympiodorus in the sixth; Stephanus of Alexandria in the seventh; a commentator known simply as the Christian (Christianus, the Christian Philosopher) in the seventh or eighth; and an Anonymous Philosopher somewhat later. Close to Stephanus stand four alchemical poems in verse, ascribed to Heliodorus, Theophrastus, Hierotheus, and Archelaus and dated to the seventh or eighth centuries — the art, by then, had a literature in every register the schools used: treatise, commentary, lecture, and poem.

What commentary does to a technical literature deserves a paragraph of its own, because everything later in this article is a variation on it. A commentator selects a base text and declares it authoritative. He breaks it into lemmata — short quoted passages — and surrounds each with explanation, which means the base text now survives inside another man’s book, in the order and the excerpts the commentator chose. He reconciles: where Democritus and Zosimos appear to disagree about the divine water, the commentator’s task is to show the fundamental accord beneath the different names, and the reconciliation becomes doctrine in its own right. And he teaches: the commentary is a school form, addressed to students, delivered as lectures, and copied as a course. An art that exists as commentary is an art that has become scholastic — portable between cities, teachable to strangers, and dependent for its survival on institutions that copy books. That dependence is the precondition of everything in Sections V to IX.

// WHO WERE THEY? //

The identification of the alchemical Olympiodorus and Stephanus with their namesakes, the Neoplatonic commentators on Plato and Aristotle, was long doubted on the ground that serious philosophers would not write on gold-making. The identification is now commonly accepted in the scholarship this article follows (Viano; Mertens). Its consequence matters: alchemy in late antiquity was not a marginal craft literature but part of the philosophical school curriculum of Alexandria.

The geography moved as the commentary tradition matured. Synesius and Olympiodorus belong to Alexandria, still the Mediterranean’s great school city, where the Neoplatonic commentators taught Aristotle and Plato in a curriculum alchemy now sat beside. Stephanus, as Section IV details, carried the Alexandrian school to Constantinople under Heraclius: the last great Alexandrian teacher became an imperial professor in the capital. After him, the tradition’s Greek life is Byzantine and bookish — Michael Psellos in the eleventh century, the monk Kosmas, Nikephoros Blemmydes in the thirteenth — a sequence of readers, excerptors, and compilers rather than of new system-builders. The art’s creative Greek centuries were the commentators’ centuries. Its Byzantine centuries were the copyists’, and it is to the copyists’ books that we now turn — after two sections on the commentators themselves, because the codices cannot be understood without knowing whose lectures they contain.

III. Olympiodorus and Christianus: Commentary as Method

Olympiodorus (sixth century) wrote the commentary that best shows the method at work. Its base text was a lost treatise of Zosimos, the Kat’ energeian — On Action — together with sentences drawn from other ancient alchemists. Around that base Olympiodorus built something Zosimos had not attempted: a doxography, a structured survey of opinions, in which the doctrines of nine monist Presocratic philosophers — the early Greek thinkers who had each proposed a single principle of all things — are set beside the alchemical doctrine of the unity of matter. The comparison is the argument. If Thales’ water, Anaximander’s boundless, and the rest can be read as early statements of the one underlying matter, then alchemy’s founding claim — that metals differ by imposed qualities on a common substrate, and can therefore be changed — is not a workshop superstition but the laboratory form of the oldest Greek philosophy. Viano’s study of the passage (on De arte sacra, §§18–27) shows the doxography following the standard pattern of Neoplatonic surveys of opinion: Olympiodorus is doing to Zosimos exactly what his school did to Aristotle, and with the same purpose — to exhibit a tradition as a single, developing, self-consistent teaching.

The practical consequence for transmission is easily missed. Because Olympiodorus quotes his base text in lemmata, fragments of Zosimos’ lost Kat’ energeian survive inside the commentary that the treatise itself does not. The commentator is thus an involuntary archivist: later copyists who transmitted Olympiodorus transmitted pieces of Zosimos with him, selected by a sixth-century teacher’s syllabus. When modern editors reconstruct Zosimos, they work substantially from such embedded quotation — from the Authentic Memoirs in the codices, from Olympiodorus’ lemmata, and from the Syriac and Arabic witnesses of Sections X and XII. The corpus is a mosaic of other people’s quotations to a degree classical literature rarely matches, and Mertens’ warning stands over the whole field: in the alchemical manuscripts the works of each author are interwoven with one another, copied one after another without gaps, their titles and subheadings not always distinguished, so that even locating where one work ends and another begins is editorial work, not reading.

Christianus and the Divine Water

The commentator known as Christianus — the Christian Philosopher — is the generation’s most shadowy figure and, for the historian of transmission, one of its most instructive. His surviving work, preserved in the Greek corpus and printed in Berthelot and Ruelle’s Collection (CAAG II, pp. 395–421 in the standard citation), is a compilation in thirty chapters On the Divine Water, dedicated to a Sergius — identified in the reference literature as probably Sergius, patriarch of Constantinople under Heraclius — and organised as commentary, chapter by chapter, on precise themes and questions in the early authors: pseudo-Hermes, Zosimos, pseudo-Democritus. His dating is debated: the reference tradition places him broadly between 500 and 800; Mertens puts the Christian in the seventh or eighth century; recent work on his text’s strikingly early description of Indian lac — the resin whose dye and shellac manufacture his procedures describe — has argued for the early seventh century, in Heraclius’ reign. This article gives the range and flags the debate rather than choosing a decade the evidence does not settle.

Christianus’ theme is the commentators’ theme in its purest form: the divine water, the active, dissolving agent of transformation, and the scandal that the ancients name it in many ways while meaning, he argues, one thing. Where pseudo-Democritus speaks in general of a unique kind and Zosimos of multiple material kinds, Christianus undertakes to show that all multiplicity leads back to the one unity — the same reconciliation Olympiodorus performed with Presocratics, performed here within the alchemical canon itself. Berthelot, editing the text, noted the method with a historian’s ambivalence: the compilation follows the general Byzantine system of drawing extracts and summaries from early authors — the method of Photius and of Constantine VII’s excerpting projects — which conserves fragments and dismembers texts in a single operation. That sentence could stand as the epigraph of this whole article. Byzantine transmission saved Greek alchemy by taking it apart.

// HOW TO READ A COMMENTATOR //

Ask of any commentary three questions. What base text does it quote, and does that text survive anywhere else? What disagreements does it reconcile, and is the reconciliation the author’s or the tradition’s? And what did the commentator leave out? The answers locate the fragment in the chain: a sentence of Zosimos may reach us only because Olympiodorus needed it for a lesson, in the form Olympiodorus’ lesson required.

IV. Stephanus of Alexandria at the Court of Heraclius

Stephanus of Alexandria (fl. c. 580 – c. 640) is the last of the great commentators and the hinge between the Alexandrian school and the Byzantine world. He studied at Alexandria, probably under Elias, in the circle of Christian successors to Olympiodorus the philosopher; he was teaching and writing commentaries there in the 580s. Shortly after the accession of the emperor Heraclius in 610 he moved to Constantinople — whether by imperial invitation is not known — and took up a chair as oikoumenikos didaskalos, ecumenical professor, at the Imperial Academy, teaching Plato, Aristotle, the quadrivium, astrology, and alchemy. Among his students was Tychicus of Trebizond, himself the teacher of the Armenian polymath Anania Shirakatsi: the Alexandrian curriculum, alchemy included, now radiated from the capital of the empire. Stephanus died sometime before Heraclius’ own death in 641.

His alchemical work survives as nine Lectures (Praxeis) On the Great and Sacred Art of Making Gold, associated with Heraclius’ court and commonly described as dedicated to the emperor. The lectures comment on the ancient alchemists in a highly rhetorical style — Viano’s characterisation — and they do something none of the earlier commentators had done so fully: they set alchemy explicitly among the other mathematical and natural disciplines, linking it with medicine, astrology, mathematics, and music. The art is presented not as a craft secret but as one member of a system of knowledge, expounded from a professor’s chair, in a capital, under an emperor’s name. Whatever the workshop reality behind the rhetoric, the institutional fact is the transmission fact: by the early seventh century alchemy had a place in the highest teaching establishment of the Greek world. A discipline with a chair gets copied. A discipline without one gets lost.

Two cautions belong to Stephanus, and this article keeps both. The first is authorship. The identification of the alchemical Stephanus with the Alexandrian professor of philosophy is, in Mertens’ formulation, not absolutely certain but quite probable; the alchemical lectures’ style has led some scholars to hesitate, and F. Sherwood Taylor, who translated the first three lectures against the Marcianus, accepted the identification. The second caution is editorial. Stephanus was not printed in Berthelot and Ruelle’s Collection (see Section VIII): his lectures were edited separately, by Julius Ludwig Ideler in the Physici et medici graeci minores (Berlin, 1841–42) from a faulty copy of the Marcianus, and Taylor’s partial re-edition of 1937–38, collating Ideler against the Marcianus itself, covers only the first three of the nine lectures. The most famous Greek alchemical author of the seventh century is still read, in large part, through a nineteenth-century edition its own editor’s materials compromised — a standing reminder that “the Greek corpus” names a set of manuscripts and a history of editions, not a stable book.

THE CORPUS IS GATHERED

The compendium of alchemical writings from which the surviving Greek codices descend is commonly placed in Constantinople in the seventh or eighth century: ancients, Zosimos, commentators, poems, and apparatus material bound into one editorial whole.

That last entry is the bridge to the codices. The Greek alchemical corpus — some forty authors in the usual count, spanning a millennium of composition — is not a library that survived; it is an anthology that was made, most plausibly in Constantinople in the seventh or eighth century, in the world the commentators had created. The makers chose the order, the attributions, and the inclusions. Everything the Byzantine, Syriac, Arabic, and eventually Latin traditions knew of Greek alchemy, they knew through that anthology and its descendants — or, in the Arabic case (Section XII), through Greek exemplars older than the anthology’s surviving copies, which is a different and luckier story.

V. The Byzantine Codices: How a Corpus Becomes a Book

A corpus becomes a book at the moment someone pays for parchment, rules the pages, and decides what goes in. For Greek alchemy that moment came repeatedly — the tradition knows a large number of medieval manuscripts — but the whole surviving tradition hangs on three principal witnesses, or four: Mertens’ formulation, which this article follows, distinguishes the Marcianus graecus 299, the Parisinus graecus 2325, and the Parisinus graecus 2327, with the Laurentianus graecus 86.16 as a possible fourth whose exact relation to the Parisinus 2327 remains unresolved. All other known Greek alchemical manuscripts appear to derive from these. The contents of the corpus range, in Mertens’ summary, from the beginning of our era to the fifteenth century: the codices are not snapshots of a moment but sedimented libraries, in which a first-century pseudepigraph, a third-century memoir, a seventh-century lecture, and a fifteenth-century addition can stand quire to quire.

The three principal witnesses differ from one another in ways that are themselves evidence, and Mertens’ comparison is worth following closely because it shows three different ideas of what the corpus was for. The Marcianus is the most beautiful of the alchemical manuscripts: a prestige object, its first title inscribed in a pyle — a magnificently decorated frame painted in four colours — and its pages carrying the lavish illustrations, above all the apparatus drawings and the Chrysopoeia leaf, for which the tradition is famous. The Parisinus 2325, by contrast, presents important omissions: its copyist, Mertens judges, was more interested in the technical content than in the philosophical and doctrinal texts, and organised the material as a workshop handbook. The Parisinus 2327 encloses a larger collection than either, including a number of texts peculiar to itself whose origin is unknown. A courtly showpiece, a practitioner’s handbook, an expanded anthology: the same corpus, three reading publics. Transmission history is, in part, the history of who a book was copied for.

Mertens records that the relations between the three principal manuscripts have not been conclusively clarified despite wide discussion: whether B and A derive from M, whether M and A are independent of one another (Viano’s judgement: very probably independent), whether L copies A or shares its exemplar — these questions decide, for any given passage, which reading is evidence and which is a copyist’s accident. An editor of Zosimos works the way a restorer works: with the witnesses side by side, and with the Syriac and Arabic versions as further witnesses to Greek exemplars now lost. The seven-century gap Mertens names — Zosimos active about 300, the oldest manuscript about 1000 — is filled with exactly this kind of subterranean copying, during which, as she puts it, it is difficult to know what was happening. The codices are the gap’s far shore.

VI. Marcianus graecus 299: The Oldest Surviving Greek Alchemical Codex

The Marcianus graecus 299 — cited in full as Venice, Biblioteca Nazionale Marciana, graecus 299 (Collocazione 584), and in the scholarship simply as M — is the oldest surviving Greek alchemical codex and the single most important witness to the corpus. Its dating is palaeographical: according to its handwriting, Mertens concludes, it probably dates from the end of the tenth or the beginning of the eleventh century. This article gives the range in that form wherever the codex is dated, because the range is what the evidence supports: no colophon survives to fix a year, and the familiar shorthand “tenth-century” in popular accounts rounds one end of a two-generation window.

The codex is a large book — roughly three hundred folios in the usual descriptions — and it opens as a planned edition: a table of contents stands at its head, listing the collection’s pieces (fifty-two entries in the count commonly reported from that table), most of them devoted to what the Byzantine tradition called the Sacred Art. The table is also the codex’s wound made legible. It corresponds only partially to the manuscript’s present contents, because the book was, in Mertens’ phrase, the victim of several accidents: it lost several quires, and some of those that remain were inverted by the binder. H. D. Saffrey’s demonstration, which Mertens endorses, is that the table of contents is in fact that of the manuscript before its various misfortunes — a catalogue of a fuller book than the one on the shelf. The Marcianus thus documents its own losses. We know, in part, what Greek alchemy we no longer have, because its oldest codex lists it.

What Is Inside

The contents span the corpus’s three levels. From the oldest stratum: works of and fragments connected with pseudo-Democritus and Hermes, the lists of alchemical symbols, and the Dialogue of the Philosophers and Cleopatra. From the second level: the works of Zosimos, dispersed through the codex in the interwoven manner described in Section III — the Authentic Memoirs in part (the Marcianus preserves, for example, the first lesson of the Visions where the Parisinus 2327 preserves all three), the chapters to Eusebia and to Theodorus, the Book of Sophe and Final Count materials — the four groups Mertens distinguishes across the witnesses, no one of which any single codex contains entire. From the third level: the commentators, including the nine alchemical lectures of Stephanus of Alexandria and the work of Olympiodorus and Christianus. And on folio 188v, the most reproduced single page in the history of alchemy: the Chrysopoeia of Cleopatra, the one-leaf diagram of concentric circles, the ouroboros enclosing the motto hen to pan — the One, the All — with the symbols of gold, silver, and mercury, and drawings of the dibikos and of a kerotakis-type apparatus.

Those apparatus drawings are the codex’s second great gift, and the one this hub’s Maria the Jewess article depends on. The Greek texts describe the kerotakis — the sealed reflux apparatus in which metals were bathed in vapours — and the tribikos, the three-armed still attributed to Maria, in words; the Marcianus tradition draws them. The drawings in M and its descendants are the source from which every modern reconstruction of the kerotakis and tribikos begins: the Egyptology division’s Zosimos treats the apparatus step by step in its Sections XI–XII, and its Section XIV states the necessary limit — the drawings are Byzantine copies of late-antique exemplars, made by scribes who drew what they saw rather than what they used, and their evidence is real but mediated. A drawing copied for seven centuries is a rumour with a pedigree. Section IX takes up what that pedigree does to technical information.

The standard modern description is Otto Lagercrantz’s, in the second volume of the Catalogue des manuscrits alchimiques grecs (Brussels, 1924–32), with an edition of the table of contents; Saffrey’s study of the codex’s history and structure is the key modern analysis. The most recent full catalogue entry is E. Mioni’s, in the Marciana’s catalogue of its Greek manuscripts (Rome, 1981). Taylor’s collation of Stephanus against M showed how much an edition can gain — and how much it can lose — from a single witness.

One more fact about M belongs in a transmission article, because it governs the codex’s modern fate. A number of later hands, most dated to the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, annotated the manuscript throughout: headings, captions, and notes in semi-uncial scripts supplement the main text. The Marcianus was not a sealed relic. It was a working book for at least four centuries after its copying — read, glossed, and handled in the same centuries in which B, A, and L were copied from the tradition it heads. The corpus’s Byzantine life did not end when its oldest codex was made. It continued in the margins.

VII. Paris and Florence: Parisinus graecus 2325, Parisinus graecus 2327, and the Laurentianus

The Parisinus graecus 2325 (B), of the thirteenth century, is the tradition’s practitioner. Where M is a monument, B is a tool: its copyist’s omissions fall, as Section V noted, on the philosophical and doctrinal texts, and its organisation serves the workshop. The point for transmission history is that selection is interpretation. A thirteenth-century reader who wanted the corpus’s recipes without its metaphysics could have them, because a thirteenth-century copyist had already decided the metaphysics was ballast. Every later reader of B inherited that decision invisibly. When modern scholars complain that the Greek tradition transmits Zosimos in abbreviation, B is one of the mechanisms: abbreviation was a service copyists performed for readers, and its cost was borne by posterity.

The Parisinus graecus 2327 (A) is the tradition’s expansion. A colophon fixes what palaeography cannot for M: the codex was copied in June 1478 at Candia (Khandak) in Crete by Theodoros Pelecanos of Corfu, passed through the hands of the Corcyrean collector Antonius Eparchus, and was acquired soon after by the royal library at Fontainebleau — the ancestor of its present home in the Bibliothèque nationale de France. A is the largest of the witnesses: it contains the complete Visions of Zosimos (lessons 1–3, ff. 84v–88v) where M holds only the first lesson, and it preserves a number of texts found in no other manuscript, whose origin Mertens records as unknown. Its construction is composite: the first half is reported as a copy of B, the second half drawn from another, not yet located source. And it carries the tradition’s most famous single image after the Chrysopoeia: Pelecanos’ own drawing of the ouroboros, dated by the codex to 1478 — a fifteenth-century scribe’s rendering of a symbol his exemplars had carried for a thousand years, and a perfect emblem of the codex tradition itself: faithful in outline, contemporary in hand.

The Laurentianus graecus 86.16 (L), in Florence’s Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, was completed in December 1492 by the scribe Antonius Dranganas. It too preserves the Visions entire (ff. 90v–95v). Its scholarly interest is its unresolved position: Mertens’ introduction to her Zosimos edition leaves open whether L is a copy of A or whether the two are gemelli — twins, copied independently from a shared exemplar. The difference decides L’s evidentiary value. If L copies A, its agreements with A prove nothing; if the two are twins, their agreements witness their lost common source, and their disagreements locate errors in one line or the other. That the question remains open, for the corpus’s fourth witness, in the standard edition, is the honest state of the field — and a useful inoculation against any account of the Greek tradition that speaks of “the text” as if it were one thing.

WHAT B TEACHES

Selection is loss with a rationale. The 13th-century handbook’s omissions show a living readership sorting the corpus by use — and show modern readers that an abbreviated witness may abbreviate its exemplar, not the author.

WHAT A TEACHES

Lateness is not worthlessness. The 1478 codex preserves whole texts its 10th-century senior lacks. In a tradition this thin, the youngest principal witness can be, for a given work, the only complete one.

WHAT L TEACHES

Uncertainty is data. An unresolved stemma is not a failure of scholarship but its accurate report: the tradition’s branching is partly unrecoverable, and editions that hide that fact overstate what anyone knows.

WHAT ALL FOUR TEACH

Survival was narrow. Four codices, copied across five centuries in Constantinople’s orbit, Crete, and Florence, carry a literature composed across a millennium. The margin between the Greek alchemy we have and none at all is four books wide.

VIII. Berthelot and Ruelle: The Corpus Enters Modern Scholarship

For five centuries the corpus lived the life described above: copied, excerpted, annotated, and read by a narrowing circle. Its entry into modern scholarship has a date and two names. In 1887–88 the chemist Marcellin Berthelot and the Hellenist Charles-Émile Ruelle published in Paris the Collection des anciens alchimistes grecs — the Collection of the Ancient Greek Alchemists, cited ever since as CAAG — in three volumes: an introduction, the Greek text, and a French translation. The edition rested principally on the great codices, the Marcianus and the Paris manuscripts, and it made the corpus, for the first time, a public object: any scholar in any library could now read Olympiodorus on Zosimos, Christianus on the divine water, the symbol lists, the poems, and the Chrysopoeia tradition, between two covers, with a translation facing the difficulties. Berthelot’s companion works framed the find: Les Origines de l’alchimie (1885) had already reproduced the Chrysopoeia leaf from the Marcianus, and La Chimie au moyen âge (1893) carried the story forward — its second volume, with Rubens Duval, presented the Syriac alchemy of Section X, and its third the Arabic. The modern field’s map of the transmission — Greek, Syriac, Arabic, Latin — is, in its first draft, Berthelot’s table of contents.

The Collection’s limits are part of its history, and a transmission article should state them as plainly as its achievement. The edition was made quickly, from the principal codices without the full collation a modern editor would require, and later scholarship has found its text faulty in detail throughout: the verdict of the mid-twentieth-century literature is that CAAG is a very significant piece of scholarship that was poorly edited — some 450 pages of Greek text that remained, for decades, a kind of terra incognita even to scholars of antiquity, because the material is difficult and the edition did not make it easier. Stephanus, as Section IV noted, was absent from it altogether and had to be read in Ideler. The twentieth century therefore rebuilt the corpus piece by piece: the eight-volume Catalogue des manuscrits alchimiques grecs (Brussels, 1924–32), edited by Joseph Bidez, Franz Cumont, Johan Ludvig Heiberg, Otto Lagercrantz, and their collaborators, described the manuscripts properly for the first time; and the Budé series Les Alchimistes grecs began the slow replacement of Berthelot’s texts with critical editions — Robert Halleux’s volume on the Leyden and Stockholm papyri (1981), and Michèle Mertens’ edition of Zosimos’ Authentic Memoirs (1995), whose introduction remains the standard account of the four Greek witnesses and the Syriac and Arabic traditions around them.

What Berthelot and Ruelle changed, beyond access, was the corpus’s question. To the copyists, the corpus was a working library of the Sacred Art. To Berthelot, a chemist of the first rank and a minister of the French Republic, it was the childhood of his own science: documents in the prehistory of chemistry, to be mined for the first appearances of apparatus, operations, and substance-names. That framing — alchemy as chemistry’s ancestor, judged by what it contributed to chemistry — governed the field for a century and is only partly the framing of the present scholarship, which, following Principe and William Newman among others, reads the texts within the natural philosophy of their own periods. The transmission did not end in 1888. It changed medium: from codex to critical edition, from the scriptorium to the series, with each new editor, like each Byzantine compiler before him, selecting, ordering, and reconciling. IX. What Copying Does: Diagrams Degrade, Commentaries Accrete

A codex tradition is not a photocopier. It is a population of hand-made objects, each produced by a scribe with a exemplar, a deadline, a level of Greek, and — crucially — a level of workshop experience that was usually none. Three characteristic injuries follow, and the Greek alchemical tradition exhibits all three so clearly that it serves historians of texts as a type case.

1. The Text Drifts, Then Freezes

Copyists misread letterforms, skip from one occurrence of a word to its next occurrence further down (the eye-skip every editor dreads), expand abbreviations wrongly, and normalise unfamiliar technical vocabulary toward familiar words. In a classical text with fifty manuscripts, such errors are noise around a recoverable signal. In the alchemical tradition, with three or four principal witnesses, an error in an early exemplar can become the text: if the ancestor of B and A misread a substance-name, both descendants carry the misreading, and only the Marcianus — or a Syriac or Arabic version made from a better Greek copy — can correct it. This is why Mertens’ four groups of Zosimos must be assembled across all the witnesses and versions at once, and why the Arabic Zosimos of Section XII can preserve, in places, a fuller text than the surviving Greek: the Arabic translators’ exemplars were copied before some of the Greek tradition’s surviving injuries occurred.

2. The Diagrams Degrade

A scribe copies words by understanding them, letter by letter; he copies a drawing by looking at it. Apparatus drawings suffer accordingly. The kerotakis in the exemplars was a functional diagram — reflux chamber, vapour tube, condensate return — drawn by someone who knew the device. Copied by scribes who had never seen one, its tubes drift from their junctions, its proportions flatten toward symmetry, and its labels migrate. By the time the drawing reaches Pelecanos in 1478 it is an icon of an apparatus rather than a plan of one: correct in its inventory of parts, unreliable in their connections. Modern reconstructions of Maria’s tribikos and the kerotakis therefore proceed as this archive’s Maria the Jewess article does — by triangulating the drawings against the texts’ verbal descriptions, against the Syriac and Arabic witnesses, and against what a working still must physically do. The image tradition and the text tradition correct each other; neither is sufficient alone. The Chrysopoeia leaf is the limiting case: a diagram that was already symbolic in its exemplar, whose concentric circles and ouroboros could survive copying because their meaning did not depend on draughtsmanship. Symbols are robust to transmission. Plumbing is not.

3. The Commentary Accretes

The third injury is growth. A commented text attracts further comment the way a wreck attracts reef: marginal glosses by readers are copied into the text by the next scribe; a commentator’s reconciliation of two ancients becomes a third authority’s doctrine; excerpts made for one purpose are re-anthologised for another, shedding their contexts. The Byzantine method Berthelot described in Christianus — excerpt and summary on the model of Photius’ Bibliotheca and the Constantinian excerpting projects — institutionalised the process: the empire preserved its literature by digesting it, and digestion changes the meal. The corpus that reached the fifteenth century therefore contains strata its first compilers never put there: Psellos’ eleventh-century reading, Kosmas’ additions, Blemmydes’ thirteenth-century work, the fourteenth- and fifteenth-century hands annotating the Marcianus. To read the corpus historically is to excavate it, layer by layer, and the dating of those layers — Letrouit’s chronology of the Greek alchemists is the famous attempt — is some of the most contested work in the field. None of this is corruption in a moral sense. It is what a living tradition does. But it means the phrase “Greek alchemy” names a thousand years of writing, and any claim about it should be asked which century it belongs to.

X. The Syriac Reception: A Narrow, Documented Bridge

East of the Greek world, the corpus crossed its first language frontier into Syriac, the Aramaic literary language of the Christian East. The Syriac reception of alchemy is real, important, and — this article insists on the adjective — narrow. It is documented by manuscripts and catalogue entries, not by the large narrative the subject sometimes attracts, and its exact documented shape is as follows. A body of treatises attributed to Zosimos was translated into Syriac; some sixty-four pages of Syriac Zosimos survive, in three manuscripts of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. The principal witness is the Cambridge codex Mm. 6.29 (15th century), in which twelve treatises attributed to Zosimos survive. The same codex carries the tradition’s workshop literature alongside them: treatises of Democritus on the preparation of gold and of silver, an extract of various technical receipts, and chemical fragments. The Egyptology division’s Islamic Egyptology (§VIII) states the archive’s adjudicated characterisation of that accompanying material, and this article repeats it without enlarging it: it is recipe material of the same Greek workshop kind as the Leyden and Stockholm papyri — though not a copy of either papyrus itself. Kind, not copy. The distinction is the archive’s, reached in its Egyptology overhaul, and it is the correct strength of the evidence: William Wright’s Catalogue of the Syriac Manuscripts Preserved in the Library of the University of Cambridge (1901) is the catalogue evidence on which the codex’s contents rest, and a catalogue establishes what a manuscript contains, not what its contents descend from.

What the Syriac Zosimos does establish is considerable. The Syriac texts differ from the Greek, and in places preserve in fuller form material the Greek compendia transmit only in abbreviation: a doctrine that looks like a Byzantine summary in Greek can stand in Syriac as the connected argument it originally was. Matteo Martelli’s work has shown the Syriac tradition working at both of the corpus’s levels. At the learned level, a Syriac alchemical treatise attributed to Zosimos — preserved in Mm. 6.29 itself (fols. 120v–129v) — draws heavily on Books IX–XI of Galen’s On Simple Drugs, in the Syriac version descending from Sergius of Reshaina’s translation: Greek pharmacy, in Syriac dress, inside an alchemical treatise. At the workshop level, Syriac collections preserve ink and workshop recipes whose technical information matches the gold- and silver-ink formulas of the Leyden Papyrus X — and one recipe, for dyeing silver sapphire-blue, surfaces in very similar form both in the Syriac Zosimos and in the Latin Mappae Clavicula (its no. 91), at the two far ends of the tradition. The practical stratum travelled in Syriac alongside the theoretical stratum. The fuller treatment is the Egyptology division’s Zosimos, Section VII, which this section follows.

Sergius of Reshaina: The Context, Stated as Context

Behind the Syriac Zosimos stands a translation culture three centuries older than the surviving Syriac alchemical manuscripts, and its founding figure is Sergius of Reshaina (died 536). Sergius was a physician and priest from Reshaina in northern Mesopotamia, trained at Alexandria in the school of Ammonius, who became the pioneer of Greek-to-Syriac translation: the ninth-century translator Hunayn ibn Ishaq credits him with Syriac versions of some thirty works of Galen — Hunayn’s own list names twenty-six medical texts — including the On Simple Drugs whose later books the Syriac Zosimos would quarry. Sergius also translated Aristotle’s Categories and Porphyry’s Introduction, wrote the first Aristotelian commentary in Syriac, and rendered theological works of pseudo-Dionysius. His translations were the first significant renderings of Greek medical and philosophical writing into a Semitic language, and they founded the practice — Greek into Syriac first, Syriac into Arabic later — on which the Baghdad movement of Section XI was built.

The discipline this article owes the reader is to say what Sergius is not evidence for. No surviving evidence shows Sergius translating alchemy. His corpus is medicine, logic, cosmology, and theology; the Syriac alchemical manuscripts are nine centuries younger than his death; and the route by which Zosimos’ treatises entered Syriac — when, where, and by whose hand — is not documented in the catalogues this article relies on. Sergius therefore enters this article as context: proof that by the sixth century a professional Greek-to-Syriac translation practice existed, staffed by Alexandria-trained bilingual scholars, through which Greek science of every kind could pass east — and did, demonstrably, for medicine and philosophy. That alchemy passed through the same culture is the reasonable inference the Syriac manuscripts themselves support. That any named sixth-century translator carried it is an inference beyond them, and it is not made here.

XI. Baghdad: The Translation Movement and the Arabic Reception

The second language frontier was the decisive one. In the eighth and ninth centuries, under Abbasid patronage — caliphal sponsorship of translation begins, on the standard account, under al-Mansur (r. 754–775) and reaches its height under al-Ma’mun (r. 813–833) — Baghdad became the centre of the largest organised translation movement of the pre-print world. Greek medicine, mathematics, astronomy, philosophy, and alchemy were rendered into Arabic, and the mechanics of the movement explain the shape of the Arabic alchemy that resulted. The translators were, in the movement’s earlier years, disproportionately the Syriac-speaking Christian scholars of Mesopotamia, heirs of the practice Sergius founded: because a Greek-to-Syriac tradition existed and a Greek-to-Arabic one did not yet, many works travelled the two-step road — Greek into Syriac, Syriac into Arabic — with figures such as Hunayn ibn Ishaq (d. 873), who revised Sergius’ Galen even as he superseded him, working both steps. The Egyptology division’s formulation, which this article adopts, is that Greek alchemy reached the Islamic world in the eighth- to ninth-century translation movement largely via Syriac.

Alchemy’s place in that movement was real but specific. It was never the movement’s centre — astronomy, medicine, and philosophy commanded the caliphal money — and its early Arabic history is correspondingly harder to document than Galen’s. What the record shows is a literature forming in Arabic across the eighth to tenth centuries: a large pseudoepigraphic production under Greek names (Plato, Aristotle, Hermes, Apollonius of Tyana, Zosimos) and Persian ones (Jamasp, Ostanes); translations whose exact status — translation proper, or Arabic composition on a Greek model — modern scholarship, in Pierre Lory’s summary for the Encyclopaedia Iranica, often cannot determine and does not pretend to; and, by the tenth century, a bibliographer who can catalogue the result. Ibn al-Nadim’s Fihrist (completed 987 CE), the great Baghdad catalogue of books known to its author, lists works under Hermes’ name, names the translators who carried Greek and Syriac material into Arabic, and — the detail Section XII develops — mentions translations of four books by Zosimos, scattered by inconsistent transliteration under garbled forms of his name. A literature that a bookseller can catalogue is a literature that exists at scale. By 987, Arabic alchemy did.

Two features of the Arabic reception distinguish it from every other link in this article’s chain, and both matter for what Latin Europe eventually received. The first is Islamicisation: in Lory’s formulation, alchemy in the Islamic world was not only translated from Greek into Arabic but re-thought within the frame of Islamic conceptions, references, and symbols — a Greek art re-founded as an Arabic science with its own authorities, its own prophetology of Hermes-Idris, and its own institutions. The second is surplus: the Arabic tradition preserves Greek material that the Greek tradition itself lost. Because the translators and their Syriac intermediaries worked from Greek exemplars older than the surviving Byzantine codices’ ancestors in some lines, Arabic versions can be fuller than the Greek that remains — the phenomenon Section IX described from the editor’s side. The next section examines the case where that surplus is largest: Zosimos himself. XII. Zosimos in Arabic: The Book of Pictures and the Book of Keys

The Arabic Zosimos is the great modern surprise of transmission studies, and this article’s angle on it is deliberately narrow: not what the Arabic books teach — the Egyptology division’s Zosimos, Section VIII, treats their contents in detail — but what their existence proves about the mechanics of transmission. The bibliographical ground is the Fihrist’s four books, noted above. Fuat Sezgin, the historian of Islamic science, located fifteen manuscripts of Zosimos material in six libraries — Tehran, Cairo, Istanbul, Gotha, Dublin, and Rampur. Mertens, assessing that material for her edition, judged the Arabic tradition extremely rich and promising while regretting how little of it was accessible. Two works, edited in the Corpus Alchemicum Arabicum (CALA) series under Theodor Abt’s direction, with Wilferd Madelung, have since shown what she meant.

The Book of Pictures (Muṣḥaf al-ṣuwar)

The Book of Pictures is an Arabic compilation of Zosimos’ teaching cast as his dialogue with Theosebeia, organised in thirteen chapters, each introduced by a full image — forty-two coloured illustrations in the surviving copy tradition, making it the earliest known illustrated alchemical book. The pictures are integral to the teaching: Zosimos and Theosebeia appear crowned with sun and moon, held in the hand of a great three-headed being, standing beside an alchemical oven drawn as large as themselves. The text incorporates fragments of works known in Greek, including material from The Sulfurs and the letters to Theosebeia, re-set in a continuous dramatic exchange. The transmission facts are these. The oldest surviving copy is dated 1211 CE (Istanbul, Archaeology Museums Library, MS 1574), identified by Sezgin in 1955 and published in facsimile by Abt and Madelung (CALA II, 2007; English translation 2011). And there was more than one Arabic version: Maslama ibn Qasim al-Qurtubi (d. 964), the Andalusian author of the Rutbat al-ḥakīm, quotes the Muṣḥaf al-ṣuwar extensively and names Zosimos as his master among the pre-Islamic alchemists — but the version Maslama had, as Madelung’s introduction to the Rutba edition establishes, differed significantly from the published version, which represents a translation produced much later in Egypt. The two Arabic versions evidently rest on different Greek originals. One Greek author, two Arabic translations, two Greek exemplars: the Arabic tradition was not a single pipe but a delta. Bink Hallum’s doctoral study of the Arabic Zosimos (Zosimos Arabus, Warburg Institute, 2008) systematised the delta into three groups — genuine translations from Greek, original Arabic works built on Greek knowledge, and outright pseudepigrapha — and Hallum’s review of the Book of Pictures facsimile (Ambix, 2009) identified its further importance: the tome of images is also a source of the Turba Philosophorum, the great dialogue of the philosophers that became one of the Latin West’s most influential alchemical texts. A thread runs from the Arabic Zosimos, through the Turba, into Latin Europe — by a route no Byzantine codex took.

The Book of Keys (Kitāb Mafātīḥ al-ṣanʿa)

The Book of the Keys of the Work is a commentary in ten chapters on an earlier Book of the Ten Keys, presented at its opening as Zosimos’ last text written for Theosebeia — his summation, offered with the claim that after it she would need no other book. Its Arabic edition and English translation (Abt and Madelung, CALA III) display the same transmission signature as the Pictures: Greek substance, Arabic literary form, and a curatorial intelligence arranging the master’s remains into a curriculum — the pictures for the eye first, the keys for the understanding last, the arrangement attributed to the author’s own design for his pupil. A further Arabic witness surfaced as recently as 1995, when translations of Zosimos were identified inside a copy of the Keys of Mercy and Secrets of Wisdom of the Persian alchemist al-Tughra’i (d. 1121): incomplete and non-verbatim, but proof that Zosimos was still being absorbed into new Arabic syntheses a millennium after his death. Set the three transmissions side by side, as the Egyptology article does, and the moral is methodological. The Greek witness is richest in technical apparatus. The Syriac preserves the lettered architecture of the lost encyclopaedia. The Arabic preserves the most continuous voice — Zosimos in dialogue, expounding images, summing up. No one witness is the real Zosimos to the exclusion of the others, and the century of scholarship that built its Zosimos on Berthelot’s Greek alone systematically understated the scale of the lost corpus.

The Arabic versions are witnesses to lost Greek books. Editors cite them as such: a translation can be evidence for its exemplar.

XIII. The Jabir Corpus: Real Debts, Arabic Creation

Every transmission story needs its moment where inheritance becomes invention, and in Arabic alchemy that moment has a name: Jabir ibn Hayyan, in Latin Geber. The corpus attributed to him is Arabic alchemy’s foundation — and the first thing modern scholarship says about it is that it is not what it claims to be. Paul Kraus’ analysis (1942–43) demonstrated, from anachronisms, quotations of Greek works translated only later, and internal contradictions, that the treatises were composed in Iraq by a school of Shi’ite alchemists during the ninth and tenth centuries. The dating the archive uses, following the Egyptology division’s Sections VII and IX, is c. 850–950: “Jabir” is the name of an anonymous school writing under a pseudonym, its traditional master the Imam Ja’far al-Sadiq (d. 765) belonging to the corpus’s own account of itself. About 215 treatises survive in the archive’s count; the total of works attributed to the name across the tradition runs far higher, into the thousands in the largest enumerations — a discrepancy that itself measures the name’s authority. Nothing in this section depends on choosing between those counts. Everything depends on the dating, which is not in serious dispute.

The corpus’s relationship to Greek sources must therefore be stated in two clauses, neither of which may swallow the other. The debts are real. The Jabirian authors worked inside the inheritance the translation movement delivered: they cite Democritus and the older Greek authorities — though often, as the Egyptology division notes, through later intermediaries rather than directly — they inherit the Greek problem-set of transmutation, the elixir, and the composition of metals, and their conceptual vocabulary descends from Greek natural philosophy as Arabic had remade it. And the corpus is not a translation. It is an Arabic creation: a systematic literature, composed in Arabic, in Iraq, two centuries after the conquests, which reorganised the inheritance into doctrines no Greek text contains. Its early form of the sulfur–mercury theory of metals — all metals composed of sulfur and mercury in varying purity and proportion — became the dominant theory of metallic composition into the eighteenth century. Its science of the balance (ʿilm al-mīzān) attempted to reduce every substance to quantitative proportions of the four natures, hot, cold, wet, and dry, computed in the corpus’s arithmology from the numerical values of the letters of its Arabic name, the hidden natures falling in the proportion 1:3:5:8. Its classification of substances is the oldest known systematic one; its instructions for deriving sal ammoniac from organic materials are the oldest known of their kind. A translation transmits. This corpus built — on Greek foundations, in an Arabic idiom, toward ends its Greek sources did not pursue.

The Latin sequel belongs to Section XVII, but one distinction must be fixed here, because the whole later confusion of the Geber tradition grows from it. A Latin reader met Jabir as Geber through genuine translations — Gerard of Cremona’s rendering of the Book of Seventy chief among them. The famous Latin Summa perfectionis, the most influential alchemical treatise of the later Middle Ages, is not such a translation: it is a separate, later work by an anonymous Latin author — pseudo-Geber, usually identified in current scholarship with the Franciscan Paul of Taranto — writing under Jabir’s borrowed name around the end of the thirteenth century. Arabic Jabir and Latin pseudo-Geber are two corpora, two centuries and two languages apart, sharing a name. The archive keeps them separate, and any account of “Geber’s” influence that does not say which Geber it means has failed its first transmission test.

XIV. Khalid ibn Yazid and Morienus: The Tradition’s Own Account, Labelled as Tradition

Arabic alchemy told a story about its own beginning, and the story became so successful that Latin Europe learned it as history. In the tradition’s account, the Umayyad prince Khalid ibn Yazid (c. 668–704 or 709), son of the caliph Yazid I and a disappointed candidate for the caliphate, turned from power to wisdom: he sought out the Byzantine monk Morienus — in Arabic Maryanus, the Roman hermit of Jerusalem, himself presented as a pupil of Stephanus of Alexandria — learned the art from him in dialogue, and ordered Greek philosophers in Egypt to translate the books of the art from Greek and Coptic into Arabic, thereby founding the translation movement itself. Ibn al-Nadim’s Fihrist transmits the translation part of the story, crediting Khalid as the first to have books of the art translated in Islam. The dialogue part survives as a book: the Arabic Masāʾil Khālid li-Maryānus al-rāhib — Khalid’s Questions to the Monk Maryanus — in which the monk instructs the prince. It is that book, in Robert of Chester’s Latin, that opens the Latin door in Section XVI. The chain the story asserts is elegant: Stephanus teaches Morienus, Morienus teaches Khalid, Khalid founds Arabic alchemy, Arabic alchemy reaches Latin. Elegance is the first thing a historian should suspect.

The modern assessment, stated at the strength the scholarship supports, is as follows. Julius Ruska’s study of the Arabic alchemists (1924) and Manfred Ullmann’s work on Khalid (1978) showed that Khalid’s alchemy is a legend built up in Arabic literary circles — in the formulation of the recent literature, a construction of the ninth and tenth centuries. The alchemical writings attributed to Khalid are pseudepigrapha, dating at the earliest from the eighth or ninth centuries; the Arabic Masāʾil itself belongs to the works associated with his name that are widely regarded as ninth- or tenth-century compositions, and one manuscript’s citation of an early tenth-century work of Ibn Umayl suggests the dialogue may have been composed in the latter half of the tenth century. Ullmann’s proposed mechanism is a gloss that grew: an early description of Khalid as pursuing what he could not attain — the caliphate, on the political reading — was reinterpreted as pursuing the unattainable art, and the reinterpretation accreted a biography. Pierre Lory’s alternative proposal gives the attribution a social logic: writings from a humbler milieu were fathered on an Umayyad prince to lend them nobility. The two proposals differ in mechanism and agree in conclusion. Khalid the historical prince — poet, hadith student, failed heir — is documented. Khalid the alchemist is the tradition’s foundation charter, composed centuries after his death.

Why, then, does this article give the legend a full section? Because a foundation charter is itself a transmission fact of the first order. The Morienus story did real work in the world. It gave Arabic alchemy a pedigree that reached, through Stephanus, to the imperial school of Constantinople and, through Khalid, to the Prophet’s own century and house — a pedigree that authorised the art in a culture that asked of any science who its master’s master was. It gave the translators a patron before the Abbasids, pushing the art’s Arabic birthday back into the Umayyad age. And it gave Latin Europe, in 1144, not a recipe collection but a scene: a monk, a prince, a dialogue, a secret handed over in conversation — the form in which Latin alchemy would imagine its own transmission for centuries, in dialogue after dialogue of master and disciple. The historian’s task is not to debunk the scene but to file it correctly: tradition, the archive’s second tier — an ancient (here, medieval) account of the past, precious as evidence for the culture that told it, unusable as evidence for the events it narrates. The documented transmission — codices, catalogues, dated translations — is Sections V to XIII and XV to XVII. This section is the story the transmitters told themselves about what they were doing, and it is labelled accordingly wherever this hub repeats it.

// THE KHALID FILE, SUMMARISED //

Documented: Khalid ibn Yazid, Umayyad prince, c. 668–704/709; a reputation for learning in poetry and hadith. Attributed from the 9th century on: alchemical treatises and poems under his name; the tutelage of Morienus; the sponsorship of the first Greek-to-Arabic translations. Modern assessment (Ruska 1924; Ullmann 1978; Lory): the alchemical Khalid is a pseudepigraphic legend of the 9th–10th centuries. First caliphal sponsorship of translation, on the standard account, belongs to the Abbasid al-Mansur (r. 754–775), two generations after Khalid’s death.

XV. The Latin Silence: How Shut the Door Was

It is tempting to imagine the Latin West waiting outside the door of Section XVI, pressing its ear to the wood. The record suggests something quieter: for alchemy proper, there was effectively no one home. Before 1144 the Latin West possesses, on the standard accounts this article follows, essentially no alchemical literature — no treatise on the transmutation of metals, no elixir theory, no Latin Zosimos, no Latin Democritus of the art. The word itself tells the story: Latin alchimia is a loan from Arabic al-kīmiyāʾ, and it enters Latin with the translations. A discipline whose very name had to be imported had not been practised, as a literature, in the importing language.

The precision matters, because the silence was specific to alchemy, not to knowledge transfer in general — and the exceptions map the channels that alchemy, for whatever reason, did not use. Medicine crossed. In the late eleventh century Constantine the African, working at Monte Cassino and in the orbit of Salerno, translated a large body of Arabic medicine into Latin — the Pantegni, his version of ‘Ali ibn al-‘Abbas al-Majusi’s Royal Book, among it — and thereby refounded Latin learned medicine on the Greco-Arabic synthesis two generations before Robert of Chester. Lapidary and craft knowledge crossed. The Latin lapidary tradition, descending from Damigeron and Plinian material and flowering in Marbode of Rennes’ verse De lapidibus around the turn of the twelfth century, transmitted lore of stones’ powers without alchemical theory. The recipe stratum crossed too: the Mappae Clavicula, a Latin compilation of workshop recipes with Greek-derived material, preserves — as Martelli’s comparison in Section X showed — a silver-dyeing recipe with a close Syriac parallel. Furnaces, inks, dyes, and medicines travelled. What did not travel, before 1144, was the art as a doctrine: the theory of transmutation, the elixir, the commented corpus, the dialogue of master and disciple. The Latin West had pieces of the workshop for centuries. It had none of the library.

Why the delay? The honest answer assembles several partial causes and labels the assembly as interpretation. Alchemy stood at the edge of the Baghdad movement’s priorities (Section XI), and the Latin translation movement, when it came, followed the same priorities in the same order — astronomy and medicine first, because courts and cathedral schools demanded them. Alchemy’s Arabic literature was also, in part, a guarded literature: the Jabirian corpus presents its teaching as the guarded heritage of an initiatic line, and the Morienus dialogue dramatises a secret disclosed to one worthy prince. And the channels mattered: the Latin movement ran through Spain and Sicily, through bilingual frontier cities, and it reached alchemy when a translator working in Spain — an Englishman, as it happens — chose an alchemical book. The door was not locked by anyone’s decree. It was simply at the end of a corridor the translators walked in order, and they reached it in 1144.

Arabic medicine in Constantine the African’s Latin versions (late 11th c.); the lapidary tradition; workshop recipe material of the Mappae Clavicula type.

DID NOT CROSS

Transmutation theory; the elixir; the sulfur–mercury doctrine; the commented Greek corpus in any form; the master–disciple alchemical dialogue.

THE TELLING ABSENCE

No Latin word for the art existed before the Arabic loan arrived. Alchimia enters Latin as a foreign substance’s name — in Robert’s title, it names the elixir itself before it names the discipline.

XVI. 1144: Robert of Chester and the Liber de compositione alchemiae

The door has a date, a translator, and a colophon. Robert of Chester, an English Arabist working in Spain — the same Robert who had translated the Qur’an for Peter the Venerable in 1143 and would translate al-Khwarizmi’s Algebra in 1145 — completed his Latin version of the Morienus dialogue on 11 February 1144. The date rests on the work’s colophon, which gives the year as 1182 of the Spanish era, the era that ran thirty-eight years ahead of the Christian count: 1182 less 38 is 1144. The Latin book’s titles multiply in the manuscripts — Liber de compositione alchemiae, the Book on the Composition of Alchemy; Testamentum Morieni, the Testament of Morienus; simply Morienus — and its Arabic source is the Masāʾil Khālid li-Maryānus al-rāhib of Section XIV, though the Latin and Arabic texts each contain sections the other lacks: the translation, like every link before it, was also an editing. The work is generally considered the first Latin translation of an Arabic work on alchemy. With it, the Latin West acquires, in a single short book, the art’s foundation scene (the monk and the prince), its founding name (Hermes, presented in the preface’s genealogy of three Hermeses), and its central promise, the composition of the elixir.

The preface transmitted under Robert’s name contains the most quoted sentence in the history of transmission, and this article handles it under the rule stated in Section IX. In paraphrase — labelled paraphrase, not quotation — the translator tells his Latin readers that the book before them is the composition of alchemy, and that since their Latin world does not yet know what alchemy is or what its composition is, he will explain it in the present text; he then gives the definition he attributes to Hermes and his successors, in which alchemy is a material substance taken from one thing and composed by one, joining the most precious substances by affinity and effect and transforming them by natural mixture into better substances. Two scholarly cautions attach to the famous sentence, and both are recorded in the recent literature (Dapsens 2016; Moureau 2020, summarising Lemay and Kahn): the attribution of the preface to Robert himself has been questioned, though the dating of the translation does not depend on that attribution; and the word alchemia in the book’s title does not yet mean the art of alchemy — it names the mysterious transmuting substance itself, the elixir, so that the title is more exactly the Book on the Composition of the Elixir. The paraphrase above is the sense the standard accounts agree on. Readers who need the Latin wording should take it from a named edition, as the archive’s rule requires, and not from any article’s memory of it — including this one.

What kind of threshold is 1144, exactly? Not the arrival of Greek alchemy in the West: nothing Greek arrived in 1144. What arrived was an Arabic book, of tenth-century-ish composition (Section XIV), dramatising a seventh-century scene, about a seventh-century Byzantine monk’s teaching, itself rooted in the third-century world of Zosimos and, behind him, in the Hermetica’s Egypt. The Latin West’s first alchemical book is therefore a transmission artefact in its entirety — Greek substance, Syriac and Arabic mediation, Latin dress — and its own preface knows it: a translator explaining an untranslated word to a world that lacks it is the transmission process describing itself. Nor did 1144 flood the West overnight. The Morienus circulated in manuscript, was printed only in the sixteenth century (Paris, 1559, in its first printing), and was followed across the next century and a half by the slow accumulation of Section XVII. A door’s opening is an event. The traffic through it is a history.

The same conversion governs Robert’s other dated work of these years and is the basis on which Karpinski and, following the Arabic-original scholarship, al-Hassan give 11 February 1144 for the Liber de compositione alchemiae.

One more honesty belongs to this section. Robert’s book was not the only candidate for “first” that the corridor produced, and the archive’s claim is the standard, narrower one: the first dated Latin translation of an Arabic alchemical work, and the earliest such translation generally recognised. Undated translations and recipe material complicate every absolute first in this field, and Section XV has already recorded the recipe stratum’s earlier crossings. What 1144 gives the historian is not a metaphysical beginning but a dated, named, checkable event — a translator, a place in Spain’s translation corridor, a colophon, a preface that announces a new subject to its readers. Beginnings that can be checked are worth more than beginnings that can only be celebrated.

XVII. Toledo After the Threshold: Gerard of Cremona and the Century That Followed

The traffic, when it came, ran through Toledo. Reconquered by Christian forces in 1085, the city held a great Arabic library tradition and a population in which Arabic, Romance, Latin, and Hebrew scholarship met; its cathedral library became the most important centre of the twelfth-century translation movement. The movement’s most productive translator was Gerard of Cremona (c. 1114–1187), who, by his students’ account, came to Toledo seeking Ptolemy’s Almagest, learned Arabic, and stayed for life. The medieval list of his translations credits him with some seventy to eighty-seven works — astronomy, mathematics, medicine, philosophy, and, within that flood, alchemy. Among the alchemical translations the medieval list assigns to the magister is the one that matters most for this article: Jabir’s Book of Seventy (Kitāb al-Sabʿīn), rendered as the Liber de septuaginta before Gerard’s death in 1187 — the Jabir corpus’s principal door into Latin, complementing Robert’s Morienus door of a generation before. A scholarly wrinkle is recorded for completeness: the manuscript Berthelot used for his 1906 edition of the Seventy names a certain Renaldus Cremonensis as translator, while the medieval list of Gerard’s works includes the Liber de septuaginta among his three alchemical translations; the attribution to Gerard rests on the list, and the wrinkle is part of the record (Burnett 2001; Moureau 2020).

Around those two doors the century’s alchemical translations accumulated. The standard enumerations of the Arabic-to-Latin alchemical traffic between 1144 and 1300 include: the Turba Philosophorum, the dialogue of the ancients whose Arabic ancestry reaches back, in part, to the Arabic Zosimos of Section XII; the Tabula Smaragdina, the Emerald Tablet attributed to Hermes; the Secret of Creation of Balinus (pseudo-Apollonius of Tyana); the De aluminibus et salibus, On Alums and Salts, transmitted under al-Razi’s name; material of al-Razi’s Secret of Secrets; and the Jabirian Book of Seventy and Great Book of Mercy. By the mid-thirteenth century, on Principe’s account, the Latin West had begun producing alchemical writings of its own — Albertus Magnus discussing the sulfur–mercury theory in his De mineralibus, Roger Bacon advocating the art — and by its end the pseudo-Geber Summa perfectionis (Section XIII) had given Latin alchemy its most systematic native treatise, under an Arabic name, in a Latin voice. The transmission had completed its characteristic final step: the receiving culture no longer only translated the art. It wrote it, and signed the writing with the transmitters’ names.

Toledo was not the only door, and this article’s focus on it should not harden into a single-route myth. Sicily, under its Norman kings, sustained its own Arabic–Greek–Latin court culture; the Crusader Levant mixed the languages in Antioch and the coastal cities; Jewish scholars mediated between Arabic and Hebrew and, in al-Andalus especially, between Arabic and the vernaculars. But for alchemy’s dated, named, book-by-book entry into Latin, the Spanish corridor — Robert in the 1140s, Gerard’s Toledo circle in the decades after — is where the record is densest, and density of record, in transmission history, is what a route is. The Egyptology division’s Islamic Egyptology (§XVIII) treats the same Toledo movement from the Hermetic side, including the Picatrix route through Alfonso X’s Castilian — the astral-magical counterpart to this section’s alchemical one — and the necessary counterpoint that the philosophical Corpus Hermeticum itself reached the Renaissance by an entirely different road, in Greek, from Byzantium, in 1460. Two Hermetic literatures, two doors, three centuries apart. Keeping the routes separate is the archive’s standing rule, and it is the whole subject of this article in miniature.

XVIII. Three Languages, One Art: What Transmission Changed

Stand back from the chain and its shape is a lesson in what survival costs. Greek alchemy composed perhaps a library; what crossed into Byzantium was an anthology. What crossed into Syriac was a selection within the anthology — twelve treatises of one author and a shelf of workshop material. What crossed into Arabic was more than crossed into Syriac, because the Arabic translators reached Greek exemplars the anthology tradition had already thinned, and what Arabic made of the crossing was not a selection but a civilisation’s science: classified, systematised, and re-founded, with Zosimos curated into dialogues of pictures and keys and the whole inheritance rebuilt in the Jabirian corpus. What crossed into Latin in 1144 was, at first, one short Arabic dialogue about a substance whose name Latin did not have. Each crossing lost most of what stood on the far bank. Each crossing also added the receiving culture’s own work — commentary, system, theory, literature — so that the art that finally stood in Latin Europe was richer in doctrine than the art that had left Alexandria, and poorer in text. Transmission is not a bucket brigade. It is a series of re-foundations, and the historian’s gratitude and suspicion should be distributed equally among them.

For the reader of this archive, the chain explains the shape of everything in the wings around this article. It explains why Zosimos must be read in three languages at once, and why his most continuous voice speaks Arabic. It explains why Maria’s apparatus reaches us as Byzantine drawings of Alexandrian devices, to be reconstructed by triangulation rather than read off the page. It explains why the Hermetica’s name rides on alchemical books the philosophical Hermetica never wrote: the name travelled with the art, through the art’s channels, and arrived in Latin in an alchemical preface of 1144 centuries before the Corpus Hermeticum arrived in Greek in 1460. And it explains the archive’s own architecture. The wings that follow this one — the Arabic golden age in its fullness, the medieval Latin art that Robert and Gerard made possible, and beyond them the Indian traditions toward which the archive’s historical sequence moves — all stand downstream of the corridor this article has walked: Alexandria to Constantinople, Constantinople to the Syriac schools, the schools to Baghdad, Baghdad to Toledo, and a single dated February morning in 1144 on which the corridor reached Latin.

// THE THRESHOLD //

On the eleventh of February, 1144, an English translator in Spain set down the last sentence of a short Arabic book, and the Latin world had alchemy.

Behind that sentence stood eleven centuries: a Panopolitan teacher dictating to his sister by letter; Alexandrian professors reconciling him with Democritus from their chairs; an emperor’s lecturer binding the art to the quadrivium; Byzantine scribes painting its furnaces in four colours and losing its quires to the binder; Syriac Christians carrying its treatises east in the language of Galen’s translators; Baghdad remaking it into a science of balances and elixirs; and a monk who may never have lived, teaching a prince who never learned, in a dialogue that taught Europe how a secret is handed over. Every hand changed what it held. The art arrived altered, abbreviated, systematised, and alive.

This article closes the Greco-Roman hub’s alchemy wing at the door its subject spent a millennium reaching. What stands on the other side — the Latin Middle Ages in their furnaces and summae, the Arabic golden age in its full extent, and beyond them the Indian alchemical traditions toward which this archive’s history moves next — belongs to the wings that follow. The door is dated. The door is named. The door, as of 1144, stands open.
