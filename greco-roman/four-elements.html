I. A System, Not a List: What the Tetrad Is

This article opens a new cluster in the Alchemy wing of the Greco-Roman hub: the Foundations. The wing's other articles begin where the texts begin — with a workshop, a recipe collection, a named practitioner at a bench. This cluster begins one level down, with the matter-theory those practitioners took for granted. An Alexandrian worker who heated a body to drive off its moisture, who sealed a vessel so that what rose would condense and fall back, who spoke of a metal's body and the spirit that could be drawn out of it and fixed in it again, was not inventing a physics on the spot. He was using one — the oldest continuously used physics in the Western record, the doctrine that the perceptible world is compounded of four elements: fire, air, water, and earth.

The first thing to understand about the tetrad is that it is a system, not a list. A list of four stuffs would be a curiosity. What Empedocles proposed in the fifth century BCE, what Plato mathematised in the fourth, and what Aristotle rebuilt into the most durable natural philosophy of antiquity is a machine for explaining change: a small stock of permanent constituents, a rule for how they combine, a pair of motive powers or an equivalent mechanism to do the combining, and — in the Aristotelian form that antiquity, medicine, and alchemy actually inherited — a derivation of the four stuffs themselves from two pairs of contrary qualities, the hot and the cold, the wet and the dry. Once the qualities are in place, the elements stop being four brute facts and become the four possible answers to a two-question test. That derivation, drawn as a square, is the single most consequential diagram in this article, and Section X gives it in full. Stated in advance, exactly as this hub states it everywhere: Fire is hot and dry; Air is hot and wet; Water is cold and wet; Earth is cold and dry.

THE FOUR, AS STUFFS

Empedocles' four “roots” (rhizōmata): fire, air (aithēr, the bright upper air), water, and earth — eternal, ungenerated, imperishable, and never transformed into one another. Everything else is their mixture and separation. See Sections IV–V.

THE FOUR, AS QUALITY-COMPOUNDS

Aristotle's four “simple bodies”: each is prime matter qualified by one term from each contrary pair — hot or cold, wet or dry. So defined, the elements can transform into one another, by the exchange of a quality. See Sections IX–X.

The difference between those two cards is the plot of this article. For Empedocles, the roots are primitive and changeless, and the world's variety is arrangement. For Aristotle, the qualities are primitive, the elements are compounds of them, and the world's variety includes genuine transformation of element into element — the very thing Empedocles' scheme forbade and the very thing an art of transmutation would later need its physics to allow. Plato stands between them, giving the tetrad a mathematics — proportion first, then geometry — and a craftsman to impose it. The Stoics and Galen then carry the scheme into the two domains where it would live longest, cosmology and medicine; and the alchemists of Roman Egypt receive it, at the end of that long transmission, not as a theory to be debated but as the grammar in which operations are described. Section XIV, the article's closing substantive section, follows the tetrad into their hands.

In practice the article paraphrases throughout, and paraphrases of ancient texts are labelled as paraphrase in the text. The handful of short verbatim quotations — from Empedocles fragment 6 in the translation printed in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's Empedocles entry, from Plato's Timaeus in Benjamin Jowett's translation, and from Aristotle's On Generation and Corruption in H. H. Joachim's translation — are each flagged at the point of use, with the translator named. // WORDS FOR “ELEMENT” //

Empedocles did not call his four “elements”. His word is rhizōmata, “roots”. The Greek word later standard for element, stoicheion (plural stoicheia), originally means a letter of the alphabet — one item in an ordered row — and its application to the physical constituents is first securely attested in Plato's circle and after. Aristotle, looking back, credits Empedocles with being the first to distinguish the four clearly (Metaphysics A 4, 985a31–3, in paraphrase), while using the later vocabulary to do it. This article follows the same convention: “roots” for Empedocles' own conception, “elements” for the doctrine in general, and “simple bodies” where Aristotle's technical usage is in view.

One framing caution, because popular accounts blur it. The four elements of this article are not the elements of modern chemistry, and the resemblance of the word conceals an opposite conception. A modern element is defined by atomic structure and is, in ordinary chemical process, exactly what does not change into another element. The ancient tetrad, in its Aristotelian form, is defined by sensible qualities and is exactly what does change: water heated and rarefied becomes air-like, air condensed becomes water-like, and the theory's whole point is to make such changes intelligible rather than miraculous. Nor are the ancient “elements” simply the stuffs of everyday experience under their everyday names. Aristotle is explicit, in paraphrase of On Generation and Corruption II.3, that the simple body corresponding to fire is “such-as-fire”, not the flame on the hearth — ordinary fire is an excess, a kind of boiling of the hot and dry, as ice is an excess, a freezing of the cold and wet. The tetrad names four poles of a qualitative space, approximated by familiar stuffs, not four samples in jars. Keep that in view and most of the scheme's apparent naivety dissolves; what remains is a serious, wrong, and extraordinarily productive theory of matter.

II. Before Four: The Single-Stuff Theories and the Problem of Change

The tetrad was an answer, and it cannot be understood without the question. Greek natural philosophy before Empedocles had spent a century asking what the world is fundamentally made of, and its first answers were singular. The tradition Aristotle reports, and modern scholarship reconstructs with due caution from his reports and the surviving fragments, runs as follows — each item here is a paraphrase of the standard account in Kirk, Raven and Schofield's The Presocratic Philosophers (2nd ed., 1983), the reference this article follows for the early material.

THALES OF MILETUS (fl. c. 585 BCE)

Water as the originating stuff (archē): the earth floats on water; moisture nourishes all things; the seeds of things are moist. Known almost entirely through Aristotle's retrospective report, and to be handled as such.

ANAXIMANDER (c. 610–c. 546 BCE)

Not a familiar stuff but the “indefinite” (to apeiron): an unbounded, ageless source from which the opposites — hot and cold, wet and dry among them — are separated out, and to which they return. The opposites, note, are already doing cosmological work a century before the tetrad.

ANAXIMENES (fl. c. 545 BCE)

Air (aēr) as the basic stuff, other stuffs arising from it by rarefaction and condensation: rarefied, air becomes fire; condensed, it becomes wind, cloud, water, earth, and finally stone. One stuff, one mechanism, a graded series — the direct ancestor of every later density-based account.

HERACLITUS OF EPHESUS (fl. c. 500 BCE)

Fire as the emblem and measure of a world that is process before it is stuff: all things exchange for fire and fire for all things, as goods for gold and gold for goods (DK 22 B 90, in paraphrase). Whether Heraclitus held a material monism at all, or a doctrine of ordered change in which fire is first among equals, is disputed; the tetrad's authors read him, in any case, as fire's partisan.

Each single-stuff theory faces the same embarrassment, and Aristotle states it against all of them in On Generation and Corruption II: if everything is really one stuff, then the manifest differences among things — hot against cold, dry against wet, heavy against light — must be explained as modifications of that stuff, and the modifications quietly become the real theory. Anaximenes' mechanism is the honest version of the problem: rarefaction and condensation are already two opposed processes doing the work that opposed qualities would later do openly. Pluralism — the move Empedocles makes — simply promotes the modifications to constituents: instead of one stuff in many states, several stuffs in many mixtures.

Parmenides: The Challenge That Set the Terms

The pluralists were not only answering the Milesians. They were answering Parmenides of Elea (born c. 515 BCE), whose poem mounted the most consequential argument of the century: in paraphrase of fragment DK 28 B 8, what-is cannot have come from what-is-not, for what-is-not cannot be thought or said; what-is therefore neither comes to be nor perishes, is one, continuous, ungenerated and imperishable, and the world of birth, death, motion, and variety that the senses report is, strictly, unintelligible. The argument is a trap with three exits, and the next generation of Greek physics can be read as a choice among them. Accept the logic and multiply the unchangeable: posit not one Parmenidean being but several, each satisfying Parmenides' conditions — ungenerated, imperishable, unalterable in itself — and let apparent change be their rearrangement. That is Empedocles' exit, and the atomists' after him. Or accept the logic and rebuild change itself on a new analysis, distinguishing the senses in which a thing can come to be. That, two generations later, is Aristotle's exit. Plato, characteristically, takes both roads in different works. The tetrad is born, in other words, as a Parmenidean pluralism: four little beings, each eternal, doing together what the one Being could not do alone — underwrite a changing world without anything ever, at bottom, being born or dying.

Empedocles states the inheritance himself, in paraphrase of fragments DK 31 B 8 and B 12: there is no birth of any mortal thing, nor any end in destructive death, but only mixture and the separation of what has been mixed; and nothing can come to be from what in no way is, nor can what-is be destroyed, for wherever it is, there it will always be. “Birth” and “death” are names men give to the mixtures and separations. The sentence could be Parmenides' in its logic; its physics is the tetrad's charter. What the roots and their powers are, the next three sections set out.

// WHY THIS PREHISTORY MATTERS TO THE WING //

Two habits of thought that alchemy never loses are already visible here. First, the explanation of manifest difference by a small set of opposed powers — hot/cold, wet/dry, rarefying/condensing — rather than by an open-ended list of substances. Second, the conservation instinct: nothing in the work is created or annihilated; it is separated, recombined, purified, and fixed. When a late-antique recipe speaks of a body “dying” and being “revived”, it is speaking Empedocles' licensed fiction — the names men give to mixture and separation — with full theoretical backing. See Section XIV.

III. Empedocles of Acragas: The Poet, the Poems, and the Evidence

Empedocles was a citizen of Acragas (Agrigento) in Sicily, born in the early fifth century BCE — conventionally c. 495–c. 435 BCE, the dates used in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's entry, which this article follows — into an aristocratic family: his grandfather, of the same name, is remembered as an Olympic victor in the horse races. The biographical tradition, preserved chiefly in Diogenes Laertius' Lives (Book VIII), written some seven centuries later, makes him a democratic champion who broke an oligarchic council of the Thousand and refused a kingship, a physician and founder of the Sicilian medical tradition, a rhetorician credited by Aristotle with the invention of rhetoric, and a wonder-worker who presented himself, in his own verse, as a god gone among mortals. The same tradition kills him picturesquely — leaping into the crater of Etna, which obligingly returned one of his bronze sandals — and modern scholarship is unanimous that the leap is legend, manufactured out of the poems' own imagery. The safe biographical sentence is short: a wealthy Acragantine of the democratic side, active in the decades after the fall of the Sicilian tyrannies, who wrote didactic hexameter poetry of extraordinary force on nature and on purification, and whose physics — unusually for a Presocratic — was still being argued with, line by line, by Plato and Aristotle a century later.

That last fact is why his evidence base is comparatively good. Empedocles wrote, according to the ancient tradition, two poems: On Nature (Peri physeōs), addressed to his disciple Pausanias, setting out the roots, the powers, the cosmic cycle, and a biology; and the Purifications (Katharmoi), addressed to his fellow citizens, setting out a doctrine of the fallen divine being (daimōn), its exile and transmigrations, and the pure life that shortens the road back. Neither poem survives whole. What survives is some 450 lines, preserved as quotations in later authors — Aristotle and his commentators above all, but also Plutarch, Sextus Empiricus, Clement of Alexandria, and the doxographical tradition descending from Theophrastus — plus one physical windfall: the Strasbourg Papyrus, fragments of a papyrus roll of the poem, identified in the Strasbourg University Library and published by Alain Martin and Oliver Primavesi in 1999, which recovered lines of On Nature previously unknown and confirmed the ordering of others.

// ONE POEM OR TWO? A LIVE DISPUTE, STATED FAIRLY //

Whether On Nature and the Purifications are two poems or one project in two registers has been contested since Catherine Osborne's 1987 challenge to the two-poem orthodoxy, with Brad Inwood, Simon Trépanier, and others developing single-work or closely-unified readings. The Strasbourg Papyrus materially strengthened the unity side: it contains lines traditionally assigned to the Purifications in physical continuity with the physical poem, showing that if there are two works they are far more closely related than the older picture — physics here, religion there — allowed. The two-poem division remains the conventional filing system, and this article uses the traditional titles for convenience, as the reference literature does. But the reader should register what the dispute means for the tetrad: the roots and the powers are not, for Empedocles, a secular mechanism onto which a religion was later pasted. Love and Strife are cosmic powers and moral facts in a single vision, and the same poem that proportions bone out of earth, water, and fire also legislates against bloodshed. The alchemical tradition, which would later refuse exactly this separation, was in that respect a faithful heir.

The fragments are cited, by universal modern convention, from Hermann Diels and Walther Kranz, Die Fragmente der Vorsokratiker (6th ed., 1951–52), where Empedocles is chapter 31: A-texts are ancient testimony about him, B-texts are the fragments themselves, numbered B 1 to B 154 in the usual count. Every DK number in this article was checked against the discussions in Kirk–Raven–Schofield and the Stanford Encyclopedia entry listed in the Sources. Two warnings travel with the numbering. A fragment is a quotation torn from a poem: its context is lost, its placing in the poem is reconstruction, and in a handful of famous cases the reconstruction is contested. And the translations differ — sometimes, on contested lines, materially. This article therefore paraphrases Empedocles as a rule, and quotes verbatim only the one passage flagged in Section I.

IV. The Four Roots: Rhizōmata

The doctrine, in its author's own opening move, is a list with divine names. The one verbatim quotation this article permits itself from Empedocles is the list itself, fragment DK 31 B 6, given here in the translation printed in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's Empedocles entry:

“Hear first of all the four roots of all things: Zeus the gleaming, Hera who gives life, Aidoneus, and Nêstis, who moistens with her tears the mortal fountain.” EMPEDOCLES, DK 31 B 6, AS TRANSLATED IN THE STANFORD ENCYCLOPEDIA OF PHILOSOPHY

The names are a riddle the tradition began solving immediately and has never quite finished. That Nestis — a Sicilian water goddess — is water is secured by her tears and her fountain. The other three assignments have been disputed since antiquity: the doxographer Aëtius identifies Hera with air and Aidoneus with earth, while other ancient reports distribute the names differently, and modern editors still flag the passage as contested. The majority modern reading, reflected in Kirk–Raven–Schofield and the reference literature, takes gleaming Zeus as fire (or as the bright aithēr in its fiery aspect), life-bearing Hera as air in Aëtius' scheme or earth in a rival one, and Aidoneus — Hades under a euphemistic name — as the remaining term. What is not disputed is the referent set: the four roots are fire, air, water, and earth, confirmed by fragments (B 17, B 21, B 109 among them) that name the stuffs plainly. The divine names do interpretive work beyond camouflage. Each root, bearing a god's name, is presented as in some sense alive and active — a point recent scholarship (Catherine Rowett's work is the reference usually cited) has pressed against the older picture of the roots as inert matter awaiting external forces. The roots are not billiard balls. They are powers in their own right, which is one reason the boundary between “stuff” and “force” in Empedocles has always been hard to police.

What a Root Is: The Parmenidean Specification

Each root satisfies, individually, the conditions Parmenides laid down for what-is. In paraphrase of B 17 and its companion fragments: the roots are equal to one another and the same in age; each presides over its own province and has its own character; nothing is added to them and nothing is taken away; they do not come to be and they do not perish; running through one another they become now this and now that, while remaining, in themselves, continually the same. Three consequences define the system.

1. NO TRANSMUTATION

A root never becomes another root. Fire does not turn into water, in Empedocles, ever. All change is mixture and separation of changeless constituents — the exact point on which Aristotle will break with him (Section IX).

2. NO VOID

Nothing is empty of the roots; the whole is full. Motion is therefore not motion into emptiness but interchange of place — the roots “incessantly exchange their places”, in the B 17 paraphrase — a plenum physics, against which atomism's void is the rival solution to the same Parmenidean problem.

3. PROPORTION IS IDENTITY

What a compound is is fixed by the ratio of roots in it. Bone, blood, flesh, and the rest differ not in kind of stuff but in recipe — see the ratios in Section V. The painter's palette, not the sculptor's block, is the system's governing image.

A word on “air”. Empedocles' word for the second root is aithēr, the bright upper air, the shining medium of the heavens — not aēr, the murky lower atmosphere of mist and cloud. The distinction is old in Greek (Homer's gods dwell in aithēr above the aēr) and it matters twice over in this article. It matters for Empedocles, whose air-root is a celestial, luminous stuff, nearer to fire in character than the damp exhalations of the lowlands. And it matters for Aristotle, who will take the word aithēr over for something else entirely — the fifth body of the heavens (Section XI) — while demoting his air-element to the aēr register. Readers who meet “aether” in later alchemical and occult literature are meeting a word with at least three ancient jobs, and the jobs must not be conflated: Empedoclean bright air, one root among four; Aristotelian celestial body, expressly not one of the four; and the Stoic and later usages, which differ again (Section XII).

Finally, the roots are perceptible in kind. Fragment B 109, in paraphrase, states the system's epistemology in one line: by earth we perceive earth, by water water, by air bright air, by fire destroying fire, by Love Love, and by Strife baneful Strife. Like is known by like, because the perceiver is himself a mixture of the very constituents he perceives, and perception works — as the fragments on effluences and pores (B 84, B 89 in the standard discussions) elaborate — by the fitting of emanations from objects into the pores of the sense organs, where the mixture answers to the mixture. The doctrine is worth pausing on because of its long echo: a cosmos in which like acts on like, knows like, and is drawn to like is a cosmos already organised by sympathy, and sympathy — systematised by the Stoics, catalogued by Bolos of Mendes — is the workshop physics of the earliest alchemy. The line from B 109 to the Physika tradition is drawn in Section XIV.

V. Love and Strife: The Motive Powers

Roots alone would be a warehouse. Empedocles' second posit makes them a world: two powers, as eternal and as unoriginated as the roots themselves, that move them. Love (Philia, Philotēs; also named Aphrodite and Kypris in the fragments) unites: it brings the unlike roots together into mixtures, blends them, and at the limit gathers all things into one. Strife (Neikos) separates: it drives the roots apart, sorts like to like, and at the limit holds each root in its own unmixed mass. Fragment B 17 — the longest continuous passage of the physical poem to survive, and the text every account of Empedocles is built on — states the alternation, in paraphrase: a twofold tale; at one time all things grow together to be one alone out of many, at another they grow apart to be many out of one; double is the birth of mortal things and double their destruction, the one born and destroyed by the coming together of all things, the other nourished and scattered by their separation; and these things never cease from their continual exchange, now through Love all coming together into one, now each borne apart by the hatred of Strife.

The Cosmic Cycle and the Sphere

Extended over cosmic time, the alternation becomes a cycle with two poles. At one pole, Love is wholly dominant: the roots are completely intermingled, their differences dissolved in a uniform, motionless, divine whole that Empedocles calls the Sphere (Sphairos) — in paraphrase of the fragments that describe it (B 27–B 29), a rounded whole rejoicing in its circular solitude, without limbs, branches, or strife, equal to itself on every side. At the other pole, Strife is wholly dominant: the roots stand completely separated, each in its own region, a world unmade into four pure masses. Between the poles the powers wax and wane against each other under what B 17 calls, in paraphrase, a broad oath and a fixed alternation of time — and in the intermediate conditions, where mixture is partial and unstable, ordered worlds like ours arise, with heavens, earth, sea, plants, animals, and men. Our world, on the traditional reading, is one such intermediate: a cosmos in which Strife is increasing, or Love is, depending on which half of the cycle we inhabit.

// ONE COSMOGONY OR TWO? THE CYCLE DISPUTED //

The symmetrical reading just given — a two-part cycle with a cosmogony and a zoogony in each half, once under waxing Strife and once under waxing Love — is the traditional interpretation, associated above all with Denis O'Brien's Empedocles' Cosmic Cycle (1969) and M. R. Wright's edition (1981). It has been challenged by interpreters who find in the fragments evidence for only one cosmogony and one zoogony per cycle, and the Strasbourg Papyrus has been enlisted by both sides. The dispute is technical, turns on the placing and reading of a small number of lines, and remains open. What no party disputes is the machinery: two powers, opposed in effect, alternating in dominance, with complete mixture and complete separation as the cycle's poles and the ordered world as an intermediate state. This article presents the traditional symmetrical cycle as the standard account and flags it, here, as an interpretation — the same discipline it applies to the one-poem question in Section III.

How the Powers Work: Mixture in Proportion

Love's work is specified by the system's most famous analogy, fragment B 23, in paraphrase: as painters, trained in their craft, take pigments of many colours in their hands and mix them in harmony, more of some and less of others, and from them produce forms resembling all things — trees, men and women, beasts and birds and water-nurtured fish, and long-lived gods — so let not your mind be deceived that there is any other source for the countless mortal things that are seen; know this clearly. A handful of pigments, unlimited pictures: the analogy is the pluralist programme in a single image, and it fixes the role of proportion at the centre of the physics. The fragments give recipes. Bone (B 96, preserved by the commentator Simplicius and reported in the standard discussions) is compounded, in paraphrase, of two parts earth, two parts water, and four parts fire, compacted by the gluing power of Harmony — Love under her joiner's name. Blood and flesh (B 98) are mixtures of the four roots in approximately equal parts. Thought itself is located in the blood around the heart, where the mixture is most nearly equal and therefore most receptive to all its constituents alike. A thing's nature is its ratio; change the ratio and you change the thing, without any root having been created, destroyed, or transmuted. It is difficult to overstate how much of the later tradition — medical mixture theory, alchemical proportioning, the whole language of the “right mixture” — is already present in this move.

Two further pieces of the physics deserve record, because both show Empedocles arguing from apparatus and observation, not only from Parmenidean logic. The first is the clepsydra (fragment B 100): a girl playing with a bronze water-lifter, closing its neck with her hand and plunging it into water — the water does not enter, because the air inside, pressing on the perforated base, holds it back; release the hand, the air escapes, and the water rushes in to take its place. In paraphrase of the fragment's point: air is a body. It occupies space, resists, and can be displaced only as another body takes its room — a demonstration, in a child's toy, of the plenum doctrine of Section IV, and one of the earliest surviving pieces of experimental argument in Greek science. The second is the doctrine of effluences and pores: all things give off streams of fine particles, and perception, magnetism, respiration, and the action of one body on another at a distance are explained by whether those streams fit the pores of the receiving body. The mechanism is crude and the reach is enormous: here, in embryo, is a general theory of selective interaction — why this acts on that and not on the third thing — which the later language of sympathy, antipathy, and specific affinity will inherit.

// ARE LOVE AND STRIFE FORCES, GODS, OR STUFFS? //

Aristotle already complains, in paraphrase of his criticisms in the Metaphysics and On Generation and Corruption, that Empedocles uses Love inconsistently — as a cause of good and of unity, yet also as a separator of like from unlike when it gathers the Sphere — and that he gives no account of what moves the powers themselves. Modern readers divide over whether Love and Strife are material constituents alongside the roots (they are sometimes spoken of in spatial terms, Love “equal in length and breadth” amid the roots in B 17), incorporeal forces, or divine agents of a kind the stuff/force distinction does not fit. The honest statement is that Empedocles' ontology is sixfold — four roots, two powers — and that the powers are sui generis in his poem: eternal, spatially extended, morally valenced, and mechanically effective at once. Later systems will tidy this away: Plato replaces the pair with a craftsman and mathematical structure, Aristotle with form, privation, and an unmoved mover, the Stoics with a single active principle. The untidiness is the price of the invention, and the invention — separate the constituents from the causes of their combination — is permanent.

What Empedocles does not give the tetrad is as important as what he gives it. He gives it no fixed assignment of the contrary qualities — hot, cold, wet, dry — as the roots' defining properties. The roots have characters, and fragments associate them with qualities in passing (the sun bright and hot, earth heavy and hard, in the paraphrase of B 21's survey of the roots' appearances), but there is no scheme in the surviving fragments that defines each root by a quality-pair, and none should be retrojected into them. The quality-pair derivation is Aristotle's construction, built two generations later for his own purposes (Section IX), and the medical writers of the intervening period supply its other parent. To that parallel tradition — without which the square of Section X cannot be understood — the article now turns.

VI. The Medical Parallel: Opposites, Powers, and the Hippocratic Body

The tetrad has two birthplaces. One is the Eleatic argument of Section II. The other is the sickroom. Greek medicine of the fifth century BCE was independently converging on the same explanatory shape — a small set of opposed powers whose balance is health and whose imbalance is disease — and the mature element-theory of the fourth century, the one Galen and the alchemists inherit, is a fusion of the philosophical and medical lines. The fusion is so complete in later sources that separating the parents takes deliberate effort; this section makes the effort, because one of the most common errors in popular accounts is to read the finished fusion back into the fifth century.

Alcmaeon of Croton (early fifth century BCE) is the tradition's starting point: in paraphrase of the testimony (DK 24 B 4), health is the isonomia — the equal rule, the political equality — of the powers in the body: wet and dry, cold and hot, bitter and sweet, and the rest; disease is the monarchia, the sole rule, of any one of them. The list is open-ended and the powers are qualities, not stuffs; but the grammar is complete already. The body is a polity of opposites; therapy is constitutional reform. Empedocles, working in the same Greek West and himself remembered as a physician, gives the Sicilian medical tradition its cosmology; the Hippocratic writers give the cosmology a clinic.

On the Nature of Man and the Four Humours

The Hippocratic Corpus — some sixty treatises, by many hands, of the late fifth and fourth centuries BCE — is not a system, and its authors disagree with each other vigorously. One treatise in it, however, became canonical for the whole later tradition: On the Nature of Man, attributed by Aristotle's school to Polybus, Hippocrates' son-in-law. In paraphrase of its central doctrine: the human body contains blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile; these are its constitution, and through them it suffers pain and enjoys health; health is when these constituents are duly proportioned to one another in strength and bulk and well mixed; pain is when one of them is in defect or excess, or is separated in the body and not mixed with the others. The treatise goes on to correlate the four humours with the seasons — phlegm, cold and wet in character, increasing in winter; blood in spring; yellow bile in summer; black bile in autumn — and with the ages of life. It also argues, against monist physicians, that the body cannot be one stuff, for one stuff could not feel pain or generate opposites.

// WHAT THE HIPPOCRATIC TEXT DOES — AND DOES NOT — SAY //

Two precisions, both load-bearing. First: On the Nature of Man presents four humours, bodily fluids with opposed characters; it does not present the four elements as the body's constituents, and the neat one-to-one alignment of humour to element — blood to air, yellow bile to fire, black bile to earth, phlegm to water — is a later systematisation, completed in the Galenic tradition and its diagrams, not a doctrine stated in the Hippocratic text itself. Second: the qualities in the medical tradition are powers (dynameis) — capacities to act on the body, to heat, cool, moisten, dry, purge, bind — before they are abstract properties. A drug, a food, a wind, a season is hot or cold, in this register, by what it does. Both precisions matter downstream: the first keeps this article's chronology honest, and the second is the direct ancestor of the alchemical understanding of qualities as handles (Section XIV).

The bridge text between medicine and philosophy is the Hippocratic On Ancient Medicine, whose author attacks — by name, in the standard reading — the import into medicine of Empedoclean-style physical hypotheses, insisting that the physician's hot, cold, wet, and dry must be learned from the body's responses, not deduced from a cosmology. The attack is itself evidence: by the late fifth century the two traditions were close enough to need policing apart. Plato, who knew both, declined to police them. In the Timaeus' physiology (Section VIII) the body's diseases are analysed directly in terms of excess, deficiency, and displacement of the four elements and their derived stuffs — the fusion performed inside a philosophical text, a generation before Aristotle gave it its canonical logical form. The medical career of the finished system, under Galen, is Section XIII's subject.

THE PHILOSOPHICAL LINE

Parmenides → Empedocles: permanent constituents, mixture and separation, proportion as identity. Supplies the stuffs and the conservation principle.

THE MEDICAL LINE

Alcmaeon → the Hippocratics: opposed powers, balance as health, qualities as capacities to act. Supplies the contrary qualities and the therapeutic imperative to restore a mixture.

Aristotle, who was a physician's son and wrote biology with a clinician's eye for the telling dissection, is the fusion's author in its final form: the elements of the philosophers, defined by the qualities of the physicians, in a scheme where the same four terms explain the weather, the heavens' lower reaches, the metals in the earth, and the fevers of the body. Sections IX–XI give that form. First, Plato — who stands between Empedocles and Aristotle chronologically, and who gives the tetrad the two things neither of them could: a mathematical interior, and a maker.

VII. Plato's Systematisation I: Proportion and the Body of the World

Plato's Timaeus (c. 360 BCE) is the most influential cosmology of antiquity — the one Platonic dialogue the Latin Middle Ages possessed entire, and the text through which the tetrad entered the bloodstream of European thought. Its frame must be stated before its physics, because the frame is an epistemic warning the author himself issues. The speaker, Timaeus of Locri, prefaces his account of the world's making with the distinction, in paraphrase of Timaeus 27d–29d, between what always is and never becomes — grasped by reason, the proper object of an exact account — and what becomes and never truly is — grasped by opinion with sensation, the proper object only of a likely story (eikōs mythos). The cosmology that follows, elements included, is offered under that label: an account as likely as the subject admits, to be received as such. Plato's systematisation of the tetrad is therefore, by its author's own classification, a model — the first element-theory in the tradition that knows itself to be one. The hub's dedicated treatment of the dialogue as a whole is its Timaeus article; what follows here is the tetrad's part in it, in the depth the Foundations cluster requires.

Why Four: The Argument from Visibility, Tangibility, and Bond

The world's body is made by a craftsman — the Demiurge — who, being good and without envy, makes it as like himself, and as perfect, as a generated thing can be. The derivation of the four elements (Timaeus 31b–32c) proceeds, in paraphrase, in three steps. What has come to be must be bodily, hence visible and tangible. Nothing is visible without fire; nothing is tangible without solidity, and nothing is solid without earth. The craftsman therefore begins with fire and earth. But two things cannot be joined without a third: there must be a bond between them, and the fairest bond is proportion (analogia) — a continued ratio in which the middle term relates to the last as the first relates to it, so that all the terms become, in effect, interchangeable and one. For a flat world, one middle term would suffice; for a solid world, two are required. The craftsman therefore sets water and air as the two means between fire and earth, in the same ratio throughout — as fire is to air, so air to water, and as air to water, so water to earth. The short verbatim anchor for this passage, in Jowett's translation, runs: “God placed water and air in the mean between fire and earth, and made them to have the same proportion so far as was possible” — and the result, in the same translation's continuation, is a world-body “harmonised by proportion” and bound in friendship (philia — Empedocles' Love, naturalised as mathematics), dissoluble by no one but its maker.

Three features of this derivation repay attention. First, the number four is derived, not assumed: two extremes given by the senses' requirements, two means given by the mathematics of solid proportion. Empedocles' list has become a theorem. Second, the whole of each element is used up in the making (32c–d, in paraphrase): no fire, air, water, or earth is left outside the world, so that the world is one, complete, ageless, and diseaseless, with nothing external to attack it — the tetrad here serves a theology of cosmic self-sufficiency. Third, the world's body is then given a shape and a soul: the sphere, most perfect of solids, rotating on its axis, ensouled with a rational World Soul compounded of Same, Different, and Being, whose revolutions are the heavens. The elements are the world's body; proportion is its skeleton; soul is its life. Later readers who took the tetrad from Plato took it, whether they knew it or not, inside this architecture of body, bond, and soul — an architecture the alchemical triad of body, soul, and spirit will uncannily recall (Section XIV).

// THE RECEPTACLE //

At Timaeus 48e–52d the dialogue introduces a “third kind” beside the intelligible model and its sensible copies: the Receptacle (hypodochē), also figured as a nurse and as a mother — the characterless medium in which the copies of the Forms appear, as shapes appear in gold that is successively moulded and remoulded (50a–c, in paraphrase), itself without any character of its own lest it distort what it receives. Aristotle reads the Receptacle as Plato's version of matter and criticises the identification (see the paraphrase of his critique in On Generation and Corruption II.1); later Platonists divide over whether it is space, matter, or both. For the tetrad the point is precise: Plato's four “elements” are, strictly, not yet elements at all before the craftsman's ordering — the Receptacle before geometry holds only disorderly “traces” of fire, air, water, and earth, shaken and winnowed like grain in a basket (52d–53b, in paraphrase). Order, for Plato, is not in the stuffs. It is imposed on them, by number. Section VIII gives the imposition.

It is worth registering, against a common simplification, that Plato's attitude to Empedocles here is adoptive but not deferential. The four stuffs are taken over; Love and Strife are not — their unifying work is reassigned to proportion and the craftsman's goodness, their separating work to the Receptacle's disorderly motion and the geometry of the particles themselves. And the stuffs are demoted in the taking: at Timaeus 48b–c, in paraphrase, Timaeus remarks that fire, air, water, and earth are commonly called elements (stoicheia) and treated as principles, but that they do not deserve even the rank of syllables in the alphabet of things — the true letters lie deeper, in the triangles of Section VIII. The metaphor is the word stoicheion itself, letter and element in one: Plato accepts the word for the four only to deny them its meaning. The tetrad survives his systematisation the way a scaffolding survives inside a building — structurally essential, officially superseded. Aristotle will reverse the demotion.

VIII. Plato's Systematisation II: Triangles, Solids, and Transformation

The second movement of the Timaeus' physics (Timaeus 53c–57d) is the boldest piece of mathematical matter-theory to survive from antiquity. Its claim, in paraphrase: the four bodies are not ultimate, because bodies have depth, depth is bounded by surfaces, surfaces are composed of triangles, and there are two elementary right-angled triangles from which all the relevant surfaces can be built — the isosceles right triangle (half of a square) and the scalene right triangle of a specific privileged form (half of an equilateral triangle, the 30-60-90 triangle whose longer leg is √3 times the shorter). From these two letters, the craftsman spells the four bodies as four of the five regular solids — the figures whose faces are identical regular polygons, whose construction Euclid's Elements Book XIII would later prove exhaustive of the possibilities.

The payoff of the construction is a mechanism of transformation — the thing Empedocles' system forbade. Because fire, air, and water are built from the same scalene triangle, their particles can dissolve into their triangles and recombine across kinds, in strict arithmetical ratios: in paraphrase of Timaeus 56d–57c, one particle of water (120 triangles) can resolve into two of air (2 × 48) and one of fire (24); one of air (48) into two of fire (2 × 24); and the reverse condensations proceed likewise, driven by the pressure of the rotating whole, which crushes the larger, blunter particles with the smaller, sharper ones and grinds the world's body in a perpetual circulation with no void left unfilled. Earth alone stands outside the cycle: built from the isosceles triangle, its particles can dissolve only into earth. Plato has thus produced, inside a creation story, the first quantitative transformation theory in the tradition — elements with a common currency (the triangle), fixed exchange rates, and one inconvertible currency at the base of the system. The parallel with the workshop is not accidental and was not lost on later readers: a physics in which bodies resolve into a common measure and recombine in ratio is a physics an art of transmutation can think with, even where the art's own theory came from elsewhere.

Kinds Within Kinds, and the Body's Economy

The dialogue does not stop at four homogeneous stuffs. Within each body it distinguishes kinds (57c–61c, in paraphrase throughout): of fire, flame, light, and the glowing of embers; of air, the bright aithēr, mist, and darkness; of water, the fusible kind — the metals, gold foremost, described as a dense, shining, meltable water — and the fluid kind of juices, oils, and wines, with ice as water solidified; of earth, stone, clay, salt, and the rest. The metals' filing under water — because they melt and flow — deserves emphasis: it is the tetrad classifying by behaviour under operation (fusibility) rather than by appearance, a habit of mind any furnace-worker would recognise, and it survives, transformed, in the alchemical treatment of metals as congealed liquids. The dialogue's final movement (72d–92c) then runs the whole physics through the human body: marrow, bone, flesh, and sinew as elemental compounds in ratio (an unmistakable inheritance from Empedocles' recipes); respiration and digestion as mechanical circulation; and disease, in the analysis at 81e–86a, as the four bodies falling into excess, deficiency, displacement from their proper places, or the production of corrupted kinds — bile and phlegm, the Hippocratic humours' names, appearing here as elemental disorders. Health, correspondingly, is proportion restored: the physician's task, as the Timaeus frames it, is applied cosmology.

// PLATO'S TETRAD: THE BALANCE SHEET //

What Plato adds: derivation of the four from first requirements; a mathematical interior (proportion, then geometry); a mechanism of transformation among three of the four; a classification of kinds by behaviour; and a physiology. What he does not add: the quality-pairs. The Timaeus speaks of hot and cold, wet and dry, as powers and affections of bodies (61d–65b treats them in the account of sensation), but it does not define the elements by them. The square of Section X is not Plato's. It is Aristotle's — built, as Aristotle builds everything, by taking his predecessors' results as data and demanding the principles that would make them necessary.

IX. Aristotle's Reconstruction: The Qualities Are the Primitives

Aristotle (384–322 BCE) studied for twenty years in Plato's Academy, and his physics is best read as an insider's reconstruction of the inheritance this article has traced: Empedocles' four stuffs, the medical tradition's opposed powers, and Plato's demand for derivation — rebuilt on new foundations. The rebuilding occupies several treatises: the Physics for the general theory of change, On the Heavens (De caelo) for the elements' places and motions, On Generation and Corruption for the elements themselves, and the Meteorology for their workings in the world below the moon. The hub's companion treatment of the matter-theory as such is its Aristotle on Matter article; this section gives the tetrad's derivation, which is the Foundations cluster's reason for existing.

The Three Principles, and What Is Wrong with Empedocles

On Generation and Corruption II.1 sets the frame, in paraphrase: there is a matter of the perceptible bodies — the substratum out of which the so-called elements come to be — but it has no separate existence; it is always bound up with a contrariety. Three originative sources must therefore be recognised: first, the substratum, that which is potentially a perceptible body (what the later tradition, following Aristotle's logic rather than this passage's letter, calls prime matter); second, the contrarieties — heat and cold being his example; and third, derivatively, Fire, Water, and the like. The elements rank only third because, against Empedocles, they change into one another: they are not immutable, since alteration — change in respect of the tangible qualities — is manifest to perception, whereas the contrarieties themselves do not change. The argument deserves its weight. Empedocles had saved Parmenides' logic by making the constituents eternal and change a rearrangement. Aristotle replies, in effect, that the saving was too expensive: it cannot explain why a body that was cold and wet is later hot and dry as the same body altering, nor why the stuffs themselves plainly pass into one another — water evaporating to air-like vapour, vapour condensing back — unless something persists through the passage that is neither stuff. That something is the substratum; what changes is its qualification. Generation and corruption of elements are, in Aristotle's analysis, the substratum's exchange of contrary qualities.

Why These Qualities: The Argument of II.2

The derivation of the quality-pairs (On Generation and Corruption II.2) is the most consequential piece of reasoning in this article, and it is given here step by step, in paraphrase of Joachim's translation, which was consulted in full for this section. We seek the principles of perceptible body. Perceptible body is, as such, tangible body; tangibility is the mark of body in the sublunary world. Therefore not every contrariety of perception can be an element of body — not white/black, not sweet/bitter — but only the tangible contrarieties. Among those, some are disqualified: heavy/light are neither active nor passive — a thing is not called heavy because it acts on another or suffers from it — whereas the elements must be reciprocally active and susceptible, since they combine and transform into one another. There remain two pairs that qualify: hot–cold, which imply power to act, and dry–moist (Joachim's “moist” renders the Greek hygron, elsewhere “wet”; this hub's canonical wording is wet), which imply susceptibility. His definitions, closely paraphrased: the hot is that which associates things of the same kind (what men call fire's dissociating is, on analysis, its gathering of the homogeneous by expelling the foreign); the cold is that which brings together homogeneous and heterogeneous alike. The moist is that which, being readily adaptable in shape, is not determinable by any limit of its own; the dry is that which is readily determinable by its own limit but not readily adaptable in shape. All other tangible differences — fine and coarse, viscous and brittle, hard and soft, and the rest — are then shown to derive from these four: the fine from the moist, the coarse from the dry, the viscous from the moist, the brittle from the dry, the soft from the moist, the hard from the dry. The four admit of no further reduction, for the hot is not essentially moist or dry, nor the moist hot or cold. Hence — the chapter's conclusion — the elementary qualities are four, and exactly four.

// ACTIVE AND PASSIVE: WHY THE PAIRS ARE NOT SYMMETRICAL IN ROLE //

Hot and cold act; wet and dry are acted on — in the vocabulary of the Meteorology's fourth book, which develops this chapter's scheme into a general theory of the homoeomerous bodies, the hot and the cold are the efficient, fashioning powers, and moisture and dryness are the matter-like determinants they fashion: the moist receives form, the dry retains it. A body's quality-pair is thus not a mere label but a little causal theory: one term says how the body works on others, the other how it takes and holds what is worked on it. Section XIV shows the alchemists using the pairs in exactly this operational sense: heat it (act on it) so that its moisture leaves (its passive constitution changes), and the residue is a new body.

One more doctrine of the treatise must be in place before the square can be drawn: the reciprocity of elemental transformation (II.4, in paraphrase). All the simple bodies are by nature such as to change into one another, since coming-to-be is a change into contraries and out of contraries. The manner of the change, however, depends on how many qualities must be exchanged — and that dependence, a matter of simple counting once the pairs are assigned, generates the square's geometry and its physics at once. It is the subject of the next section, which is this article's centre.

X. The Quality Square

On Generation and Corruption II.3 performs the assignment, in the passage on which the whole later tradition rests. The elementary qualities being four, they can be coupled in six ways; but contraries refuse to be coupled — the same thing cannot be hot and cold, or wet and dry. There remain exactly four lawful couplings, and they attach to the four apparently simple bodies. The passage is quoted verbatim, in H. H. Joachim's translation: “For Fire is hot and dry, whereas Air is hot and moist (Air being a sort of aqueous vapour); and Water is cold and moist, while Earth is cold and dry.” Every diagram in every later textbook, medical chart, and alchemical treatise that sets the elements in a square is a drawing of this sentence.

Aristotle draws the physical moral himself in II.4, in paraphrase: transformation between elements that share a quality is quick and easy, because only one quality must be overcome — fire becomes air when its dryness is mastered by moisture; air becomes water when its heat is mastered by cold; and so round the square's perimeter. Transformation between diagonal contraries is slow and difficult, because both qualities must be exchanged, and in practice proceeds through an intermediate: fire does not pass directly to water, but by way of air or earth. The square is thus not a static classification but a map of possible changes with their relative costs — a state diagram, two millennia before the term. It also encodes the elements' characters, for Aristotle adds (II.3, in paraphrase) that each element is characterised par excellence by a single quality: Earth by dry rather than by cold, Water by cold rather than by wet, Air by wet rather than by hot, and Fire by hot rather than by dry. The dominant quality is the element's signature in action — why earth-stuffs are spoken of as drying, fire-stuffs as heating, in the practical literature ever after.

Two clarifications protect the square from its own afterlife. First, the qualities are not, for Aristotle, ingredients in the sense of parts: a body is not hot by containing a lump of heat. The qualities are the substratum's differentiae — the respects in which one primary body differs from another and acts on it. The scholastic tradition would later debate, with great refinement, whether the qualities define the elements' essences or flow from unknown substantial forms; the On Generation and Corruption text supports the plainer reading that the couplings are how the simple bodies are differentiated and known, and this article does not import the later debate into the fourth century BCE. Second, the square's two axes are not independent decorations: the hot–cold axis carries the active powers and the wet–dry axis the passive determinants (Section IX). Every element is therefore specified as one power-to-act wedded to one mode-of-being-acted-on — a complete little theory of interaction in four terms. When the medical and alchemical traditions take the square up, it is this completeness they are taking: a body can be located, its behaviour predicted, its transformation costed, and its remedy — the contrary quality, administered in the right vehicle — prescribed, from two coordinates.

Any source that assigns the pairs otherwise — air cold, or water hot, in some late and local schemes — is departing from the Aristotelian standard of On Generation and Corruption II.3, and this hub notes such departures as departures where they occur (see the Stoic revision, Section XII, which reassigns the qualities on a different principle and must not be confused with the square).

XI. Natural Place and the Fifth Body: Aether, Distinguished

The square describes what the elements are; Aristotle's cosmology describes where they go. In On the Heavens and the Physics, each simple body has a natural place and a natural motion toward it when displaced: the cosmos is a sphere with an absolute centre and an absolute circumference, and place in it is not relative. Earth, the absolutely heavy, moves toward the centre; fire, the absolutely light, toward the circumference; water and air, heavy and light by comparison, take the intermediate shells — water above earth, air above water, fire uppermost of the four, reaching to the sphere of the Moon. A stone falls not because it is pulled but because it is, in the theory's deliberately teleological sense, going home: natural motion is a body's actualisation of its own form, and it ceases at its place as a process ceases at its end. Forced motion — the thrown stone's rise, the bellows' blast — is the contrary condition, requiring a continuing mover. The doctrine of natural place is the tetrad's dynamics, and it survived, through the Arabic and Latin traditions, as common sense about why things fall until the seventeenth century dismantled it.

Above the Moon, everything changes — and the change is the point. The heavens, in Aristotle's observation and argument (On the Heavens I.2–3, in paraphrase), move neither toward the centre nor away from it but in circles, eternally and without variation; no generation, corruption, growth, or alteration is observed in them across all recorded time. But circular motion is no more the natural motion of fire or air than of earth or water: each of the four has its straight-line motion, and a body has one natural motion. Therefore there exists a fifth simple body, prior to the four, whose natural motion is circular. Having no contrary — for circular motion has no contrary motion — it has no contrary qualities, and is therefore ungenerated, imperishable, unalterable, and without growth or diminution: the body of the stars and their spheres. Aristotle notes, in the same passage, that the ancients gave the name aithēr to the uppermost region, and he offers his celebrated etymology — that it “runs always” (aei thein), in paraphrase of I.3, 270b — taking over Empedocles' bright-air word for a body Empedocles never posited.

// “FIFTH ELEMENT” AND “QUINTESSENCE”: TWO LATER LABELS //

Aristotle himself does not call the celestial body “the fifth element” in so many words, and he never calls it the quintessence: quinta essentia is a Latin coinage of the medieval tradition, naturalised by the scholastics and glorified by the Renaissance. “Fifth body” (the Greek tradition's pempton sōma) is the accurate ancient label. The distinction is not pedantry. The celestial body is expressly not a fifth member of the tetrad's system: it has no qualities from the two pairs, enters into no mixtures, undergoes no transformations, and plays no part in sublunary physics or in any operation an alchemist could perform. It belongs to this article only to be distinguished and dismissed — and to explain why later talk of a quintessence extractable in the laboratory, a fifth essence obtainable from wine, blood, or herbs by distillation, is a medieval and Renaissance innovation wearing an Aristotelian name: Aristotle's aether is the one body in his cosmos that no art could ever extract, because extraction is a sublunary change and it cannot change.

The finished Aristotelian cosmos is therefore a two-storey structure: below the Moon, the realm of the four elements, of generation and corruption, of straight motions, mixture, and decay; above it, the realm of the fifth body, of eternal circular motion, impinging on the lower world only as mover and as the source, through the sun's annual circuit, of the seasonal cycle that drives generation below. The boundary is absolute, and its absoluteness is what later ages found both compelling and intolerable: compelling, because it explained the heavens' evident perfection; intolerable, because a single nature kept suggesting it should have a single physics. The Stoics, the tetrad's next systematists, take the intolerable side: one cosmos, one material continuum, no fifth body, and the heavens made of the tetrad's own fire. Their revision follows.

XII. The Hellenistic Career I: The Stoic Revision

The Stoics — Zeno of Citium (c. 334–c. 262 BCE), Cleanthes, Chrysippus, and their school — inherit the tetrad and rebuild it inside a physics that is in almost every structural respect Aristotle's opposite. The hub's full treatment is its Stoic Physics article; this section gives the tetrad's Stoic fate, which the alchemical tradition absorbed along with the Aristotelian version and never cleanly separated from it.

Stoic physics begins, in the standard report of Diogenes Laertius (VII.134, in paraphrase), by distinguishing principles (archai) from elements (stoicheia) — a distinction Aristotle's scheme does not draw in this form. The principles are two, and they are bodies: the active principle — God, reason (logos), the craftsmanlike fire that orders all things — and the passive principle — unqualified substance, matter without qualities of its own. The principles are ungenerated and imperishable and are never found apart: the active is wholly blended with the passive, through and through, in the mixture the Stoics call total blending (krasis di' holou), in which two bodies occupy the same place entirely while each preserves its own nature. The elements, by contrast, are generated and perishable — the first differentiated bodies into which the passive principle is articulated under the active principle's working, and into which, at the cosmos's periodic dissolution, they are resolved again. The elements are, in short, a middle storey: below the principles, above the compounds. Empedocles' roots were eternal; Aristotle's elements were transformable but primitive; the Stoic elements are transformable and derivative.

Fire First, and the Breath That Binds

The Stoic cosmogony (Diogenes Laertius VII.135–137, in paraphrase) runs the tetrad as a sequence from a single term: substance turns from fire through air into moisture; the thick part of the moisture condenses as earth, the thin part rarefies, and, rarefied further, kindles again as fire. Fire is thus both the starting point and the end: at the conflagration (ekpyrōsis) the whole cosmos resolves into fire — into the active principle in its purest bodily form — and from that fire, by the same sequence, an identical cosmos is regenerated, in an eternal recurrence of world-cycles beside which Empedocles' alternation of Love and Strife is the clear ancestor. Within the standing cosmos, the elements divide by role (in paraphrase of the reports collected in the standard discussions): fire and air are active, water and earth passive; and the active pair's blend is the system's central physical agent, pneuma — breath: a tensional blend of fire and air that pervades all bodies, holds them together, and endows them with their qualities. Pneuma at its lowest tension is mere cohesion (hexis), the holding-together of a stone; at higher tensions it is nature (physis) in plants and soul (psychē) in animals; in the cosmos at large it is the World Soul itself, God in his bodily presence. The qualities a body has are, on this scheme, literally the state of the breath within it.

// THE STOIC QUALITIES ARE NOT THE SQUARE //

The Stoic sources characterise the elements singly, not by Aristotle's pairs: in the report most often cited, fire is the hot, air the cold, water the wet, earth the dry — one defining quality apiece, with air reassigned from Aristotle's hot to cold (as the cold partner whose tension against fire's heat constitutes pneuma's elasticity). The assignment serves the active/passive division, not the transformation geometry of Section X, and the two schemes answer different questions. Later eclecticism — medical, astrological, alchemical — habitually runs them together, speaking of pneuma in one sentence and the square in the next. This hub keeps them distinct: the square of Section X is Aristotle's, stated as this hub states it everywhere — Fire = hot + dry, Air = hot + wet, Water = cold + wet, Earth = cold + dry — and the Stoic single-quality scheme is a revision of it, intelligible only against it.

What the Stoic revision contributes to the tetrad's career is, above all, continuity. Aristotle's elements are discrete kinds with a substratum; the Stoic cosmos is a single material continuum, everywhere pervaded by an active, rational, fiery breath, in which qualities are tensions and bodies are local states of one world-animal. Sympathy, on this physics, is not a mystery but a mechanism: distant parts of the cosmos affect one another because they are organs of one body, continuous in one pneuma. The line of descent into alchemy is direct. Bolos of Mendes' catalogue of sympathies and antipathies (c. 200 BCE — the dating this hub uses throughout) is contemporary with the early Stoa and breathes the same air; and when the alchemical texts speak of spirits pervading bodies, of nature acting on nature, of the whole work as the management of an indwelling active breath, the Stoic stratum of the tetrad's tradition is one of the two sources — the Aristotelian square being the other — from which that language draws its intelligibility. Section XIV takes up both.

XIII. The Hellenistic Career II: Galen, the Humours, and the Temperaments

If the Stoics gave the tetrad its cosmology of the Roman period, Galen of Pergamum (129–c. 216 CE) gave it its body. Galen — physician to Marcus Aurelius, dissector, controversialist, and the most voluminous medical author of antiquity — fused the Hippocratic humours, the Aristotelian elements and qualities, and a Platonic psychology of the soul into the system that would govern learned medicine, Greek, Arabic, and Latin, for some fifteen hundred years. The hub's full treatment is its Humours and Galen article; this section gives the tetrad's place in the fusion, which is the form in which most pre-modern readers — including the medically literate among the alchemists — actually met the four elements.

Galen's elementary treatise, On the Elements According to Hippocrates, argues the philosophical foundations explicitly: against both the atomists and the monists, the body's constituents are the four elements, known not by reasoning alone but by the tangible qualities through which they act and suffer — Galen is, on this point, Aristotle's conscious heir, and says so. The humours are then the elements' representatives in the animal economy: blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile are the proximate constituents of the blooded body, each humour bearing the quality-pair of its corresponding element. The completed alignment — Hippocratic fluids, Aristotelian pairs, Empedoclean stuffs — is the fusion whose absence from the Hippocratic text itself Section VI flagged; in its Galenic and post-Galenic form it runs:

Every cell of that table is the square of Section X at work: the humour's qualities are its element's qualities, exactly as this hub states them everywhere — Fire hot and dry, Air hot and wet, Water cold and wet, Earth cold and dry. The table's remaining columns show the system's characteristic growth by correspondence: season and age and temperament attach to the quality-pairs because the pairs are the system's true currency, and anything that can be characterised as hot or cold, wet or dry, can be filed. The four temperaments in the last column — the sanguine, choleric, melancholic, and phlegmatic constitutions — are, strictly, a post-Galenic systematisation of Galen's doctrine of mixtures (kraseis): Galen's own treatise On Temperaments distinguishes nine mixtures, one well-tempered and eight ill-tempered, built from the two quality-axes and their combinations, and the tidy fourfold psychology of the textbooks is the tradition's simplification of his richer scheme. This article states the fourfold form because it is the form the later tradition used, and flags it, here, as a simplification.

Krasis: Mixture as the Master Concept

The concept that does the work, in Galen and in everything downstream of him, is krasis — mixture, in the strong sense of a thorough blending in which the ingredients act on one another and the result is a uniform body with a determinate qualitative state. A body's krasis is its temperament: its position, that is, on the two axes of the square, hot against cold and wet against dry, as those axes are realised in this particular compound of elements and humours. Health is a good mixture (eucrasia) appropriate to the kind of creature and its age; disease is a bad mixture (dyscrasia) — a displacement along an axis — and therapy is the axis travelled back: the hot condition treated by cooling remedies, the wet by drying, in the graded pharmacology of Galen's On the Powers of Simple Drugs, where every simple is assigned its quality and its degree — hot in the first degree, cold in the second, and so on — so that prescription becomes arithmetic on the square. Two features of this edifice matter for the tetrad's alchemical career. First, the qualities have become quantitative: they come in degrees, they can be balanced against each other, and a compound's state is a resultant. Second, the physician's art is defined as the deliberate alteration of a body's mixture by contrary qualities deliberately applied — which is, word for word, also a definition of the alchemist's art, applied to a different patient. The patient is the metal; the clinic is the furnace; the pharmacology is the recipe collection. Section XIV closes that circuit.

From Galen the system passes, with the rest of Greek medicine and philosophy, into Syriac and Arabic — where the elements (arkān, “pillars”, or usṭuqussāt, transliterated stoicheia), the qualities, the humours (akhlāṭ), and the temperaments (mizāj, the exact Arabic counterpart of krasis) structure the medicine of al-Rāzī, Ibn Sīnā, and their successors, and the matter-theory within which Arabic alchemy will reconceive the metals (see the hub's Transmission article) — and back into Latin Europe in the twelfth-century translations, whence it governs the universities' physics and medicine until the seventeenth century's mechanical philosophy and the eighteenth century's chemistry retire it, piece by piece. The four temperaments survive in ordinary language — sanguine, choleric, melancholic, phlegmatic — as the tetrad's fossils in the modern vocabulary, long after the physics that minted them has gone.

XIV. What Alexandria Took: The Tetrad in the Alchemists' Hands

The alchemy of Roman Egypt did not debate the tetrad; it spent it. That is the fact this closing section documents: by the time the earliest surviving alchemical texts were written, the four elements and their qualities had passed from philosophical doctrine into the working assumptions of a craft — the medium in which recipes are intelligible, not a thesis recipes argue for. Tracing exactly what was taken, and in what form, is the Foundations cluster's whole purpose, and it can be done with precision, because the texts are explicit at the joints.

1. The Pseudo-Democritean Stratum: Sympathy in a Four-Element World

The tradition's root document is the Physika kai Mystika ascribed to Democritus — in the form in which it survives, a work of the 1st century CE, standing in a line of descent from Bolos of Mendes (c. 200 BCE), the compiler of the great catalogue of sympathies and antipathies from whom the work's framework derives. (Both datings are this hub's canonical positions, argued in full in its Greek Alchemy: Origins article.) The Physika kai Mystika is, in its surviving epitome, overwhelmingly a recipe collection — for the dyeing of metals yellow and white, of stones green, of wool purple — and its physics is rarely stated. But its framework, inherited from Bolos' Physika, is the sympathy-physics of Section XII: nature rejoices in nature, nature conquers nature, nature masters nature, in the tradition's most famous aphorism (paraphrased here; the hub's origins article treats the saying and its transmission in full). Sympathy is Empedocles' like-to-like, Stoically mechanised: bodies act on one another selectively, by kinship of constitution. And kinship of constitution, in a four-element world, means shared qualities and shared elemental ratios. The dyer's art that fills the epitome is, in the tetrad's terms, the art of imposing a quality-complexion on a body that lacks it: to make copper wear gold's colour is to treat copper's surface — and, in the stronger claims of the tradition, copper's substance — with the agents that carry yellowness, dryness, and fixity into it. The recipe does not cite Aristotle. It does not have to. The square is underneath it, the way grammar is underneath a sentence.

2. Zosimos: The Four Elements as the Work's Measure

With Zosimos of Panopolis (fl. c. 300 CE), the first alchemical author whose works survive in bulk, the tetrad steps out of the framework and into the sentences. The hub's dedicated treatment is its Zosimos of Panopolis article; the point here is narrow and documentary. In the visionary allegory of the Book of Zosimos — the discourse of the sacrificing priest Ion, in the standard modern translations and discussions — Zosimos states the method of the whole art in a single clause, given here in close paraphrase with its key phrase verbatim: all things are interwoven and all are taken apart, all are moistened and all are dried, in the bowl-shaped altar; and for each thing it is “by method and by weight of the four elements” that the interlacing and the separation of the whole is accomplished, for no bond can be made without method. The phrase repays slow reading. The four elements are the work's measure: combination and separation — Empedocles' two processes, the whole of his physics — are performed by weight of the elements, that is, by proportioned quantities of the four constituents, under a method (the text's word is the natural method, breathing in and breathing out, increasing and decreasing). Zosimos' definition of his science, preserved in the same corpus, runs in the same terms — in paraphrase: the composition of waters, movement, growth, the taking-off and putting-on of bodily nature, the separation of the spirit from the body and the bonding of the spirit within the body. Every term of the definition is an operation on a four-element compound: separate its volatile, spirit-bearing part from its fixed body; then fix the spirit in a body again, improved. The tetrad supplies the ontology (bodies are elemental mixtures); the definition supplies the programme (unmix, purify, remix).

3. Qualities as Handles: How Operations Work on Bodies

The deepest thing the Alexandrians took from the tetrad is neither the list of four nor the square as a diagram. It is the doctrine of Section IX in its operational form: a body is worked on through its qualities. The Aristotelian analysis had established that the tangible qualities are how bodies act and suffer — the hot and the cold as powers to act, the wet and the dry as modes of susceptibility — and that elemental transformation is the exchange of a quality in a persisting substratum. The workshop translates that analysis, term by term, into a repertory of operations:

HEATING AND COOLING

The active axis, directly applied. Calcination, roasting, fusion, and the graded fires of the furnace are the hot in its degrees — Galen's pharmacology of degrees (Section XIII) and the alchemist's regimen of fire are one idea in two clinics. To heat a body is to set its dominant quality to work on its passive constitution.

MOISTENING AND DRYING

The passive axis, deliberately travelled. Dissolution, maceration, washing, and the work of the “divine water” moisten: the body's dry determinateness is loosened, its form made receivable. Drying, desiccation, and fixation do the reverse: the loosened body is given a new limit and made to keep it. Zosimos' “all things are moistened and all things are dried” is this axis stated as the work's rhythm.

SEPARATION BY QUALITY

Distillation and sublimation — the operations of Maria the Jewess's apparatus, the tribikos and the kerotakis (see the hub's Maria the Jewess article) — sort a compound by its qualities made kinetic: what is hot-and-wet in tendency, the volatile and spirituous, rises; what is cold-and-dry, the fixed and earthy, stays. The still is the square with a fire under it.

TINCTURE AS QUALITY TRANSFER

A tincture (baphē) works, in the theory the texts presuppose, by carrying a quality-complexion — colour first, and with colour the whole elemental ratio colour announces — from a perfected body into an imperfect one, as dye passes into wool and makes the wool, not merely coats it. The doctrine of the tincture is the quality-theory at its most consequential: if a body's nature is its quality-pair in its substratum, then a body whose qualities are truly changed is truly another body.

That last inference is the hinge on which the whole art turns, and it is worth stating plainly, because it locates alchemy's theoretical centre with more accuracy than the usual talk of primitive chemistry. Empedocles' physics forbids transmutation: the roots never become one another, and a recipe that claimed to make gold from copper could claim, in strict Empedoclean terms, only a new mixture wearing gold's accidents. Aristotle's physics permits it: elements transform by quality-exchange, compounds are what their qualities in their substratum make them, and nothing in the theory bars the conclusion that a base metal — a body of a determinate quality-complexion, fusible like Plato's metallic water — might, by the sustained application of contrary qualities in the right vehicles and degrees, be brought to the complexion of gold. Whether the Alexandrian texts state that programme in so many words is a question for the wing's text-by-text articles, and the answer varies by author and century. What this article establishes is the precondition: the Alexandrians inherited, from the tetrad's Aristotelian and medical career, a physics in which the programme is thinkable — in which qualities are handles, bodies have handles because they are quality-compounds, and the art is the disciplined pulling of them.

4. The Language Kept: Element Names and Cover-Names

Finally, the tetrad survives in Alexandria as vocabulary. The alchemical corpus speaks of bodies (sōmata), spirits (pneumata), and souls — the volatile sulphurous and mercurial agents that rise from bodies under fire, the tincturing powers they carry, and the fixed residues they leave — in a triad the later tradition will systematise; and it speaks, constantly, of earths, waters, airs, and fires both literal and covered. An alchemical “earth” may be the element, a mineral, a residue, or a metal under a cover-name; a “water” may be the element, a solvent, a vapour, or the divine water that is also sulphur water in the texts' deliberate pun. The hub's Decknamen Glossary exists to grade, entry by entry, how securely each covered referent can be recovered; the point for the Foundations is that the ambiguity itself is a tetrad-effect. In a four-element world, the same name legitimately ranges over the cosmic constituent, its best sublunary approximation, and any workshop body that shares its quality-pair — as Aristotle's own “such-as-fire, not fire” (Section I) had already licensed. Reading the corpus without the tetrad, a modern reader sees confusion. Reading it with the tetrad, the same passages show a technical language exploiting, sometimes to the edge of honesty, a systematic polysemy its physics supplied.
