I. A Cycle That Had to Be Made

Every schoolchild can be taught to recite them, and that is precisely the fact that needs explaining. The twelve labours of Heracles — Greek Hēraklēs, Roman Hercules — present themselves to a modern reader as a single, ancient, self-evident list: lion, hydra, hind, boar, stables, birds, bull, mares, girdle, cattle, apples, Cerberus. The list feels as if it must always have existed, in that order, since the Bronze Age. It did not. The labours began as separate local stories, attached to separate places — a lion troubling Nemea, a water-serpent in the marsh at Lerna a few miles away, a boar on an Arcadian mountain, a cattle-king in Elis — each story intelligible on its own, each claiming Heracles for a landscape that wanted a Heracles story of its own. Heracles was the most portable of Greek heroes: because he had wandered everywhere in the course of his service, any community could plausibly locate an episode in its own territory, and a great many did. The cycle is what happened when that scattered, competitive, ever-growing material was at last gathered, counted, and closed.

The Greek word matters. A labour is an athlos (plural athloi): not work in the sense of employment, but a contest — an ordeal undertaken for a prize, the word from which athlete descends and which belongs to the vocabulary of the games. Emma Stafford, in the study of Heracles on which this article draws throughout, makes the point that the term underlines Heracles' character as the athlete par excellence: his service is imagined not as drudgery but as a series of prize-contests, each with its own rules, its own conditions of victory, and — crucially, as Sections VIII and XI show — its own possibility of being disallowed, like a result struck from the record by a judge. The collective name for the twelve, dōdekathlos (“the twelve-contest”), is itself a later convenience: Stafford notes that the word was not used in antiquity for the set, however ancient the set became. Even the cycle's title is an editorial act.

Three layers must be kept apart throughout this article, and the hub's method requires them to be named whenever they threaten to blur. First, the myth as told: the stories themselves, in the versions given by named ancient authors — Homer and Hesiod mentioning individual feats in passing, Euripides listing a divergent set in a choral ode, Diodorus Siculus narrating a full career in the first century BCE, and the mythographer conventionally called Apollodorus giving, in the first or second century CE, the ordered prose account that has become the standard. Second, the cultic and historical footing: what can be said from sanctuaries, sculpture, inscriptions, and historians about Heracles as an object of worship — the metopes at Olympia, the dual cult Herodotus describes at Thasos, the festivals and gymnasia of Section XXIII. A story about Heracles is not evidence for his cult, and his cult is not evidence for any story. Third, the later allegorical reading: the interpretations imposed on the finished cycle by moralists, Stoics, Neoplatonists, and eventually alchemists, who read the twelve as a map of the soul's or the Work's progress. That third layer is real history — the history of reception — but it is not what the stories originally meant, and this article confines it to Section XXIV and to the companion treatment in Alchemical Allegory, keeping the labours themselves in their own terms.

THE CYCLE AT A GLANCE

Twelve athloi performed in the service of Eurystheus, king of Tiryns and Mycenae. In Apollodorus' account ten were commanded; two of the ten were disallowed; two more were imposed; twelve were performed. The first six belong to the Peloponnese; the last six carry Heracles to Crete, Thrace, the Black Sea, the far west, and finally under the earth.

THE ORDER USED HERE

This article follows Apollodorus, Library 2.5, in J. G. Frazer's Loeb translation — the order modern retellings standardly use. Where Diodorus, Euripides, Hyginus, or the Olympia metopes order or select differently, the difference is stated in place, not silently harmonised.

THE EARLIEST COMPLETE SET

Not a poem but a building: the twelve metopes of the Temple of Zeus at Olympia, c. 470–457 BCE, six over each porch. Section III examines what that fixes — the number, and a membership — and what it does not: a narrative order, and any text.

A word on the hero himself, since the cycle cannot be understood without the oddity of its performer. Heracles is the son of Zeus and the mortal Alcmene (Greek Alkmēnē), wife of Amphitryon, and a descendant through both parents of Perseus. His name is usually analysed as “glory of Hera” (Hēra + kleos) — an irony the tradition exploits relentlessly, since Hera is his persecutor from before his birth to the pyre, and since one strand of the tradition, preserved in Diodorus (4.10.1, paraphrased in Section IV), explains the name by the glory he won precisely through the serpents Hera sent to kill him. He was born, in the name he first bore, Alcides (Alkeidēs), after his grandfather Alcaeus; Apollodorus (2.4.12) reports that the Pythian priestess at Delphi first called him Heracles at the moment she assigned him his service. For the wider institutions within which he sits — hero cult at the tomb, epic fame in song, and the unique doubleness of a hero worshipped also as a god — see this hub's Heroes and Epic, whose Sections IV–V treat Heracles in brief; the present article is the full treatment of the labours themselves, and the two are written to be read together without duplication of their central arguments.

II. The Sources and Their Orders

No single ancient author is the source for the twelve labours. What survives is a shelf of partial witnesses, spread over a thousand years, which agree on a core and disagree — instructively — on order, membership, and framing. An honest account of the cycle is therefore an account of its witnesses first.

The Early Witnesses: Episodes Without a List

The Iliad knows Heracles as a figure of the generation before the Trojan War, already dead, already the type of the suffering hero: it knows his servitude to Eurystheus, imposed through Hera's trickery over his birth (19.95–133), it knows Eurystheus sending him to fetch the hound of Hades (8.362–369), and it knows that even Heracles, dearest of men to Zeus, could not escape death (18.117–119). It lists nothing. Hesiod's Theogony (c. 700 BCE) knows more episodes, but knows them as genealogy: the labours enter the poem where their victims belong in the family tree of monsters. So the cattle of Geryon appear at lines 287–293, in the line of Phorcys and Ceto; Cerberus at 310–312, the Hydra of Lerna — reared by Hera in her anger and killed by Heracles with the help of Iolaus — at 313–318; and the Nemean lion, offspring of Echidna, reared by Hera and settled in the hills of Nemea as a plague to men, at 327–332, all paraphrased from the Evelyn-White translation used in this hub's Theogony article. The pseudo-Hesiodic Shield of Heracles and the fragmentary Catalogue of Women add further episodes. The pattern is constant: in the earliest surviving literature the labours exist, severally and vividly, and the cycle does not.

Lyric and drama filled individual episodes out into whole works. Stesichorus of Himera (early sixth century BCE) composed a Geryoneis — a substantial lyric poem on the Geryon labour alone, fragments of which survive on papyrus and show the monster given a voice, a mother, and a pathos no summary preserves. Pindar and Bacchylides touch labours in passing, as exempla. A fragment of Pindar has even been thought to preserve part of the word (dōd)ekatos, “twelfth”, in a Heracles context — Stafford reports the suggestion and its fragility together: only part of the word survives, and a possible reference is not a canon. Antiquity itself credited the gathering of the labours into one poem to the lost epic Heraclea of Peisander of Rhodes, dated variously to the seventh or sixth century BCE. That Peisander wrote a Heraclea in at least two books, that later writers treated him as the poet who fixed Heracles' familiar equipment of club and lionskin, and that a tradition made him the first to number the labours at twelve, are all attested claims about a lost poem. The poem is gone; its contents cannot be checked; and this archive records the attribution as an ancient attribution, not as a fact about what Peisander's text contained.

Euripides' Chorus: A List That Is Not the List

The most instructive divergent witness is also one of the most beautiful. In Euripides' Heracles (c. 416 BCE), while the hero is believed lost in Hades, the chorus of old Thebans sings his deeds as a kind of funeral eulogy in advance (lines 359–424 in the usual numeration; the ode is also cited as 348–441). Read in the translation hosted by the Center for Hellenic Studies and in the excerpts collected in Timothy Gantz's Early Greek Myth source-chapters, the ode runs, in paraphrase: first, the lion, whose skin he wears; then — where Apollodorus will put the Hydra — the Centaurs, shot down with his bow; then the golden-horned hind; then the mares of Diomedes; then Cycnus, the guest-murdering son of Ares, killed by the river Anaurus; then the Hesperides, the slain dragon, the calming of the sea, and the bearing of the heavens for Atlas, treated as a sequence of its own; then the expedition against the Amazons for the queen's girdle; then, only now, the Hydra, burned and its venom taken for his arrows, coupled with Geryon; and last, as the play's own action requires, the descent for Cerberus, from which the chorus fears he will not return. Of the canonical twelve, the boar, the stables, the birds, and the Cretan bull are absent altogether; three parerga — the Centaurs, Cycnus, and the sea-calming — stand in their place; and the Hesperides labour is split in two around them. A modern scholarly summary of the ode counts twelve deeds, of which eight are canonical labours. Euripides, writing two generations after the Olympia metopes had carved a twelve, still felt free to compose a different twelve for the stage — because his list is not a catalogue but a song, shaped to a dramatic moment, and because the canon, even then, bound sculptors more tightly than it bound poets.

Diodorus: The Full Career, Rationalised

The first surviving continuous prose account of the whole career is Diodorus Siculus, Library of History Book 4 (first century BCE), read for this article in C. H. Oldfather's Loeb translation. Diodorus opens with a remarkable methodological apology (4.8, paraphrased): myth should not be scrutinised with the same sharp eye as contemporary events; audiences applaud Centaurs and three-bodied Geryon in the theatre though persuaded no such beings existed; and Heracles, who “brought under cultivation the inhabited world” by his labours, deserves to have his benefactions remembered rather than picked apart. The apology announces the treatment. Diodorus' Heracles is a civilising benefactor on a world tour: his labours are interleaved with the founding of cities, the draining of marshes, the clearing of beasts from Crete and Libya, the making of roads over the Alps, and the punishment of lawless rulers — a euhemerising, almost historical progress, in which the cattle-raid on Geryon becomes a full military expedition with an army, a fleet, and campaigns in Iberia, Celtica, and Italy on the way home (4.17–24, paraphrased).

Diodorus' order differs from Apollodorus' at three paired points, and the differences are most clearly shown in the table in this section. His third labour is the boar and his fourth the hind, where Apollodorus has hind third and boar fourth. His fifth is the birds and his sixth the stables, where Apollodorus has stables fifth and birds sixth. And at the end the swap is structural: Diodorus makes Cerberus the eleventh labour (4.25–26) and the apples of the Hesperides the twelfth and last (4.26–27), where Apollodorus ends with Cerberus. Diodorus also differs in substance at points the table cannot show. His Heracles undertakes the service under a bargain struck before his birth (Section IV); his madness comes after Zeus has already ordered the service and Heracles has refused the first summons (4.10.6–4.11.1, paraphrased); he does not operate Apollodorus' disqualification arithmetic — the Hydra and the stables are not struck from his count, and the twelve are twelve from the outset, not ten-plus-two; his Augeas labour is commanded to be done without the help of any other man and carries no wage bargain (4.13.3); his Diomedes is fed to his own mares by Heracles directly (4.15.3–4); and his Hippolyta labour ends not with a queen killed through Hera's rumour but with a full Amazon battle, a list of named Amazon champions, and Melanippe ransoming herself with the girdle (4.16, paraphrased). Neither account is a corruption of the other. They are two late, learned reconciliations of a tradition that had never been single.

Apollodorus: The Standard Order and Its Status

The account modern retellings follow is the Library (Bibliothēkē) attributed to Apollodorus — in current scholarly usage, Pseudo-Apollodorus, since the work, a systematic prose handbook of Greek myth in three books and an epitome, is now generally dated to the first or second century CE and cannot be by Apollodorus of Athens, the second-century BCE scholar whose name it carries. Its Heracles narrative runs from 2.4.8 to 2.7.8, and the labours occupy 2.5.1–2.5.12: one numbered section per labour, in the order this article follows. Apollodorus' virtues are compression and completeness: he gives variants where he knows them (“Acusilaus says… but some say…”), names the authorities embedded in his narrative, and preserves, inside the story, the disqualification mechanism that explains the number twelve (Section VI). His order became the standard not because it is the oldest — it is among the latest of our witnesses — but because his handbook survived entire and was mined by everyone after him. Hyginus, in the Latin Fabulae 30 (attributed to a writer of the Roman imperial period), gives a bare list of the same twelve with the boar and hind, and birds and stables, swapped in the manner the table shows — a reminder that even list-makers working from the finished canon did not agree on its internal sequence.

III. Olympia: All Twelve in Stone, c. 460 BCE

The earliest surviving complete set of twelve is not a text at all. The Temple of Zeus at Olympia — the great Doric temple of the Panhellenic sanctuary in Elis, built c. 470–457 BCE in the decades after the Persian Wars — carried, above the porches at either end of its inner building, twelve carved metopes, the square relief panels of the Doric frieze: six over the east porch, six over the west, one labour to a panel. Heracles was the inevitable subject. Olympia was his sanctuary in a special sense: tradition made him the founder of the Olympic Games (Diodorus 4.14 gives him the founding as an interlude in the labours themselves, after the Cretan bull), the labours were athloi — contests — in the home of contests, and the temple of his father Zeus was the natural frame for the son's ordeals. The metopes survive, fragmentary but substantially recoverable, divided between the Archaeological Museum at Olympia and the Louvre; their subjects are identified by the surviving sculpture itself and by the description of the traveller Pausanias, who saw them in place in the second century CE.

“Most of the labours of Heracles are represented at Olympia. Above the doors of the temple is carved the hunting of the Arcadian boar, his exploit against Diomedes the Thracian, and that against Geryones at Erytheia; he is also about to receive the burden of Atlas, and he cleanses the land from dung for the Eleans. Above the doors of the rear chamber he is taking the girdle from the Amazon; and there are the affairs of the deer, of the bull at Cnossus, of the Stymphalian birds, of the hydra, and of the Argive lion.” — PAUSANIAS, DESCRIPTION OF GREECE 5.10.9, AS GIVEN IN THE PERSEUS TEXT

Pausanias' sentence is the key document, and it repays the close reading the Perseus Digital Library's entry on the Olympia metopes gives it. He names eleven labours. Cerberus — which the findspots and the surviving fragments show did occupy a panel — is missing from his list, an inadvertent omission in an otherwise careful inventory. He lists the porches in the order he viewed them, not in narrative order. Reconstructing a reading order for the twelve is therefore a scholarly construction, and it has been constructed in more than one way. Read as a visitor approaching and entering would encounter them — west porch first, each porch left to right — the sequence runs: Nemean lion, Hydra, Stymphalian birds, Cretan bull, Ceryneian hind, Amazon's girdle; Erymanthian boar, mares of Diomedes, Geryon, Hesperides (Atlas with the apples), Cerberus, Augean stables. That reconstruction, associated with Martin Robertson's argument from the findspots, gives a satisfying frame — lion first, as everywhere; the local Elean labour, the stables, last, as a capstone in the sanctuary's own territory — and the Oxford Handbook of Heracles' survey of the lists reports the suggestion, credited to Boardman and Gantz, that the stables' anomalous last place reflects exactly that local pride: Elis honouring its own labour at the climax. But the same survey lists the metopes' order with the stables twelfth precisely as a deviation from every literary list, and other arrangements of the panels have been defended, including a rearrangement after earthquake damage. The honest summary is: the membership is certain, the count is certain, the original reading order is argued.

What, then, do the metopes fix? They fix the number. By about 460 BCE, at the most Panhellenic address in Greece, the labours were publicly and monumentally twelve — not ten, not an open series. They fix a membership: the twelve panels are the twelve of the later canon, the same feats Apollodorus and Diodorus and Hyginus list, with no Centaurs, no Cycnus, no Antaeus among them — the side-feats are already, in stone, side-feats. They fix an iconography: Athena appears as Heracles' divine helper in several panels — with the birds, with the stables, at the Hesperides — a visual theology of assisted labour that the texts distribute more sparingly; and the Heracles of the metopes is notably weary in places, a labouring, burdened figure — seated, resting, receiving the apples from Atlas with Athena's support — rather than a triumphant strongman, an interpretation in marble of what an athlos costs. And they fix a terminus: whatever Peisander may or may not have done two centuries earlier, the canon demonstrably exists, complete, by the middle of the fifth century BCE.

What they do not fix is equally important. They do not fix a narrative: a metope shows a moment, not a story, and several panels — the hind, the bull — are unintelligible as narrative without a text or a guide, as Pausanias' own glosses show he understood. They do not fix the order in which the labours were performed, since their arrangement answers to architecture and to a visitor's path, not to chronology — the stables last at Olympia are first-six in every text. They do not fix the frame: nothing at Olympia tells a viewer why Heracles labours, whether for purification, by Zeus's bargain, or in Euripides' bleaker sequence. And they do not prove that the canon was created by the temple. Stafford's formulation, reported in Heroes and Epic and followed here, is that the Olympia metopes are the first clear instance of the collection of twelve, and that it has been suggested the number was in part dictated by the twelve spaces the architecture offered — six and six. That suggestion cuts both ways, and should be allowed to: a temple needing twelve panels would canonise a twelve; a tradition already counting twelve would give a temple its programme. The evidence shows the two in place together around 460 BCE and cannot, by itself, say which summoned the other.

A generation later, the Temple of Hephaestus at Athens (c. 450 BCE) carved Heracles again — but only nine labours, in its own order (lion, Hydra, hind, boar, mares, Cerberus, Amazons, Geryon across two panels, Hesperides), as the Oxford Handbook's list-survey records. Even after Olympia, a major temple could select rather than recite. The canon was available; it was not yet a straitjacket.

One further witness belongs in this section because it is stone's contemporary and shows the canon still molten. Sophocles' Trachiniae (Women of Trachis, date debated within the fifth century) has Heracles, in agony, address his own arms and recall what they subdued (1089–1100, paraphrased from the excerpts in Gantz): the Nemean lion, the Lernaean Hydra, the army of Centaurs, the Erymanthian beast, the three-headed hound of Hades underground, and the dragon that guarded the golden fruit at the ends of the earth. Five labours and a parergon, chosen for a dying man's retrospect, in no canonical order. Tragedy, lyric, and prose each kept their own Heracles long after the sculptors of Olympia had agreed on twelve panels. The canon was a fact about the tradition; it was never a law over it.

IV. The Frame: Hera, the Delayed Birth, and Eurystheus First

The labours have a master, and the master has a grievance written into the order of births. The frame story, in its fullest form at Apollodorus 2.4.8–2.4.12 and Diodorus 4.9, is this. Zeus lay with Alcmene in the likeness of her absent husband Amphitryon — Diodorus (4.9.2–3, paraphrased) adds that Zeus prolonged the night to three times its length, and insists, in his rationalising manner, that the union was for the sake of begetting a benefactor, not from desire. Alcmene bore twins of different fathers: Iphicles, Amphitryon's son, and Heracles, Zeus's. But before the birth Zeus had announced among the gods — in the Iliad's telling (19.95–133), in answer to Hera's prompting of a boast he could not retract — that the descendant of Perseus born that day would rule over his neighbours. Hera, who hated the child of her husband's infidelity before he existed, hastened to Argos, delayed Alcmene's labour by binding the birth-goddesses, the Eileithyiae, and brought Eurystheus, son of Sthenelus and grandson of Perseus, into the world first — a seven-month child, Apollodorus says (2.4.5, paraphrased). Zeus's word stood. The weaker cousin was king of Tiryns and Mycenae by divine pronouncement; the stronger was, by the same pronouncement, born to be ruled by him. The whole servitude is contained in that delivery room: the labours are what happens when cosmic precedence is settled by a trick of timing.

Diodorus gives the frame a contractual form that Apollodorus does not (4.9.5, paraphrased): Zeus, outmanoeuvred but unwilling either to break his word or to abandon Heracles, persuaded Hera to agree that Eurystheus should indeed be king, but that Heracles, after serving him and performing twelve labours of Eurystheus' prescribing, should receive the gift of immortality. In Diodorus, therefore, the number is twelve from before the hero's birth, the service is a bargain between gods, and immortality is its stated prize from the start. In Apollodorus, by contrast, the oracle will speak of ten labours and twelve years (2.4.12), and the twelve of the finished cycle will be produced by Eurystheus' disqualifications, inside history, by a king's spite. The two frames cannot both be the tradition's single original; they are two different explanations of the same finished fact — that there were twelve, and that they ended in godhead — one theological and prospective, the other legalistic and retrospective. This article reports both, in their places, and builds its narrative on Apollodorus' frame while flagging Diodorus' at every point where they diverge.

The infant's first ordeal came at eight months, or in some tellings sooner. Hera sent two serpents into the twins' cradle; Iphicles screamed, and Heracles — so Apollodorus 2.4.8 and Diodorus 4.10.1, paraphrased — seized a serpent in each hand and strangled them, and was found in the morning holding the dead snakes. The scene is among the most painted in all Greek art, and its function in the frame is precise: the persecution that will drive the man begins with the child, and the child's answer to it — bare hands, no weapon, a grip that does not relax — is the template of the first labour and the last. Diodorus (4.10.1) adds his etymology: the Argives, learning what had happened, named the boy Heracles because he had gained kleos, glory, through Hera — the persecutor unwillingly supplying both the trials and the name they glorified. Other traditions make the naming Delphic (Apollodorus 2.4.12) or an attempted propitiation of Hera. The sources disagree about the name as they disagree about so much else; they agree that the name and the hatred belong together.

Grown, Heracles was educated — Apollodorus (2.4.9, paraphrased) lists his teachers and the gifts of the gods, a sword from Hermes, bow and arrows from Apollo, a golden breastplate from Hephaestus, a robe from Athena, and the club he cut for himself — and he first made his name in war, not in service. The Minyans of Orchomenus exacted a yearly tribute of a hundred cattle from Thebes for the killing of their king Clymenus; Heracles mutilated the tribute-collectors when they came, defeated the Minyan army, killed their king Erginus, and imposed a double tribute in reverse (Apollodorus 2.4.11; Diodorus 4.10.3–5, paraphrased). Creon, king of Thebes, gave him his eldest daughter Megara in marriage as a prize of valour. Heracles had a wife, three sons by her — Therimachus, Creontiades, and Deicoon in Apollodorus' naming — a city grateful to him, and a reputation without equal in Greece. It is at exactly this point, with everything to lose, that the frame closes on him. How it closes is the subject of the next section, and it is the point on which the tradition most consequentially disagrees with itself.

V. The Madness and the Chronology Problem

In the standard account — the account this hub's Heroes and Epic summarises, and the one Apollodorus and Diodorus both, in their different ways, presuppose — the sequence is: madness first, labours after, as expiation. Hera sent a madness (lyssa) upon Heracles. In Apollodorus (2.4.12, paraphrased) he flung his own children by Megara, and two children of Iphicles, into the fire; Megara herself survives in this version, to be given in marriage to Iolaus after the labours (2.6.1). In Diodorus (4.11.1–2, paraphrased) he tried first to kill Iolaus, who escaped, and shot his children with his bow, believing them enemies; on recovering, he recognised “the mistake he had made through a misapprehension” — the historian's careful phrasing is reported here as paraphrase of Oldfather's rendering — grieved, shunned company, and at last resolved to undergo the dangers. Condemning himself to exile, the Apollodoran Heracles was purified by Thespius and went to Delphi to ask where he should dwell. The labours, on this construction, are a sentence and a purification: the service is what a polluted man owes, and its completion is what makes him clean — and, the oracle adds, immortal.

Euripides reverses it. In the Heracles (c. 416 BCE) the labours are already complete when the play begins. Heracles has been absent on the last of them, the descent for Cerberus; in his absence the usurper Lycus has seized Thebes, killed Creon, and marked Megara, the children, and old Amphitryon for death. Heracles returns — with Cerberus still in tow, a stage property of appalling casualness — rescues his family, and kills Lycus. Then, at the summit of the completed career, Hera acts: Iris and Lyssa, Madness personified, appear above the house, and Lyssa — in one of the most extraordinary scenes in Greek tragedy — protests: she recites Heracles' services to gods and men, the monsters cleared from land and sea, and declares she does not wish to drive this man mad. Iris, speaking for Hera, overrules her. Lyssa obeys against her will, and Heracles, in the madness, believing he is assaulting the house of Eurystheus, kills Megara and all three children. He wakes bound to a pillar, amid the bodies, to Amphitryon's telling. What follows — his resolve to live rather than die, Theseus' arrival and offer of purification and a home in Athens, the famous exchange in which Heracles rejects the poets' tales of gods' adulteries and chains as unworthy of belief even as he submits to necessity — is tragedy's deepest exploration of the hero. But for the historian of the cycle, the play's first fact is its chronology: the crime the labours were supposed to expiate has not yet happened when the labours end. The service, in Euripides, cannot be a penance. It is undertaken, the play's background suggests, under a prior obligation to Eurystheus unconnected with blood-guilt — and the madness is not its cause but its ghastly sequel, Hera's answer to a completed success.

The two sequences are irreconcilable, and no ancient source reconciles them. Apollodorus and Diodorus: madness → purification → Delphi → service as expiation. Euripides: service → return in triumph → madness → a second, unexpiated catastrophe answered not by further labours but by Theseus' friendship and the decision to endure. Which is older cannot be demonstrated from surviving evidence: Euripides is by centuries the earlier surviving author, but he was famous in antiquity for innovating against received myth, and a tragedian's reversal is exactly the kind of stroke his audience was trained to notice. The scholia and later critics already debated such Euripidean innovations. This article follows the Apollodoran sequence for its narrative, because the article's subject is the cycle as a cycle — and flags, here and in Section XXII, that the Euripidean sequence exists, is earlier in survival, and makes a different and arguably darker sense: that the labours purchase nothing, and Hera's hatred is not a debt to be discharged but a condition of the hero's existence.

Euripides returned to the family in the Heracleidae (Children of Heracles, usually dated c. 430 BCE, though dates into the 420s have been argued). There Heracles is already dead, and the persecution has descended a generation: Eurystheus, through his herald Copreus — the same herald who conveys the labours' commands in Apollodorus — demands that Athens surrender the hero's children, who have fled from city to city with Iolaus, now an old man, and with Alcmene. Demophon, son of Theseus, refuses; an oracle requires the sacrifice of a nobly-born maiden; a daughter of Heracles — named Macaria in the later tradition, unnamed in the play's text as transmitted — offers herself; the Athenians win the battle, Iolaus is miraculously rejuvenated in the fighting and captures Eurystheus, and Alcmene demands and obtains his death despite Athenian scruple, Eurystheus prophesying from the edge of execution that his buried body will protect Athens against the children's own descendants (paraphrased from the play's standard summaries and the excerpts consulted). The Heracleidae matters here for two reasons. It shows the labours' frame — Eurystheus' enmity, Copreus' heralding, Iolaus' loyalty — fully formed on the Athenian stage a decade or more before the Heracles. And it shows what the cycle was for, in fifth-century Athens: a charter. Athens shelters the persecuted children as it sheltered, in its own civic mythology, every suppliant; the Heraclidae will become the Dorian invaders of the Peloponnese in the mythic charter of Sparta's kings, who claimed Heraclid descent; and Eurystheus, the labours' master, ends as an Attic cult-hero in his own right, buried in the land he invaded. The frame outlived the hero, and went on doing political work.

One more variant of the motive deserves recording, because it removes the madness altogether. A strand of the tradition, reflected in Diodorus' pre-natal bargain (Section IV) and summarised in the reference literature from the ancient scholia, made the service purely fated: Zeus, to save Heracles from being merely Hera's victim, obtained her agreement that if he performed the labours in Eurystheus' service he should become immortal — the labours as a ladder, not a sentence. Euripides' own background, in which service precedes any crime, belongs nearer this strand than to the penance strand. The tradition, in short, offers three motives for the same twelve tasks — expiation for a crime committed, bargain for a godhead promised, and fate imposed by a birthright stolen — and distributes them differently in every major telling. A retelling that gives one motive as simply “the” reason for the labours has chosen, and should say so. This article's narrative choice is Apollodorus': expiation, with immortality promised as its crown.

VI. Delphi, Tiryns, and the Terms of Service

Purified by Thespius, Heracles went to Delphi and asked the god where he should dwell (Apollodorus 2.4.12, paraphrased from Frazer's translation). The Pythian priestess, the account says, then first called him Heracles — hitherto he had been Alcides — and told him to dwell in Tiryns, serving Eurystheus for twelve years, and to perform the ten labours imposed on him; and so, she said, when the tasks were accomplished he would be immortal. Every term in the oracle does work. The new name marks the new life, as a sentence marks a man. Tiryns, the great Cyclopean fortress in the Argolid a few miles from Mycenae, is Eurystheus' seat in Apollodorus' usage — the two citadels are used almost interchangeably in the tradition, Eurystheus being king of both, and this article follows Apollodorus in naming Tiryns the place of service and Mycenae the place of delivery. The twelve years give the service a term: it is not slavery for life but a sentence with a date. The ten labours give it a count. And the promise of immortality converts the sentence, at its end, into the bargain of the other strand of the tradition — Apollodorus quietly holding both motives at once, penance in the foreground and godhead at the horizon.

The mechanics of the service are given in the first labour's aftermath and never change. Heracles reported to Tiryns and did as he was bid. After the Nemean lion (Section VII), Eurystheus, amazed and frightened, forbade him to enter the city thereafter: the fruits of the labours were to be exhibited before the gates. Commands were conveyed through a herald, Copreus, son of Pelops the Elean — himself a killer in exile, purified by Eurystheus, a herald whose own polluted past mirrors his master's servant's. And Eurystheus, Apollodorus reports with the mythographer's flat “they say”, had a bronze jar (pithos) made, to hide in under the earth. Diodorus (4.12.2) attaches the jar to a different labour — the boar, carried in alive on Heracles' shoulders, at whose sight the king hid himself in a bronze vessel — and vase-painting made the jar Eurystheus' comic signature, the great king crouching in his pot while the hero holds up the boar. The detail is worth dwelling on because it inverts the service's formal structure: the master commands, judges, and disallows; the master also hides. Greek audiences plainly relished the inversion, and the tradition never lets Eurystheus out of the jar for long.

The Arithmetic: Ten, Two Struck, Twelve

The count that governs the cycle is produced, in Apollodorus, by adjudication. The oracle commanded ten. Heracles performed ten — in eight years and a month, Apollodorus notes at 2.5.11, a startlingly precise chronology for a myth, and one of the fingerprints of the mythographic method: the handbook wants dates and totals. Eurystheus then refused to acknowledge two of the ten. The Hydra was disallowed because Iolaus had helped (2.5.2). The Augean stables were disallowed because the labour had been performed for hire (2.5.5). Two further tasks were therefore imposed as eleventh and twelfth: the apples of the Hesperides (2.5.11) and Cerberus (2.5.12). Twelve performed; ten counted; the service complete. The mechanism deserves the emphasis this article gives it, because it reveals what kind of object the canonical cycle is. The twelve is not a number found in nature, like the months; it is a number reached — by an oracle's ten, a king's two objections, and a mythographer's reconciliation of the result with the twelve that Olympia had carved and the tradition already recited. Apollodorus is not inventing the disqualifications: the Hydra's helper and the stables' wage are old elements of those stories, and each disqualification follows from a genuine internal feature of its labour — help received, payment bargained. What the mythographer contributes is the use of those features as counting-rules. It is the most elegant piece of editorial engineering in Greek myth, and it should be admired as engineering, not mistaken for a Bronze Age datum.

RULE ONE: NO HELP

Established at the Hydra (Apollodorus 2.5.2). A labour performed with a helper's decisive assistance does not count. The rule is applied retrospectively, by the master, after the feat is done — and it is not applied to Athena's help at the birds (2.5.6), a divine assistance the tradition treats as belonging to a different category. The inconsistency is in the sources, and is reported, not resolved, here.

RULE TWO: NO WAGE

Established at the stables (Apollodorus 2.5.5). A labour bargained for a reward is hired work, not an athlos, and does not count. Note that Augeas in fact refused to pay: the labour was disqualified not by a wage received but by a wage agreed — the bargain itself corrupted the contest.

THE TERM

Twelve years of service (Apollodorus 2.4.12); the first ten labours performed in eight years and a month (2.5.11). Diodorus knows no such arithmetic: in his account twelve are prescribed from the outset by the bargain of the gods (4.9.5).

THE PRIZE

Immortality on completion (Apollodorus 2.4.12; Diodorus 4.9.5, 4.26.4). The prize is stated at the start in both prose accounts — the reader always knows what the service is for, even when the hero, in Diodorus' telling of the summons, despairs of it.

With the terms set, the labours themselves can be told. They are told here in Apollodorus' order, one section each, with the variants that matter — Diodorus' differing details, Hesiod's genealogies, tragedy's glances, and the local traditions Pausanias and others preserve — given in place. Each labour is treated first as myth as told; cultic footings are noted where a labour generated one, and gathered in Section XXIII.

VII. Labour One: The Nemean Lion

The command (Apollodorus 2.5.1): bring the skin of the Nemean lion. The beast, in Apollodorus, was invulnerable and begotten by Typhon; in Hesiod (Theogony 327–332, paraphrased) it was the offspring of Echidna, reared by Hera and settled by her in the hills of Nemea as a plague to men — the persecutor's own animal, placed in the hero's path as his first task. Nemea lies in the hills between Corinth and the Argolid, a valley later famous for its own Panhellenic games in honour of Zeus; the lion ravaged the district and its flocks, and no weapon could wound it.

The feat, in Apollodorus (paraphrased from Frazer): on the way, Heracles lodged at Cleonae with a day-labourer named Molorchus, who would have sacrificed a victim for his guest's safety. Heracles told him to wait thirty days: if he returned safe, sacrifice to Zeus the Saviour; if not, sacrifice to Heracles himself as to a hero — the cycle's first quiet anticipation of the cult of Section XXIII, planted in its first episode. At Nemea he tracked the lion and shot at it; the arrow told him what the stories meant by invulnerable. He took up his club and pursued it; the lion retreated into a cave with two mouths; Heracles built up one entrance, entered by the other, put his arm round its neck, and held it, choking, until it died. Diodorus (4.11.3) gives the same mechanics — the beast lairing in the cleft of Mount Tretus, the other opening closed, the grappling and the choking — in the sentence quoted here from Oldfather's translation because it states the labour's principle exactly:

“The first Labour which he undertook was the slaying of the lion in Nemea. This was a beast of enormous size, which could not be wounded by iron or bronze or stone and required the compulsion of the human hand for his subduing.” — DIODORUS SICULUS, LIBRARY OF HISTORY 4.11.3, TRANS. C. H. OLDFATHER (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY)

The trophy. The dead lion posed the labour's last problem: an impenetrable skin cannot be cut by any blade. The tradition's answer, given in the later mythographers and presupposed everywhere in art, is that Heracles cut it with the lion's own claw — the only edge that could pierce it — and thereafter wore the skin as his armour, the lion's scalp as his helmet, its gaping jaws framing his face. Diodorus (4.11.4, paraphrased) makes the function explicit: the skin covered his whole body and served as protection against the perils to follow. From this labour onward, Heracles is visually unmistakable — club, bow, lionskin — in every medium Greek art possessed; Peisander was credited in antiquity with fixing exactly this equipment in poetry. He carried the lion to Cleonae on the thirtieth day, finding Molorchus on the point of sacrificing to him as to a dead man, and sacrificed instead with him to Zeus the Saviour; then to Mycenae, where Eurystheus' amazement produced the standing rules of Section VI — delivery at the gates, commands by herald, and the bronze jar. The first labour thus generates the cycle's whole machinery: the hero's uniform, the master's fear, and the protocol of service.

Variants and footing. Theocritus' twenty-fifth Idyll, a Hellenistic miniature epic, expands the lion-fight into a full narrative with the Molorchus hospitality at its centre — evidence of how a single labour could still sustain an independent poem centuries after the canon closed. Pausanias (2.15.2) records the lion's cave shown at Nemea and the mountain Tretus in his own day: the labour had acquired the usual local furniture of a Greek myth, a place to point at. At Olympia the lion stands first in the reconstructed reading order, Heracles shown not in the struggle but with the beast already dead — the Severe Style's preference for the moment after effort, the labourer resting on his club. The Nemean Games themselves were connected in local tradition with other myths (the death of the infant Opheltes) rather than with the lion; the lion's cultic footprint is the sacrifice to Zeus Soter at Cleonae inside the story, not a festival outside it.

VIII. Labour Two: The Lernaean Hydra

The command (Apollodorus 2.5.2): kill the Hydra of Lerna. Lerna is a marshy coastal district a few miles south of Argos, a place of springs — the spring Amymone, the Alcyonian lake through which Dionysus was said to have descended to Hades — and of a real, perennial wetness that Greek imagination populated accordingly. The Hydra, in Hesiod (Theogony 313–318, paraphrased), was Echidna's offspring, reared by Hera in the swamp out of her anger at Heracles; Apollodorus gives it a huge body and nine heads, eight mortal and the middle one immortal. It came out of the marsh and ravaged the cattle and the country of the plain.

The feat (Apollodorus 2.5.2, paraphrased): Heracles came to Lerna in a chariot driven by Iolaus — his nephew, son of Iphicles, the companion of the whole career, here entering the cycle in the role that will define the labour's fate. With fiery arrows he forced the Hydra from its den beside the springs of Amymone and grappled with it; it coiled round his foot and held him; and clubbing its heads accomplished nothing, for as fast as one head was smashed, two grew in its place. A huge crab, sent — the wider tradition says by Hera — came to the Hydra's aid and bit his foot; he killed it. (The crab was placed among the stars as the constellation Cancer, in the catasterism literature.) Then Heracles called on Iolaus, who set fire to the neighbouring wood and, with the burning brands, seared the necks as the heads were cut off, preventing the regrowth. The sprouting heads mastered, Heracles cut off the immortal head, buried it, and laid a heavy rock on it beside the road through Lerna to Elaeus. The body he slit open, and in its gall — its venom — he dipped his arrows.

That last act is the labour's long shadow. The Hydra's venom makes Heracles' arrows invariably fatal and their wounds incurable from this point in the career, and the career's tragedies are written with them: the centaur Chiron, wounded by accident and unable either to live cured or to die (Section X); the centaur Nessus, shot at the river Evenus, whose envenomed blood, preserved by Deianira as a supposed love-charm, will kill Heracles himself (Section XXII); and Philoctetes, heir of the bow, whose festering wound will structure a tragedy of Sophocles and a condition of Troy's fall. A labour's weapon becomes the hero's death: the cycle contains its own ending inside its second episode, and Greek storytellers knew it — Euripides' chorus, in the ode of Section II, couples the Hydra's burning directly with the smearing of the arrows, in a single breath.

The disqualification. The labour was performed, the monster dead, the proof delivered — and Eurystheus struck it from the count. In Frazer's translation of Apollodorus 2.5.2, as read for this article:

“However, Eurystheus said that this labour should not be reckoned among the ten because he had not got the better of the hydra by himself, but with the help of Iolaus.” — APOLLODORUS, LIBRARY 2.5.2, TRANS. J. G. FRAZER (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY, 1921)

Variants. Diodorus (4.11.5–6, paraphrased) inflates the Hydra to a hundred necks, makes the searing Heracles' own devised scheme executed at his command by Iolaus, and — consistently with his twelve-from-the-outset frame — records no disqualification: the episode closes with the dipping of the arrows in the venom “in order that… the wound which the point made might be incurable”. Earlier art is freer still: Archaic vase-painting shows Iolaus as a full combatant at the Hydra, sword in hand among the heads, not a torch-bearer at the margin — the helper's role was originally larger, and the disqualification reads, in part, like a later legalism imposed on an older partnership. Iolaus had his own cult in Boeotia and at Agyrium in Sicily (Diodorus 4.24.4–6 describes the precinct, the annual honours, and the hair-offerings of the boys of the city in remarkable detail), and his presence in the cycle is not an intrusion but a second heroic career woven through the first. The rule that disqualified the labour was Eurystheus'; the tradition's verdict, which kept Iolaus at Heracles' side in cult and story alike, was otherwise.

IX. Labour Three: The Ceryneian Hind

The command (Apollodorus 2.5.3): bring the Ceryneian hind alive to Mycenae. The hind — a female red deer, in Greek elaphos — was at Oenoe in the Argolid; it had golden horns — antlers, on a doe, the first of the labour's impossibilities — and in some tellings brazen hooves; and it was sacred to Artemis. The command is constructed as a trap of a new kind. The lion and the Hydra were to be killed; the hind is to be taken alive and unharmed, though it is faster than any hunter and belongs to a goddess who punishes exactly this offence. Force is useless and impiety is fatal: the labour tests not strength but endurance and, at the end, diplomacy.

The feat (Apollodorus 2.5.3, paraphrased): wishing neither to kill nor to wound it, Heracles hunted it for a whole year. The chase carried across Arcadia; at last, weary, the hind took refuge on Mount Artemisius and came down to the river Ladon, and there, as it was about to cross, Heracles shot it — a shot the account is careful to present as a capture, not a wound of consequence — caught it, put it on his shoulders, and hastened through Arcadia. On the way, Artemis and Apollo met him, and the goddess would have taken the hind from him, rebuking him for attempting to kill her sacred animal. Heracles pleaded necessity and laid the blame on Eurystheus — the plea that the command was not his choice — and appeased her anger, and carried the beast alive to Mycenae. The plea deserves notice: it is the hero's one legal defence, used here against a goddess, and it works. The service that enslaves him also indemnifies him; he is an instrument, and the tradition lets the instrument say so.

Variants. Diodorus (4.13.1), who places the hind fourth, strips the episode to its moral and openly collects the variants, in Oldfather's rendering paraphrased here: some say he took it in nets; others, that he tracked it and mastered it in its sleep; others, that he wore it out by running it down; what is certain is that he accomplished the labour by sagacity, without force and without danger. The sentence is a small manifesto for Diodorus' Heracles, the benefactor whose intelligence ranks with his strength, and it is quoted in spirit here because it shows an ancient author doing openly what this article does throughout: laying the variants side by side and declining to choose beyond what the story requires. Pindar, in the third Olympian ode, knows a different geography for the hind altogether: Heracles pursued it to the land of the Hyperboreans behind the North Wind, in the country of the Ister, where he saw the olive trees he later brought back to shade the racecourse at Olympia — the labour folded into the aetiology of the Games. The hind's year-long chase, in the fullest tellings, is thus also a tour of the world's northern edge, a first rehearsal for the westward journeys of the later labours. At Olympia the hind's metope shows Heracles holding the living animal by the horns, Athena present: capture, not slaughter, rendered in stone.

// A DOE WITH ANTLERS //

Ancient commentators already noticed that female deer do not grow antlers, and the golden horns of a hind were read in antiquity itself as a mark of the marvellous — or emended, in some retellings, into a stag. The impossibility is best left as it stands: the labour's quarry is a creature that violates categories, sacred, golden, female and horned, and is to be mastered without being harmed. Category-violating quarry, handled by rule, is the cycle's recurring signature.

X. Labour Four: The Erymanthian Boar

The command (Apollodorus 2.5.4): bring the Erymanthian boar alive. The boar ravaged Psophis in north-western Arcadia, sallying from Mount Erymanthus, one of the great mountain masses of the Peloponnese. Like the hind, it is to be captured, not killed — but where the hind's difficulty was speed and sanctity, the boar's is mass and violence: a huge animal, in its own country of snow and thicket, to be taken living and carried out on a man's shoulders. Diodorus (4.12.1–2, paraphrased) makes the calibration explicit: strike too softly and the tusks take you; strike too hard and the labour fails, for the beast must live. He adds the jar: when the king saw Heracles carrying the boar on his shoulders, he was terrified and hid in a bronze vessel — the detail Apollodorus attaches to the lion. The discrepancy is minor and characteristic; the jar migrates to whichever delivery frightened Eurystheus most, and in the visual tradition it is the boar delivery, the live animal kicking above the rim of the pot, that painters chose.

The feat and its catastrophe. Apollodorus' boar is dispatched in a few lines — chased with shouts from its thicket, driven exhausted into deep snow, trapped, and carried to Mycenae — because the section's real narrative weight falls on what happened on the way, the most consequential parergon in the cycle. Passing through Pholoe, Heracles was entertained by the centaur Pholus, a civilised member of a savage kind, son of Silenus. Pholus served his guest roast meat, eating his own raw; Heracles asked for wine; Pholus feared to open the jar of wine that belonged to the centaurs in common — in Diodorus (4.12.3), a jar left by Dionysus himself generations before, to be opened only when Heracles came. Heracles opened it. The scent drew the centaurs, armed with rocks and fir-trees; Heracles repelled the first with firebrands and shot the rest in pursuit as far as Malea, where they crowded round Chiron, the wise and immortal centaur, exiled there from Pelion. An arrow meant for the crowd passed through the arm of the centaur Elatus and struck Chiron in the knee. It was a Hydra arrow. Heracles drew it out and applied a remedy Chiron himself supplied, but the wound of the Hydra's venom does not heal; Chiron, who could not die, longed for death, and was released only when Prometheus offered himself to Zeus as immortal in Chiron's stead — an exchange the tradition also connects with Prometheus' liberation (Section XIX). The other centaurs scattered — Eurytion to Pholoe, where he will meet Heracles again; Nessus to the river Evenus, where he will wait twenty years for his revenge — and Pholus, drawing an arrow from a corpse and marvelling that so small a thing could kill so great a creature, dropped it on his own foot and died on the spot. Heracles buried him and went on to the boar.

The episode is told here at length, though it is formally a side-feat, because it demonstrates the cycle's inner economy better than any labour proper. A opened jar, a host's courtesy, a guest's appetite: from these, two deaths among friends, an immortal's surrender of immortality, and the dispersal of a whole people across the map of the later story — Nessus to the Evenus, Eurytion to Olenus, Chiron to his exchange. Apollodorus himself registers the pollution: before the descent for Cerberus, Heracles must be cleansed specifically of the slaughter of the centaurs (2.5.12). Diodorus adds the institutional sequel (4.14.3, paraphrased): Demeter instituted the Lesser Mysteries for Heracles' sake, to purify him of that guilt. The boar was delivered alive, and the labour counted. Its price was paid by almost everyone except the man who performed it.

XI. Labour Five: The Stables of Augeas

The command (Apollodorus 2.5.5): clean out the cattle-yard of Augeas in a single day. Augeas, king of Elis in the western Peloponnese — son of the Sun, or of Poseidon, or of Phorbas; Apollodorus lists the three fathers without choosing — possessed enormous herds, and the yards where the cattle stood had not been cleaned, in the developed tradition, for thirty years. The labour is the cycle's deliberate insult: not a monster but dung; not danger but disgrace. Diodorus (4.13.3, paraphrased) states the motive in so many words: it was a spirit of insult that induced Eurystheus to lay the command on him, and Heracles' problem is framed as a problem of honour — how to perform the ignoble task without incurring the disgrace, without submitting to anything unworthy of the immortality he is earning. Both prose accounts then solve the labour the same way, and it is the only labour solved by engineering.

The feat (Apollodorus 2.5.5, paraphrased): Heracles approached Augeas without revealing Eurystheus' command — the concealment is essential to everything that follows — and offered to clean the yard in one day for a wage: a tenth of the cattle. Augeas, incredulous, agreed. Heracles took Augeas' son Phyleus to witness the bargain, breached the yard's foundations, cut channels, and diverted the two rivers that flowed nearby, the Alpheus and the Peneus, through the yard and out by another opening. The rivers did the work in the day. (Diodorus names the Alpheus alone and stresses the added condition, absent in Apollodorus, that the work be done without any other man's help.)

The dispute and the disqualification. When Augeas learned that the work had been done at Eurystheus' command — that he had been made, unknowing, an instrument of the service — he refused to pay, and denied he had ever promised. He agreed to arbitration; the arbitrators sat; Phyleus, called as witness by Heracles, testified against his own father that the bargain had been made. Augeas, before the vote, in rage ordered both Phyleus and Heracles out of Elis. Phyleus went into exile at Dulichium. Heracles left — and remembered. After the labours he would return with an army, kill Augeas and his sons, and install the exiled Phyleus on his father's throne (2.7.2; Section XXI). The labour's sequel is a war, and the war's justification, in the tradition's own terms, is not the insult but the broken oath: Augeas is the cycle's type of the wage-refusing king, twin to Laomedon of Troy, whose refusal of a promised reward to Heracles likewise ends in a sack (Section XV). Eurystheus, for his part, would not admit the labour among the ten, alleging that it had been performed for hire (2.5.5, paraphrased). The reasoning is the mirror of the Hydra rule: an athlos is a contest for honour; a job bargained for a tenth of the herd is a contract, and contracts are not contests — even though the wage was never paid, and even though the bargain was Heracles' own device for approaching the task without revealing the command. The two disqualifications together define the service's unwritten constitution, and both were supplied, as Section VI argued, by features internal to the stories themselves.

Footing. The labour is Elis' own, and Elis kept it: the Alpheus is Olympia's river, the stables' cleansing is the local labour that the Olympia metopes promoted to the climactic twelfth panel (Section III), and Pausanias (5.1.9–10 and the Elean books generally) preserves the local Augeas traditions, including the king's vast herds and the tomb-lore of his family. A minority strand of the tradition, noted in the reference literature, disqualified a different labour instead — the birds, on the ground of Athena's help — and left the stables counted; Apollodorus' version, wage and all, is the one that shaped the canon, and the one followed here.

XII. Labour Six: The Stymphalian Birds

The command (Apollodorus 2.5.6): drive away the Stymphalian birds. At Stymphalus in north-eastern Arcadia lay a lake embosomed in deep wood, to which countless birds had flocked, Apollodorus says, for refuge from the wolves. The developed tradition armed them: bronze beaks, claws, and feathers, feathers that could be shot like arrows, a taste in some accounts for human flesh, and droppings that blighted the crops. Apollodorus' own account is sparer — the birds are a multitude in an inaccessible place — and the spareness is worth preserving, because the labour's real problem, as he tells it, is not the birds' weaponry but their number and cover: a flock beyond shooting, in a wood and marsh a man cannot effectively enter. Heracles, he says, was at a loss how to drive them from the wood.

The feat (Apollodorus 2.5.6, paraphrased): Athena gave him brazen castanets — krotala — which she had received from Hephaestus. He climbed a mountain overhanging the lake and clashed them; the birds, unable to abide the sound, fluttered up in fright; and as they rose, Heracles shot them. The labour is the cycle's clearest case of a problem solved by noise and timing rather than force: the device converts an unshootable multitude on the ground into targets in the air, one volley at a time. Diodorus (4.13.2), who places the birds fifth, makes the device Heracles' own invention — he fashioned a bronze rattle himself, and by maintaining a continual din forced the birds to abandon the lake — a characteristic Diodoran transfer of credit from goddess to hero, in line with his benefactor's self-sufficiency, and a genuine variant, not a slip: where Apollodorus' Heracles receives divine equipment throughout his career (Section IV), Diodorus' increasingly devises. Pausanias (8.22), visiting Stymphalus, reports the local sequel: the birds were said to have fled to an island in the Black Sea, where the Argonauts would meet them again — the labour exporting its problem, in the Greek manner, to the edge of the map and into another hero's story.

The rule that was not applied. Athena's castanets are decisive help, from a god, in a labour that counted. Set beside the Hydra's disqualification for Iolaus' help, the inconsistency is plain, and this article does not harmonise it. The most that can be said from the sources is that the tradition appears to distinguish human partnership — which shares the kleos and so divides it — from divine patronage, which magnifies the labourer without competing with him; Athena helps Heracles across the whole career, in art as in text, without ever being treated as a co-performer. The minority variant that disqualified the birds instead of the stables (Section XI) shows that some ancient counters of the labours felt the difficulty and resolved it the other way. At Olympia, Athena stands in the birds metope itself, handing the hero the dead birds or receiving them — the goddess inside the panel, in the sanctuary where the canon was carved, and the labour counted there. The visual canon and the prose rule were never fully reconciled, because they were never the same kind of authority.

XIII. Labour Seven: The Cretan Bull

With the seventh labour the cycle leaves the Peloponnese. The first six labours form a home circuit — Nemea, Lerna, Ceryneia and Erymanthus in and around the Argolid and Arcadia, Elis in the west of the peninsula, Stymphalus in its north-east — all within the world Eurystheus rules or neighbours. From here the map opens: an island across the sea, the Thracian north, the Amazonian east, the Iberian far west, the garden beyond the world, and the world below. The widening is the cycle's geography made narrative, and Section XX returns to it; the bull is its hinge.

The command (Apollodorus 2.5.7): bring the Cretan bull. Apollodorus opens the section with a scholarly alternative, in Frazer's paraphrase followed here: Acusilaus, the early prose mythographer, said this was the bull that carried Europa across the sea for Zeus; but others said it was the bull Poseidon sent up from the sea when Minos, king of Crete, had promised to sacrifice to the god whatever should appear from it. Minos, seeing the bull's beauty, sent it among his herds and sacrificed another; Poseidon, in anger, made the bull savage. The second version binds the labour into the great Cretan complex — Pasiphae's passion for the same bull, in the developed tradition, and the Minotaur born of it — and makes the bull's madness a god's punishment for a king's substituted sacrifice: another broken bargain about an animal, rhyming with Augeas. Heracles came to Crete, asked Minos for help, and was told to fight and catch the bull himself. He caught it, brought it to Eurystheus and showed it to him, and then let it go free.

The sequel in the landscape. The released bull did not retire. Apollodorus (2.5.7) traces its afterlife with a mythographer's pleasure in continuity: it roamed to Sparta and through Arcadia, crossed the Isthmus, and came to Marathon in Attica, where it harried the inhabitants — until, in the Athenian tradition, Theseus captured it, led it through Athens, and sacrificed it to Athena or Apollo, in the deed that stood beside Heracles' labours as the Athenian hero's credential (see Heroes and Epic, Section VI). One labour's loose end becomes another hero's first proof: the cycle generates the next generation's material as it goes. Diodorus (4.13.4) gives the crossing a different marvel — Heracles, having secured Minos' aid, brought the bull back to the Peloponnese riding on its back across the sea — and interposes, before the next labour, the founding of the Olympic Games (4.14.1–2, paraphrased): the hero at the height of the home circuit establishing the contest of contests on the Alpheus, with a crown of wild olive as its only prize, because he himself had conferred his benefits without monetary reward. The interlude is Diodorus' alone among the prose accounts, and it states his reading of the whole cycle in one act: the labours are the benefactions of which the Games are the memorial.

XIV. Labour Eight: The Mares of Diomedes

The command (Apollodorus 2.5.8): bring the mares of Diomedes the Thracian to Mycenae. Diomedes — not the Achaean hero of the Iliad, but a Thracian king, son of Ares and Cyrene — ruled the Bistones, a warlike people of the Thracian coast, and owned mares that ate human flesh, fed, in the developed accounts, on strangers and the shipwrecked. The labour takes Heracles for the first time against a human establishment: not a beast in a landscape but a king, his grooms, his army, and a stable that is an institution of murder. The cycle's moral register changes with its geography; the outer world is not emptier than the Peloponnese but worse governed.

The feat (Apollodorus 2.5.8, paraphrased): Heracles sailed with a band of volunteers, overpowered the grooms at the mangers, and drove the mares down to the sea. The Bistones came in arms to the rescue; Heracles entrusted the mares to Abderus — a son of Hermes, from Opus in Locris, described by Apollodorus as a minion, a beloved companion, of Heracles — and turned to fight. While he fought, the mares killed Abderus, dragging him after them. Heracles defeated the Bistones, slew Diomedes, and compelled the rest to flee; he founded the city of Abdera beside Abderus' grave — a real city of the Thracian coast, later the home of Democritus and Protagoras, carrying a labour's casualty in its name into the history of philosophy — and brought the mares to Eurystheus. Eurystheus let them go; they came to Mount Olympus, Apollodorus says, and there were destroyed by wild beasts: the man-eaters eaten, at the mountain of the gods, by the ordinary predators of the world. Diodorus (4.15.3–4) compresses and sharpens: the mangers were of bronze and the mares chained with iron because of their strength; Heracles threw Diomedes himself to them, and when they had eaten the man who taught them their diet, they were mastered; brought to Eurystheus, they were consecrated to Hera, and their breed, Diodorus claims, survived to the reign of Alexander of Macedon. The two versions differ on who fed the mares their master — Apollodorus' Heracles kills Diomedes in battle, Diodorus' executes him by his own animals — but agree on the poetic justice the tradition required: the feeder fed.

Variants. Euripides' Alcestis (produced 438 BCE) already uses the mares as the type of a labour in progress: its Heracles arrives at Pherae on his way, as he tells Admetus, to the Thracian king's man-eating team, in the play whose plot — his wrestling Death for Alcestis' life — is itself one of the great parerga. The mares' labour was thus common property on the Athenian stage a generation after Olympia. The labour also shows the cycle's changing personnel: Abderus is the second beloved companion the service costs Heracles — after the friends of the boar episode, before Hylas, lost on the Argonaut voyage in the tradition that attached Heracles briefly to Jason's expedition — and the founding of Abdera is the first city-foundation inside Apollodorus' labours, the civilising motif that Diodorus will expand until it organises his whole account.

XV. Labour Nine: The Girdle of Hippolyta

The command (Apollodorus 2.5.9): bring the girdle — the war-belt, zōstēr — of Hippolyta, queen of the Amazons. She held it, Apollodorus explains, as the gift of Ares and the token of her superiority over all the rest. And the motive is the cycle's most deflating: Heracles was sent to fetch it because Admete, daughter of Eurystheus, desired to have it. A princess's wish for a famous accessory sends the hero to the edge of the known world and a queen to her death. The mythographers report the motive without comment; the disproportion is the comment. The Amazons dwelt about the river Thermodon in Pontus, on the southern shore of the Black Sea, at Themiscyra — a people, in Apollodorus' ethnographic aside, who cultivated war, reared their daughters for it, and cauterised the right breast that it might not hinder the javelin. The labour is the cycle's easternmost reach, balancing Geryon in the west.

The voyage out is, in Apollodorus, a string of parerga that the narrative refuses to hurry. At Paros, two of his men were killed by the sons of Minos; Heracles killed the sons, besieged the rest, and accepted two grandsons of Minos aboard in recompense. In Mysia he was entertained by Lycus, fought for him against the Bebryces, killed their king Mygdon, and gave Lycus the conquered land, which was called Heraclea after him — Heraclea Pontica, a real and major Black Sea city, again carrying the hero's passage in its name. At Themiscyra, the catastrophe was a misunderstanding engineered by the frame's persecutor. Hippolyta came out to meet him, asked why he had come, and promised to give him the girdle. The labour, uniquely, was about to be completed by diplomacy. Then Hera, in the likeness of an Amazon, went through the host spreading the word that the strangers were carrying off the queen. The Amazons armed and charged the ship on horseback; Heracles, seeing them in arms, suspected treachery, killed Hippolyta and stripped her of the girdle, fought the rest, and sailed away (2.5.9, paraphrased). No labour ends worse relative to its beginning. The queen who offered the prize dies for a rumour her killer did not start and she did not believe; the girdle is delivered; the labour counts. The tradition registers the waste in its variants: in some tellings the queen who dies is not Hippolyta but her sister Melanippe, and Hippolyta survives to be ransomed or to marry Theseus in the entangled Theseus-Amazon tradition; in Diodorus (4.16, paraphrased) there is no embassy and no rumour at all, but a pitched battle in which Heracles kills a roll of named Amazon champions — Aella the swift, Philippis, Prothoe, Eriboea, Celaeno, Eurybia, Phoebe, Deianeira, Asteria, Marpe, Tecmessa, Alcippe — captures the commander Melanippe, gives the captive Antiope to Theseus, and accepts the girdle as Melanippe's ransom. Diodorus' version is a war; Apollodorus' is a tragedy of misinformation. Both end with the belt in Mycenae.

The voyage home produced the labour's most consequential side-feat. At Troy, Heracles found the city under a plague and a sea-monster, the wages of Apollo and Poseidon's unpaid wall-building; King Laomedon had exposed his daughter Hesione to the monster, as the oracles required. Heracles promised to kill the monster for the divine mares Zeus had given Laomedon in compensation for Ganymede; he killed it and saved her; Laomedon refused the mares. Heracles sailed away threatening war (2.5.9) — and kept the threat after the labours, in the expedition of Section XXI that sacked Troy a generation before Priam's war and made Heracles, in the epic tradition's own chronology, the first sacker of the city the Achaeans would need ten years to take. The pattern of the wage-refused king, set at Elis with Augeas, here acquires its second and greater instance; between them, Augeas and Laomedon define the sin the Heracles cycle punishes most reliably — not cruelty, not impiety, but the broken bargain with the labourer.

XVI. Labour Ten: The Cattle of Geryon and the Pillars

The command (Apollodorus 2.5.10): fetch the cattle of Geryon from Erythia. Erythia, the “red” island, lay in the ocean beyond the Straits, near — Apollodorus says, identifying the mythic geography with the map of his own day — what is now called Gadira, Gades, modern Cádiz. Geryon, son of Chrysaor (who sprang from Medusa's neck) by Callirrhoe, daughter of Ocean, had the body of three men grown together at the waist and parted into three below — the triple body that Hesiod already knew (Theogony 287–293) and that Stesichorus' Geryoneis had made the subject of a great poem: the earliest labour to receive a surviving literary monument of its own. His red cattle were herded by Eurytion and guarded by Orthus, the two-headed hound, brother to Cerberus, offspring of Typhon and Echidna. The labour is a cattle-raid — the heroic deed of the Indo-European story-store, the raid of the Táin and of the Vedic hymns alike — pushed to the sunset edge of the world.

The journey out remakes the map. Passing through Libya, Heracles set up, at the boundaries of Europe and Libya over against each other, the two Pillars of Heracles — the headlands at the mouth of the Mediterranean, Calpe (Gibraltar) and Abyla (Ceuta) in the usual identifications — as tokens of his journey (2.5.10, paraphrased). Diodorus (4.18.4–7) pauses over the Pillars with an antiquarian's delight, reporting the rival theories without deciding: some say Heracles built out the two promontories and narrowed the strait, to keep the great sea-monsters of the ocean out of the inner sea; others say the continents were once joined and he cut the passage through. On this question, Diodorus concludes, in Oldfather's rendering paraphrased here, it will be possible for every man to think as he may please — the historian's shrug preserved as method. Oppressed by the heat on the march, Heracles bent his bow at the Sun; Helios, admiring his hardihood, gave him the golden goblet — the cup in which the Sun himself sails the ocean each night — and in it Heracles crossed to Erythia. There Orthus rushed him and was clubbed; Eurytion came to help and was killed; the herdsman of Hades' cattle, Menoetes, who was pasturing nearby, carried the news to Geryon; and Geryon came up with him by the river Anthemus, driving the cattle, and was shot dead. Heracles embarked the cattle in the goblet, sailed to Tartessus, and returned the cup to the Sun.

The drive home is the longest narrative in Apollodorus' labours, a cattle-drive across the whole of southern Europe, and it functions as a gazetteer of Heracles' western foundations. In Liguria the sons of Poseidon, Ialebion and Dercynus, tried to steal the cattle and were killed. At Rhegium a bull broke away, swam to Sicily, and gave — by the folk etymology Apollodorus retails — the name italus, bull, to Italy; the bull was recovered from King Eryx in Sicily only after a wrestling match to the death. At the Ionian Sea, Hera sent a gadfly that scattered the herd through the mountains of Thrace; Heracles recovered some, blamed the river Strymon and filled it with rocks so that it ceased to be navigable, and delivered what remained to Eurystheus, who sacrificed the cattle to Hera — the persecutor receiving, at the master's hands, the spoils of the tenth labour, the frame's bleakest joke. Diodorus' homecoming (4.19–24) is grander and more Roman in scale: the founding of Alesia in Celtica, a road engineered over the Alps, a halt at the site of Rome with prophecies of the tithe-custom of Roman Hercules-worship, battles with Giants at the Phlegraean Fields, the circuit of Sicily with its hot springs and hero-cults, and honours at Agyrium where, his tenth labour ending, Heracles first accepted sacrifice on equal terms with the Olympian gods — Diodorus' benefactor already crossing, inside the labours, the line Section XXIII examines. The two accounts share a skeleton — pillars, cup, triple king, gadfly, delivery — and differ in everything that reveals their authors: Apollodorus epitomises a myth; Diodorus writes the prehistory of the Mediterranean world.

Stesichorus' Geryoneis (early 6th century BCE) survives in papyrus fragments substantial enough to show its manner: Geryon deliberates, is warned by his mother Callirrhoe, and faces death with a dignity the summaries never grant him. The fragment is this archive's standing proof that the labours, before they were a list, were literature — individual poems in which even the monster had an interior.

XVII. Labour Eleven: The Apples of the Hesperides

The command (Apollodorus 2.5.11): when the ten had been performed in eight years and a month, Eurystheus — who did not acknowledge the Hydra or the stables — ordered, as an eleventh labour, the golden apples of the Hesperides. The apples were Earth's wedding gift to Zeus and Hera, planted at the world's western edge — Apollodorus corrects the geography explicitly: not in Libya, as some have said, but on Atlas among the Hyperboreans, the mythical people behind the North Wind, the tradition cheerfully locating its western garden in the far north because both are simply beyond. They were guarded by an immortal dragon with a hundred heads, offspring of Typhon and Echidna, which spoke with many kinds of voices — the serpent Ladon of the other accounts, already in Hesiod's catalogue of the brood of Ceto — and by the Hesperides themselves, the nymphs of the evening: Aegle, Erythia, Hesperia, and Arethusa, in Apollodorus' naming.

The way had first to be found, and the finding is a chain of parerga (Section XIX gathers them). Heracles fought Cycnus, son of Ares, on the way — Ares championing his son, until a thunderbolt parted the combatants. At the river Eridanus, nymphs, daughters of Zeus and Themis, directed him to Nereus, the shape-shifting Old Man of the Sea; Heracles seized him in his sleep and held him through all his transformations until he revealed where the apples and the Hesperides were — the Proteus-pattern, the wrestling of a knowledge out of a god who must be gripped, not asked, which Odysseus repeats with Proteus in the Odyssey. Through Libya: Antaeus. Through Egypt: Busiris. Through Arabia: Emathion killed. And on the Caucasus, the labour's most famous encounter: the eagle that devoured the liver of Prometheus, shot; the Titan released; and Prometheus, in gratitude, giving the counsel on which the labour's climax depends.

The feat (Apollodorus 2.5.11, paraphrased): Prometheus had advised Heracles not to go after the apples himself but to relieve Atlas of the burden of the sky and send him. Heracles came to Atlas, took the advice, and took the sky on his own shoulders. Atlas fetched three apples from the Hesperides — and, tasting freedom, announced that he would carry the apples to Eurystheus himself, leaving Heracles under the heavens permanently. Heracles, Prometheus' pupil in more than geography, agreed — and asked Atlas just to hold the sky for a moment while he put a pad on his head for the long carry. Atlas laid the apples on the ground and resumed the burden. Heracles picked up the apples and departed. The trick is the cycle's most perfect small scene: the strongest being in the world escapes the heaviest burden in the world not by strength, which he is exercising to its limit merely by standing there, but by a request too small to refuse. Apollodorus immediately records the variant: some say Heracles did not get the apples through Atlas at all, but killed the guardian dragon and plucked them himself. Both versions were carved and painted; the Atlas version dominates at Olympia, where the metope — Atlas bringing the apples, Heracles under the sky, Athena's hand lightening the load from behind — is the cycle's most reproduced image, and its most theological: heaven itself, for one panel, rests on the labourer, and a goddess helps him bear it.

The delivery ends in restitution. Eurystheus, receiving the apples, gave them back to Heracles; Heracles gave them to Athena; and Athena conveyed them back to the garden, for it was not lawful that they should be laid down anywhere (2.5.11, paraphrased). The eleventh labour's prize cannot be kept, displayed, or eaten: it can only be returned. The pattern will complete itself in the twelfth, whose prize must likewise be carried back to where it belongs. The last two labours, the two added beyond the oracle's ten, are the two whose objects refuse possession — a fact about the cycle's construction that no ancient author states and every reader can verify.

Diodorus' apples are a different labour altogether, and his candour about it is exemplary (4.26.2–4, paraphrased from Oldfather). The writers of myths disagree, he says: some report golden apples in gardens in Libya, guarded by a formidable dragon; others assert that the Hesperides possessed flocks of sheep of surpassing beauty, called “golden apples” poetically — mēla meaning both apples and sheep in Greek — as Aphrodite is called golden for her loveliness; others still, that the sheep were of a peculiar golden colour, and that “Dragon” was the name of their shepherd, a man of strength and courage who killed would-be thieves. On such matters, Diodorus concludes, every man may form the opinion that accords with his own belief. At any rate — his narrative resumes — Heracles slew the guardian, brought the apples to Eurystheus, finished his labours, and waited to receive the gift of immortality, as Apollo had prophesied. His Atlas is not a trick victim but a grateful sage: Heracles had rescued the Atlantides from Busiris' pirates, and Atlas in return not only assisted the labour but taught Heracles astrology — the science of the sphere — so that the bearing of the heavens, in Diodorus' rationalising reading, is the myth's enigmatic way of recording that Heracles brought the doctrine of the celestial sphere to the Greeks (4.27.3–5, paraphrased). The two eleventh labours — a trick with a pad, and a lesson in astronomy — are the clearest single exhibit of the two prose traditions' characters.

XVIII. Labour Twelve: Cerberus

The command (Apollodorus 2.5.12): bring Cerberus from Hades. This article treats the labour strictly as a labour — as the twelfth task, its conditions, its performance, and its delivery. The descent itself, Heracles' journey among the dead as one of the great Greek katabaseis alongside Orpheus, Theseus, and Odysseus, with its topography, its initiatory framing, and its afterlife in mystery cult and philosophy, is the subject of this hub's companion article, Descents to the Dead, and is not duplicated here. What follows is the task.

Cerberus, in Apollodorus, had three heads of dogs, the tail of a dragon, and on his back the heads of all sorts of snakes; Hesiod (Theogony 310–312) gives him fifty heads and calls him the brazen-voiced hound of Hades, offspring of Typhon and Echidna, who fawns on those who enter and devours any who would leave. He is not, in the labour's terms, a monster to be killed: he is a guardian to be borrowed. The conditions define the feat. First, preparation: Heracles went to Eleusis and sought initiation into the Mysteries — it was not then lawful for foreigners to be initiated, so he was adopted by Pylius to qualify; and because he had not been cleansed of the slaughter of the centaurs, Eumolpus cleansed him first, and then he was initiated (2.5.12, paraphrased). The labour that enters death begins, uniquely in the cycle, with purification and initiation — the one task for which strength is formally insufficient preparation. Diodorus (4.25.1) agrees on the initiation and differs on the personnel: at Athens, with Musaeus, son of Orpheus, in charge of the rites. Second, the descent: at Taenarum in Laconia, the cave-mouth of the descent to Hades, he went down. Third, the condition imposed at the throne: when Heracles asked Pluto for Cerberus, Pluto ordered him to take the animal provided he mastered him without the use of the weapons he carried.

The feat (Apollodorus 2.5.12, paraphrased): at the gates of Acheron he found the hound; cased in his breastplate and covered by the lionskin — the first labour worn into the last — he flung his arms round its head and, though the dragon in its tail bit him, never relaxed his grip and pressure until it yielded. Bare hands, as with the lion; a grip that does not relax, as in the cradle: the cycle's method closes over its final object. On the way down and up, the task's margins filled with the dead: the souls fled, save Meleager — whose sister Deianira Heracles there promised, in the tradition, to marry, planting the next act of his life in the underworld itself — and the Gorgon, an empty phantom at which he drew his sword before Hermes told him what it was; Theseus he raised from his seat by the hand, but when he would have raised Peirithous, the earth quaked and he let go; the stone of Ascalaphus he rolled away; Menoetes, herdsman of Hades' cattle, challenged him to wrestle and had his ribs broken, and was spared at Persephone's request. Diodorus (4.26.1) has Persephone welcome Heracles like a brother, free both Theseus and Peirithous, and give him Cerberus in chains. The variants matter to the descent article; to the labour, what matters is the delivery. Heracles carried Cerberus up — ascending, Apollodorus notes, through Troezen — showed him to Eurystheus, and carried him back to Hades. The proof exhibited, the guardian restored, the boundary resealed: the twelfth labour, like the eleventh, ends in restitution. The service was complete; the count was satisfied; the oracle's promise fell due.

In Apollodorus the descent is the climax by construction: the added labours ascend in impossibility from a garden at the world's edge to the world below it, and a hero who has exhibited death's own watchdog at Mycenae and returned it has nothing left to be set. Euripides' play presupposes the same climax — Heracles enters fresh from Cerberus, the labours done. Diodorus alone reverses the pair, making Cerberus eleventh and the apples last (4.25–27): his Heracles descends, returns, and finishes not under the earth but under the sky, with Atlas and the sphere — the benefactor's education completed where the penitent's ordeal, in the other tradition, ends in the dark. The disagreement over the last labour is a disagreement over what the cycle finally is: an expiation crowned by death faced and survived, or a cultivation crowned by knowledge received.

XIX. The Parerga: Side-Feats on the Road

Around the twelve stands a penumbra of further deeds — the parerga, literally “beside-the-work” things — performed on the way to and from the labours proper, or in the interstices of the service. The category is ancient, and it is load-bearing: Euripides' chorus (Section II) promotes three parerga into its list of twelve, and the distinction between a labour and a side-feat is therefore one of the instruments by which the canon policed itself. A parergon is not a lesser deed. Several of them are more famous than most labours. It is a deed not commanded by Eurystheus — and the definition, humble as it sounds, is the whole difference between the cycle and the career.

Antaeus

On the road to the Hesperides, in Libya, Heracles met Antaeus, son of Poseidon — some said of Earth herself — who forced every stranger to wrestle and had roofed a temple to his father with the skulls of the defeated. The secret of his strength was contact: as long as he touched the earth, his mother, he grew stronger. Apollodorus (2.5.11, paraphrased) has Heracles perceive the mechanism in the bout itself: he lifted Antaeus aloft, held him clear of the ground, and broke and killed him in the air. Diodorus (4.17.4) places the bout on the Geryon expedition instead, and expands it into the pacification of Libya — the giant killed, the beasts cleared, the desert brought under cultivation. The bout became antiquity's favourite emblem of a strength with a condition attached, and of the intelligence that finds the condition: it is the hind's lesson, sagacity over force, in its most brutal form.

Prometheus Freed

On the same journey, at the Caucasus at the world's eastern edge, Heracles shot the eagle that devoured the liver of Prometheus and released the Titan from his bondage (Apollodorus 2.5.11, paraphrased). Apollodorus' details repay attention: Heracles chose for himself, in exchange, the bond of olive — a token submission, a wreath-bond by which Zeus's sentence was formally preserved while its substance ended — and presented Chiron to Zeus, the immortal who consented to die, as the exchange by which the accounts of immortality were balanced (linking the freeing to the centaur catastrophe of Section X). Diodorus (4.15.2) gives the release an earlier place and a moral cause: Heracles, seeing Prometheus suffer for the benefit he had conferred on men — the gift of fire — killed the eagle and then persuaded Zeus to cease from his anger, rescuing the benefactor of mankind. In Aeschylus' lost Prometheus Unbound, known from fragments and summaries, the release was the climax of a trilogy and involved the revelation of the secret that could have unseated Zeus. However told, the freeing is the cycle's one act of direct, successful negotiation with the order of Olympus on another's behalf — and Prometheus repays it inside the eleventh labour itself, with the counsel about Atlas that wins the apples. The side-feat and the labour are gears in one machine.

The Centaurs, Busiris, Cycnus, and the Rest

The Centaurs recur as a distributed parergon across the whole career: the battle at Pholus' cave on the boar road (Section X), the killing of the centaur Eurytion — the same Eurytion who had fled to Pholoe — when he came to seize the bride Mnesimache from Dexamenus at Olenus, an episode Apollodorus tucks into the stables section itself (2.5.5); and the fatal meeting with Nessus at the Evenus, which belongs to the post-labour story and Section XXII. Euripides' chorus counts the Centaur-fight of Pelion among the twelve; Apollodorus counts no centaur among them, yet makes their slaughter the pollution that must be cleansed before Cerberus can be attempted. Busiris, king of Egypt, who sacrificed strangers on an altar of Zeus under cover of an oracle about a nine-year famine — having begun, in the story's grim logic, by sacrificing the Cypriot seer Phrasius who proposed the remedy — seized Heracles for the altar; Heracles burst his bonds and killed Busiris and his son on the spot (Apollodorus 2.5.11). Cycnus, son of Ares, who murdered his guests by the river Anaurus, Heracles fought on the Hesperides road, Ares intervening until Zeus's thunderbolt parted them (Apollodorus 2.5.11); elsewhere in his career he killed a second Cycnus, son of Ares and Pelopia, in single combat at Itonus (2.7.7). Add the wrestling of the river-god Achelous for Deianira's hand, the rescue of Hesione, the killing of the sons of Poseidon on the Geryon drive, the freeing of Alcestis from Death in the tradition Euripides stages, and the brief, disastrous attachment to the Argo, and the penumbra is seen to be not a margin but a second, shadow cycle — the deeds Heracles did because he was Heracles, travelling, rather than because a king commanded them. The canon is the service; the parerga are the life.

XX. The Shape of the Whole: Outward, Westward, Downward

Read as a sequence — which is how the canon, once fixed, taught Greece to read it — the twelve describe a single expanding figure. The first six labours are Peloponnesian: a circuit of the Argolid, Arcadia, and Elis, performable, in story-terms, as journeys of days. Their quarry is local and their problems are those of the near landscape: an invulnerable beast, a regenerating serpent, a sacred deer, a boar in snow, a filthy yard, a lake of birds. Even their complications are neighbourly — a host at Cleonae, a nephew's help, a son's honest testimony, a goddess's castanets. The second six are the world: Crete across the sea; Thrace in the north; the Amazons at the Black Sea's far shore; Geryon at the sunset, beyond the straits Heracles himself pillars; the garden of the Hesperides beyond geography altogether, reached by wrestling the sea-god for directions; and Hades, which is not on the map at all. The hero who begins by clearing his master's neighbourhood ends by exhibiting the watchdog of death at his master's gate. The service's radius is the measure of the servant's growth, and the tradition — whether or not any single author designed it so — preserves the gradient faithfully in every list: no surviving order puts Cerberus early or the lion late.

The figure has a second axis. The labours alternate, with increasing sophistication, between killing, capturing, fetching, and restoring. The lion and the Hydra are killed. The hind and the boar must be taken alive — an escalation, since a dead boar is a failure. The stables are neither killed nor captured but cleansed, the one labour whose object is a condition, not a creature. The birds are dispersed. The bull is captured and released. The mares are stolen from a king and released by him. The girdle is fetched from a queen who offered it. The cattle are raided and delivered — to be sacrificed by the master to the persecutor. The apples are fetched and returned. Cerberus is borrowed and returned. The last four labours in Apollodorus' order yield their performer nothing he keeps: a belt for another man's daughter, cattle for another man's altar, apples that must go back to their tree, a hound that must go back to its gate. The cycle that begins with a skin the hero wears for the rest of his life ends with prizes that pass through his hands like water. Whether that arc was designed or is the sediment of the stories' own logics, it is the arc the finished cycle presents, and the allegorists of Section XXIV were not imagining things when they found a pattern of progressive emptying in it. They were reading a real shape — and then, characteristically, claiming it as a doctrine.

A third axis is the method. Of the twelve, exactly two are won by the naked strength for which Heracles is proverbial — the lion, strangled, and Cerberus, gripped — and they are the first and the last, the frame. Between them, the victories belong to fire (the Hydra), endurance (the hind), weather (the boar, taken in snow), hydraulics (the stables), acoustics (the birds), seamanship and diplomacy (the bull, the girdle as offered), violence organised as war (the mares, the girdle as taken, Geryon), and outright trickery (Atlas). Diodorus saw the point and built his interpretation on it: his hind section praises the sagacity that stood Heracles in no less stead than his strength, and his stables section marvels at the ingenuity that performed an ignoble task without disgrace. The cycle, read whole, is not a celebration of force. It is a curriculum in the limits of force, taught by a hero whose force is unlimited — which may be the most Greek thing about it, and is certainly the thing that made it usable, for two millennia, as a school of everything from kingship to the spiritual life.

XXI. After the Labours: Iphitus, Omphale, and the Wars

The labours end; the life, disastrously, continues. Apollodorus' narrative after 2.5.12 is a sequence of the hero's own wars and offences, no longer commanded and no longer indemnified by the plea of necessity — and the contrast is the point. The servant who could tell Artemis that Eurystheus was to blame becomes, in freedom, a man whose violence answers to no master. The chief episodes, in Apollodorus' order (2.6–2.7, paraphrased throughout), are these.

Heracles gave Megara to Iolaus in marriage — the wife of the Apollodoran version, who survived the madness, handed to the companion of the labours, in an arrangement the mythographer reports without explanation and modern readers have never found comfortable — and sought a new wife himself. At Oechalia, King Eurytus had offered his daughter Iole to whoever could beat him and his sons at archery. Heracles won, and was refused, the family fearing, in Apollodorus' plain statement of their reason, that if he had children he would kill them again. Not long after, when cattle were stolen and Iphitus — Eurytus' son, the one member of the family who had argued for Heracles — came to him in friendship to search for them together, Heracles, in a second madness, threw his guest from the walls of Tiryns and killed him. The murder of Iphitus is the post-labour career in miniature: uncommanded, unprovoked, against a friend, under the same lyssa that the labours had supposedly expiated. Purification was refused by Neleus at Pylos; granted by Deiphobus at Amyclae; but a dire disease followed the guilt, and at Delphi — where the priestess at first would not answer him, and Heracles carried off the tripod and fought Apollo for it until Zeus threw a thunderbolt between them — the oracle at last prescribed a second servitude: he was to be sold as a slave and serve three years, the price to be paid to Eurytus in compensation.

Hermes sold him to Omphale, queen of Lydia. The Lydian servitude generated its own folklore — the hero spinning wool in women's clothes while the queen wore the lionskin, in the Hellenistic and Roman telling that Apollodorus does not give, preferring a sober list of services rendered: the Cercopes thieves bound at Ephesus, the vine-digger Syleus killed, the body of Icarus buried. The servitude's interest for this article is structural. The man who had served a king for twelve years to expiate one killing serves a queen for three to expiate another, and the tradition presents the second service without surprise: servitude is the cycle's grammar for guilt, and Heracles' life is punctuated by it. Freed and cured, he took his deferred revenges in order — Troy sacked and Laomedon and his sons killed, Podarces ransomed by Hesione's veil and renamed, from the ransom (priasthai, to buy), Priam; Elis invaded, Augeas and his sons killed, Phyleus the honest witness installed as king, and the Olympic Games, in Diodorus' placement already founded, in Apollodorus' now celebrated with altars to Pelops and the twelve gods; Pylus taken and Neleus and his sons killed, Nestor alone surviving, and Hades himself wounded fighting for the Pylians; Sparta subdued and the sons of Hippocoon killed, at the cost of Iphicles' life. The wars are the labours' dark mirror: the same strength, the same itinerary, the same king-killing outcomes — with no oracle behind them and no count to complete. And they end, as the cycle's logic requires, not in a triumph but in a marriage: at Calydon, Heracles wrestled the river Achelous for Deianira, broke off his horn, and married her.

XXII. Deianira, Nessus, the Pyre on Oeta, and Apotheosis

The death of Heracles is the labours' final consequence, assembled out of their own materials. Years after the wars, travelling with Deianira toward Trachis, Heracles came to the river Evenus, where the centaur Nessus ferried travellers for hire — Nessus, who had fled to this river from Pholus' cave in Section X, arriving at his appointed place in the story twenty years on. Heracles crossed by himself and entrusted Deianira to Nessus to carry over; midway, Nessus attempted to violate her; she cried out; and Heracles, from the far bank, shot him through the heart with an arrow — a Hydra arrow, dipped in the venom of the second labour (Apollodorus 2.7.6, paraphrased). Dying, Nessus called Deianira to him and told her that if she mixed the blood from his wound with what he had spilled, she would have a love-charm that would keep Heracles from ever loving another woman more than her. She collected it and kept it by her. The dying centaur's lie is the most efficient revenge in Greek myth: it requires no further action from him, only time, a rival, and a wife's fear.

Time supplied both. Heracles sacked Oechalia at last, killed Eurytus and his sons, and led Iole captive — the bride refused him a decade before, now a prisoner in his train. Landing at Cenaeum in Euboea to sacrifice to Zeus, he sent the herald Lichas to Trachis for fine raiment; from Lichas, Deianira learned of Iole, and fearing to be displaced, she smeared a tunic with the blood of Nessus, believing it a love-charm, and sent it to him. The fullest surviving telling is Sophocles' Trachiniae, where Deianira's deliberation, her discovery of the charm's true nature — a tuft of wool she had used to apply it, cast into the sun, shrivels and foams — and her silent exit to suicide are tragedy's property; Apollodorus (2.7.7, paraphrased) gives the mechanism bare. Heracles put on the tunic and began the sacrifice; as the tunic warmed, the poison of the Hydra began to corrode his skin; tearing at it, he tore away his own flesh with it. In his agony he hurled Lichas — the innocent messenger — from the headland into the sea. Carried to Trachis, where Deianira had hanged herself on learning what she had done, he charged his son Hyllus to marry Iole when he came of age, and had himself carried to Mount Oeta, where he built a pyre, mounted it, and ordered it lit. No one of his company would do it. Poeas, a passer-by searching for his flocks, lit it — and received, in payment, Heracles' bow, the bow that would pass to Poeas' son Philoctetes and be required, by oracle, for the fall of Troy.

The apotheosis. While the pyre burned, Apollodorus concludes, it is said that a cloud passed under Heracles and, with a peal of thunder, wafted him up to heaven; thereafter he obtained immortality, was reconciled to Hera, and married her daughter Hebe, Youth, by whom he had sons Alexiares and Anicetus (2.7.7, paraphrased). Diodorus (4.38–39) closes his account with the same translation and the honours paid him thereafter as hero and god. Hesiod already knew the ending: the Theogony (950–955) has Heracles, his great work done, dwelling on Olympus unwounded and ageless, married to Hebe. And the Odyssey (11.601–604) preserves the tradition's discomfort with it in four famous lines: Odysseus in Hades meets the eidōlon, the phantom, of Heracles — while “he himself”, the lines add, feasts among the immortal gods. Ancient scholars already suspected those lines as an interpolation harmonising the hero's two destinations; modern editors remain divided. The suspicion and the lines together are the evidence for the fact Section XXIII develops: Greek religion did not resolve Heracles into either a dead hero or a born god, because in cult he was, demonstrably, both at once. The pyre on Oeta — where a sanctuary and a periodic fire-ritual were still shown and performed in Pausanias' day (10.23.9 records the Oetaean rites and the local account) — is the hinge: a cremation that is also an ascension, a hero's funeral that produces a god.

XXIII. Hero and God: The Cult of Heracles

Everything so far has been myth as told. This section turns to the second layer the hub requires: Heracles as an object of cult, where the evidence is not poems but sanctuaries, inscriptions, calendars, historians reporting ritual, and the stubborn fact that no Greek city seems to have been without him. Heracles received cult in more places, in more forms, than any other hero — as a god, as a hero, and in a number of places in both capacities at neighbouring altars, a doubleness Greek religion elsewhere avoids and in his case institutionalised. The labours are the cult's charter-myth: the suffering that earned the godhead is the story the worshipper was expected to know, and several of the cult's features are unintelligible without the cycle — the gymnasium's patron is the athlete of the athloi; the averter of evil is the clearer of monsters; the saviour is the man who went down to Hades and came back.

The proof-text is Herodotus, and it is quoted in full in this hub's Heroes and Epic (Section V); it is given here again because this article's subject cannot be closed without it. Investigating Heracles in Histories 2.44, Herodotus travelled to Tyre, where the temple of the Phoenician god the Greeks called Heracles — Melqart — was dated by its priests to the city's foundation, 2,300 years before; and to Thasos, where he found a temple of Heracles founded by Phoenicians five generations before the Greek Heracles, son of Amphitryon, was born. His conclusion, in A. D. Godley's Loeb translation:

“Therefore, what I have discovered by inquiry plainly shows that Heracles is an ancient god. And furthermore, those Greeks, I think, are most in the right, who have established and practise two worships of Heracles, sacrificing to one Heracles as to an immortal, and calling him the Olympian, but to the other bringing offerings as to a dead hero.” — HERODOTUS, HISTORIES 2.44.5, TRANS. A. D. GODLEY (LOEB CLASSICAL LIBRARY)

The verbs carry the doctrine: thyein, to sacrifice as to a god, for Heracles the Olympian; enagizein, to make the offerings due to the dead, for Heracles the hero. Pindar had already compressed the doubleness into a phrase — hērōs theos, “hero-god” (Nemean 3.22) — and later writers describe festivals at which Heracles received both rites in sequence, the chthonic offering first and the Olympian sacrifice after, at the same sanctuary on the same day; Sicyon and Cos are among the places where a dual or divided cult is attested in the sources gathered by the standard scholarship (Burkert's Greek Religion treats Heracles as the paradigm case of the hero who crosses into godhead). Thasos adds the historical depth: the island's great sanctuary of Heracles, with its monumental gate carved with the hero's figure, served a cult Herodotus believed Phoenician in foundation and older than the Greek hero — the Greek Heracles absorbing, by interpretatio graeca, the cult of Tyrian Melqart, whose worship Phoenician settlement carried the length of the Mediterranean, to Gades itself, at the very straits of the tenth labour, where the temple of Melqart-Heracles at the Pillars was famous into the Roman period. Myth and cult meet at the map's edge: the hero who set up the Pillars had, in historical fact, a Phoenician god's temple standing by them.

The cult's ordinary forms were these. The Heracleia — festivals under that name were celebrated in many cities, commonly in the summer month the Athenians called Metageitnion, and at Athens associated with the Heracleia at Cynosarges and Marathon among others — combined sacrifice, athletic contests, and communal feasting, the hero's athloi re-performed as games in his honour. At Marathon, where the local Heracles-sanctuary was among Attica's oldest, the Heracleia's games were a fixture of the Attic festival calendar, and the sanctuary gave the plain its sacred geography long before the battle made it famous. The gymnasium was his characteristic urban address: Heracles, with Hermes, was the presiding power of the gymnasia and palaestras of the Greek world, patron of the ephebes who trained in them, his image — the Farnese type descends from Lysippos' weary Heracles — standing in baths and exercise-grounds from Athens to Rome; the Athenian gymnasium at Cynosarges, outside the walls, was a Heracles sanctuary before it was a school, and gave the Cynic philosophers their name by meeting there. As Alexikakos, Averter of Evil, and Soter, Saviour — the Thasian coinage styles him Hērakleous Sōtēros Thasiōn, “of Heracles, Saviour of the Thasians” — he was invoked at doors and crossroads, his club and image set up as apotropaic furniture of daily life; as a healer he presided over hot springs, the “baths of Heracles” of proverb, at Thermopylae, at Edipsos, and wherever else warm water rose, in grateful memory, the Sicilian tradition said, of the springs the nymphs opened for the labourer on the Geryon drive. Founders claimed him: cities named Heraclea dot the map from Pontus to Italy to the Straits, royal houses — the Spartan kings, the Macedonian Argeads, Mark Antony after them — claimed descent from him, and the Dorian states traced their title to the Peloponnese through the returning Heracleidae of Section V. No other hero's cult is at once so local — every gymnasium, every spring — and so imperial.

// NO TOMB //

Uniquely among great heroes, Heracles had no universally recognised tomb — for the sufficient reason that his story ends with the body taken up from the pyre. Places showed the pyre-site on Oeta, relics, and the marks of his passage; what none could show was a grave with the hero in it. Burkert's framework makes the point sharply: a hero is a dead man with an address. Heracles' address was Olympus, and his cult is what hero cult becomes when the death, alone among heroes' deaths, does not hold.

Rome inherited all of it and added the Ara Maxima, the Great Altar in the Forum Boarium, where Hercules received the tithe-vow Diodorus already describes Roman merchants making (Section XVI), and where the cult's Greek rite and archaic exclusions were antiquarian showpieces by the Augustan age. Hercules the victor, Invictus, became an imperial emblem — Commodus' lionskin portraits are the notorious limit — and the labours, in sarcophagi and mosaics from Spain to Syria, became the Roman world's favourite image of a life of meritorious suffering rewarded. The cult layer and the story layer here fully converge: it is because the cycle ends in earned godhead that it could serve, in pagan Rome and then, transposed, in Christian moralising, as the type of virtue rewarded through toil. That transposition is the doorway to the last layer.

XXIV. Later Readings, and a Note on the Alchemical One

The finished cycle invited allegory the way a lock invites keys. Already in the classical period the sophist Prodicus' fable of Heracles at the Crossroads — the young hero choosing between Virtue's steep path and Vice's easy one, preserved in Xenophon's Memorabilia (2.1.21–34, paraphrased) — had converted the labourer into a moral emblem, and the conversion never reversed. Stoics read the labours as reason's victories over the passions and the monsters as vices; Euhemerists read the hero as a benefactor-king magnified by gratitude; Neoplatonists read the descent and the apotheosis into the soul's itinerary; the mytho-hermetic tradition, whose great early-modern monument is Antoine-Joseph Pernety's Les Fables égyptiennes et grecques dévoilées (1758), read the whole of classical myth as veiled natural philosophy. Each reading found real features of the cycle to fasten on — the progressive emptying of Section XX, the initiation before the descent, the pyre that separates a mortal part from an immortal one — and each, in the hub's judgement, is evidence for the history of its readers rather than for the meaning of the Archaic and Classical material. They are therefore treated in this archive as reception, in their own articles, and not allowed to gloss the labours in place.

One sentence of that reception concerns this hub's second wing directly, and it is given here in the disciplined form the subject requires. The alchemical tradition — from late-antique and Byzantine commentators through the Latin and early-modern mytho-hermetic writers — read the twelve labours as stages of the Work: the monsters as the unworked matter in its several resistances, the searing of the Hydra and the cleansing of the stables as operations of fire and water, the descent for Cerberus as the Work's passage through death and blackness, and the apotheosis as its completion, with individual labours mapped, in different authors, onto different operations, metals, and stages, in systems that do not agree with one another and were never meant to be checked against Apollodorus. That reading is a substantial, documentable chapter of the labours' afterlife; it is not a key to what the labours meant at Olympia or in Euripides; and its full treatment — the texts, the mappings, and the evidence for and against their antiquity — belongs to this hub's Alchemical Allegory article, to which the reader is referred. It is flagged here, and only flagged, because an archive of both wings owes the reader the signpost and owes the myth the restraint.
