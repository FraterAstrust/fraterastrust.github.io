HEROES AND EPIC

Heracles · Theseus · Jason · The Homeric Question

// THE DEAD WHO STAYED POWERFUL :: THE SONGS THAT MADE THEM EVERYONE'S //

A hero in Greek religion was, first of all, a dead person with an address — a tomb where offerings could be delivered. A hero in Greek epic was something else: a name carried by song into every city in the Greek world. This article keeps those two institutions apart, because the Greeks lived with both at once, and because confusing them is where most popular accounts of Heracles, Theseus, and Jason go wrong.

I. Two Institutions, One Word: Hero Cult and Epic

English uses one word, hero, for two Greek things that a Greek of the Classical period would have experienced as quite different arrangements. The first is hero cult: a set of rituals performed at a fixed place, usually a tomb, for a dead figure believed to retain power after death and to exercise it, for good or ill, over the community that lived around that place. The second is epic: song — later, text — that carried the deeds of figures such as Achilles, Heracles, and Jason across the whole Greek-speaking world, detaching them from any single tomb or town and making them the common property of Greeks everywhere. The same name could live in both arrangements at once. Heracles had tombs and shrines where he received the offerings due to the dead, and he also strode through poems sung from Ionia to Sicily. Those are not two aspects of one thing. They are two institutions, with different geographies, different economics, and different kinds of authority, and this article treats them separately throughout.

The distinction was given its sharpest modern form by Gregory Nagy in The Best of the Achaeans (1979; second edition 1999). In the account that follows from Nagy's analysis — paraphrased here, not quoted — hero cult is local: it belongs to a particular polis or locality, it is anchored to a physical tomb, and the honour it confers (Greek timē) is delivered by the worshipping community in ritual, again and again, for as long as the cult is maintained. Epic, by contrast, is Panhellenic: it circulates beyond any one city, sheds local peculiarities as it travels, and confers a different kind of honour — kleos, the glory of being heard of, which the song itself creates and renews at every performance. A hero could be enormous in cult and minor in epic, or the reverse. Most heroes, in fact, were purely local: the Greek landscape was dense with named and unnamed heroes — founders, ancestors, athletes, war dead, figures known only as “the hero” of a particular shrine — who never appear in any surviving poem at all. Epic's cast list is the small, famous tip of a very large cultic iceberg.

Walter Burkert's Greek Religion (German original 1977; English translation by John Raffan, 1985) remains the standard synthesis of the cultic side, and its chapter on the heroes sets out the framework this article uses: the hero is a dead human being — unlike a god, a hero has a biography that ends in a death, often a violent or uncanny one — whose power operates from the grave, within a limited territory, and who must be kept well-disposed by offerings. Heroes could help: they healed, they protected cities, they appeared in battle. They could also harm, and a neglected or angered hero was a danger precisely because he was near. The gods of Olympus, treated in this hub's Olympians article, were by contrast deathless, mobile, and worshipped everywhere under shared names. The hero's defining features are therefore the opposite of the Olympian's: mortality, locality, and a grave.

HERO CULT

Local and place-bound. Centred on a tomb or heroon. Maintained by a specific community. Confers timē through repeated ritual. The hero is dead, near, and capable of helping or harming his own people in particular.

PANHELLENIC EPIC

Mobile and supra-local. Carried by singers, then by texts. Maintained by performance traditions that cross city boundaries. Confers kleos — fame in song — which survives as long as the song is sung, anywhere.

WHERE THEY MEET

A Panhellenic figure could acquire local cults (Heracles everywhere; Achilles at specific tombs and islands), and a local cult figure could be absorbed into epic's cast. The traffic ran both ways, but the institutions never merged.

WHERE THEY DO NOT MEET

Most cult heroes have no epic. Most epic narrative — the journeys, combats, and speeches — was never ritual. A story about a hero is not evidence for his cult, and a cult is not evidence for the truth of any story told about him.

One further distinction governs everything below. The gods had a genealogy and a theogony: Hesiod's poem explained where they came from and how their order was established, once, in the deep past. Heroes had no theogony. They were generated continuously by Greek communities — by a remarkable death, a remarkable tomb, an oracle's instruction, a city's political need — throughout the Archaic and Classical periods and beyond. Hero cult is therefore not a fossil survival from a single primitive age. It is a living institution that kept producing new heroes, including, as Section VII shows, in the full daylight of the fifth century BCE, for reasons an Athenian politician could have explained to you.

II. The Anatomy of Hero Cult: Tomb, Heroon, and Offering

A hero's cult place is called a heroon (Greek hērōion). In form it could be many things: an actual tomb, Bronze Age or recent; a mound; a small walled precinct (temenos) with an altar; a chapel-like building housing a tomb or a cenotaph; or, in later periods, a substantial shrine with dining rooms, because hero festivals, like festivals of the gods, commonly involved a shared meal. What identifies a heroon archaeologically is not its architecture but its deposits: votive offerings — pottery, figurines, miniature weapons, inscribed dedications naming the honorand — accumulated at a spot treated as the hero's point of contact with the living. The earliest cult place whose honorands are named by objects found on the site is the shrine on the ridge at Therapne above Sparta, conventionally called the Menelaion, where dedications to Menelaus and Helen begin around c. 700 BCE. That date matters: it puts named, epic-connected hero cult at the same horizon as the fixation of the Homeric poems themselves (Section XIII), and the question of which came first — epic fame or tomb cult — is one of the genuinely open questions in the study of early Greek religion.

Offerings Poured Down: The Textbook Model

Older handbooks, following a real strand of ancient evidence, drew a sharp ritual contrast. To the Olympian gods one performed thysia: sacrifice on a raised altar (bōmos), the victim's head drawn up toward the sky, the god receiving the smoke of the burning thigh-bones and fat, and the worshippers feasting on the meat. To heroes and the powers below the earth one performed enagizein (the noun is enagisma): offerings directed downward, into a low hearth (eschara) or a pit (bothros) dug into the ground, the victim's head pressed down, blood and libations — wine, oil, honey, water, collectively choai — poured into the earth for the dead power to receive, and in the extreme form the victim burned entire (holocaust), with nothing eaten. The hero, on this model, is a chthonic power — of the earth, chthōn — and is fed the way the dead are fed: downward.

// WORDS FOR THE DEAD //

Chthonic (from chthōn, earth) marks powers addressed as belonging to the ground and what is under it. Enagizein is the verb ancient authors use for making offerings to the dead and to heroes as dead; thyein is the ordinary verb of sacrifice to gods. Herodotus uses exactly this pair of verbs for the two cults of Heracles (Section V) — the vocabulary itself encodes the distinction.

The Revision: What Hero Shrines Actually Did

The textbook model is an ancient idea, attested in ancient texts, and it describes some real rituals. As a description of what most hero sanctuaries actually did, it has been substantially revised. Gunnel Ekroth's study of the sacrificial rituals of Greek hero cults (2002, from her Stockholm dissertation) examined the epigraphic and archaeological record sanctuary by sanctuary and concluded — in the finding for which her work is now standardly cited — that the main ritual at hero cults of the Archaic to early Hellenistic periods was in fact thysia, ordinary sacrifice followed by communal dining, with the meat eaten by the worshippers; that low hearths, pits, holocausts, and the vocabulary of enagizein are documented only rarely in hero contexts before the Roman period; and that the neat Olympian/chthonic opposition owes as much to later lexicographers and scholiasts, systematising a distinction, as to Archaic practice. This article therefore states both layers honestly: the downward offering is how Greek writers themselves conceptualised feeding a hero, and Herodotus' contrast in Section V proves the concept was alive in the fifth century; but the excavated and inscribed record shows hero cults feasting like everyone else. An honest archive reports the idea and the practice, and does not let either erase the other.

Tombs of Strangers: The Eighth-Century Evidence

Hero cult in the broad sense emerges in the archaeological record in the eighth century BCE, the same century in which the Homeric poems were taking shape. Across Greece, offerings begin to appear at tombs of the Bronze Age — Mycenaean chamber tombs and tholoi a half-millennium old by then — whose occupants the offerers cannot have known by any continuous tradition. Scholars distinguish, following J. N. Coldstream and Anthony Snodgrass, between tomb cult (veneration directed at an ancient grave as such) and hero cult proper (veneration of a named hero at his recognised shrine), and Carla Antonaccio's work on the archaeology of ancestors argued that much eighth-century tomb cult was about claiming land and ancestry in a period of community formation, not about Homer. Whether the spread of epic caused the turn to ancient tombs — communities identifying their local graves with epic heroes they had heard sung — or whether both are parallel products of the eighth century's social transformations, remains debated. What is not debated is the synchronism, and the most spectacular single piece of evidence for the world that produced it predates the eighth century altogether. It is the subject of the next section.

III. Lefkandi: The Toumba Building and the Debate Over It

Lefkandi, on the west coast of Euboea between Chalkis and Eretria, was one of the most prosperous communities of the Greek Early Iron Age — the period older scholarship called the Dark Age. On the hill called Toumba (“the mound”), excavations by the British School at Athens and the Greek Archaeological Service, reported in a preliminary account by Mervyn Popham, Evi Touloupa, and Hugh Sackett in 1982 and fully in the Lefkandi publication series, uncovered something without parallel for its date: a very large apsidal building, roughly 47 metres long and 10 metres wide in the excavators' reconstruction, oriented east–west, with a porch at the east end, a great central room, smaller rooms toward the apsidal west end, mudbrick walls on a stone socle, a thatched roof, and — in the original reconstruction — a surrounding veranda of wooden posts that has often been noted as a distant anticipation of the peristyle of the later Greek temple. The building is dated to the mid-tenth century BCE, c. 1000–950 BCE, and it stood only briefly.

Beneath the floor of the central room lay two deep shafts. One held the skeletons of four horses, apparently sacrificed and thrown or driven in headfirst, two of them still wearing iron bits. The other, larger shaft held a double burial. A man, cremated, his ashes wrapped in a fringed linen cloth — itself an extraordinary survival — and placed in a bronze amphora imported from Cyprus that was already an antique, perhaps a century old, when it was buried; beside the urn, an iron sword, a spearhead, and a whetstone. Next to it, inhumed in a wooden coffin or on a bier, a woman, wearing gold: earrings, hair spirals, a necklace with faience and rock-crystal beads, and an antique gold pendant of Near Eastern manufacture far older than the burial itself; an iron knife with an ivory handle lay near her head. The excavators and most subsequent readers have taken the knife, the simultaneity of the burials, and the woman's inhumation beside a cremated man to raise the possibility — it is no more than that — that she was killed to accompany him. East of the shafts, a rock surface scorched by fierce fire with a circle of postholes has been interpreted as the site of the cremation pyre.

After the burial, the building was demolished and the whole structure was covered by a great mound of earth — the Toumba itself. Around and against that mound, in the decades that followed (broadly c. 950–825 BCE), the community buried its dead in a rich cemetery, the Toumba cemetery, as if the mound had become a focus of prestige and memory. It is this sequence — monumental building, princely burial, deliberate mounding, subsequent burials clustering at the spot — that led the excavators to call the structure a heroon: a building erected over a hero's grave, and the burials beneath it the earliest candidate in Greek archaeology for the burial of a man his community then treated as a hero.

// THE INTERPRETATION DEBATE, STATED PLAINLY //

The heroon reading is influential but not uncontested, and this archive does not present it as settled. Alexander Mazarakis Ainian argued, in a line of interpretation developed across his work on early Greek rulers' dwellings, that the Toumba building is better read as the house of a living ruler — a chieftain's hall — in which the burials were subsequently made, rather than a shrine built for the dead: on this reading the building's life came first and the heroisation, if any, attached to a former residence. Others have questioned details of the reconstruction, including the height of the building and whether the outer posts formed a veranda or a fence. What no interpretation disputes is the sequence of building, rich burial with horse sacrifice, demolition, and mound, or its date. The debate is about what the building was for — and it is a useful first lesson in how much of hero cult's early history rests on readings of mute earth and postholes.

Why Lefkandi anchors this article is its date and its shape. Around 950 BCE — two centuries before the Menelaion dedications, in a Greece without temples in the later sense, without writing in general use, and without the Homeric poems in any fixed form we can point to — a community staged a funeral of Homeric splendour: cremation, an antique bronze vessel for the ashes, sacrificed horses, gold, and a mound. Readers of the Iliad will recognise the grammar: it is the funeral of Patroclus, with its pyre, its sacrificed horses, and its mound, enacted in the real world at roughly the era when the epic tradition was forming. Whether Lefkandi shows epic reflecting life, life imitating a sung tradition already in circulation, or both drawing on a shared heroic funeral custom, cannot be decided from one site — and scholars have argued all three. The safe statement is the strongest one the evidence allows: the institution that hero cult would become — a powerful dead man, a marked place, a community organising its memory around both — is materially present at Lefkandi by the middle of the tenth century BCE, before any text in this article was written down.

IV. Heracles: The Labours Before There Were Twelve

Heracles (Greek Hēraklēs, Roman Hercules) is the limiting case of every generalisation in this article, because he overflows both institutions. His name is usually analysed as “glory of Hera” — an irony the tradition itself exploits, since Hera, his divine stepmother, is his persecutor throughout: it is Hera who delays his birth so that his cousin Eurystheus is born first and rules (the story the Iliad itself tells, at 19.95–133), Hera who sends the snakes into his cradle, and Hera who drives him mad to kill his own wife and children, the crime for which, in the standard later account, the labours are imposed as purification and service. His mortal mother is Alcmene; his father, in the tradition, is Zeus. He is therefore exactly the hero of Section I at maximum scale: a mortal man with a death — a terrible one, by poison and fire (Section V) — who nonetheless ends among the gods. Every Greek city could claim some episode of his wanderings, which is one reason he, and not a more local figure, became the Panhellenic hero par excellence: his myth had to be portable, because he had been everywhere.

A Series in Search of a Number

The labours — Greek athloi, contests or ordeals — are tasks imposed by Eurystheus, king of Tiryns and Mycenae, on a Heracles bound to serve him. The crucial historical fact about them is that the set was fluid for centuries. The Iliad knows the servitude to Eurystheus and names individual tasks, including the descent for Cerberus, the hound of Hades (8.362–369), and presents Heracles' whole career as the type of the hero who suffered and died despite being dearest to Zeus (18.117–119); it nowhere lists twelve. Hesiod's Theogony (c. 700 BCE) knows several labours as separate episodes woven into its monster genealogies — the cattle of Geryon (287–293), the Hydra of Lerna, reared by Hera and killed by Heracles with Iolaus' help (313–318), Cerberus (310–312), the Nemean lion (327–332) — again with no numbered cycle. Individual labours circulated as individual stories: Stesichorus' lyric Geryoneis (early sixth century BCE) made a whole poem out of the Geryon episode alone. Antiquity itself credited a lost epic, the Heraclea of Peisander of Rhodes (dated variously to the seventh or sixth century BCE), with first gathering the labours into a single poem; the poem is lost, and what it contained cannot be checked, so the credit is recorded here as ancient attribution, not as fact.

The earliest complete set of twelve that survives is not a text at all. It is sculpture: the twelve metopes of the Temple of Zeus at Olympia, built c. 470–457 BCE, whose carved panels — six over the east porch, six over the west — displayed twelve labours, one to a panel, in the temple of the sanctuary where Heracles was honoured as the legendary founder of the Olympic Games. Emma Stafford's study of Heracles, on which this paragraph draws, makes the point precisely: the canonical twelve do not seem to be established before the Classical period, and the Olympia metopes are the first clear instance of the collection — it has even been suggested that the number was in part dictated by the twelve spaces the architecture offered. A cycle that modern readers meet as timeless was, on the evidence, fixed late, in stone, at a Panhellenic sanctuary: epic's fluid tradition crystallised by a temple's decoration.

Ten, Then Twelve: The Mythographer's Arithmetic

The fullest surviving prose account is the Library (2.4.8–2.5.12) of the author conventionally called Apollodorus — the work is now generally dated to the first or second century CE and attributed to an unknown mythographer, “Pseudo-Apollodorus”; it is cited here in J. G. Frazer's Loeb translation. In Apollodorus' telling, the oracle at Delphi directs Heracles to serve Eurystheus and perform the labours imposed, and the arithmetic of the cycle is explained inside the story: Eurystheus refused to count two of the completed ten. The Hydra was disqualified — in Frazer's translation of Apollodorus 2.5.2, Eurystheus said the labour “should not be reckoned among the ten because he had not got the better of the hydra by himself, but with the help of Iolaus” — and the cleansing of the Augean stables was disqualified because Heracles had bargained for a wage. Two further tasks were therefore imposed, the apples of the Hesperides and the capture of Cerberus, bringing the performed total to twelve (Apollodorus 2.5.11). The episode is worth dwelling on because it shows what kind of object the “twelve labours” are: not a datum of the myth but a negotiated count, rationalised after the fact by a mythographer reconciling a ten-task servitude with a twelve-item canon that his culture had already fixed. Other sources vary the order, substitute episodes, and preserve labours the canon excludes; Heracles also performed a penumbra of further deeds — the parerga, “side-works” — on the way to and from the labours proper. The canon is real, but it is a late editorial fact about the tradition, and this archive dates it as such: fluid in Homer and Hesiod, gathered by degrees in Archaic poetry and art, complete in stone at Olympia around 460 BCE, and canonised in prose by Apollodorus half a millennium later still.

V. Heracles Between Hero and God: Thasos and the Apotheosis

Heracles died — in the tradition, horribly. Deianeira, his wife, fearing to lose him to a captive girl, sent him a tunic smeared with what she believed was a love-charm: the blood of the centaur Nessus, whom Heracles had shot with an arrow dipped in the Hydra's venom. The tunic burned into his flesh. Carried to Mount Oeta, he built his own pyre and had it lit; the tradition then divides his end: the mortal part burned away, and Heracles himself was taken up to Olympus, reconciled with Hera, and married to her daughter Hebe, Youth. Hesiod's Theogony already ends his story that way (950–955), and the Odyssey's underworld scene contains the same doubleness — Odysseus meets Heracles' eidōlon, his phantom, in Hades, while “he himself” feasts among the immortal gods (11.601–604; the lines were already suspected by some ancient scholars, and their status is still discussed). The myth, in other words, could not decide whether Heracles was dead or divine, because Greek religion had decided, in practice, that he was both — and it maintained two cults to prove it.

The proof-text is Herodotus. In Histories 2.44, investigating whether the Greek Heracles was a man or an ancient god, Herodotus reports travelling to Tyre in Phoenicia, where he found a great temple of Heracles — the Phoenician Melqart — whose priests dated it to the city's foundation, 2,300 years before his visit; and to the island of Thasos in the northern Aegean, where he found a temple of Heracles founded by Phoenicians who had settled the island while searching for Europa, five generations, he calculates, before the birth of the Greek Heracles son of Amphitryon. His conclusion, in A. D. Godley's Loeb translation, is quoted here verbatim because it is the single most important sentence in the ancient evidence for this article's whole subject:

“Therefore, what I have discovered by inquiry plainly shows that Heracles is an ancient god. And furthermore, those Greeks, I think, are most in the right, who have established and practise two worships of Heracles, sacrificing to one Heracles as to an immortal, and calling him the Olympian, but to the other bringing offerings as to a dead hero.” — Herodotus, Histories 2.44.5, trans. A. D. Godley (Loeb Classical Library)

Every element of Section II is present in that sentence. The two worships are distinguished by verb and by attitude: sacrifice (thyein) to Heracles the immortal, the Olympian; offerings as to the dead (enagizein) to Heracles the hero. Herodotus is not confused by the doubleness; he approves of it, as the correct ritual response to a genuinely double being. The Thasos evidence adds the historical layer: the island's Heracles cult was, on Herodotus' own report, Phoenician in foundation and older than the Greek hero — the Greek Heracles had absorbed, by the process scholars call interpretatio graeca, the cult of the Tyrian god Melqart, whose worship the Phoenicians carried across the Mediterranean. Heracles the hero-god is thus not only a mythological paradox but a documented case of cults merging across cultures, with the seams still visible to a travelling historian in the fifth century BCE.

The pattern generalises. Heracles received cult as a god, as a hero, and in some places in both capacities at neighbouring altars; he was worshipped as a healer, as a protector of gymnasia and of travellers, as Alexikakos, Averter of Evil. Asclepius shows the same trajectory — a hero of epic, killed by Zeus's thunderbolt in the early tradition, who by the Classical period received cult of fully divine scale at Epidaurus and across the Greek world, the world of the healing sanctuaries and dream incubation treated in this hub's Mysteries and Healing Cults article. The boundary between hero and god, which the textbook model draws as a line, was in practice a slope, and the greatest heroes slid up it. What never changed was the grammar underneath: even the deified Heracles kept his tomb-traditions and his hero-offerings somewhere, because his story — unlike a god's — contained a death. Apotheosis did not erase the hero. It added a second register on top of the first, and Greek cult, characteristically, kept paying both.

VI. Theseus: Athens Builds Its Hero

If Heracles shows a hero outgrowing every local boundary, Theseus shows the opposite process, and shows it with unusual clarity because it happened late, fast, and in a city that kept records: Theseus is, in large part, an Athenian construction. That statement needs care. Theseus is not an invention from nothing — he appears, briefly and marginally, in early epic: the Iliad names him once, in a catalogue of great fighters of a former generation (1.265), and the Odyssey knows his Cretan adventure with Ariadne (11.321–325) and his descent to the underworld with his friend Peirithous (11.631). But in Homer he is a minor figure, a name in other heroes' stories. The Theseus who dominates Athenian art, festival, and self-understanding — the unifier of Attica, the slayer of the Minotaur, the founder of Athenian institutions, the democratic king who (in Euripides' Suppliants) lectures a Theban herald on the virtues of democracy — is the product of the sixth and fifth centuries BCE, built up by Athens as the city's own answer to Heracles.

The Growth of a Cycle

The material record dates the growth. The Metropolitan Museum's survey of Theseus in Athenian art, a standard summary of the iconographic evidence, traces the pattern: the earliest surviving representation of Theseus in art, on the François Vase of c. 570 BCE, already shows the Cretan episode, but depictions remain few before c. 540 BCE; between c. 540 and 525 they multiply, centred on the Minotaur; after c. 525 the repertoire diversifies into a full cycle of deeds — above all the journey from Troezen to Athens along the Saronic Gulf, on which the young Theseus clears the road of a series of murderous brigands: Periphetes the Club-bearer, Sinis the Pine-bender, the Crommyonian sow, Sciron, Cercyon the wrestler, Procrustes of the bed. The structural point is unmistakable, and Plutarch's Life of Theseus states the motive inside the story itself: the young hero, fired by the fame of his cousin Heracles, deliberately chooses the dangerous land route to earn a fame like his, and kills each brigand by the method the brigand had used on travellers. The road cycle is a set of labours, composed on the Heraclean model, for a hero who needed labours. Athens was equipping its founder with the standard heroic curriculum — a generation or two after the democracy's foundation, at exactly the moment when a young, ambitious, newly self-conscious city most needed a founder who could stand beside the Panhellenic Heracles whom Dorian Sparta and other rivals claimed as their own ancestor.

// THE SYNOIKISMOS //

Theseus' greatest political deed, in Athenian tradition, was the synoikismos: the unification of the many settlements of Attica into a single political community centred on Athens, with one council-house and one magistracy. Thucydides (2.15) reports the tradition and treats it as the origin of Athens' unified structure. Whether any historical unification happened under a king named Theseus is unverifiable; what the attribution does is give the unified state a founder, a date in the heroic age, and a charter older than any rival's.

The literary record matches the vases. Bacchylides, writing for Athenian performance in the early fifth century BCE, devoted two dithyrambs (Odes 17 and 18) to Theseus: Ode 17, the Youths or Theseus, dramatises the voyage to Crete and Theseus' dive to the sea floor to prove his descent from Poseidon — a scene vase-painters took up in the same decades — and Ode 18 stages Aegeus in dialogue with a chorus of Athenians as news of the unknown young hero's road-deeds reaches the city. Tragedy then made Theseus the voice of Athens itself: in Euripides' Suppliants (c. 423 BCE) he embodies Athenian democracy, asylum, and the burial of the dead as civic doctrine, and in Sophocles' Oedipus at Colonus (produced posthumously, 401 BCE) he is the just king who receives the polluted outcast and guarantees his grave. None of this is Bronze Age survival. It is a Classical city writing its values backwards into a heroic founder, in full view of the documentary record — which is precisely what makes Theseus the best case study in this archive for how heroic tradition is made.

Two cautions complete the picture. First, “constructed” does not mean “fake” in the sense of a fraud perpetrated on a gullible public: myth-making of this kind was the normal technology by which Greek cities thought about themselves, and the Athenians who promoted Theseus also genuinely worshipped him, as the next section shows. Second, Theseus never displaced Heracles in cult the way he did in Athenian civic imagery; Heracles kept his Attic shrines, and Theseus himself, in the myth, was said to have rededicated most of his own precincts to Heracles on his return from the underworld, keeping only four (Plutarch, Theseus 35.2, citing Philochorus). The rivalry was real but it was a rivalry of emphasis and patronage, conducted by festivals, buildings, and bones.

VII. Cimon, Skyros, and the Bones of Theseus

The promotion of Theseus reached its most concrete act in 476/5 BCE. After the Persian Wars, Athens — now head of the Delian League — consulted Delphi, and the Pythia instructed the Athenians to bring home the bones of Theseus from the island of Skyros, where the hero, exiled from Athens in the myth, had been killed by King Lycomedes, and to bury and guard them honourably in Athens. The general Cimon, son of Miltiades, conquered Skyros from its Dolopian inhabitants in the League's early campaigns (Thucydides 1.98 records the conquest and enslavement) and, in the story told by Plutarch in both the Life of Theseus (36) and the Life of Cimon (8), set himself to find the grave. The account that follows is Plutarch's, in Bernadotte Perrin's Loeb translation, and it is quoted, not paraphrased, so that the reader can see exactly what kind of evidence a hero's relic was.

“Cimon took the island… and being ambitious to discover the grave of Theseus, saw an eagle in a place where there was the semblance of a mound, pecking, as he says, and tearing up the ground with his talons. By some divine ordering he comprehended the meaning of this and dug there, and there was found a coffin of a man of extraordinary size, a bronze spear lying by its side, and a sword.” — Plutarch, Life of Theseus 36.1–2, trans. Bernadotte Perrin (Loeb Classical Library)

Plutarch continues (36.2, in Perrin's translation, paraphrased only in the connective tissue here): the relics were carried to Athens on Cimon's trireme and received “with splendid processions and sacrifices, as though Theseus himself were returning to his city”, and the hero was laid to rest “in the heart of the city, near the present gymnasium”, where his tomb became “a sanctuary and place of refuge for runaway slaves and all men of low estate who are afraid of men in power, since Theseus was a champion and helper of such during his life”. The chief festival, the Theseia, was celebrated on the eighth day of the month Pyanepsion, the anniversary in the myth of Theseus' return from Crete, and Theseus received honours on the eighth day of every month. The shrine — the Theseion — stood somewhere near the Agora, in the direction of the later Gymnasium of Ptolemy (Pausanias 1.17.2–6 describes its paintings); its exact site has never been securely identified. The famous temple above the Agora long called the Theseion is in fact the Temple of Hephaestus, as inscriptions found within it established: the name attached itself to the wrong building in early modern times, because the Hephaisteion's sculpture is full of Theseus — a misattribution this archive notes because it shows the hero's Athenian prominence literally written into the modern map.

The political reading is not modern cynicism; it is written on the face of the sources. Cimon was Athens' leading aristocratic general, a philolaconian conservative whose career was built on the Persian War generation's prestige, and the recovery of Theseus served several legible purposes at once: it obeyed Delphi; it sanctified the annexation of Skyros, a League operation of naked imperial character, by wrapping it in the recovery of a national relic; it gave the young empire's capital a founder's tomb at its centre, of the kind Greek cities treated as a guarantee of divine protection; and it associated Cimon's name with the city's founder at the height of Cimon's career. Plutarch's own Cimon (8.5–7) tells the story as an episode of that career, and the apparition of Theseus in arms at Marathon in 490, which Plutarch (Theseus 35.5) says many soldiers believed they had seen fighting before the Athenian line, shows the hero already militarised in Athenian imagination a decade before Cimon sailed. The bones themselves — a large skeleton, a bronze spearhead, a sword — were presumably a Bronze Age or Early Iron Age burial on Skyros, of the kind the Aegean is full of; nothing about them could identify an individual, and no one in 476 BCE had a method that could. That is not the point, and it was not the point then. A hero's relic does not work by forensic identification. It works because a city, on an oracle's word and a general's discovery, agrees to treat these bones as his — and from that agreement flow the shrine, the festival, the asylum right, and the protection. Section I's distinction is here visible in a single act: epic and civic myth supplied the kleos, the famous name; the bones supplied the place; and the cult — local, ritual, Athenian — began the moment the trireme docked.

// HOW TO READ A RELIC STORY //

Ask three questions. Who benefits, and how soon? (Cimon; immediately.) What independent act does the relic sanctify? (The conquest and cleruchy of Skyros.) And what does the shrine then do in the city? (Asylum for slaves and the powerless; a festival in the civic calendar; a founder's presence at the city's heart.) A relic story that answers all three is not thereby false in its own terms — but it is fully explained as politics and cult, and no residue of it needs a Bronze Age king to account for it.

VIII. Jason and the Argo: A Story Older Than Its Epic

The voyage of the Argo is the clearest case in Greek tradition of a story that everyone already knew. When the Odyssey's Circe describes the Clashing Rocks, she remarks that one ship alone has ever passed them: the Argo, on her way back from Aeetes — and Homer calls her, in a phrase usually rendered “the Argo, known to all” (pasi melousa, Odyssey 12.69–72; paraphrased here). The remark is tossed off in passing, with no explanation of who Jason was or what the Fleece might be, because none was needed: the audience of the Odyssey, whenever exactly in the eighth to seventh centuries BCE we place the poem's formation, was expected to know the whole story already. The Argonautic tradition is therefore older than our earliest complete telling of it by half a millennium. What survives is a scatter of early treatments — the lost Corinthian epic tradition associated with Eumelus (eighth century BCE), the lyric poet Mimnermus' reference to Jason's voyage to Aeetes' city (seventh–sixth century BCE), passing knowledge in Hesiodic poetry — and then, in 462 BCE, Pindar's Pythian Ode 4, the longest and richest early account, composed for Arcesilas IV of Cyrene, whose city claimed descent from the Argonaut Euphemus. Only in the third century BCE does a complete epic of the voyage survive: Apollonius' Argonautica (Section IX). The layers are the point. Jason's story is not a book; it is a tradition that books repeatedly, and differently, crystallise.

The Story the Layers Share

Stripped to the elements most versions share: Jason, son of Aeson, is the rightful heir of Iolcus in Thessaly, usurped by his half-uncle Pelias. Pelias, warned by an oracle to beware a man with one sandal, receives Jason — who arrives with one sandal, having lost the other crossing a river — and sends him on the impossible errand that is meant to kill him: fetch the Golden Fleece from Colchis. The Fleece is the hide of the miraculous flying ram Chrysomallos, which had once rescued the children Phrixus and Helle from their stepmother (Helle fell into the strait still called the Hellespont); Phrixus, reaching Colchis at the eastern end of the Black Sea, sacrificed the ram to Zeus and gave its fleece to King Aeetes, son of Helios the Sun, who hung it on an oak in the grove of Ares, guarded by a dragon that never slept. Jason builds — or has built, by the craftsman Argus, with Athena's help — the Argo, a fifty-oared ship into whose prow Athena sets a speaking timber from the oracle oak of Dodona, and assembles a crew of the heroes of the generation before Troy: Heracles and Hylas, Castor and Pollux, Orpheus, Peleus, Telamon, the winged sons of Boreas, and dozens more. The catalogue of Argonauts is itself a layer-detector: it functions as a muster-roll of Panhellenic heroism, and its membership shifts between sources as different cities and families claimed ancestors aboard.

Colchis matters to the story's geography of wonder. It lies at the world's edge as Archaic Greeks mapped it — the far eastern shore of the Black Sea, in what is now western Georgia — close to the rising of the Sun, whose child its king is, and on the road to the Ocean that rings the earth. Greek colonists and traders were active on the Black Sea coasts from the seventh century BCE, and the Colchis of myth is partly built from that contact: a real, rich, gold-associated land (later writers connect the Fleece itself with the Colchian practice of pegging fleeces in streams to catch alluvial gold — a rationalising explanation recorded by Strabo and Appian, offered here as an ancient guess, not as the myth's established origin) transposed to the edge of the map, where wonders belong. The voyage out passes the Clashing Rocks (the Symplegades) at the mouth of the Black Sea; the tasks at Colchis require yoking fire-breathing bronze-hooved bulls, ploughing the field of Ares, sowing dragon's teeth, and destroying the earthborn warriors who spring from them; and none of it is achievable by Jason's strength. It is achievable because Aeetes' daughter falls in love with him. That dependence is the hinge on which the whole tradition, and especially its greatest retelling, turns.

IX. Apollonius: A Scholar Rewrites the Voyage

Apollonius of Rhodes — Apollonius Rhodius — was a scholar-poet of Ptolemaic Alexandria, associated with the great Library, in the tradition that made him a pupil of Callimachus and, in some accounts, Callimachus' rival. His Argonautica, in four books of Homeric hexameters, is generally placed in the mid-third century BCE (the precise dating, and the ancient story of a failed first recitation and a revised second edition produced in exile on Rhodes, rest on late biographical tradition and are treated by modern scholars with caution). It is the only Hellenistic epic to survive complete, and its character is the opposite of the oral tradition that stands behind Homer: this is a literary epic, composed in writing, by a researcher, for readers who knew Homer by heart and expected to catch the allusions. Apollonius' poem is dense with learned geography, ethnography, aetiology — explanations of how places, rites, and customs got their names and origins — and with Homeric language deliberately varied, corrected, and recombined. To read it after the Iliad is to watch a tradition become self-conscious.

Its boldest choices are structural. Heracles, the tradition's greatest hero, sails with the expedition for part of Book 1 — and is then left behind, marooned in Mysia searching for his lost companion Hylas, abducted by a spring nymph, while the Argo sails on without him (1.1207–1357). The removal is programmatic: the old heroism of brute force literally cannot complete this voyage, and the poem says so through its plot. Jason, by contrast, is characterised by what scholars call his amēchania — helplessness, resourcelessness: he despairs at each crisis, is chosen as leader as much for diplomacy as for prowess, and succeeds through counsel, through the crew's collective skills, and decisively through Medea. Book 3, the poem's centrepiece, belongs to her: Hera and Athena enlist Aphrodite, whose son Eros shoots Medea with desire; Apollonius then renders her interior conflict — love against loyalty to her father, shame against longing — in an extended psychological portrait that ancient epic had never attempted at this scale, before she meets Jason by night at Hecate's temple and gives him the drug, the Prometheion, that will make him invulnerable for the day of the tasks, together with the tactical knowledge (the thrown stone that sets the earthborn warriors fighting each other) that wins the field. In Book 4 she charms the dragon to sleep, flees with the Fleece, and — in Apollonius' version — lures her young brother Apsyrtus to his death at Jason's hands, a murder whose pollution drives the poem's haunted return voyage, by routes (up the Danube, down the Rhone, past Circe's island) that follow Hellenistic geographical speculation rather than any sailor's chart.

// A LIBRARY EPIC //

Apollonius wrote for the first culture in Greek history that possessed Homer as a text on a shelf, edited and commented in the same institution where Apollonius worked. The Argonautica is therefore evidence for Section XIII's endgame: by the third century BCE, epic is no longer only sung tradition but a scholarly object — and a poet can build a new epic out of footnotes to the old ones, expecting his audience to hear both layers at once.

The prose summary in Apollodorus (Library 1.9.1–28) and the Roman retellings — Valerius Flaccus' unfinished Latin Argonautica, Ovid's Medea episodes — descend from the same layered tradition, each selecting differently. The methodological moral is the one this hub applies everywhere: when sources disagree about the Argonauts — the crew list, the route home, the manner of Apsyrtus' death (dismembered and scattered by Medea to delay pursuit, in one strand; ambushed as a grown pursuer, in Apollonius) — the disagreement is not a problem to be solved by picking a winner. It is the tradition's normal state, visible because, unusually, several of its layers survive side by side.

X. Medea: The Transformation of a Figure

Medea is the test case for everything this article has claimed about the fluidity of heroic tradition, because her most famous act — the deliberate murder of her own children — can be shown, on surviving evidence, to be a late and probably invented element that later ages mistook for the myth itself. The earlier Medea is a different figure: a princess of Colchis, granddaughter of the Sun, a pharmakis — a worker with drugs and herbs — whose defining power in early tradition is as often beneficent as terrible. In Thessalian and Boeotian tradition she rejuvenates the aged — Jason's father Aeson in one strand, the ram in the famous cauldron demonstration in another — and the Corinthian tradition treated her as a queen of Corinth by inheritance from her father Aeetes, who had held the land from Helios, and as a benefactor of the city. Hesiod's Theogony (992–1002) ends its catalogue with Jason bringing Medea home as his bride, with no shadow on her. The monster of the later stage is a construction, and its construction can be dated and its builders named.

What Happened to the Children, Before Euripides

The death of Medea's children at Corinth was told, before the fifth century BCE, in at least two forms in which Medea does not kill them in revenge. In the version associated with Eumelus' Corinthian epic, Medea, hoping to make her children immortal, hid them — buried them, in the scholiast's summary — in the sanctuary of Hera at Corinth, and they died: a mother's catastrophic ritual error, not a murder. In the version associated with the epic poet Creophylus, and preserved in the ancient scholarly notes (the scholia) on Euripides' play, Medea fled Corinth after the killing of King Creon and left her children as suppliants on the altar of Hera Akraia; there the Corinthians — in some accounts the relatives of Creon — killed them, and then spread the report that Medea herself had done it. Pausanias, visiting Corinth in the second century CE, was still shown the tomb of Medea's sons Mermerus and Pheres and records (2.3.6–11) that they were stoned to death by the Corinthians, that infant deaths in Corinth were afterwards attributed to their angry ghosts, and that the city maintained expiatory rites — annual sacrifices and a grim statue of Fear (Deima) — until the Roman destruction of Corinth ended them. A Corinthian cultic aetiology, in other words, preserved for six centuries a version in which Corinth, not Medea, bore the guilt — and paid for it annually.

In 431 BCE Euripides staged his Medea at Athens, and in his play Medea, abandoned by Jason for a Corinthian royal marriage, deliberately kills her two sons herself, as the supreme injury she can inflict on their father, and escapes in the chariot of her grandfather the Sun. The ancient scholarly tradition already recognised the filicide as the play's innovation: the scholium on Medea 264 reports the older Corinthian version and treats Euripides' as the departure, and a later anecdote — that the Corinthians bribed Euripides with five talents to transfer the guilt to Medea — is preserved in the same scholarly tradition and is recorded here as what it is, an anecdote about the play's reception, not a document of a transaction. One scholarly complication must be stated fairly: fragments of a Medea by a tragedian named Neophron also contained the filicide, and it has been argued that Neophron, not Euripides, invented it; the prevailing view, following D. L. Page's edition of Euripides' play, is that Neophron's fragments are later than 431 BCE and imitate Euripides, so that the invention stands with Euripides unless new evidence appears. Either way, the direction of the evidence is unambiguous: a motif absent from the earlier tradition appears in an Athenian tragedy of 431 BCE and thereafter becomes canonical, carried by Seneca, Ovid's lost Medea and his surviving treatments, and the whole later tradition, until “Medea killed her children” reads as the myth's timeless core. Diodorus Siculus (4.55) already complained, in the first century BCE, that the tragic poets' love of the marvellous had made the accounts of Medea inconsistent — an ancient witness to the process this section describes.

// WHY THIS CASE MATTERS //

Medea's filicide is the strongest available demonstration that a “well-known myth” may be a datable literary invention — by a named author, for a named festival, in a named year — which outcompeted the older local tradition in the Panhellenic medium (Athenian tragedy, then the school and library tradition) while the older version survived only in a local cult's expiatory rites and in scholars' footnotes. Epic and drama confer kleos; cult preserves timē and, with it, sometimes the older story. Section I's two institutions are not an abstraction. At Corinth they kept two different Medeas alive for six hundred years.

XI. The Homeric Question: Analysts and Unitarians

The Iliad (roughly 15,700 lines) and the Odyssey (roughly 12,100 lines) are poems in dactylic hexameter, in an artificial poetic dialect — a Kunstsprache mixing Ionic and Aeolic forms of different periods, never spoken by anyone — set in a Bronze Age world they describe with a mixture of genuine memory, archaism, and anachronism. Antiquity attributed both to a single blind singer, Homer, about whom the ancient biographical tradition (the Lives of Homer, the contest of Homer and Hesiod) preserves legend, not evidence: no contemporary record of such a person exists, and seven cities' rival claims to his birthplace tell us about the cities, not about him. The Homeric Question is the scholarly name for the problem the poems themselves pose: how did works of this scale, unity, and linguistic peculiarity come into existence, and when, and by whom or by what process? Stated honestly at the outset: the Question has been under continuous investigation for over two centuries, parts of it have been genuinely solved, and its centre — the exact process by which these two poems were composed and fixed — remains debated. This section and the next two give the history and the present state of play, without pretending to a consensus that does not exist.

The modern debate opens with F. A. Wolf's Prolegomena ad Homerum (1795). Wolf argued, from the history of writing in early Greece and from internal evidence, that poems of this length could not have been composed and preserved in the form we have them without writing, that they were therefore likely assembled from shorter songs composed by Homer and expanded by successors (rhapsodes), and that their text was edited into something like its present shape only in the sixth century BCE. The nineteenth century split over his legacy into two camps whose names still organise the history. The Analysts — Karl Lachmann's “lay theory” is the type specimen — dissected the poems into originally independent short songs (lays) stitched together by editors, and treated inconsistencies of plot, language, and material culture as seams between the pieces. The Unitarians argued from the poems' large-scale architecture — the Iliad's wrath-plot announced in its first lines and discharged in its last book, the Odyssey's interlaced double plot — that a single designing intelligence must stand behind each poem, and treated the same inconsistencies as the ordinary tolerances of a long work. Both camps amassed real evidence; both, by the early twentieth century, had reached positions the other could not refute, because both were describing the same poems with a shared false assumption: that composition meant writing, and that the only alternatives were one author with a pen or many authors with scissors.

XII. Parry, Lord, and the Singers of Tales

The false assumption broke in the late 1920s, in the work of a young American scholar, Milman Parry (1902–1935). In his Paris theses of 1928 and the papers that followed, Parry analysed the most obvious peculiarity of Homeric style: its dense system of formulae — repeated phrases, above all the noun-epithet combinations (“swift-footed Achilles”, “much-enduring divine Odysseus”, “rosy-fingered Dawn”) that recur hundreds of times. Parry's definition of the formula, as standardly cited from his work, is “a group of words which is regularly employed under the same metrical conditions to express a given essential idea”. His demonstration had two steps. First, the system displays economy or thrift: for a given hero and a given metrical slot, the tradition generally keeps exactly one formula — a parsimony no individual stylist imposes on himself, but exactly what a tradition of singers, composing in performance at speed, needs and maintains. Second, the system's scale and consistency exceed any single lifetime: it must be the accumulated instrument of generations of singers. Homeric diction, Parry concluded, is a traditional language for verse-making, and the poems composed in it are, in their technique, oral poetry — whether or not the particular performances that became our texts were ever written down at the moment of composition.

Parry then did the thing that separates his work from armchair theory: he went to watch oral epic being made. In 1933–1935, with his assistant Albert B. Lord (1912–1991), he travelled through Bosnia, Herzegovina, Montenegro, and neighbouring regions of what was then Yugoslavia, recording the singers called guslari — performers who sang heroic songs to the one-stringed gusle — on aluminium discs and in dictation, building the collection now held at Harvard as the Milman Parry Collection of Oral Literature. The fieldwork was designed as an experiment: if the formulaic theory was right, living singers should compose the way Parry said Homer composed — not by memorising fixed texts, but by recombining formulae and typical scenes (themes: assemblies, arming, feasts, journeys) in performance, producing a song that is the same story and never the same wording twice. That is what the recordings showed. Singers insisted they repeated songs “word for word” while demonstrably varying them; a song's length expanded or contracted with the occasion; and the tradition's master singer, Avdo Međedović, dictated and sang epics of Homeric scale — his Wedding of Smailagić Meho runs to over 12,000 lines — proving that length alone is no argument against oral composition. Parry died in an accident in 1935, at thirty-three, with the synthesis unwritten. Lord returned to Yugoslavia in 1935 and again in the 1950s, re-recording the same singers, and in 1960 published the book Parry had projected: The Singer of Tales, still the classic statement of the oral-formulaic account of Homer.

// WHAT THE FIELDWORK DID AND DID NOT PROVE //

The South Slavic analogy demonstrates a possibility, powerfully: long heroic epic can be composed orally, by formulaic technique, without writing. It does not, by itself, prove that the Iliad was so composed — analogy is not identity, and scholars including John Miles Foley, who did more than anyone to develop oral-tradition studies after Lord, insisted that comparison must always be tempered by contrast. The Greek case rests on the Greek evidence — the formulaic system itself, the Kunstsprache, the poems' textual behaviour — with Yugoslavia as the control that shows what such evidence means.

The effect on the Homeric Question was to dissolve its original form. “One poet or many?” turned out to be a badly put question about an oral tradition, in which a monumental poem can be both traditional in every phrase and the creation of a single singer's single performance — the position Lord himself reached, holding that the Iliad and the Odyssey as we have them are each, in Parry's late phrase, the finished work of a single singer, orally composed and taken down by dictation. Nagy's work added the dimension of time: a Panhellenic tradition, performed at festivals before audiences from many cities, progressively sheds local variants and converges — over centuries of re-performance — toward the relatively fixed texts we inherit, a model Nagy calls the evolutionary formation of the Homeric poems, in which “Homer” names the tradition's mythical embodiment as much as any historical man. Analysts' seams and Unitarians' architecture both find explanations in this framework: the seams are the tradition's strata; the architecture is what a master singer, or a tradition converging under Panhellenic performance conditions, can achieve.

XIII. Where the Question Stands: Oral-Derived Texts and the Fixation Debate

The current scholarly middle position — “middle” not because it splits a difference but because it is where the evidence has pushed most working Homerists — can be stated in four propositions, with the confidence level of each marked.

First, the poems are oral-derived. John Miles Foley's term oral-derived text names a text whose features descend from oral tradition, whatever the exact circumstances of its writing-down. That the Iliad and Odyssey are oral-derived in diction, composition technique, and narrative grammar is as close to consensus as the field produces. Pure Analyst compilation and pure pen-and-paper authorship are both minority positions now.

Second, the poems took shape in the period c. 750–650 BCE. The Iliad is generally placed earlier in that range and the Odyssey somewhat later; linguistic arguments (notably Richard Janko's statistical study of the epic dialect's development) and historical arguments (the poems' material world, their references and silences) converge on the later eighth to later seventh century as the era of monumental composition. Individual scholars argue for dates outside the range — earlier for more of the tradition's substance, later for the final shaping — and the honest citation is the range with the debate named, as here. What the range dates is the poems' formation, not their materials: the tradition behind them demonstrably contains much older elements, including names, objects (the boar's-tusk helmet of Iliad 10 is the famous case), and story-patterns reaching back toward the Bronze Age whose world the poems remember and misremember at once.

Third, the route from performance to text is debated, and the debate is real. The main candidates are these. Dictation: a master singer, in the era when alphabetic writing was newly available in Greece, dictated his monumental song to a scribe, the dictation itself permitting a length and finish beyond ordinary performance (Lord's position, defended with linguistic arguments by Janko). Gradual textualisation: the poems were written down at some point and then continued to evolve in performance and transmission for generations, crystallising only slowly (Nagy's evolutionary model, in which relative fixity increases over time and full fixation is late). Authorial writing: a literate poet composed with writing from the start, using the traditional technique as a style (a minority but persistent position). The surviving evidence does not at present decide between these, and responsible accounts — this one included — say so.

Fourth, the Athenian chapter of the story is tradition, not established fact. Ancient sources report that the tyrant Peisistratus, or his son Hipparchus, in sixth-century BCE Athens, had the Homeric poems collected, arranged, or regulated: Cicero (De oratore 3.137) credits Peisistratus with arranging the previously disordered books of Homer; the pseudo-Platonic Hipparchus (228b) credits Hipparchus with bringing the poems to Athens and requiring the rhapsodes at the Panathenaia to perform them in sequence, each taking up where the last left off; later sources expand the story into a full editorial commission — the so-called Peisistratean recension. Modern scholarship treats this material with disciplined suspicion. The Panathenaic performance rule is plausible and may well be historical in some form; a sixth-century Athenian edition that created our text is not demonstrated, the sources for it are late and mutually inconsistent, and J. A. Davison's classic 1955 examination of the evidence remains the standing warning against building on it. The recension is therefore presented in this archive as what it is: an ancient tradition about the text's fixation, possibly preserving a memory of Athenian regulation of performance, and not a documented event. What is documented is the sequel: the Alexandrian scholars of the third and second centuries BCE — Zenodotus of Ephesus, Aristophanes of Byzantium, and Aristarchus of Samothrace — produced editions and commentaries, devised critical signs for suspect lines, and debated variant readings line by line; the division of each poem into twenty-four books is generally credited to this scholarly world; and the medieval manuscripts, above all the tenth-century CE Venetus A of the Iliad with its marginal scholia preserving Alexandrian scholarship, transmit the text from which all modern editions descend.

// THE QUESTION, ANSWERED AT THE LEVEL THE EVIDENCE ALLOWS //

Who wrote the Iliad? A tradition of singers, over generations, in a formulaic technique built for composition in performance — brought to monumental form, most scholars would say, by one or two master singers in the period c. 750–650 BCE, and fixed in writing by a route (dictation, gradual crystallisation, or some combination) that scholarship has not settled. That sentence is less satisfying than “Homer, a blind genius, wrote it” and less tidy than “a committee compiled it”. It has the advantage of being what the evidence, read whole, supports.

The heroes of this article lived, then, in both of the worlds its title names, and the two worlds explain each other. Hero cult gave the dead a local address and a community's continuing care; epic gave them kleos without borders, at the price of the tradition's freedom to remake them — to fix Heracles' labours at twelve for a temple's sake, to build Theseus into Athens' founder for a city's sake, to turn Medea into a child-murderer for a tragedy's sake. The Homeric Question is simply the largest case of the same process, studied at the level of the text itself: two poems made by a tradition, carrying heroes made by cults and cities, fixed at last by scholars — and still, in every generation including this one, being argued back open. For the gods whose order frames the heroes' world, see Theogony and the Succession Myth and The Olympians; for the cults in which heroes and gods alike were approached for healing and revelation, see Mysteries and Healing Cults.
